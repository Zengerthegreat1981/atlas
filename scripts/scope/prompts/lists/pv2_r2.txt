# The Spiritual and the Secular

Does existential therapy have a spiritual dimension, or does admitting one betray the project? The dispute weighs heavier in the Arab context than in the context where it arose — because the school grew up in a secular setting where the question was optional, and is carried into a setting where a living religious framework is the norm rather than the exception.

## The case for opening

Viktor Frankl gives the human being a spiritual dimension of its own, one that does not fall ill and cannot be reduced to the psychological and the bodily. This is the core of his break with Freud, and the foundation of the meaning-centred school he built after his experience in the Nazi concentration camps, set out in *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946). For Frankl, the spiritual dimension is where freedom, responsibility, and the search for meaning live; to ignore it is to amputate the person.

Emmy van Deurzen reaches a similar place from a different direction: her four dimensions of existence include a fourth, spiritual dimension — values, belief, and larger meaning — alongside the physical, social, and personal. Both hold that ignoring this dimension cuts something essential out of the human being, and that a therapy which cannot speak of values and ultimate meaning cannot speak of the things that actually bring people to therapy.

Paul Tillich belongs on this side too: in *The Courage to Be* (1952) he treated faith as a matter of ultimate concern, and described anxiety in three forms — of death, of meaninglessness, and of guilt.

## The case for refusal

Existential philosophy in its origins arose against the collapse of metaphysical guarantees. Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) makes the point in its starkest form: existence precedes essence, there is no given human nature and no transcendent guarantor, and the human being is condemned to freedom. From this side, reintroducing transcendence through the back door of a "spiritual dimension" empties the project — it restores, in therapeutic dress, exactly the guarantees the philosophy set out to live without.

The refusal is not hostility to religion as a human fact. It is a claim about method: the therapist's task is to stay with the client's freedom and finitude, not to supply a framework that resolves them. A spiritual dimension, on this view, risks turning therapy into pastoral care, and the therapist into a guide towards meanings the client has not chosen.

## What it changes in the room

Three very different practices branch out of this dispute. May the therapist work on the client's religious belief as a resource — drawing on prayer, scripture, or communal belonging as genuine therapeutic material? Or treat it as any other content, neither privileged nor excluded, describing it phenomenologically like everything else? Or avoid it, steering the work back to secular ground whenever belief enters the room? A therapist's answer to the philosophical question decides which of these three they actually do, often without noticing the decision has been made.

## Why it concerns us more

The school arose in a secular context where the question was optional, and it is carried into a context where the religious frame is the original setting, not the exception. Importing the dispute unchanged, without rethinking it, is exactly what this Atlas warns against. A tradition of this question already exists here: al-Ghazali, in *Ihya Ulum al-Din* (late eleventh century), treated the examination of the soul and its diseases of the heart as an inseparable part of the religious system, not an optional addition — the reverse of the order Frankl started from.

## What has not yet been written

As far as we know, there is no sufficient systematic work on how existential therapy is practised inside a living religious framework rather than as a cultural background. That is a gap an Arab researcher could fill — by describing what actually happens when therapist and client share a religious world and work existentially within it, and what changes when they do not share it.

## What follows in practice

If you favour spiritual openness (Frankl, Tillich, Marcel), you allow the religious and spiritual dimension to be a legitimate source of meaning in the session, and you let a devout client use their framework of faith as an authentic therapeutic resource rather than a topic to be set aside or handled with cold clinical neutrality. This suits many clients with strong religious backgrounds, but it risks the therapist sliding — deliberately or not — into a preaching or missionary stance that imposes or favours their own religious outlook.

If you favour the strict secular position (Sartre and most of the French current), you keep complete neutrality towards religious belief and treat meaning as a purely human construction, independent of any transcendent reference. This protects against imposing any particular religious frame in a religiously diverse therapeutic setting. The risk is that a devout client feels their most important frame of knowledge is excluded or treated with implicit doubt, which weakens the therapeutic alliance.

In the Arab context in particular, this dispute is the most practically urgent one for an Arab therapist, since the great majority of clients carry an active religious frame in their daily lives — and adopting the strict secular position without awareness of this context may distance the therapist from their clients more than it brings them closer.

# Structures or None?

Are there fixed existential structures? The dispute takes place inside the British current itself, and though it looks technical, its consequences run deep. The question is whether human existence has general structures — dimensions or concerns that every person lives in — whose knowledge makes training, guidance, and organisation possible, or whether any general structure imposed on experience betrays the phenomenological method at its root. The argument explains why two British texts from the same school can read as if they came from two different schools, and why it is so difficult to write a single introduction to the British current.

## The First Position — Structures Exist

The first position holds that there are general structures of human existence, and that knowing them is what makes therapy teachable. Emmy van Deurzen formulated the four dimensions of existence — physical, social, personal, and spiritual — in *Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy in Practice* (1988; 3rd ed., 2012) as a systematic training tool, giving new therapists a map of the territories in which a client's difficulties can appear. Irvin Yalom, in *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980), offered a parallel structure: four ultimate concerns — death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness — that every person must confront. Karl Jaspers, earlier, had described four limit situations (*Grenzsituationen*) — death, suffering, struggle, and guilt — in *Philosophy* (1932) as the boundaries where ordinary life breaks down. In each version, the claim is the same: beneath cultural and personal variation, human beings face the same givens, and a therapist who knows them can orient herself, teach others, and compare cases.

## The Second Position — No Structures

The second position holds that any general structure imposed on experience betrays the phenomenological method itself. Ernesto Spinelli, in *Practising Existential Psychotherapy: The Relational World* (2007), argues that therapy is entirely relational: what matters is what appears in this relationship, now, without a prior map. A four-dimensional scheme, however useful, tells the therapist what to look for before the client has spoken, and thereby replaces description with classification. The strict phenomenologists in the British current insist that the therapist must stay with what shows itself in this particular encounter, and that the work is relational through and through. The gain is fidelity to experience in its singularity; the cost is that teaching becomes difficult, because there is no stable content to transmit, and comparison becomes nearly impossible, because there are no fixed categories to compare.

## The Real Question Beneath It

The underlying question is whether systematic teaching is possible without classification. If the answer is no, then the school is condemned to be an individual art that cannot be passed on — each therapist must reinvent it alone. If the answer is yes, then the school has accepted some measure of the very "imposition" it criticises in other approaches. The dispute also has a cross-cultural edge: those who affirm fixed structures assume that a framework such as Yalom's four concerns can travel across cultures and be measured comparatively, while those who reject structures regard any such comparison as a distortion of local experience. Research on existential anxiety or meaning across cultures is therefore possible on one side of the debate and incoherent on the other.

## Contemporary Status

The dispute remains unresolved, and it structures the British current from within. Van Deurzen's dimensional model dominates training, because training needs a curriculum; Spinelli's relational model dominates the theoretical conscience of the school, because it states most purely what the phenomenology was meant to be. Practitioners typically move between the two, using dimensions as a background orientation while insisting that no dimension be imposed on a particular client. The tension is productive rather than paralysing: it keeps the school honest about the gap between its maps and the territory, and it explains why the British current, more than any other, has produced both the most systematic textbooks and the most radical critiques of systematisation in existential therapy.

The structures debate also decides what a textbook of existential therapy can be. A book organised around fixed dimensions can be written once and taught everywhere, with exercises, case examples, and assessment criteria attached to each dimension — which is why van Deurzen's textbook has run through three editions and become the standard training text. A book faithful to the no-structures position cannot offer that: Spinelli's work is instead a sustained reflection on the therapeutic relationship, with a structural model that he insists is a heuristic rather than a map of reality. The difference is visible on the shelf, and it is one reason the two authors, though colleagues and co-founders of the same institutions, are cited for opposite lessons about what existential therapy is.

# Thomism versus Scotism: Intellect or Will?

One of the greatest debates of Latin scholasticism, between the **Dominicans** (the Thomist branch) and the **Franciscans** (the Scotist branch). The core question: what is primary in the human being: the intellect (Intellectus) or the will (Voluntas)?

## The first position: Thomism (intellect first)

- **Thomas Aquinas** (1225-1274), in the *Summa Theologiae* (Part I-II, Question 9, written 1265-1274): the human intellect is higher than the will, because it "participates" in the divine intellect. The will "follows" the intellect (ratio boni).
- **Ethics**: the intellectual (prudential) virtue is higher than the moral virtue, because it "directs" the will.
- **Theology**: God's attributes are rationally "intelligible", even if the human intellect is deficient.
- **Politics**: natural law is "rational" (Lex Naturalis Rationalis).

## The second position: Scotism (will first)

- **Duns Scotus** (1266-1308), in the *Ordinatio* (Book II, Distinction 25, lectures at Oxford and Paris around 1300): the will is higher than the intellect, because the "supreme good" is not grasped rationally but is willed voluntarily.
- **Ethics**: the moral virtue may be higher than the intellectual, because it is willed "for its own sake".
- **Theology**: God's attributes are "voluntary" (Voluntas Divina) and not only intellectual. God's will is "absolute", not limited by the intellect.
- **Politics**: natural law may be "separable" (Abstrahendo a Deo) in some of its aspects.

## The main points of difference

- **Human freedom**: Aquinas (in most of his currents) says: "freedom = rational choice" (Libertas = electio rationalis). Scotus: "freedom = spontaneous will" (Libertas = voluntas spontanea).
- **Truth and necessity**: Aquinas: "truth is necessary" (Ens et verum convertuntur). Scotus: "some truths may be 'contingent' (Contingentia) in the divine intellect".
- **Divine justice**: Aquinas: "justice = granting the good to each according to his desert". Scotus: "justice = a free divine voluntary decision".

## Why does the debate matter?

1. **In political philosophy**: Scotism paved the way for William of Ockham (nominalism), for the Franciscan Spirituals, and for Conciliarism (the theory of church councils above the Pope).
2. **In modernity**: Kant (voluntary duty) and the philosophy of the will (Schopenhauer) are indebted to Scotism, not to Thomism.
3. **In liberation theology**: Gustavo Gutiérrez (liberation theology) resumed Thomism (social justice).
4. **In contemporary Islamic philosophy**: Muhammad Abduh, Mustafa Abd al-Raziq and others tried to resume Thomism.

## Sources

- Aquinas, Thomas. *Summa Theologiae*. 1265-1274.
- Duns Scotus, John. *Ordinatio*. around 1300.
- Cross, Richard. *Duns Scotus*. Oxford University Press, 1999.

# The Unconscious Question

Is there an unconscious? The dispute looks theoretical, but its practical effect is direct in every session. The question divides existential therapy from psychoanalysis at the root: whether human beings harbour hidden mental contents that drive their behaviour, or whether everything relevant is present in awareness, including the ways people avoid knowing what they know. The rejection of the unconscious was one of existential philosophy's sharpest breaks with Freud, and the debate over whether that rejection can be sustained has shaped the relationship between the two traditions ever since.

## The Position of Rejection

Jean-Paul Sartre argued in *Being and Nothingness* (1943) that consciousness is transparent to itself: nothing is hidden from me. What looks like repression is *mauvaise foi*, bad faith — I know, and I avoid knowing that I know. The move is logical as well as descriptive: an unconscious that "knows" something and hides it from the same person is, for Sartre, a contradiction. Medard Boss went further in *Psychoanalysis and Daseinsanalysis* (1957; English translation, 1963): the unconscious is a theoretical construction that is never given in experience, and nothing we observe requires it. For Boss, what psychoanalysis calls unconscious material is better described as aspects of a person's being-in-the-world that have been closed off or left unarticulated — a description of openness and its narrowing, not a hidden storehouse. Ludwig Binswanger, in *Basic Forms and the Knowledge of Human Existence* (1942), likewise built his analysis of patients' worlds without a dynamic unconscious, treating disturbed existence as a constriction of the possible worlds a person can inhabit.

## The Modified Acceptance

Contemporary currents inside existential therapy itself, and on its borders with psychoanalysis, argue that wholesale rejection of the unconscious leaves real phenomena unexplained: recurring patterns the client genuinely cannot see, dreams that disclose what waking reflection does not, defences that operate before any choice is made. The middle formulations propose that what is "unconscious" is not a cellar full of repressed contents but what has not yet been articulated, or what is implicit in a way of being rather than stored in a vault. This preserves the existential emphasis on lived experience while allowing the therapist to work with what the client does not yet know. It also brings existential practice closer to psychoanalysis, making it easier to teach to those who come from an analytic background, and easier to defend against the charge that it ignores the most stubborn features of human suffering.

## The Practical Effect

The two positions produce entirely different rooms. A therapist who rejects the unconscious works on what the client is avoiding now, and on the client's responsibility for that avoidance: the language is that of choice, evasion, and bad faith, and the work is direct, present, and confrontational. A therapist who accepts the unconscious works on what the client does not know in the first place and on what emerges over time: dreams, free association, and recurring patterns become central, and the therapist's interpretations carry a weight the first model refuses. The difference also decides training: whether a trainee needs instruction in dream analysis and free association at all, or whether skills of presence and direct encounter are enough.

## The Danger on Both Sides

Each position carries its own risk, and the debate survives because both risks are real. Refusing the unconscious can slide into blaming the client for what they do not control, treating a genuine blind spot as a wilful evasion. Accepting it can slide into removing the client's responsibility for what they do control, treating every choice as the product of forces beneath awareness. The contemporary status is a tense coexistence: the strict Sartrean rejection remains the school's philosophical signature, while in practice many existential therapists work with a softened, phenomenological version of the unconscious — the unformulated rather than the repressed — and the boundary between that and psychoanalysis remains the most porous in the tradition.

The debate also colours the school's view of its own history. Existential therapy defined itself against psychoanalysis partly by rejecting the unconscious, and the rejection became part of its identity — a badge of philosophical seriousness. That makes the middle position psychologically difficult inside the school: to accept even a softened unconscious feels like a concession to the tradition it broke from. Yet the break was never clean. Binswanger himself had been a psychoanalyst, Boss trained in Freudian analysis, and the early existential therapists knew Freud's texts intimately. The unconscious debate is therefore also a debate about the school's origins: whether its founding gesture of refusal should be preserved as doctrine or treated as a historical moment that the school has outgrown.

# Frankl's Concentration Camp Experience

Between 1942 and 1945, the Viennese psychiatrist Viktor Frankl (1905–1997) was imprisoned in a series of Nazi concentration camps, lost his wife, his parents, and his brother, and observed — under conditions of extreme deprivation — the ways in which prisoners held on to, or lost, a sense of meaning. This is the founding event of logotherapy, the meaning-centred approach to psychotherapy, and the raw material of Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning*. It is recorded here as a historical event: what happened, when, and where. The first-person account of how Frankl lived it from the inside belongs to a different entry (exp-frankl-lived-experience).

## What Happened, When and Where

On 25 September 1942, Frankl was deported from Vienna with his wife Tilly Grosser and his parents, Gabriel and Elsa Frankl, to the Theresienstadt ghetto and camp in occupied Czechoslovakia. His father died there in 1943. On 19 October 1944, Frankl and his wife were transported to Auschwitz-Birkenau, where they were separated on arrival; he never saw her again. His mother was murdered at Auschwitz. Frankl himself spent only a few days at Auschwitz before being selected for transfer to forced labour: he arrived at Kaufering III, a subcamp of the Dachau system, on 25 October 1944, and worked there as a slave labourer for about five months. In March 1945 he was moved to the Türkheim subcamp, also in the Dachau system, where he worked as a physician. The camp was liberated by American troops on 27 April 1945. Meanwhile his wife Tilly had been transferred to Bergen-Belsen, where she died in 1945, and his brother Walter also perished. Of his immediate family, only Frankl himself and his sister Stella — who had emigrated to Australia before the deportations — survived.

The dates matter because the popular telling often compresses the story. Frankl was not in Auschwitz for years; his time there was a transit of days, and most of his imprisonment was spent in Theresienstadt and in the Dachau subcamps of Kaufering and Türkheim.

## What Frankl Observed

Frankl's central observation, made while he was still a prisoner, was that the capacity to endure seemed to depend less on physical strength than on whether a prisoner could hold on to a personal meaning: a person to return to, a task left unfinished, a conviction that suffering could still be met with dignity. He later described prisoners who gave up on such a meaning as dying more quickly, and those who kept one as surviving longer — not because meaning protected the body, but because it organised the will to go on living. He also observed the opposite with equal honesty: that some prisoners with every reason to live died, and that survival depended on luck, health, age, and sheer chance as well as on attitude. The observation is therefore presented here as Frankl's own, drawn from his experience and later developed into a theory, not as an established causal finding. Modern readers should not turn it into a rule — that those who died simply lacked meaning — which would blame the victims for their own murder. Frankl himself resisted that reading.

## What Followed: The Book and the School

Before his deportation, Frankl had hidden the manuscript of his first book on meaning in his coat; it was confiscated on his arrival at Auschwitz and never recovered. On his return to Vienna after liberation, Frankl learned the full extent of his family's deaths. Within days he began dictating a new version of the lost manuscript from memory, adding a direct account of his camp experience, and the work was completed in nine days of dictation in 1945. It appeared in German in 1946 and in English in 1959 as *Man's Search for Meaning*.

From the same experience grew logotherapy and existential analysis as a school: the claim that the primary human motivation is the will to meaning, that meaning can be found in creative work, in love and encounter, and in the attitude taken toward unavoidable suffering, and that the therapist's task is to help the patient discover the meaning waiting in their own situation. The Viktor Frankl Institute in Vienna, founded in 1992, later became the institutional home of this teaching.

## Why an Event, Not a Biography

This entry records the event itself — a dated, located sequence of deportations, transfers, deaths, and liberation — rather than the life of Viktor Frankl as a whole (thk-frankl) or his inner experience of it (exp-frankl-lived-experience). The distinction is deliberate: the event is what happened in the world between 1942 and 1945, verifiable against transport lists, camp records, and the accounts of other survivors, while the experience entry answers a different question, how Frankl lived it from the inside. Keeping the two apart protects both: the history is not softened into a personal story, and the personal story is not flattened into a chronology.

## A Methodological Note

This is a documented historical account, not an inspirational use of Frankl's suffering. The camps were sites of systematic murder and cruelty, and Frankl's survival was exceptional; most of the people deported alongside him did not survive, whatever meaning they held on to. The meaning Frankl found is inseparable from the specific, terrible context in which he found it, and any use of his story that forgets that context misuses it.

# Frankl's First-Person Account of Meaning in the Camp

Viktor Frankl's account of his years in the Nazi concentration camps is not a history of those camps. It is a description of what it was like, from the inside, to go on finding meaning when nearly everything that makes a life liveable had been taken away. First published in German in 1946 and in English in 1959 as *Man's Search for Meaning*, the book is the raw material on which Frankl built logotherapy — and a clear practical example of the phenomenological method: describe experience as it is lived, before theorising about it.

## The Difference from the Historical Event

The companion entry on the camps themselves (evt-frankl-camps) records the facts: when Frankl was arrested, which camps he was sent to, what happened there. This entry records something else — the texture of the experience as Frankl himself described it. The distinction matters because the two kinds of knowledge answer different questions. History tells us what was done to the prisoners; Frankl's first-person account tells us what a prisoner could still do within himself while it was being done. That inner record is what makes this entry the foundation of an entire therapeutic school rather than a memoir.

Frankl was careful about the status of his account: it was a witness's report, not a scientific study, and he warned against generalising from his own survival. What he offered was what he noticed, in himself and in his fellow prisoners, about how human beings endure — and sometimes fail to endure — extreme suffering.

## Remembering His Wife

The most famous passage in the book describes a forced march at dawn, in bitter cold, when Frankl's thoughts turned to his wife, Tilly. He did not know whether she was alive — she was not; she died in Bergen-Belsen — but in that moment, he writes, the image of her face filled his mind so completely that the cold, the hunger, and the guards fell away. "Love," he concluded, "is the only way to grasp another human being in the innermost core of his personality."

The passage is often quoted sentimentally, but its philosophical weight is precise: love was not a consolation prize for a man who had lost everything else; it was a way of knowing. In remembering his wife, he was not escaping reality but reaching the deepest reality available to him: the person he loved remained present to him as a meaning, regardless of whether she was physically alive.

## The Last Freedom

From such observations Frankl drew the principle that became the centre of logotherapy: "Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances." The guards controlled his body, his movements, his food, and his sleep. They could not control the stance he took towards what was happening to him. Frankl noticed that prisoners who kept some task before them — a manuscript to finish, a child waiting, a loved face held in memory — were better able to get up each morning than those who had lost every attachment to the future.

He summed up the principle with a line from Nietzsche that he repeated to himself in the camp: "He who has a why to live can bear almost any how." The line was not decoration. It was a daily test: could he find a "why" that would carry him through this particular day?

## The Three Avenues of Meaning — Tested, Not Invented

Frankl held that meaning can be found in three ways: through creative work or a deed, through experiencing something or encountering someone in love, and through the attitude we take towards unavoidable suffering. A common misunderstanding is that he invented this scheme in the camps. He did not. He had begun formulating it in the late 1920s, and he carried a complete manuscript of his first book on the subject, *Aerztliche Seelsorge*, sewn into the lining of his coat when he was arrested in 1942. The manuscript was confiscated and destroyed in the camp.

What the camp did was test the scheme under the harshest possible conditions. With no work available and no loved ones beside him, only the third avenue remained: the attitude towards suffering itself. The classification thus changed, in Frankl's own account, from a desk hypothesis into lived certainty. After his liberation in 1945, he rewrote the confiscated manuscript and published it in German in 1946, and later in English as *The Doctor and the Soul* (1955), founding logotherapy on the claim that the "will to meaning" is the primary human drive — not Freud's will to pleasure, and not Adler's will to power.

## Why Phenomenology Needs This Account

This entry is the Atlas's working example of the phenomenological tradition associated with Husserl (ctx-phenomenology-husserl): description of experience as lived, before any theorising about it. Frankl did not arrive at his theory of meaning by deduction and then illustrate it with camp stories. He described what he lived, noticed what the descriptions had in common, and only then named the pattern. The method is the message: if you want to know what meaning is, you must begin with someone's actual experience of finding it, or losing it.

That is also why the account has therapeutic force. A therapist working existentially does not hand a client Frankl's conclusions as doctrines. The conclusions matter because they were earned inside an experience the reader can recognise — the discovery that even when nothing can be changed about a situation, something can still be chosen about one's stance towards it.
