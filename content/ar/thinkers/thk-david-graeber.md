# ديفيد غريبر (David Graeber)

أنثروبولوجي وفيلسوف أمريكي (1961–2020) اشتهر بأعماله عن الأنارکية والقيمة والدين والدولة. جمع بين البحث الميداني والكتابة النظرية والنشاط السياسي.

## Biographical Context: Anthropologist, Activist, Public Intellectual

David Graeber (1961–2020) was born in New York City and studied at the University of Chicago, where he encountered both anthropological training and left-wing political thought. He conducted ethnographic fieldwork in Madagascar in the 1990s, examining indigenous concepts of value and exchange, and later worked with activists and social movements. Graeber held positions at Goldsmiths College, London (2008–2013) and the London School of Economics (2013–2020), though his academic career was marked by conflicts with institutional authority; he was controversially dismissed from Yale in 2007. More than any previous economic anthropologist, Graeber was a *public intellectual* and political activist: he was intellectually formative to the Occupy Wall Street movement (2011), writing widely accessible critiques of capitalism, bureaucracy, and debt. He combined scholarly rigor with a commitment to anarchist politics and indigenous sovereignty. Graeber's work exemplified a new anthropological praxis where ethnographic understanding informed direct political engagement. He died in September 2020 at age 59, having achieved both scholarly influence and popular readership rare among anthropologists.

## Theoretical Contributions: Debt, Value, and Moral Economy

Graeber's distinctive theoretical contribution centers on **debt** as a foundational category for understanding human social life. Challenging assumptions that barter and gift-giving were humanity's "original" economy, Graeber argued that **credit systems** (mutual obligations tracked over time) were in fact the earliest economic form. Only later did commodity money and market exchange emerge—and even then, credit logics persisted alongside and often dominated by commodities. His second major insight was that debt and credit are **inherently moral phenomena**: owing someone creates not just economic obligation but ethical relationship. When a creditor demands repayment with violence or coercion, moral economy is violated. This reframing meant that economic anthropology must attend to **ethics**, **justice**, and **morality**—not just structures and logics. Graeber developed a theory of **value** distinct from Marx: value is not labor-time but emerges from social relationships and moral judgments about what is valuable. Therefore, what is valued in one society (ritual shells, feathered cloaks, moka pigs) is determined by local moral systems, not universal principles of utility or scarcity. His framework also emphasizes the **state's role in debt**: modern monetary systems are often explained as emerging from barter's inefficiency, but Graeber argued that taxation (the state's demand for payment in state-issued money) is what creates demand for money and therefore stable value systems. He also introduced the concept of **bullshit jobs**—forms of paid labor that serve no social value and whose performers themselves recognize as pointless. This critique of modern employment extended economic anthropology into contemporary capitalism.

## Major Works: Debt, Bullshit Jobs, and the Utopia of Rules

**Debt: The First 5,000 Years** (2011) is Graeber's magnum opus and has become one of the most widely read anthropological texts globally. Written for a general audience, the book traces the history of debt, credit, and money from Mesopotamia through the present, arguing that credit systems long predated coinage and that the rise of commodity money was not inevitable but politically constructed. Graeber draws on Sumerian temple records, Islamic philosophy, colonial accounts, and ethnographic data to show how debt has been central to human relationships across historical periods. The book challenges dominant economic narratives (barter→money→modern finance) and proposes instead that credit logics have been primary and persistent. Most provocatively, Graeber argues that debt crises (when creditors demand total repayment and collapse mutual obligation systems) are recurring features of history and that debt *cancellation* (jubilees) are necessary social practices. The book's impact has been enormous: it became required reading for activists, development scholars, and economists skeptical of mainstream finance. **Bullshit Jobs: A Theory** (2018) expanded Graeber's critique to contemporary labor. Drawing on surveys and interviews, he argued that large portions of modern employment are *bullshit*—roles that create no genuine social value but exist to sustain bureaucratic power and the illusion of meaningful work. The book sparked global conversation about the meaning of work and the waste inherent in modern economies. **The Utopia of Rules: On Technology, Stupidity, and the Secret Joys of Bureaucracy** (2015) analyzed how bureaucratic systems appear to rationalize social life but actually create irrationality and suffering. Graeber traced the genealogy of bureaucracy and proposed that technology is not neutral but embeds bureaucratic logic, constraining human possibility. **Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology** (2004) presented his theoretical vision most explicitly, arguing that anthropology is equipped to imagine and evaluate radical alternatives to state and market forms of organization.

## Ethnographic Focus: Madagascar and the Moral Anthropology of Exchange

Graeber's fieldwork was conducted primarily in Madagascar in the 1990s, where he studied the Betsileo people's concepts of value, exchange, and obligation. Rather than asking what goods are exchanged, he asked: *what do Betsileo consider valuable, and why?* He documented how moral relationships shape economic behavior and how concepts of debt and obligation are embedded in kinship and ancestors' presence. His ethnographic work preceded and informed the theoretical arguments in *Debt*: the realization that in Madagascar, value was determined by moral relationship, not intrinsic properties of goods. He also conducted research in Madagascar on anarchist practices and indigenous political thought, integrating ethnographic work with his growing political commitments. This ethnographic grounding distinguished Graeber from some contemporary economic critics who rely on secondary sources; his fieldwork allowed him to demonstrate that his theoretical arguments had ethnographic substance.

## Influence and Debates: Revolutionary Potential and Historical Claims

Graeber's work has been enormously influential in activist circles, development anthropology, and global conversations about economics. *Debt* became a touchstone for critics of neoliberalism and of debt-based capitalism; activists cite it to argue for debt forgiveness and alternative economic systems. His concept of **bullshit jobs** went viral, resonating with workers experiencing meaninglessness and precarity. However, his work has also drawn substantial critique from academic anthropologists and historians. Historians of ancient economics (particularly cuneiform specialists) challenged some of Graeber's interpretations of Sumerian data, arguing that he overstated the case for debt-based systems in early states. Economists questioned whether credit systems could function at scale without money, and whether Graeber's narrative adequately accounts for the practical advantages of commodity money. Some anthropologists critique his romanticization of non-state, non-capitalist systems and worry that his anarchist commitments sometimes cloud historical analysis. Others argue that his concept of value is underspecified and that "morality" can obscure material interests and power relations. Additionally, some Indigenous scholars have engaged Graeber's work on Indigenous economics critically, appreciating his defense of non-capitalist logic while questioning whether anthropology (even sympathetic anthropology) adequately represents Indigenous self-determination. Feminist scholars have noted that Graeber's work on debt, while feminist-sympathetic, sometimes underspecifies gender dimensions of debt and obligation.

## Postcolonial Position: Anti-Imperial Anthropology and Indigenous Sovereignty

Graeber's explicit political commitment to opposing imperialism and defending Indigenous sovereignty distinguished his approach. He critiqued anthropology's historical complicity with colonialism and argued that the discipline must reorient toward alliance with Indigenous movements and anti-capitalist struggles. His work on Madagascar incorporated engagement with Malagasy independence and postcolonial politics. In later work, he became increasingly focused on Indigenous resistance to state and capitalist projects, arguing that anthropological knowledge should inform and support Indigenous self-determination. This positioned him within postcolonial anthropology's critique of Western knowledge production, though some scholars question whether solidarity from outside can adequately support Indigenous autonomy. Graeber's anarchist commitment to horizontal organization and autonomy aligned him with Indigenous governance models in some respects, though the fit is complex.

## Legacy and Current Relevance

Graeber's influence extends well beyond academic anthropology. *Debt* reshaped global conversations about debt justice, debt forgiveness, and alternative economies. Development organizations, activists, and policymakers engage his work when designing alternatives to debt-based development. The concept of "bullshit jobs" has been applied to critique precarity, automation, and the future of work. His theoretical innovation—placing *debt and morality* at the center of economic anthropology—has redirected scholarship toward questions of justice, ethics, and obligation rather than pure structural analysis. In the context of contemporary economic crises (pandemic, climate, post-2008 inequality), Graeber's arguments for debt cancellation and radical economic alternatives have urgent political traction. Indigenous movements cite his work supporting non-state, non-capitalist possibilities. His death in 2020 was widely mourned across activist and scholarly communities, and his work continues to inspire new projects in economic anthropology and activism.

## Gaps and Contested Interpretations

Several significant debates and limitations persist. First, **historical evidence**: Graeber's interpretation of Sumerian debt records has been challenged by specialists who question whether cuneiform evidence supports his narrative about early credit systems. Some scholars argue he projects backward onto ancient systems concepts developed for modern societies. Second, **the cash economy problem**: critics question whether credit systems could stabilize at scale without commodity money and whether Graeber adequately explains the historical persistence of money and exchange. Third, **agency and morality**: some scholars worry that by emphasizing moral relationship, Graeber sometimes obscures structural inequality and power that constrains what is considered "moral." Fourth, **romantic primitivism**: critics argue that Graeber can idealize pre-capitalist and non-state societies, potentially obscuring real violence, coercion, and inequality in those contexts. Fifth, **gender**: some feminist scholars note that Graeber's treatment of gender in debt systems is underdeveloped; women's debt and obligation systems deserve more analysis. Finally, **the concept of bullshit jobs**: some economists and sociologists question whether Graeber's category is sufficiently precise and worry that subjective assessments of job "bullshitness" may not align with actual economic function.
