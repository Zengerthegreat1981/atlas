# مارشال سالينز (Marshall Sahlins)

أنثروبولوجيٌّ أمريكيّ، أجرى عملَه الميدانيَّ في جزر فيجي وهاواي، ومن أبرز منظِّري الأنثروبولوجيا الاقتصادية والبنيوية الثقافية في النصف الثاني من القرن العشرين.

## Biographical Context: Formation as an Economic Anthropologist

Marshall Sahlins was born in 1930 in Chicago and studied at the University of Chicago, a central node for post-war American anthropology. He conducted his first fieldwork in Fiji in the 1950s, examining kinship and exchange systems, before moving to Hawaii where he conducted the ethnographic work foundational to *Islands of History*. Throughout his long career, he held positions at the University of Michigan (1956–1973) and the University of Chicago (1973–1990), establishing himself as a leading voice in anthropological theory. Sahlins exemplified the postwar American anthropologist whose work spanned ethnography, comparative analysis, and broad theoretical ambition. He was influenced by the cultural materialism debates of the 1960s-70s, but developed his own distinctive position: a culturalist approach that emphasized how cultures generate meaning systems and how these systems shape economic behavior. He remained intellectually active into his nineties, revising and extending his arguments in light of new critiques. Sahlins passed away in 2021, having left an indelible mark on economic anthropology, the anthropology of the Pacific, and the relationship between structure and history.

## Theoretical Contributions: Substantivism, Affluence, and the Domestic Mode of Production

Sahlins's theoretical contributions center on challenging the universalization of capitalist economic logic to non-Western societies. He developed a **substantivist** position in the substantivism-formalism debate: arguing that economies are always embedded in cultural and social relations that vary fundamentally across societies, and therefore cannot be analyzed using a single rationalist framework (marginal utility, supply-and-demand) assumed to apply everywhere. For Sahlins, the economic behavior that appears "irrational" from a capitalist perspective (the destruction of wealth in potlatch, the cultivation of prestige rather than profit) is entirely rational within its own cultural logic. He introduced the concept of the **domestic mode of production** (DMP)—the characteristic economic organization of kinship-based societies where production is scaled to consumption rather than accumulation for exchange, and where redistribution (not market exchange) is the dominant mode of allocating resources. This stood in contrast to capitalist and tributary modes of production. Crucially, Sahlins argued that in the DMP, economic production is thoroughly embedded in kinship obligations and ritual: the household is simultaneously an economic, social, and ritual unit. His argument about the **original affluent society**—that hunter-gatherer societies achieved material sufficiency with relatively modest labor input, making them "affluent" by satisfying desires rather than constantly expanding production—challenged evolutionary narratives that portrayed non-industrial societies as trapped in poverty. Instead, Sahlins suggested that "poverty" and "affluence" are culturally defined; modern industrial societies produce *scarcity* through endless expansion of wants. This reframing was politically consequential, delegitimizing narratives of "development" as inevitable progress from want to abundance.

## Major Works: Stone Age Economics, Islands of History, and Beyond

**Stone Age Economics** (1972) is Sahlins's most enduring contribution. Collating ethnographic data on Pacific peoples and incorporating Richard Lee's research on the !Kung San (Bushmen), Sahlins argued that hunter-gatherers worked only 15–20 hours per week to meet subsistence needs, leaving ample leisure time—contradicting narratives of "nasty, brutish, and short" lives. He distinguished between gift economies (where exchange creates social bonds and status differentiation) and commodity economies (where value is determined by supply and demand). The book's impact was immediate and lasting; it became foundational to development criticism, environmental anthropology, and debates about human nature and social evolution. However, the specific claims about labor hours and the !Kung have been questioned and revised by subsequent researchers. **Islands of History** (1985) represents Sahlins's most sophisticated theoretical work, applying structural analysis to historical events. Analyzing Captain Cook's contact with Hawaii, Sahlins argued that the Hawaiian encounter was *structured* by Hawaiian cosmological categories—specifically, the myth of the god Lono—which shaped how Hawaiians interpreted Cook's arrival and European goods. Yet the encounter itself transformed Hawaiian structure: the category "Lono" was redefined through contact. This dynamic between structure and history, where cultural categories shape historical events but are themselves transformed by those events, became influential in postmodern anthropology and historical anthropology. **Culture and Practical Reason** (1976) collected essays on cultural materialism, Marxism, and economic anthropology, defending the autonomy of culture against economic determinism. Later works like **Apologies to Thucydides** (1994) and **The West is Eating the Rest** (2013) extended his comparative historical vision, arguing that Western capitalist expansion cannot be understood without attention to the cultural logics of indigenous peoples and the ways they selectively incorporated or resisted Western forms.

## Ethnographic Focus: Fiji, Hawaii, and Pacific Exchange Systems

Sahlins's ethnographic work centered on the Pacific, particularly the complex gift and exchange systems of Melanesia and Polynesia. His Fiji fieldwork examined how kinship organization structured economic reciprocity and how status was marked through possession and distribution of valuables. More importantly, his engagement with Hawaii allowed him to document and analyze **moka** (competitive exchange of pigs and shells among big men in the Highlands of New Guinea, though Sahlins primarily worked with Polynesian analogues like the Hawaiian *ahu'ula* and feathered cloaks). The Pacific islands became for Sahlins what the Trobriands were for Malinowski—a privileged site for understanding how non-capitalist economies work. He was particularly attuned to how these economies sustained systems of chiefly power and prestige, how women's exchanges were integrated (or not) into the prestigious cycles, and how contact with Europeans disrupted and transformed these systems. His attention to material objects (shells, pigs, cloth) as repositories of meaning and value also influenced material culture studies.

## Influence and Debates: Substantivism, Development Theory, and Critique

Sahlins became a central figure in the **substantivism debate** of the 1960s-70s, squaring off against "formalists" like Harold Schneider who argued that economic principles (scarcity, optimization) apply universally. The debate was important for economic anthropology's self-definition: did anthropology study *economies* (implying a universal object) or *modes of livelihood* that vary fundamentally? Sahlins's position has proven more influential in contemporary anthropology, particularly in development studies and political ecology, where scholars draw on his arguments that "development" imposes capitalist logic on fundamentally different systems. **Graeber's *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*** (2011) explicitly builds on Sahlins's framework while extending it backward in time and toward anarchist critique. The **original affluent society** concept has been both celebrated and critiqued: Richard Lee's own later work suggested his initial !Kung data may have been influenced by the drought year in which he worked, and some critics argue that Sahlins idealized pre-contact societies. Nevertheless, the concept remains central to degrowth, environmental justice, and indigenous sovereignty movements. Sahlins's work on structure and history has been enormously influential in postmodern anthropology and world anthropology, but has also been critiqued for its reliance on structuralism and its occasional moments of cultural determinism (the argument that Hawaiian structure *determined* their interpretation of Cook). Postcolonial and indigenous scholars have engaged Sahlins variably: appreciating his defense of cultural autonomy while questioning whether structural analysis adequately represents indigenous agency and political struggle.

## Postcolonial Position: Culture and Sovereignty

Sahlins's approach to colonialism emphasizes the active role of colonized peoples in interpreting and selectively incorporating European goods and concepts rather than passively victimized. *Islands of History* analyzes Cook's death not as a triumph of Hawaiian resistance but as a meaningful event interpreted through Hawaiian categories. This approach respects Indigenous agency and complexity but has been critiqued for potentially underplaying violent dispossession and political domination. Sahlins himself became increasingly engaged with indigenous Hawaiian sovereignty movements in his later years, arguing that Hawaiian cultural independence and political autonomy are inseparable. His work on the agency of colonized peoples has influenced postcolonial anthropology, though some scholars find his emphasis on cultural interpretation and structural logic sometimes obscures material violence and economic exploitation. Sahlins's later writings show deepening commitment to indigenous self-determination and critique of Western universalism, particularly in his engagement with Pacific island nations' struggles for sovereignty.

## Legacy and Current Relevance

Sahlins remains central to economic anthropology, development anthropology, and debates about alternatives to capitalism. The **original affluent society** concept has become a touchstone for environmental anthropology, indigenous sovereignty movements, and degrowth scholarship. In the Global South, scholars engaged with alternative economies, solidarity economics, and critiques of development cite Sahlins to argue that pre-capitalist and non-capitalist economic logics remain viable and desirable. His analysis of gift economies has informed recent anthropological work on open-source communities, volunteer labor, and peer-to-peer networks—spaces where value is created and distributed outside commodity logic. His theory of structure and history is foundational to historical anthropology and has influenced postcolonial approaches to understanding indigenous peoples' selective engagement with colonialism. Contemporary work on the **Anthropocene** and ecological crisis often returns to Sahlins's suggestion that Western societies have created artificial scarcity through endless expansion of wants, making his critique of "progress" narratives urgently relevant. Pacific Island anthropologists continue to build on his empirical and theoretical work, extending his analysis to contemporary issues of climate change, economic dependence, and cultural sovereignty.

## Gaps and Contested Interpretations

Several debates persist around Sahlins's work. First, **the empirical basis of the original affluent society**: Richard Lee later acknowledged that his !Kung labor time data came from a drought year and may not represent typical conditions. The generalization from this case to all hunter-gatherers has been questioned. Second, **the question of gender**: Like Mauss, Sahlins's analysis of gift economies sometimes marginalizes women's exchanges and female perspectives on value. Later feminist anthropologists have noted this limitation. Third, **structure versus agency**: Critics argue that Sahlins's structural-cultural analysis can obscure the contingency of history and downplay individual and collective agency in shaping events. His interpretation of Cook's death, for instance, has been reexamined by Hawaiian scholars who emphasize the complexity of Hawaiian responses and decisions. Fourth, **the danger of idealization**: some scholars worry that romanticizing "original affluent societies" or the "domestic mode of production" can obscure the real violence of colonialism and the genuine disruption it caused. Fifth, **the concept of "gift economy"**: contemporary anthropologists debate whether Sahlins's ideal type of "gift" versus "commodity" exchange actually maps onto ethnographic reality or whether these are analytical abstractions that obscure more complex and mixed systems. Finally, **the relevance to modernity**: it remains unclear how substantially Sahlinsian concepts apply to modern economic systems or whether they remain useful primarily for understanding historical and contemporary indigenous and non-capitalist economies.
