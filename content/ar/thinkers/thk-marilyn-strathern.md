# مارلين شترايثرن (Marilyn Strathern)

أنثروبولوجيّة بريطانية متخصصة في الجنس والقرابة والنسوية الأنثروبولوجية، وفي تطبيق الفكر النسوي على المشاكل النظرية الأساسية للأنثروبولوجيا.

## Biographical Context: Bridging Gender and Kinship

Marilyn Strathern was born in 1941 and studied at Newnham College, Cambridge, during a period of intellectual ferment in British anthropology. She conducted her initial fieldwork in the Mount Hagen region of Papua New Guinea (1964–1965) during her doctoral research, returning for subsequent fieldwork in 1967–1968 and later visits. This longitudinal engagement with the Hagen people became the ethnographic foundation for her theoretical contributions. She held academic positions at the University of Manchester (1974–1993) and Cambridge University (1993–2008), where she was the first woman to hold the chair of social anthropology. Strathern has been influential in feminist anthropology, gender studies, and kinship theory. Retiring from Cambridge in 2008, she has remained intellectually active. Her career exemplifies how feminist anthropology transformed the discipline's understanding of economic exchange, kinship, and personhood.

## Theoretical Contributions: Gender, Dividual Persons, and Relational Value

Strathern's central theoretical contribution is demonstrating that **gender is not a secondary dimension of kinship and exchange systems but constitutive of them**. In societies like the Hagen, where exchange centers on moka (competitive exchanges of pigs and valuables by men), women are not passive; they are essential participants whose valuables (such as decorated cloths and shells) circulate in parallel systems with their own prestige logic. Strathern challenged the assumption that "prestigious" (male) exchanges are economy proper while female exchanges are peripheral. She showed that gender relations, understood as the flow of value and obligation between men and women through marriage, reproduction, and labor, *are* the economic system, not something separate from it. Her concept of **dividual personhood** represents a major reconceptualization of anthropological understanding. In Western thought, persons are treated as bounded, individual, and autonomous; property and relationships belong *to* the person. Strathern argued that in Melanesian contexts (and potentially beyond), persons are fundamentally **dividual**—composed of relationships and partible across multiple social contexts. A person exists only through relationship; they are always already social and interconnected. This insight extends to understanding value and exchange: valuables are not owned by autonomous persons but are traces of relationships; exchanging valuables regenerates relational systems. This concept challenges the individualism embedded in both liberal economics and much anthropological analysis. She also emphasized **relationality** as an epistemological stance: anthropology must attend to how concepts and relationships are generated through interaction, not treat culture as fixed objects to be observed from outside. Her writing style itself enacts relational thinking—dense, self-reflexive, requiring readers to think alongside her.

## Major Works: Gender of the Gift and After Property

**The Gender of the Gift** (1988) is Strathern's most important monograph, synthesizing decades of Melanesian fieldwork. Rather than asking "what do women do in exchange systems?" she asks "how does gender constitute exchange systems?" The book demonstrates that Hagen economy (the moka system of big men competing through generous distribution) is enabled by women's labor and valuables but also depends on controlling women's reproductive capacity. Women are simultaneously **producers of value** (through their labor, their children, their wealth), **valuables themselves** (circulated through marriage), and **agents of circulation** (they move between groups and carry knowledge). Strathern argues that understanding gender relations *as* economic relations reveals that Western economics has always depended on naturalizing women's reproductive and nurturing work as outside economy. Her essay collections include **Partial Connections** (1991) and **Property, Substance and Effect** (1999), which develop her relational ontology and apply it to questions of knowledge, technology, and kinship in late modernity. **After Property** (2016) returns to Melanesian contexts to examine how contemporary intellectual property regimes (patents, copyright, genetic IP) are reshaping concepts of ownership and personhood, arguing that indigenous property concepts offer alternatives to Western capitalist logic.

## Ethnographic Focus: Papua New Guinea, Mount Hagen, and Moka

Strathern's ethnographic work centers on the Hagen people of Papua New Guinea, particularly their elaborate system of competitive exchange called **moka**. In moka, men accumulate pigs and valuables (shells, cassowaries, money) and display them in public ceremonies, distributing them to rivals and allies. Through repeated cycles of giving and receiving, men establish prestige and maintain political relationships. Strathern's innovation was to show that this system cannot be understood without analyzing women's parallel exchanges of valuables and women's role in producing and controlling the pigs and goods that men circulate. She documented how marriage creates obligations between groups and how women negotiate their positions within kinship and exchange. Her attention to material goods (the shells, pigs, and cloths) and to bodily experience (pregnancy, labor, menstruation) grounded kinship theory in material and embodied reality. Her work with Hagen people spanned decades and remained deeply collaborative; later works explicitly engage with Hagen people's own analytical responses to her interpretations, exemplifying a reflexive anthropological practice.

## Influence and Debates: Feminist Anthropology and Kinship Theory

Strathern's work has been foundational to **feminist anthropology** and has transformed anthropological understanding of kinship. By centering gender, she demonstrated that kinship is not primarily about genealogy or descent but about **gendered obligations and flows of value**. This reorientation influenced subsequent work on kinship across the discipline. Her concept of **dividual personhood** has been enormously influential and has been extended (sometimes contested) in studies of personhood across non-Western societies. Some scholars argue her framework applies to contexts beyond Melanesia; others caution against universalizing. The concept has implications for understanding intellectual property, body parts, genetic material, and other contexts where Western liberal personhood assumes bounded individuals who own themselves and their property. Strathern's work on relationality has influenced science and technology studies (STS) and studies of kinship in the context of assisted reproduction and genomics. Some feminist scholars have critiqued her work for emphasizing structure and relationship sometimes at the expense of women's experience or narrative; others praise her for avoiding essentialist accounts of "women's experience." Her theoretical density and style have been both celebrated as rigorous and critiqued as inaccessible. Younger anthropologists working on gender, Indigenous property, and alternative kinships frequently reference Strathern's framework.

## Postcolonial Position: Melanesian Autonomy and Intellectual Respect

Strathern's relationship to postcolonial theory is ambivalent. On one hand, she has consistently treated Melanesian thought and analysis as sophisticated and valuable, not subordinate to Western theory. Her later work explicitly engages with Melanesian scholars' critiques of her own interpretations, modeling collaborative and respectful anthropological engagement. On the other hand, her theoretical apparatus is deeply Western (drawing on Western feminist theory, poststructuralism), raising questions about whose concepts are privilege in analysis. In recent years, Strathern has engaged more directly with decolonization, supporting Melanesian sovereignty and critiquing how IP regimes (which she analyzes) serve imperial and capitalist interests. Her work on property has become relevant to Indigenous land claims and sovereignty, though the relationship between her theoretical framework and Indigenous political projects requires careful negotiation.

## Legacy and Current Relevance

Strathern's theoretical frameworks remain central to contemporary anthropology, particularly in feminist anthropology, kinship studies, and gender theory. Her concept of relationality and dividual personhood informs work on Indigenous sovereignty, alternative kinships, and environmental anthropology (where relational ontologies are being recovered and defended). Her analysis of gender and value has influenced economic anthropology, particularly scholarship examining women's economic work and value creation that has been made invisible by capitalist and patriarchal frameworks. In the context of contemporary debates about kinship (same-sex marriage, donor gametes, surrogacy), scholars draw on Strathern to argue that kinship is fundamentally social and variable, not determined by biology or heterosexuality. Her critique of Western property concepts has become relevant to Indigenous land rights and IP struggles. Feminist scholars continue to develop her insights about gender and economy, extending her framework to new contexts. Academic anthropology prizes Strathern's work highly, making her one of the most influential anthropologists of the late twentieth century.

## Gaps and Contested Interpretations

Significant debates persist around Strathern's work. First, **the universality of dividual personhood**: scholars question whether the concept accurately describes Melanesian peoples or whether it is a theoretical construct readable into ethnographic data. Some Melanesian scholars have offered different interpretations. Second, **gender and agency**: some feminist anthropologists critique Strathern for emphasizing relational structure sometimes at the expense of women's agency, resistance, or diverse experiences within gendered systems. Third, **the applicability beyond Melanesia**: extending dividual personhood and relational ontologies to non-Melanesian contexts remains debated. Fourth, **relationality and power**: critics worry that an exclusive focus on relationality can obscure structural inequality and domination within relationships. Fifth, **temporality and change**: some scholars argue that Strathern's framework, while excellent for synchronic analysis, struggles with understanding historical change and processes of transformation. Sixth, **her engagement with decolonization**: some postcolonial scholars question whether her theoretical frameworks, however sympathetically applied, can fully support Indigenous self-determination or whether they remain embedded in Western academic authority.
