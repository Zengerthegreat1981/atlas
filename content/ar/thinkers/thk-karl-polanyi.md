# كارل بولاني (Karl Polanyi)

مؤرخ اقتصادي وعالم اجتماع نمساوي-مجري، صاحب مفهوم «التجذر» (Embeddedness) الذي أعاد تعريف علاقة الاقتصاد بالمجتمع، وناقد جذري لفكرة «السوق الحرة الذاتية التنظيم» بوصفها خيالاً تاريخياً لا واقعاً طبيعياً.

## Biographical Context: Intellectual Formation and Displacement

Karl Polanyi was born in 1886 in Vienna, Austria-Hungary, into an intellectual Jewish family (his brother was the mathematician Michael Polanyi). He studied law and philosophy at the University of Budapest and began his career as a journalist and social theorist in interwar Vienna, where he engaged with socialist and Christian socialist circles. In the 1920s-1930s, he published essays on political economy and history while supporting the Austrian cooperative movement. With the rise of fascism, Polanyi emigrated to Britain in 1933, where he worked in adult education and began researching the history of economic thought. During World War II, he moved to the United States, where he held positions at Bennington College and Cornell University. His major work, *The Great Transformation*, was written in America but published in 1944 as the war was concluding. Polanyi spent his final years in Canada, at the University of Toronto, where he remained intellectually productive until his death in 1964. His trajectory—from imperial Vienna through fascist-threatened Europe to North American exile—shaped his historical perspective on how capitalism and nationalism emerged together and their catastrophic consequences. Unlike many economic theorists who worked from armchair theorizing, Polanyi grounded his arguments in historical research across multiple languages and cultures.

## Theoretical Contributions: Embeddedness, Fictitious Commodities, and Market Society

Polanyi's central argument is that **markets are not natural, autonomous domains governed by universal economic logic, but rather are always embedded in social, political, and cultural institutions**. This concept of **embeddedness** (though Polanyi uses the term more as "the economy is embedded in society" rather than adopting the later concept popularized by Granovetter) fundamentally challenges economic orthodoxy. Before industrial capitalism, Polanyi argued, economic activity was organized through three mechanisms: **reciprocity** (mutual gifting and obligation based on kinship), **redistribution** (centralized collection and reallocation by authorities), and **market exchange** (secondary and limited in scope). The emergence of **self-regulating markets** in nineteenth-century England was not the natural outcome of economic evolution but a deliberate political and ideological project. This market society required treating **land, labor, and money**—which Polanyi calls "fictitious commodities" because they were not produced for sale—as commodities bought and sold like manufactured goods. Land (nature) was removed from common property and turned into real estate; labor (human activity) was commodified as wage labor; money became a commodity circulated through financial markets. This process of **disembedding** the economy from society required tremendous state intervention and produced profound social disruption. Polanyi predicted that this disembedding would generate a **"double movement"** (Doppelbewegung): the market would expand, creating economic crises and social suffering, prompting a countermove by society to protect itself through regulation, labor protections, welfare provisions, and strategic controls on markets. This double movement between market expansion and protective regulation characterizes modern history. Crucially, Polanyi insisted that a purely self-regulating market is **impossible**—it would destroy the natural and human foundations on which it depends. Therefore, all modern economies are "mixed" systems that combine markets with regulatory and redistributive mechanisms.

## Major Works: The Great Transformation and Substantive Economics

**The Great Transformation: The Political and Economic Origins of Our Time** (1944) is Polanyi's magnum opus and remains foundational to multiple disciplines. Written during World War II as fascism and communism offered competing totalitarian responses to capitalism's crises, the book argues that the nineteenth-century attempt to create a self-regulating market system generated the very instabilities (economic collapse, mass unemployment) that fascists exploited. The book traces the history of the market system from medieval times, showing how it required sustained political effort to construct (the Poor Laws in England, colonial expansion, industrial regulation). Polanyi demonstrates through historical evidence that ostensibly "natural" market mechanisms were actually political constructions. The book's historical sweep and theoretical sophistication made it immediately influential in development economics, sociology, and later anthropology. **Primitive, Archaic, and Modern Economies** (1968, posthumously edited with Harry Pearson and George Dalton) collected essays theorizing non-capitalist economies and arguing for "substantive economics"—an approach that studies how societies organize production, distribution, and consumption without assuming capitalist logic. Essays like "Ports of Trade" analyzed ancient Mediterranean trading cities as systems of regulated exchange rather than competitive markets. This work became foundational to **economic anthropology**, providing theoretical framework for understanding how diverse societies organize economy differently. **The Livelihood of Man** (1977, published posthumously) offered his mature reflections on the human economy and critiques of utilitarian economics. These works collectively establish Polanyi as both historian of capitalism and theorist of alternative economic systems.

## Ethnographic Focus: History and Comparative Analysis

Polanyi himself conducted no ethnographic fieldwork in the anthropological sense. Instead, his method was **historical and comparative**: analyzing archival sources, ancient texts, colonial records, and contemporaneous ethnographic studies to demonstrate how diverse societies organized economy. He drew extensively on anthropological accounts of the Pacific, Africa, and Americas to illustrate how reciprocity and redistribution operate. His essay on the **kula ring** (the famous Melanesian exchange system documented by Malinowski) demonstrated that this was not "primitive barter" but a sophisticated system of prestige and political alliance organized through regulated exchange. He analyzed the **potlatch** of Pacific Northwest Coast peoples as redistribution that reinforced chiefly authority. By juxtaposing these ethnographic examples with historical analysis of England, the Mediterranean, and Central Europe, Polanyi showed that modern market society represents one particular configuration of economy, not its natural endpoint. This comparative-historical method influenced later anthropology and world-systems theory.

## Influence and Debates: Economic Anthropology, Development Theory, and Global Justice

Polanyi's influence on **economic anthropology** has been profound and enduring. Scholars like Marshall Sahlins explicitly built on Polanyian framework when developing substantivism against formalism. His concept of **embeddedness** was revived and developed by sociologist Mark Granovetter (1985), who used it to explain why economic behavior is shaped by social networks and relationships. This spawned the field of **economic sociology** centered on Polanyi's insight that economy is social. In **development economics and policy**, Polanyi's critique of self-regulating markets became foundational to critiques of neoliberalism and market fundamentalism. Scholars arguing that development should attend to local social institutions, not impose market logic, draw on Polanyi. His concept of "fictitious commodities" has become central to critiques of commodification of nature (environmental economics), labor (labor rights), and recently data and genetic material. The **global justice** movement cites Polanyi when arguing that water, seeds, land, and human genes should not be commodified. Some Marxist scholars have engaged Polanyi, though others critique his framework as insufficiently attentive to class struggle and capital accumulation. Postcolonial scholars appreciate his critique of market imperialism but note that his historical focus on England sometimes marginalizes colonial impacts on non-Western societies. Indigenous scholars engage Polanyi when analyzing traditional economies and arguing for economic sovereignty against capitalist imposition.

## Postcolonial Position: Market Imperialism and Civilization Narratives

Polanyi's position on colonialism is more implicit than explicit. His critique of market expansion as politically imposed—not natural or inevitable—applies to the global imposition of capitalist markets through colonialism. Yet his historical focus emphasizes English and European history, potentially marginalizing how colonialism was central to establishing global markets. His discussion of "archaic" societies (using period ethnological terminology) sometimes presents non-Western economies as historical stages rather than contemporaneous alternatives. However, his fundamental argument—that capitalist markets require political force to establish and are not natural or superior—implicitly supports Indigenous and postcolonial resistance to market imposition. Some postcolonial scholars have extended Polanyi's framework to analyze how colonialism disrupted pre-capitalist economies and imposed market logic. His later work (*Primitive, Archaic, and Modern Economies*) shows greater attention to the validity and complexity of non-capitalist systems, potentially supporting decolonial efforts.

## Legacy and Current Relevance

Polanyi's relevance has grown significantly since the 2008 financial crisis and the rise of critiques of neoliberalism. His argument that self-regulating markets are impossible and that all economies require regulatory "embeddedness" has become central to progressive economics and policy. The concept of **fictitious commodities** has become urgent in debates about climate change (can nature be commodified?), healthcare (should medicine be commodified?), and data (should personal information be monetized?). Scholars working on **environmental economics** and **ecological anthropology** draw on Polanyi to argue that nature cannot be reduced to commodity status. The **global justice movement**, **degrowth theory**, and **indigenous sovereignty movements** all reference Polanyi when arguing for economic systems that embed markets in social and ecological values. In academic anthropology, Polanyi remains foundational to economic anthropology and is regularly assigned to students learning the discipline's history. Contemporary anthropologists working on alternative economies, solidarity economies, and commons governance cite Polanyi. The COVID-19 pandemic revived interest in Polanyi, as disruptions to supply chains and market systems made visible the dependency of "market society" on underlying social and biological systems. His prediction about "double movement" (market expansion generating protective countermoves) remains applicable to contemporary debates about regulation versus deregulation.

## Gaps and Contested Interpretations

Several significant gaps and debates persist in Polanyi scholarship. First, **the extent of state involvement in "self-regulating" markets**: some scholars argue that Polanyi overstates the degree to which markets were intended to be self-regulating and underestimates how much states were always involved in managing them. Second, **the universality of embeddedness**: some economists question whether the concept adequately captures how markets do sometimes achieve substantial autonomy from social constraints, or whether embeddedness is too vague. Third, **the historical narrative**: Polanyi's story of progressive market expansion followed by protective countermoves is contested; some scholars argue historical reality was messier and less linear. Fourth, **gender and reproduction**: Polanyi's framework, like much economic theory, marginalizes women's reproductive and care work that sustains all economies. Feminist scholars have noted this gap. Fifth, **the concept of "double movement"**: critics question whether Polanyi adequately explains why protective movements sometimes fail or are co-opted; his theory can seem overly deterministic. Sixth, **colonialism and imperialism**: postcolonial scholars argue that Polanyi's focus on England marginalizes how colonialism was essential to establishing global markets and that his framework needs to integrate postcolonial perspectives more fully. Finally, **contemporary applicability**: scholars debate whether Polanyi's framework, developed to explain nineteenth-century market expansion, adequately addresses twenty-first-century dynamics like financialization, automation, and digital platforms.
