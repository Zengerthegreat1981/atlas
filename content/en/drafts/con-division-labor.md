# Division of Labor

The division of labour is the allocation of different tasks to different persons or groups within a society, together with the arrangements by which their work and products are combined. Anthropologists treat it as a social and cultural arrangement as much as a technical one: it shapes interdependence, status, exchange and ideas about what kinds of people are suited to what kinds of work.

## Classical Approaches

Adam Smith opened *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) by linking the division of labour to rising productivity, using the example of pin manufacture. Karl Marx, in the first volume of *Capital* (1867), distinguished the division of labour within society from the division of labour within the workshop, and connected the latter to the subordination of workers to capital. Émile Durkheim's *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) asked how specialisation affects social cohesion. He contrasted "mechanical solidarity", based on likeness among members, with "organic solidarity", based on the interdependence of differentiated parts, and associated the shift with changes in law from predominantly repressive to restitutive sanctions. He also described abnormal forms, including the "anomic" and the "forced" division of labour. Later anthropologists criticised the contrast for underestimating exchange and differentiation in small-scale societies.

## Specialisation by Person and Group

In many societies tasks are allocated mainly by gender and age. George P. Murdock and Caterina Provost compared the sexual division of labour across a large cross-cultural sample (1973) and found that some activities tend to be assigned to one sex while many vary widely between societies. Judith K. Brown (1970) argued that women's participation in subsistence activities is related to how compatible those activities are with child care. Feminist anthropologists have stressed that such allocations cannot simply be read off from biology, since they are bound up with ideas of gender, property and authority.

Specialisation may also attach to households, kin groups, communities or ranked groups. Craft specialists such as smiths or potters occupy distinct social positions in many societies, and in South Asia hereditary occupations have been associated with caste. Differences between communities in what they produce can sustain regional exchange. Archaeologists study craft specialisation as one dimension of social complexity, distinguishing, for example, between independent producers and those attached to elite patrons.

## Wider Debates

Marshall Sahlins, in *Stone Age Economics* (1972), analysed the household-based "domestic mode of production", organised largely around divisions of sex and age. In the study of capitalism, research has examined the separation of paid work from unpaid domestic and care work, and the international division of labour that links workplaces in different parts of the world, as in studies of factory labour, migration and global supply chains.
