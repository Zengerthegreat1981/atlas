# Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ)

The Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ) is a standardized self-report personality inventory designed to measure three major, biologically anchored dimensions of personality: Extraversion, Neuroticism, and Psychoticism, supplemented by a validity scale assessing social conformity and dissimulation.

## Historical Development

Developed by Hans J. Eysenck and Sybil B. G. Eysenck at the Institute of Psychiatry in London, the original EPQ was published in 1975 with 90 dichotomous (Yes/No) items. It represented the evolution of earlier psychometric instruments, including the Maudsley Medical Questionnaire (1952), the Maudsley Personality Inventory (1959), and the Eysenck Personality Inventory (1964). Whereas the 1964 inventory measured only Extraversion and Neuroticism, the 1975 EPQ introduced Psychoticism as a third major trait dimension.

In 1985, Hans Eysenck, Sybil Eysenck, and Paul Barrett published the revised edition (EPQ-R), expanding the full adult version to 100 items to improve the psychometric stability of the Psychoticism scale. A standardized 48-item Short Scale (EPQ-RS, 12 items per scale) and a Junior EPQ for children and adolescents aged 7 to 15 were also established.

## Dimensional Structure and Psychometrics

The EPQ assesses four scales:
1. **Extraversion (E)**: Assesses sociability, liveliness, assertiveness, sensation-seeking, and surgency. High scorers are outgoing and stimulation-oriented; low scorers (introverts) are quiet, introspective, and reserved.
2. **Neuroticism (N)**: Assesses emotional instability, anxiety, moodiness, and vulnerability to stress. High scorers exhibit heightened autonomic reactivity.
3. **Psychoticism (P)**: Assesses tough-mindedness, nonconformity, impulsivity, sensation-seeking, and interpersonal hostility. High scores reflect non-conformist and aggressive tendencies rather than clinical psychosis.
4. **Lie Scale (L)**: Assesses social desirability, defensive dissimulation, or genuine social conformity.

The Extraversion and Neuroticism scales consistently exhibit high internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha coefficients routinely ranging between 0.80 and 0.90, alongside strong test-retest reliability. The Psychoticism scale has historically demonstrated lower internal consistency (alphas typically between 0.65 and 0.78), reflecting the heterogeneous nature of its constituent items.

## Biological Theory

Eysenck grounded the questionnaire in a biological model of personality:
- **Extraversion** was hypothesized to stem from baseline cortical arousal regulated by the ascending reticular activating system (ARAS). Introverts possess higher baseline cortical arousal, leading them to avoid high sensory stimulation.
- **Neuroticism** was linked to the activation thresholds of the limbic system (visceral brain) and autonomic nervous system.
- **Psychoticism** was later associated theoretically with hormonal and monoaminergic mechanisms, including testosterone levels and dopaminergic function.

## Cross-Cultural Standardization and Arabic Versions

The EPQ and EPQ-R have undergone extensive cross-cultural adaptation. In the Arab world, comprehensive standardization programs led by Ahmed Abdel-Khalek in collaboration with Hans and Sybil Eysenck established norms across Egypt, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, Lebanon, and Sudan. Factor analyses affirmed the cross-cultural universality of the three-factor structure, showing strong factorial congruence for Extraversion, Neuroticism, and the Lie scale, while confirming the cross-cultural sensitivity and lower reliability of the Psychoticism scale.

## Criticisms and Legacy

The primary critique of the EPQ focuses on the Psychoticism dimension, both regarding its psychometric heterogeneity and its potentially misleading clinical label. In contemporary academic personality research, the Five-Factor Model has largely superseded Eysenck's PEN model, although the EPQ remains historically significant for pioneering biologically grounded psychometrics.
