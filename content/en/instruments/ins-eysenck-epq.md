# Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ)

A well-known self-report questionnaire (90 items in the original 1975 version, 100 in the revised EPQ-R) that measures three major biologically oriented dimensions of personality (Psychoticism, Extraversion, and Neuroticism), with a scale for detecting lying and social conformity.

## Design and Standardization

It was designed by Hans Eysenck and his wife Sybil Eysenck (Hans J. Eysenck & Sybil B.G. Eysenck) and published in 1975, from work at the Institute of Psychiatry in London, as a development of their earlier Eysenck Personality Inventory (EPI, 1964), which was limited to the dimensions of Extraversion and Neuroticism, by adding the dimension of Psychoticism. It was standardized on British samples and has since been translated and used in many other countries.

## Structure and Psychometric Properties

The questionnaire measures four scales: (1) Extraversion/Introversion (E), (2) Neuroticism/Emotional Stability (N), (3) Psychoticism/Social Toughness (P), and (4) the Lie scale (L). It has good to excellent internal reliability (0.80-0.90) for the Extraversion and Neuroticism scales, and slightly lower for the Psychoticism scale, with construct validity supported by extensive factor-analytic studies across multiple cultures.

## Clinical and Research Use

It is used in psychophysiological research and forensic psychology, in studying predisposition to mental disorders, and in health research, based on Eysenck's theory, which links extraversion to the level of cortical arousal (regulated by the ascending reticular activating system) and neuroticism to the reactivity of the limbic system. This biological model has received mixed empirical support and remains a theory rather than an established mechanism.

## Limitations and Criticism

The Psychoticism dimension in particular is criticized for its weak internal reliability compared with the other two dimensions, and for its name overlapping with clinical psychotic disorders even though it measures a general personality trait (toughness and aggressiveness) and not a pathological symptom; its academic use has also declined in favor of the Big Five model, which has greater factorial consensus.

Eysenck's research connected with this questionnaire contributed to founding an entire branch of psychology known as biological differential psychology, which links stable personality traits to individual differences in the physiological activation of the central nervous system.

Eysenck later developed, in collaboration with Sybil Eysenck and Paul Barrett (1985), a revised version known as the EPQ-R (100 items), which revised items to improve the psychometric properties of the Psychoticism scale in particular, which had suffered from comparatively weaker internal reliability than the Extraversion and Neuroticism scales since the first version of the questionnaire.

A children's version (Junior EPQ) also exists and has been used in research with children and adolescents. The continuing academic controversy over the name of the Psychoticism dimension, and its linguistic link to clinical psychosis, remains a standing criticism.
