# Death Attitude Profile-Revised (DAP-R)

The Death Attitude Profile-Revised (DAP-R) is a 32-item self-report questionnaire that measures attitudes toward death as a multidimensional construct rather than as a single variable. Developed by Paul Wong, Gary Reker, and Gina Gesser and published in 1994, it revised the earlier Death Attitude Profile (1987–88) into the five-factor structure described below. Its central contribution is the recognition that a person's stance toward death cannot be reduced to a binary of fear versus fearlessness: the same person may fear death, avoid thinking about it, accept it calmly as a natural fact, welcome it as a gateway to a better life, and see it as an escape from suffering — all at once, in different proportions. The DAP-R measures each of these stances separately.

## What it measures

The DAP-R has five subscales. Fear of Death (seven items) measures dread and anxiety about death itself. Death Avoidance (five items) measures the tendency to avoid thinking or talking about death. Neutral Acceptance (five items) measures the view of death as a natural, inevitable part of life, neither feared nor welcomed. Approach Acceptance (ten items) measures the belief in a pleasant afterlife and a positive view of death as a transition to a better existence. Escape Acceptance (five items) measures the view of death as a release from a painful or burdensome life. Each item is rated on a seven-point scale from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree," and each subscale is scored as a mean, so that a respondent's profile is a pattern across the five dimensions rather than a single number.

## Development and structure

The DAP-R is a revision of the original Death Attitude Profile, which Gesser, Wong, and Reker developed in the late 1980s. The revision refined the item set and the subscale structure, treating death avoidance as a distinct attitude rather than merely the absence of fear. The chapter presenting the DAP-R appeared in Robert Neimeyer's Death Anxiety Handbook in 1994, the landmark collection that consolidated death anxiety research as a field.

## Psychometric properties

The five DAP-R subscales show acceptable to very good internal consistency across studies, with Cronbach's alpha typically ranging from about 0.65 for Neutral Acceptance to 0.97 for Approach Acceptance. Construct validity was established through predicted patterns of association: Fear of Death correlates positively with Templer's Death Anxiety Scale (1970), Approach Acceptance correlates with belief in an afterlife, and Neutral Acceptance correlates with indifference toward death. The instrument has been translated into many languages and used widely in research on ageing, palliative care, and thanatology. The psychometric properties of Arabic versions are not documented in this entry, so use in Arabic-speaking samples should be treated as research use without established norms.

## Uses and limits

The DAP-R is used mainly in research: studies of death attitudes across the lifespan, in palliative care and oncology, in training for medical and nursing staff who work with dying patients, and in the psychology of religion. Its multidimensional profile makes it a finer tool than unidimensional death anxiety scales for distinguishing, for example, a person whose fear of death is existential and ordinary from one whose death anxiety is excessive and clinical, or a person who accepts death calmly from one who welcomes it as an escape from unbearable pain — distinctions that matter greatly in end-of-life care. Its limits are equally clear. It is a research instrument, not a clinical diagnostic tool: there are no established clinical cut-off scores separating normal from pathological death anxiety. Scores are strongly shaped by the respondent's culture and religious beliefs, so cross-cultural comparisons require care. And like all self-report measures of death attitudes, it depends on respondents' willingness to reflect honestly on a subject many prefer to avoid.

## Key works

Wong, Reker, and Gesser's 1994 chapter is the primary source, presenting the instrument, its rationale, and its validation. Gesser, Wong, and Reker (1987–88) document the original profile from which it was revised. Neimeyer's Death Anxiety Handbook (1994), in which the DAP-R chapter appeared, remains the essential collection for the field, and Templer's 1970 Death Anxiety Scale is the classic unidimensional measure against which the DAP-R defined itself.
