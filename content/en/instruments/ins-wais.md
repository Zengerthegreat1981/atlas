# Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale

The standard clinical battery for assessing general intelligence and cognitive functions in individuals aged 16 to 90 through four main composite indices.

## Structure and Psychometric Properties

The fourth edition (WAIS-IV) consists of 10 core subtests and 5 supplemental subtests, from which four composite indices emerge: (1) the Verbal Comprehension Index (VCI), (2) the Perceptual Reasoning Index (PRI), (3) the Working Memory Index (WMI), and (4) the Processing Speed Index (PSI), in addition to the Full Scale IQ (FSIQ) and the General Ability Index (GAI). Reliability of the Full Scale IQ is reported at about 0.98 in the WAIS-IV technical manual, with somewhat lower but still high values for the index scores.

## Clinical and Research Use

It is among the most widely used adult intelligence batteries, used as part of neuropsychological and psychological assessment (for example after brain injury), in evaluation of intellectual disability and learning difficulties, and in forensic evaluations. It is one component of assessment, not a diagnostic test on its own.

## Who Designed It, When, and the Standardization Sample

David Wechsler created the first version (WAIS) in 1955 as an extension of the Wechsler-Bellevue Intelligence Scale (1939), and its development continued through WAIS-R (1981) and WAIS-III (1997) up to WAIS-IV, which Pearson released in 2008. A fifth edition (WAIS-5) was released by Pearson in 2024. The fourth edition was based on an American standardization sample of about 2,200 adults, stratified according to the American population census (age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational level, and geographic region).

## Limitations and Criticism

Administering and scoring the test requires specialized clinical training and a long time (60-90 minutes), and some verbal comprehension items are affected by the examinee's educational and cultural background. It is also subject to the Flynn Effect, which necessitates periodic restandardization so that old norms do not overestimate the mental ability of current examinees.

## Relation to the History of Intelligence Measurement

The Wechsler approach represents a break with the one-dimensional Binet-Simon approach (a single mental age divided by chronological age), as it offered a multidimensional structure of intelligence that separated verbal and performance abilities from its first version (later editions replaced the Verbal and Performance IQs with the four index scores), a conception that influenced later intelligence batteries built on multi-layered factorial models such as the CHC model.
