# The Rorschach Inkblot Test

A famous projective psychodiagnostic instrument consisting of 10 cards bearing ambiguous symmetrical inkblots (five achromatic, two with red accents, and three multicolored) to assess personality structure, perceptual processes and psychological conflicts.

## Structure and Psychometric Properties

The examinee is shown 10 standard cards and gives responses to what the blots might be, followed by an Inquiry phase in which the examiner clarifies where and why each response was seen. The test is scored according to the Exner Comprehensive System (CS) or the R-PAS system through defined criteria: location (the whole blot W or a detail D), determinants (form F, movement M, color C, shading Y), and content and popularity of response. Inter-rater reliability for coding is generally reported as acceptable to good when examiners are well trained, but varies across variables.

## Clinical and Research Use

It is used as one component of broader psychological assessment, for example in exploring thought organization and personality functioning, and it has been used in forensic evaluations. Its use for diagnosis or for predicting specific outcomes such as suicide risk is contested, and it should not be used as the sole basis for any clinical or legal decision.

## Design and Standardization

The test was designed by the Swiss psychiatrist Hermann Rorschach and published in 1921 in his book "Psychodiagnostik" shortly before his death. The Exner Comprehensive System (CS), which dominated practice for decades, relied on a non-patient American normative sample (about 600 adults), and was criticized for the sample's representativeness and for classifying many non-patient respondents as showing pathological indicators. The R-PAS system (Meyer, Viglione, Mihura, Erard, and Erdberg, 2011) was developed with international norms to address this shortcoming.

## Limits and Criticism

It has been subjected to sharp psychometric criticism, most notably the book by Wood, Nezworski, Lilienfeld and Garb (2003), which argued that many Comprehensive System variables lacked adequate validity and that its norms tended to over-pathologize. Defenders, such as Mihura and colleagues' 2013 meta-analyses, argue that a number of variables show validity comparable to other personality measures, so the evidence is mixed and varies by variable. Clinical training in the instrument is also demanding. The test has been used in complex legal cases such as child custody and assessment of criminal dangerousness, a use that has provoked debate over the admissibility of expert testimony based on a projective instrument under standards for admitting scientific evidence in American courts.

## Relation to the History of Psychological Diagnosis

The Rorschach represents a transitional link between traditional descriptive clinical diagnosis and the quantitative psychometric movement that later prevailed in American clinical psychology, since its development over decades (from Exner to R-PAS) attempted to turn an interpretive projective instrument into a standardized coding system that is repeatable and empirically testable.
