# Toronto Alexithymia Scale (TAS-20)

The Toronto Alexithymia Scale, in its current twenty-item form the TAS-20, is the most widely used self-report measure of alexithymia — difficulty identifying, describing, and processing one's own emotions. Developed by Michael Bagby, James Parker, and Graeme Taylor and published in 1994, it operationalised a concept coined two decades earlier by Peter Sifneos to describe patients in psychotherapy who seemed unable to put feelings into words. The scale's importance extends beyond measurement: because it turns the capacity to experience and name emotions into something quantifiable, it bears on the theoretical debate over whether emotions are universal, biologically given responses or constructions built from learned concepts and language.

## What it measures

The TAS-20 has three subscales. Difficulty Identifying Feelings (seven items) measures trouble distinguishing emotions from one another and from the bodily sensations of arousal — for example, not knowing whether one is sad, angry, or simply tired. Difficulty Describing Feelings (five items) measures trouble finding words for emotions and communicating them to others. Externally Oriented Thinking (eight items) measures a cognitive style that focuses on concrete external details rather than inner experience, imagination, and feeling. Each item is rated on a five-point scale, giving a total from 20 to 100. Scores of 61 or above are conventionally taken to indicate alexithymia, scores of 52 to 60 borderline alexithymia, and scores of 51 or below its absence, although these thresholds are guidelines for research rather than clinical diagnoses.

## Development and structure

The TAS-20 is the third generation of the scale. The original 26-item Toronto Alexithymia Scale, developed in the 1980s, showed a four-factor structure and psychometric weaknesses; a revised version followed, and in 1994 Bagby, Parker, and Taylor published the twenty-item version with a cleaner three-factor structure, validated in two companion papers in the Journal of Psychosomatic Research — one on item selection and factor structure, the other on reliability and validity. The three factors correspond to core features of the clinical description of alexithymia in the psychosomatic literature: difficulty identifying feelings, difficulty describing feelings, and an externally oriented cognitive style (a fourth feature, a limited fantasy life, was dropped as a scored factor in the 20-item version). Later confirmatory factor analyses, including Parker, Taylor, and Bagby (2003) in community samples, replicated the three-factor structure.

## Psychometric properties

The TAS-20 shows good internal consistency overall, with Cronbach's alpha typically above 0.80 for the total score, and acceptable test-retest reliability over short intervals. The Difficulty Identifying Feelings and Difficulty Describing Feelings subscales are the most reliable; the Externally Oriented Thinking subscale has consistently shown lower reliability, and several researchers advise against interpreting it on its own. The scale correlates with measures of depression, anxiety, and somatic complaints, which is both a finding and a problem: it raises the question of whether alexithymia is an independent trait or partly a reflection of general psychological distress. The scale has been translated into many languages.

## Uses and limits

The TAS-20 is used in research on psychosomatic medicine, post-traumatic stress disorder, autism spectrum conditions, eating disorders, and emotion regulation, and increasingly in studies of how people with alexithymia respond to psychotherapy, where some studies suggest that high scores are associated with slower progress in treatments that rely heavily on emotional insight and verbal expression. Its most famous limitation is a paradox built into its method: it is a self-report measure of a condition defined by difficulty reporting one's inner states, so that the people it is meant to identify may be the least able to answer it accurately. It also depends on the respondent having the emotional vocabulary whose absence it measures. For this Atlas, the instrument is of interest in connection with Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion: if emotions are built from learned concepts, as Barrett argues in How Emotions Are Made (2017), then difficulty with emotional concepts might be expected to affect emotional experience, and alexithymia has been discussed in this light. This remains an interpretive link rather than a settled empirical conclusion.

## Key works

Bagby, Parker, and Taylor's two 1994 papers are the primary sources for the instrument. Taylor, Bagby, and Parker's Disorders of Affect Regulation (1997) places alexithymia in its full clinical and theoretical context. Sifneos (1973) is the origin of the concept itself, and Barrett (2017) is the contemporary theory of constructed emotion with which the scale is discussed in this Atlas.
