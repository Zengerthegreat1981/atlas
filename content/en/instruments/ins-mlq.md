# Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ)

The Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ) is a ten-item self-report instrument that measures two distinct dimensions of experienced meaning: the presence of meaning in one's life, and the search for meaning. Developed by Michael Steger and colleagues and published in 2006, it is the most widely used measure of meaning in life in contemporary psychology. Its central insight is that having meaning and looking for meaning are independent: a person can possess meaning and still search, search without finding, or neither search nor possess. By scoring the two dimensions separately, the MLQ makes it possible to distinguish these very different human situations instead of collapsing them into a single score.

## What it measures

The MLQ has two five-item subscales. Presence of Meaning asks whether the respondent feels their life has purpose, significance, and coherence — items such as "I understand my life's meaning" and "My life has a clear sense of purpose." Search for Meaning asks whether the respondent is actively looking for meaning or purpose — items such as "I am looking for something that makes my life feel meaningful." Each item is rated on a seven-point scale from "absolutely untrue" to "absolutely true." The two subscales are scored separately and are typically only modestly correlated, which is itself a finding: searching for meaning does not imply lacking it, and possessing meaning does not end the search.

## Development and structure

Steger, Frazier, Oishi, and Kaler developed the MLQ to repair the weaknesses of earlier meaning measures, above all the Purpose in Life test of Crumbaugh and Maholick (1964), which mixed the presence of meaning with its search and with other constructs such as boredom and control. The team wrote a large pool of items, tested them on American undergraduate samples, and used factor analysis to arrive at a clean two-factor structure with five items per factor. The resulting instrument was short enough for routine use in surveys and experiments, and its two dimensions mapped directly onto a long-standing theoretical debate: Viktor Frankl's claim that the search for meaning is a primary human motive, and the distinction in the literature between meaning as something found and meaning as something made.

## Psychometric properties

The MLQ shows good internal consistency for both subscales, with Cronbach's alpha typically around 0.86 for Presence and 0.87 for Search, and acceptable test-retest stability over periods of weeks to months. Its factor structure has been replicated across many samples and cultures, including translations into dozens of languages. The two subscales show different patterns of association, which supports their separation: Presence of Meaning correlates positively with life satisfaction, positive affect, and self-reported well-being, and negatively with depression and anxiety, while Search for Meaning correlates more weakly or negatively with well-being, and positively with rumination and neuroticism in Western samples — though in some East Asian samples the search correlates positively with well-being, suggesting that the experience of searching is culturally shaped. No well-documented Arabic norming data for the MLQ exists within this project's sources, although the instrument has appeared in scattered Arabic-language studies.

## Uses and limits

The MLQ is used across positive psychology, logotherapy research, well-being studies, and research on adolescents and adults facing illness, loss, or life transitions. It is the closest available instrument for testing Frankl's central claim that human beings are by necessity meaning-seeking creatures, and it has become the standard outcome measure in meaning-centred psychotherapy trials. Its limits follow from its brevity and its method. As a self-report measure of an abstract and personal concept, it depends on what "meaning" means to each respondent, with no shared definition supplied, so two people with identical scores may understand the questions quite differently in practice. It measures the subjective sense of meaning, not the sources of meaning — work, relationships, faith — which require a companion instrument such as the Sources of Meaning questionnaire. And because it was developed on Western undergraduate samples, its factor structure and its correlates cannot simply be assumed to hold identically in other cultures.

## Key works

Steger and colleagues' 2006 paper is the primary source for the instrument, its development, and its psychometric properties. Frankl's Man's Search for Meaning (1959 in its English edition) supplies the theoretical background against which the MLQ is usually read. Steger, Kashdan, Sullivan, and Lorentz (2008) deepened the understanding of the search dimension, and Heintzelman and King (2014) reviewed the wider literature on meaning in life of which the MLQ is now the central measure.
