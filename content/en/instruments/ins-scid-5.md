# Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5)

A widely used semi-structured diagnostic interview in psychological research and clinics; it guides the examiner step by step in systematically verifying the precise diagnostic criteria for DSM-5 disorders (mood, psychosis, anxiety, trauma, and personality disorders).

## The branching standard structure (Decision Tree)

The interview poses initial screening questions for each diagnostic group (mood disorders, psychosis, anxiety...), and moves automatically to the expanded detailed module only when the screening criteria are met, which shortens administration time and systematically checks for the exclusion of organic and substance causes before any psychological diagnosis is fixed.

## Psychometric accuracy and inter-rater reliability

The instrument is the first reference for ensuring diagnostic agreement among examiners (Inter-rater Reliability) in pharmacological and academic research, as the protocols of major clinical trials often require the use of the SCID or its equivalent to ensure that the sample of participants actually meets the stated diagnostic criteria before they are admitted to the study.

## Authorship and standardization

The SCID series began in the 1980s under Robert Spitzer, Janet Williams, Miriam Gibbon, and Michael First, with a version for DSM-III-R published in 1990. The version linked to DSM-5 was developed under Michael B. First, with Janet Williams, Rhonda Karg, and Robert Spitzer, and appeared in 2015-2016 as a clinician version (SCID-5-CV) and a broader research version (SCID-5-RV); a separate interview covers personality disorders (SCID-5-PD). The instrument is not standardized on a "standardization sample" in the usual psychometric sense of standardized tests (as with personality scales or normed questionnaires); rather, its properties are tested through inter-rater reliability studies on multiple clinical samples.

## Criticism of the instrument

Administering the SCID-5 requires specialized training and a relatively long time (often an hour or more), which limits its use in fast-paced or resource-limited clinical settings; the quality of its results also depends greatly on the examiner's skill and strict adherence to the protocol, and its inter-rater reliability declines in some complex diagnoses or those with overlapping symptoms. And because it is a clinical interview and not a self-report questionnaire, it requires the examiner to be present in person or by direct connection, unlike self-screening instruments such as the PHQ-9, which can be distributed widely without comparable training cost.
