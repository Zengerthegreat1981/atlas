# A Point of Encounter: Existential Therapy and ACT

This entry describes a live encounter, not a comparison table. That table already exists in this Atlas as rel-act. What follows instead is an account of what actually happens when the two frameworks meet in practice: an ACT therapist uses the language of values, presence, and acceptance — words that sound deeply existential — and an existential therapist wonders whether the resemblance is real or only verbal. The encounter is descriptive and exploratory. It declares no winner and proposes no hybrid model.

## The Meeting Point in the Room

Imagine an ACT session in which a client has stopped doing what matters to them because anxiety makes every step feel dangerous. The therapist does not set out to eliminate the anxiety. Instead, the work turns toward values: what kind of life would be worth living even with the anxiety present? What committed actions, however small, would express that life? The client is invited into the present moment, to notice thoughts as passing events rather than commands, and to act toward their values while carrying the discomfort.

An existential therapist listening to this description would recognise the territory immediately. Choosing how to live in the face of anxiety, refusing to let pain-avoidance become the purpose of life, taking responsibility for a direction rather than waiting for certainty — these are existential themes, close to the four ultimate concerns Irvin Yalom set out in *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980): death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness. Both give a central place to personal values and committed choice in spite of difficulty, and both reject pain-avoidance as a primary therapeutic goal.

## Values, Presence, Acceptance

Three words carry most of the encounter. Values, in ACT, are freely chosen directions for living — qualities of action such as honesty, kindness, or creativity — that orient committed action, clarified through exercises and revisited as treatment proceeds. Presence, contact with the present moment, is one of the six processes of the ACT hexaflex model, cultivated through mindfulness: attending to what is happening now rather than living inside rumination or dread. Acceptance is the willingness to experience unwanted thoughts and feelings without struggling to control them, freeing energy for valued action.

Each of these has an existential cousin. Values resemble the existential emphasis on authorship — the idea that a person must take a stand on what their life is for, without a ready-made answer. Presence resembles the existential attention to being-here-now, to the immediacy of lived experience rather than to abstractions about it. Acceptance resembles the existential refusal of bad faith, the refusal to pretend that freedom and anxiety can be escaped by some technique. ACT practitioners, including the framework's founder Steven C. Hayes, have drawn on Yalom's map of the ultimate concerns as a way of reading the hexaflex against a background of existential givens.

## The Deep Methodological Split

And yet the split beneath the meeting is deep and methodological, not merely verbal. ACT is built on a behavioural and contextual foundation: functional contextualism as its philosophy of science, and relational frame theory (RFT) as its account of human language and cognition, set out by Hayes, Barnes-Holmes, and Roche in *Relational Frame Theory: A Post-Skinnerian Account of Human Language and Cognition* (2001). Its aim is a model of behaviour change that can be tested, measured, and refined through trials, outcome measures, and replicable protocols.

Existential therapy starts somewhere else entirely. It begins not with a model of behaviour but with a phenomenological analysis of the structure of human being itself — thrownness, Being-with, temporality, and the givens Yalom described — and it treats the therapeutic encounter as a meeting between two people in which those structures show themselves. Its evidence is the quality of the encounter and the client's deepening relationship to their own existence, not a score on an outcome measure. Van Deurzen's *Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy in Practice* (2012) presents the work in these terms: disciplined attention to lived experience that resists reduction to a protocol. The difference is not that one side is scientific and the other is not; the two sides mean different things by explanation, evidence, and what a therapy is for.

## Where the Bridge Usually Fails

The bridge between the two frameworks most often fails at the word "values." In ACT practice, values are commonly treated as something that can be identified, clarified, and written down — through questionnaires, card sorts, and worksheets — and then used as a compass for committed action. An existential therapist will often feel that something essential has been lost in that move: for the existential tradition, the difficulty is not mainly identifying one's values but facing the anxiety of choosing them at all, in the knowledge that no authority can certify the choice. The anguish is in the choosing, not in the listing. A protocol that treats values as settled objects can therefore look, from the existential side, like avoidance of the very confrontation the therapy exists to hold open.

## No Winner, No Merger

This dialogue is descriptive and exploratory, and it ends without a verdict. It does not argue that ACT is existential therapy with better measures, or that existential therapy is ACT without the science, and it does not propose merging the two into a hybrid model without a careful methodological justification — a merger would need to explain how a behavioural science of language and a phenomenology of Being could share one account of the person, and no such account currently exists. What the encounter offers instead is a clearer view of both sides: ACT shows existential therapy what its themes look like operationalised and tested, and existential therapy shows ACT what they look like followed to the depth where measurement stops helping.
