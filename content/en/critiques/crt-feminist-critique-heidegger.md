# Feminist Critique of Dasein's Gender Neutrality

This is an external critique of the existential framework as a whole, not an internal debate within it. Its target is a founding assumption of Martin Heidegger's philosophy: that *Dasein* — the being whose being is an issue for it, the human existence analysed in *Being and Time* (1927) — is gender-neutral. Feminist critics argue that this neutrality is not achieved at all, and that the very abstraction that presents Dasein as "without body and without sex" quietly smuggles in masculine assumptions about what a human life is. The charge is therefore not that Heidegger drew the wrong conclusion from his own premises, but that the premises themselves carry a bias his framework cannot see from inside.

## What Heidegger Claims

In *Being and Time*, Heidegger insists that Dasein is not a particular kind of entity, such as a human organism with a sex, but the being for whom its own being matters. Its structures — thrownness, Being-with, being-toward-death, authenticity and inauthenticity — are presented as formal features of existence as such, prior to sex difference. On this view, the analysis of Dasein is neutral ground: whatever sex a person happens to be, the existential structures are the same. That neutrality is part of why existential therapy could borrow his concepts — thrownness, Being-with, authenticity — as if they applied equally to every client. The feminist critique asks whether that "as if" is doing hidden work.

## Irigaray and the Forgetting of Air

The most sustained feminist engagement with Heidegger is Luce Irigaray's *L'Oubli de l'air chez Martin Heidegger* (*The Forgetting of Air in Martin Heidegger*, 1983; English translation, 1999). Irigaray argues that Heidegger's thinking is organised around earth — solid ground, rootedness, dwelling on firm foundations — while it forgets air, the element that surrounds, sustains, and precedes all dwelling. Air, for Irigaray, is the forgotten medium of life and of breath, and its forgetting is double: a forgetting of the feminine and a forgetting of the element on which life depends before any "thrownness" into the world. Dasein's supposed neutrality, on this reading, is achieved by abstracting away the body that breathes, gestates, and is sexed — and the body that is abstracted away turns out, on inspection, to be a masculine body imagined as the universal one.

## The Wider Critical Current

Irigaray is not alone. Simone de Beauvoir had already, in *The Second Sex* (1949), shown how the supposedly universal human subject of philosophy was in practice a male subject, with woman defined as the Other — a move that later feminist readers applied directly to Heidegger. In the Anglophone world, the current took institutional shape with *Feminist Interpretations of Martin Heidegger* (2001), edited by Nancy J. Holland and Patricia Huntington, a collection of fourteen essays ranging from sharp accusations of structural masculinity in the framing of the question of Being to more conciliatory readings that see in the analysis of Dasein a ground feminism can build on rather than merely attack. Tina Chanter's work on Heidegger, including *Ethics of Eros* (1995), presses the question of the body and sexual difference against Heidegger's abstractions, while Margaret Whitford's *Luce Irigaray: Philosophy in the Feminine* (1991) traces the dispute over whether Irigaray's project extends phenomenology or breaks with it — a disagreement among her interpreters that remains unresolved. Joan Stambaugh's English translation of *Being and Time* (1996) is the text through which many of these readers encountered Heidegger in English, and her work as a translator and interpreter belongs to the background of the debate, even though she is not herself one of its feminist critics.

## How Neutrality Hides Masculinity

The critics' shared move is to ask what disappears when Dasein is declared neutral. Three disappearances recur. First, the body: birth, pregnancy, and the long dependence of the infant are not part of the existential analytic, so the "thrown" human arrives in the world already adult, upright, and self-sufficient — a picture that fits one kind of bodily life better than another. Second, care: Heidegger's Being-with and his account of care (*Sorge*) are formal structures, but the concrete labour of caring for others, historically assigned to women, is never examined as a mode of Being in its own right. Third, relationality: authenticity is described as a solitary, resolute owning of one's own death, a model of independence that can make interdependence look like inauthenticity.

## External Critique, Not Internal Debate

The distinction matters for this Atlas. An internal debate (a dbt- entry) is a disagreement between positions that share the same framework — two Heideggerians arguing over how to read a passage, for example. This critique does not share the framework. It judges the framework itself, from outside, on the grounds that its starting point is not neutral ground but a position with a history. Accepting it would not revise a conclusion inside Heidegger's system; it would require rethinking the system from its foundations. Heidegger never replied directly to Irigaray; the responses recorded here come from later interpreters.

## What Follows for Existential Therapy

The critique has a practical edge for the therapeutic school built on Heideggerian concepts. If a therapist takes thrownness, Being-with, and authenticity as neutral descriptions of every client's existence, they may silently treat a model of solitary, independent self-ownership as the standard of psychological health — and read a client's embedded, relational, bodily life (pregnancy, caregiving, mutual dependence) as a failure of authenticity rather than as a different mode of Being. The feminist critique asks the therapist to notice whose experience the "neutral" categories were modelled on, and to keep them open to experiences they were not designed to hold.
