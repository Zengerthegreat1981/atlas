# Stages of First Language Acquisition

A regular developmental sequence that infants and children pass through in acquiring their first language. It has been documented precisely through multiple longitudinal studies. It begins before the first word is spoken and extends to the construction of complex sentences.

## Babbling

Before babbling, infants produce cooing sounds in the first months. From roughly six to ten months, typically developing infants begin "canonical" babbling, producing repeated syllables such as "ba-ba" or "da-da." These do not yet carry a specific meaning, but they reflect growing motor control of the speech apparatus. Babbling is shaped over time by the sounds of the surrounding language, and deaf infants exposed to sign language have been reported to babble manually.

## The Holophrastic Stage

Around the twelfth month, the child utters first single words that sometimes carry a complete communicative function. The word "water" alone, for example, may mean "I want water," depending on context and tone. This is why these words are called holophrases, meaning "whole phrases."

## Two-Word Stage and Telegraphic Speech

Between about eighteen and twenty-four months, the child begins combining two words ("Mama went"). Then the child develops "telegraphic speech": utterances that omit function words, such as articles and prepositions, and keep the essential content words. This resembles the style of abbreviated telegrams. Even so, these utterances carry an internally consistent grammatical structure, not random strings.

## Stages by Mean Length of Utterance (MLU)

Roger Brown divided early development into five stages, measured by mean length of utterance (MLU), in his study of three children (Adam, Eve and Sarah). Each stage has distinctive grammatical features. In his data, 14 grammatical morphemes of English were acquired in a fairly consistent order across the three children, beginning with the present progressive *-ing* and the prepositions *in* and *on*, followed by the plural *-s*, and only later forms such as the regular past *-ed* and the third-person singular *-s*. The order was relatively regular across children despite variation in individual pace.

## Vocabulary Spurt

Between about sixteen and twenty months, many children go through what is known as the "vocabulary spurt." The rate of acquiring new words accelerates sharply after a relatively slow period in the first months, bringing the child's vocabulary to hundreds of words by the second birthday. Some researchers have suggested that this spurt coincides with the child's growing realization that things have names, though the cause of the spurt remains debated.

## Overgeneralization

In the early stage of sentence construction, children exhibit a temporary incorrect pattern. It is nonetheless positive evidence that they are learning rules rather than merely memorizing. After learning a regular morphological rule, such as adding "-ed" to form the past tense in English, the child mistakenly applies it to irregular verbs that they had previously pronounced correctly by direct memorization, saying "goed" instead of "went." This phenomenon is known as U-shaped development: performance temporarily regresses before advancing again toward the final correct form, once the exceptions are absorbed.
