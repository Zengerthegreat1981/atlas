# Content Analysis

Content analysis is a family of research methods for the systematic study of texts, images, broadcasts and other recorded communication. The researcher defines a body of material, draws a sample from it where necessary, sets out categories in an explicit coding scheme, and applies those categories consistently, so that patterns in what is said or shown can be described, compared and checked by others. It is used to study newspapers, political speeches, television, advertising, textbooks, policy documents, interview transcripts and social media posts.

## Origins and Development

Systematic counting of newspaper content was practised in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and Max Weber proposed a study of the press, including measurement of newspaper content, to the German Sociological Society in 1910. The method took firmer shape in the United States in the 1930s and 1940s, in studies of propaganda and mass communication associated with Harold Lasswell and colleagues. Bernard Berelson's *Content Analysis in Communication Research* (1952) became a standard statement, defining it as a technique for the objective, systematic and quantitative description of the manifest content of communication. Siegfried Kracauer argued in the same year that this emphasis on frequencies neglected meaning, and called for a qualitative content analysis. Later work, notably Ole Holsti's textbook (1969) and Klaus Krippendorff's *Content Analysis: An Introduction to Its Methodology* (1980), broadened the method to include inferences from texts to the contexts of their production and use.

## Procedure

A typical study states a research question, specifies the population of documents and the unit of analysis (a word, theme, article or image), builds a codebook, trains coders, tests the scheme, and then codes the material. Reliability is central: independent coders should reach similar results from the same material, and agreement is often reported with measures such as Cohen's kappa or Krippendorff's alpha. Validity concerns whether the categories actually capture the concept of interest.

## Quantitative and Qualitative Forms

Quantitative content analysis counts the presence, frequency or prominence of categories and relates them to other variables, for instance how often different groups appear as sources in news reports. Qualitative content analysis keeps the discipline of explicit categories but develops them in closer dialogue with the material and attends to context and latent meaning. It overlaps with thematic analysis and differs in emphasis from discourse analysis, which examines how language constructs social realities.

## Strengths, Limits and Current Use

The method is unobtrusive, can cover long periods and large collections, and allows replication. Its limits are that categories may strip statements of context, and that content alone cannot show how audiences understand messages or what effects they have; those questions require other evidence. Computer-assisted techniques, including dictionary methods and machine learning classifiers, now make it possible to code very large digital archives, but they still depend on human judgment to define categories and to validate results.
