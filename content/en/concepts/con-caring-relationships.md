# Caring Relationships

## Meaning and Definition

Caring relationships are a central concept in care ethics. The term refers to the moral bonds that arise from genuine attention to the concrete needs of another, such as the relationship between a mother and her child or a nurse and his or her patient. These bonds are a source of moral value no less important than abstract rules and reciprocal contracts. In this conception, ethics is not built at the expense of rights and duties between independent, equal individuals, but on the response to the vulnerability and dependence of the other. Such a relationship is by its nature unequal, between one who cares and one who is cared for.

## Roots in Islamic Ethics

This meaning resonates deeply in the Islamic concepts of "mercy" (*rahma*) and "maintaining family ties" (*silat al-rahim*). Care among relatives and the weak is not treated as an optional virtue but as a religious duty on which the rules of maintenance (*nafaqa*) and custody (*hadana*) are built. The hadith "He is not of us who does not show mercy to our young or honor our elders" establishes an ethical standard based on relationship rather than on abstract contract. Ibn Miskawayh, in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, also discussed love (friendship) as an end of human association and a means of perfecting souls, drawing, in the Aristotelian manner, on distinctions among kinds of friendship, the highest of which rests on virtue rather than benefit. This latter kind is closest to a genuine caring relationship.

## Western Historical Context

The concept emerged philosophically through Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* (1982), which criticized Kohlberg's theory of moral development for favoring the logic of abstract justice, and proposed that women tend to frame ethics around preserving relationships and avoiding harm. Nel Noddings developed this approach philosophically in *Caring* (1984), defining the moral act as a response to the call of the "cared-for," arising from a genuine "engrossment" in his or her situation, rather than from applying a general rule. This approach faces objections, including the risk that the idea of care may be exploited to justify confining women to a traditional role, and the difficulty of extending the ethics of care beyond intimate relationships into broad political justice.

## Applied Example

Care relationships are clearly visible in nursing. A good nurse does not merely apply the medical protocol, but responds to the particular anxiety of the patient and his or her family context. Virginia Held, in *The Ethics of Care* (2006), regards care as the basis for a complete alternative ethical theory, not merely a subsidiary virtue.

## Objection and Balance

It is feared that focusing on caring relationships may become a justification for overburdening the carer (usually a woman) without return or recognition. For this reason, Held and Joan Tronto (*Moral Boundaries*, 1993) called for integrating the logic of care with the demands of distributive justice rather than replacing them, so that care work is recognized socially and economically rather than left as a silent individual burden.

## Further Primary Sources

Several other texts shape the study of caring relations. Milton Mayeroff's *On Caring* (1971) described caring as helping another to grow, before care ethics emerged as a school. Sara Ruddick's "Maternal Thinking" (1980), later expanded into a book of the same title (1989), drew moral concepts from the practice of raising children. Annette Baier's essay "Trust and Antitrust" (1986) argued that moral philosophy had neglected trust between unequal and dependent parties. Eva Feder Kittay's *Love's Labor* (1999) examined dependency work and argued that those who care for dependents themselves need support, a demand that a just society must answer. Berenice Fisher and Joan Tronto's "Toward a Feminist Theory of Caring" (1990) distinguished phases of care, including caring about, taking care of, care-giving, and care-receiving. Michael Slote's *The Ethics of Care and Empathy* (2007) places empathy at the centre and extends the approach to questions of justice.
