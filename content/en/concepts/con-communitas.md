# Communitas: The Shared Feeling of Being Together as Equals

**Communitas** is the intense, spontaneous feeling of togetherness and equality that arises when people pass through a shared experience outside the ordinary structures of status and role. The term is associated above all with Victor Turner, who adopted the Latin word for community to name something he found again and again in ritual life: a moment in which rank, wealth, age, and office fall away, and people experience one another simply as fellow human beings. Communitas is not a society or an institution; it is a mode of relationship — direct, immediate, and egalitarian — that appears in the middle of rituals, pilgrimages, festivals, and moments of crisis, and then fades as ordinary structure reasserts itself. Turner argued that human social life needs both: structure, which organises and divides, and communitas, which dissolves and unites.

## Definition and Development

Turner developed the concept in *The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure* (1969), building on Arnold van Gennep's study of rites of passage. Van Gennep had shown that passages — birth, initiation, marriage, death — move people through three phases: separation from an old status, a liminal phase in which they are "betwixt and between," and reincorporation into a new status. Turner seized on the middle, liminal phase: there, stripped of their normal identities, initiates experience communitas — a bond of equals who share the same uncertainty and the same ordeal. In the liminal phase of Ndembu initiation in Zambia, for example, novices were humbled, dressed alike, and forbidden the privileges of their former rank; in that shared lowliness they discovered a fellowship that ordinary life denied them.

Turner distinguished three kinds of communitas. Spontaneous communitas is the unplanned feeling of unity that erupts in a shared ordeal or joy — the fellowship of pilgrims on the road, of soldiers after battle, of strangers helping one another in disaster. Normative communitas is the attempt to preserve that feeling in an enduring group, as in monastic orders or communes that try to live permanently as equals. Ideological communitas is the vision of such a society, expressed in utopian writings and millenarian movements. Most attempts at normative communitas, Turner observed, eventually develop their own structure — rules, leaders, ranks — because a human group cannot live indefinitely without organisation.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Turner's contrast between structure and anti-structure became one of anthropology's most influential frameworks. Structure is the differentiated, hierarchical order of society: statuses, roles, offices, and the norms that govern them. Anti-structure is the dissolution of that order in liminality and communitas, where the normal rules are suspended and new possibilities appear. Ritual, for Turner, is the social mechanism that moves people between the two: it takes them out of structure, lets them experience communitas, and returns them to structure renewed — or sometimes, changed, carrying the insights of the liminal phase back into ordinary life. This made ritual not a conservative repetition of the past but a source of creativity and social transformation.

Émile Durkheim had described something similar half a century earlier: in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912), he wrote of "collective effervescence," the electric excitement of a crowd gathered in ritual, in which individuals feel themselves lifted out of themselves and into the group. Turner's concept has often been compared with this, but it gives closer attention to the quality of the bond, its egalitarianism, its spontaneity, and its relationship to the passage through liminality. Later scholars, such as Edith Turner (in *Communitas: The Anthropology of Collective Joy*, 2012) and Bjørn Thomassen, extended the analysis to pilgrimage, protest, and performance, showing communitas at work far beyond the initiation rites where Victor Turner first found it.

## Ethnographic Examples

The classic ethnographic setting of communitas is pilgrimage. Victor and Edith Turner, in *Image and Pilgrimage in Christian Culture* (1978), described pilgrims to shrines such as Lourdes and Guadalupe: people of every class and nation, stripped of their ordinary markers, walking together, sharing food and shelter, and experiencing a fellowship that the hierarchies of their home parishes denied them. The pilgrim's road is liminal space — neither home nor destination — and on it, communitas flourishes. Similar bonds have been described in the Hajj, where pilgrims wear simple white garments (ihram) that visually minimise distinctions of wealth and nationality, and in the great Hindu pilgrimages of India.

Disaster studies describe the same phenomenon: in the aftermath of earthquakes and floods, survivors often report an extraordinary spirit of mutual help and equality that lasts until the authorities and the old hierarchies return.

## Contemporary Debates

Contemporary debate has questioned both the concept and its politics. One criticism is that communitas is not as egalitarian as Turner claimed: even in liminal settings, old hierarchies of gender, race, and class often persist, and the feeling of unity may be experienced differently by those at the top and those at the bottom. Feminist anthropologists have pointed out that the liminal humbling of male initiates can depend on the subordination of women, and that communitas among men may be bought at women's expense. A second criticism is that Turner romanticised anti-structure, treating it as liberating by definition, when the dissolution of structure can also be terrifying — in war, in forced migration, in the liminality of statelessness.
