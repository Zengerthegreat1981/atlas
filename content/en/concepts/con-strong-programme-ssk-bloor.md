# The Strong Programme in the Sociology of Scientific Knowledge

A methodological framework demanding that the content of scientific theories themselves, even the correct and well-established ones, be studied as a social product requiring sociological explanation, not scientific error alone.

## The Central Idea

In *Knowledge and Social Imagery* (1976), David Bloor put forward the "Strong Programme", developed with Barry Barnes and the Edinburgh group, based on four tenets: "causality" (the sociologist should look for the causal conditions, social among others, that bring about beliefs and knowledge); "impartiality" (with respect to truth and falsity, rationality and irrationality, success and failure, both sides of each dichotomy require explanation); "symmetry" (the same types of cause should explain true and false beliefs); and "reflexivity" (the patterns of explanation should be applicable to sociology itself). This radically goes beyond the earlier position of Robert Merton, who studied the institutional norms of science as a social system but exempted the content of correct theories from social explanation, considering that their correctness is explained logically/empirically alone.

## The Four Principles in Detail and Their Intellectual Sources

Bloor, who worked in the Science Studies Unit at the University of Edinburgh, drew from the sociologist Émile Durkheim the idea of treating beliefs (including scientific ones) as "social facts" open to objective causal explanation regardless of their presumed truth or falsity, and from the philosophy of the later Ludwig Wittgenstein the idea that the standards of logical "correctness" are themselves rooted in collective social practices ("language games") and not in a rational standard transcending the social context. In this way the Strong Programme directs a direct critique at the traditional positivist philosophy of science, which separated the "context of discovery" (open to social and psychological explanation) from the "context of justification" (subject to logic and experiment alone), insisting that this very separation is philosophically indefensible.

## The Exemplary Case Study: The Hobbes-Boyle Debate

The Strong Programme was applied in practice in famous empirical studies, most notably Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer's study of the seventeenth-century debate between Thomas Hobbes and Robert Boyle over the air pump (in their book *Leviathan and the Air-Pump*, 1985). They showed that the victory of Boyle's experimental method, based on the collective witnessing of experimental facts, was not the result of its purely logical superiority, but the product of specific social and political agreements about who counts as a reliable "witness" and how scientific credibility is socially constructed. This embodied the principle of "symmetry" in practice by treating the winning party (Boyle) and the losing party (Hobbes) with the same sociological explanatory tools.

## Influence

This work was a founding influence on the field of "science and technology studies" and influenced the philosophy and history of science, despite the sharp controversy it provoked over epistemic "relativism" in the "science wars" of the 1990s.
