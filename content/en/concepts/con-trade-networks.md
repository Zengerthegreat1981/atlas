# Trade Networks

Trade networks are the webs of exchange that link people, communities, and regions through the movement of goods, services, and information. Long before the modern global economy, shells, salt, obsidian, spices, textiles, and metals travelled thousands of kilometres along routes maintained by traders, middlemen, and kin. Trade networks do more than move things: they carry ideas, languages, religions, and technologies, create relationships of trust and obligation, and reshape the societies they connect. Anthropologists study trade as a social institution, not merely an economic transaction, asking who trades with whom, what can and cannot be exchanged, and how exchange creates political power.

## Definition

A trade network is a set of connected exchange relationships sustained over time. It may be organised around markets, where strangers buy and sell at agreed prices; around long-distance partnerships, in which traders in different regions maintain exclusive relationships with one another; or around gift exchange, where the movement of goods seals alliances and creates debts. Networks can be local, linking villages in a region, or intercontinental, as in the Silk Roads that connected China to the Mediterranean or the trans-Saharan caravan trade that linked West Africa to North Africa. The goods traded range from everyday necessities such as grain and salt to luxuries such as silk, ivory, and gold, whose scarcity gives them prestige value. Crucially, trade is rarely a matter of isolated individuals seeking profit: it is embedded in social relations of kinship, friendship, ethnicity, and religion, which provide the trust that makes exchange possible.

## Origins and Development

Archaeology shows that long-distance exchange is very old. Obsidian from Anatolian sources reached settlements hundreds of kilometres away in the Neolithic period, and shells from the coast have been found far inland in many parts of the world. By the Bronze Age, tin and copper were traded across Europe and the Near East to make bronze, and by the first millennium BCE the Mediterranean, Indian Ocean, and Eurasian steppe were linked by overlapping commercial networks. The great trading systems of the premodern world — the Silk Roads, the Indian Ocean monsoon trade, the trans-Saharan routes — moved not only goods but also Buddhism, Islam, and Christianity, along with writing systems, crops, and diseases. European expansion from the sixteenth century onwards reorganised these networks into a single world economy, as Fernand Braudel and Immanuel Wallerstein argued, drawing the Americas, Africa, and Asia into circuits of silver, sugar, slaves, and manufactured goods. Colonialism and later globalisation intensified these connections while concentrating control in the hands of a few powerful states and corporations.

## Key Debates

One major debate concerns the nature of premodern trade. Karl Polanyi argued that in early societies exchange was organised by reciprocity and redistribution rather than by markets, and that the self-regulating market was a modern invention. Critics reply that markets and profit-seeking are far older and more widespread than Polanyi allowed, and that his distinction between embedded and disembedded economies was too sharp. A second debate concerns the effects of trade on the societies it connects. Does trade spread prosperity and knowledge, or does it create dependence and inequality, as world-systems theory claims? A third debate concerns the role of trust and culture: trade networks often follow ethnic and religious lines — Armenian, Jewish, Gujarati, and Chinese merchant diasporas are classic examples — because shared identity supplies the trust that contracts alone cannot. Finally, anthropologists study what cannot be traded: in many societies, sacred objects, land, and persons are inalienable, and attempts to commodify them provoke moral outrage.

## Contemporary Relevance

Trade networks are the skeleton of the contemporary world economy. Container shipping, air freight, and digital platforms have made supply chains faster and more complex, while also making them fragile, as the disruptions of the COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated. Debates over free trade, tariffs, and supply-chain resilience dominate politics, and the rise of e-commerce has created new networks of small traders linked by platforms rather than by face-to-face relationships. At the same time, informal trade — cross-border smuggling, street markets, and unrecorded exchange — remains enormous, especially in Africa and Asia, sustaining livelihoods that official statistics miss. Understanding trade networks as social as well as economic phenomena is essential to grasping how globalisation actually works on the ground.
