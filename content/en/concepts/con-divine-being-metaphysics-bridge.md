# Divine Being and Metaphysics

The history of the relationship between talk of God and the inquiry into being begins with a question: is God one of the beings, even the greatest of them, or is He the principle of being itself? Philosophers and theologians in Islam, Christianity and Judaism answered this question in closely related formulations, and for several of them historical transmission (notably from Avicenna to Latin and Jewish authors) is well documented, though each thinker also developed the question in his own way.

## Islamic Thought First

In the "Metaphysics" (*Ilahiyyat*) of *The Cure* (*al-Shifa'*) and in *Remarks and Admonitions* (*al-Isharat wa-al-Tanbihat*), Ibn Sina offered the most precise Arabic formulation of the question: every existent is either contingent in its existence in itself or necessary in its existence in itself; the contingent exists only through a cause, and the chain of causes cannot go on to infinity, so there must be a Necessary Existent whose quiddity is nothing but its existence. This is the "proof of the truthful" (*burhan al-siddiqin*), which proves God through being rather than through creatures. Amos Bertolacci, in his study of the reception of the *Metaphysics* in Avicenna (2006), showed how he made being, as being, the subject of first philosophy and then made God the cause of the existence of its subject. Al-Ghazali, in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (1095), took issue with part of this structure and rejected the idea that God is a necessary cause from which the world emanates eternally; Ibn Rushd, in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*, responded with a counter-critique. As for Ibn 'Arabi, his *Bezels of Wisdom* (*Fusus al-hikam*) and other works are the source of the doctrine later called the unity of being (*wahdat al-wujud*; the phrase itself is attributed to later followers and interpreters), and Mulla Sadra, in *al-Asfar al-arba'a* (*The Four Journeys*, completed in the 1630s), restated the primacy of existence and its gradation, so that for him being is one reality with differing degrees.

## The Transmission to the Latin and Jewish Traditions

Moses Maimonides, in *The Guide for the Perplexed* (completed c. 1190), drew on Avicennian arguments for a Necessary Existent and made negation the privileged method for speaking of God's attributes. The distinction between essence and existence, developed by Avicenna and available in Latin translation, was taken up by Thomas Aquinas, in *On Being and Essence* (early 1250s) and the *Summa Theologiae* (1265/66-1273), who made God the "subsistent existence" (*ipsum esse subsistens*). Étienne Gilson read Exodus 3:14 ("I am who I am") as support for this understanding and called it the "metaphysics of Exodus" in *The Spirit of Mediaeval Philosophy* (1936; French original 1932), a reading that other historians contest. David Burrell, in *Knowing the Unknowable God* (1986), compared Ibn Sina, Maimonides and Aquinas, and showed how each of them treats the language of divine attributes as neither purely univocal nor merely equivocal.

## Modern Criticism

Kant, in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781), criticised the ontological proofs, since existence is not a predicate added to a concept. Heidegger called this kind of thinking "onto-theology" in *Identity and Difference* (1957), because it treats the question of being as the question of a highest being who grounds all other beings. Jean-Luc Marion, in *God Without Being* (1982; English 1991), then proposed thinking of God in terms of gift and love rather than being. In analytic philosophy, Alvin Plantinga, in *The Nature of Necessity* (1974), restated the ontological argument in the language of possible worlds.

## Limits of the Connection

It should not be assumed that "being" means one thing in all these schools: being in Ibn Sina is predicated analogically (*bi-l-tashkik*) of the Necessary and the contingent; the doctrine of the unity of being is a mystical and metaphysical position and is not simply the same as Avicenna's necessary-existent argument; and al-Ghazali's criticism rests partly on the claim that the philosophers' God does not act by will and choice. Another challenge is that the language of ontological inquiry is severely abstract, and so lacks the religious experience with which the science of religions is concerned.
