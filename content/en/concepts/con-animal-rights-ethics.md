# Animal Rights

## Meaning and Definition

Animal rights ethics is a branch of contemporary moral philosophy that asks whether non-human animals possess an independent moral standing, such that their interests, or even their rights, carry binding moral weight for human beings, rather than being merely incidental considerations that follow from human interests. The debate divides into two main schools: the "animal welfare" school (Animal Welfare), which is content with reducing animal suffering without denying the human right to use animals, and the more radical "animal rights" school (Animal Rights), which regards the use of animals as tools for human purposes as a moral violation in itself, regardless of how humanely that use is carried out.

## Historical Context

The roots of the debate go back to the English philosopher Jeremy Bentham, who wrote in 1789 his famous statement that the question is not "can they reason?" but "can they suffer?", thereby placing the capacity to feel pain at the foundation of moral consideration. The real launch of the field, however, came in the 1970s with the Australian philosopher Peter Singer's book *Animal Liberation* (1975), which popularized the concept of "speciesism" (a term coined by Richard Ryder in 1970) as a moral bias comparable to racism and sexism, from a utilitarian perspective that measures suffering regardless of species. By contrast, the American philosopher Tom Regan, in *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983), presented a rights-based theory independent of utilitarianism, founded on the idea that beings that are "subjects-of-a-life" (subjects-of-a-life) possess an inherent value that cannot be reduced to calculations of overall utility.

## Principal Positions

Singer adopts a utilitarian position that holds that suffering is an evil to be reduced wherever it occurs, and that excluding the interests of animals merely because they are not human is an unjustified bias. Yet he does not, in principle, object to using animals if the overall benefit exceeds the harm. Regan rejects this calculative logic from its foundations and holds that animals have basic rights that cannot be traded for any amount of collective benefit, just as the rights of a human being may not be sacrificed for the welfare of the majority. There are also feminist and care-based approaches, such as that of Carol Adams in *The Sexual Politics of Meat* (1990), which links the exploitation of animals to patriarchal structures.

## Objections and Criticism

These positions are objected to on the grounds that they equate beings that differ greatly in rational capacities and consciousness, and that granting animals rights similar to human rights clashes with basic life practices such as agriculture and medicine. Philosophers such as Carl Cohen ("The Case for the Use of Animals in Biomedical Research," 1986) also argue that rights are a reciprocal moral concept that presupposes the being's capacity to bear corresponding duties, which animals lack.

## Illustrative Example

The debate over raising animals in intensive farming (factory farming) embodies the disagreement in practice. Advocates of welfare are content to demand better conditions of rearing, whereas advocates of rights hold that the only ethical solution is to end the use of animals for food altogether.
