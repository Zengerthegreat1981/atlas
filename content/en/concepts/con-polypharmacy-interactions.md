# Polypharmacy & Drug Interactions

## Definition

Polypharmacy is a patient taking several medications at the same time. In psychiatry it often means combining several psychiatric drugs (such as two antipsychotics, or an antidepressant with a mood stabilizer and a hypnotic), or combining them with physical-health medications. Drug interactions take two forms: pharmacokinetic (one drug affects the absorption, metabolism, or excretion of another) and pharmacodynamic (the effects add up or oppose each other at the same receptor).

## The Arab Heritage on Compound Drugs

Medieval Arab medicine paid attention to compound drugs. Abu Yusuf al-Kindi (d. c. 873 CE), in his treatise on determining the degrees of compound drugs (*De Gradibus*), set out a mathematical gradation of a drug's strength after mixing, attempting to link the degree to the composition. Ibn Sina (d. 428 AH / 1037 CE), in the *Canon*, set out conditions for testing a drug: that it be free of impurities, that its effect be tested on a suitable body, and that the repetition of the result be observed. These rules are read today as a precursor to the experimental method in pharmacology, although they do not replace modern controlled trials (see the entry on Ibn Sina). Al-Razi (d. c. 313 AH / 925 CE) is reported to have advised beginning with diet, and then with simple drugs as far as possible before compound ones, which resembles the modern principle of prescribing "the least necessary."

## Numbers and Risks

Mojtabai and Olfson found in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 2010 that the share of visits to US office-based psychiatric practices that included two or more psychiatric medications rose from about 42.6% in 1996-1997 to 59.8% in 2005-2006. Part of this combination rests on clinical grounds (such as adding lamotrigine in bipolar depression), but much of it lacks sufficient evidence. Tiihonen and colleagues, in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 2012, followed a nationwide Finnish cohort of patients with schizophrenia. They found that antipsychotic polypharmacy was not associated with increased mortality, whereas long-term benzodiazepine use was associated with higher mortality; as an observational study it cannot establish causation. *The Maudsley Prescribing Guidelines* (Wiley, successive editions) document the evidence for combinations and their limits.

## Mechanisms of Interaction

The hepatic cytochrome P450 system controls the metabolism of many psychiatric drugs. Fluoxetine and paroxetine inhibit the enzyme CYP2D6, raising the levels of some tricyclic antidepressants and antipsychotics. Fluvoxamine inhibits CYP1A2, raising the levels of clozapine and caffeine. Tobacco smoking induces CYP1A2, so the levels of olanzapine and clozapine change when smoking stops. Serotonin syndrome, which Boyer and Shannon described in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2005, results from combining serotonergic drugs. Many drugs prolong the QT interval, so cardiac rhythm disturbance is a concern. Serotonergic antidepressants increase the risk of bleeding when combined with antiplatelet agents or NSAIDs. Lithium levels can rise with ACE inhibitors, thiazide diuretics, and anti-inflammatory drugs (see the entry on mood stabilizers and lithium).

## Vulnerable Groups

Older adults experience high rates of polypharmacy. The American Geriatrics Society issued the Beers Criteria in the *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society* in 2019 to identify potentially dangerous drugs, including benzodiazepines and anticholinergics. The problem is compounded by herbs and supplements that may interact with medications, such as St John's wort, which induces CYP3A4 and lowers the levels of several drugs. This matters in contexts where folk remedies are used widely.

## Limits

Not every multiple prescription is an error. Some conditions, such as treatment-resistant psychosis or bipolar disorder, require combinations. Clinical guidance generally emphasizes periodic review of the justification for each drug and clear responsibility for the prescription.
