# Imperialism

Imperialism is the extension of a state's power over other territories and peoples, exercised through conquest, political control, economic domination, settlement, or cultural influence. The term is used both descriptively, for the policies and systems by which empires are built and maintained, and critically, for the unequal relationships between metropolitan centres and subordinated peripheries. Anthropology has been centrally concerned with imperialism because the discipline itself grew up alongside European colonial expansion, and because ethnographic knowledge was often produced within, and put to the service of, imperial rule.

## Origins and Development

The modern concept of imperialism took shape in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the age of the "new imperialism" when European powers partitioned Africa and competed for territory in Asia and the Pacific. John A. Hobson, in *Imperialism: A Study* (1902), argued that imperial expansion was driven by domestic economic pressures: underconsumption at home pushed financiers and industrialists to seek profitable outlets abroad, and the costs were borne by ordinary taxpayers while the gains accrued to a narrow elite. Vladimir Lenin, writing *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917), recast this argument in Marxist terms, presenting imperialism as a structural phase of capitalism marked by the dominance of finance capital, the export of capital rather than goods, and rivalry among great powers. Both accounts treated empire as an economic system rather than a mere matter of national pride or strategic rivalry.

Historians and anthropologists later widened the picture. John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson argued in "The Imperialism of Free Trade" (1953) that Britain frequently preferred informal control — trade, investment, and influence over local rulers — to formal annexation, so that imperial power could be exercised without direct rule. The insight fits anthropological observations that domination operated through many channels: through missionaries, traders, administrators, and scientific expeditions, as well as through armies and flags. The distinction between formal and informal empire remains a basic tool for analysing how metropolitan power reaches into distant societies.

## Imperialism and Anthropology

The relationship between anthropology and imperialism has been one of the discipline's most searching debates. Some early ethnography was commissioned or funded by colonial governments, and knowledge about colonised peoples — their kinship, law, land tenure, and religion — was used to govern them more effectively. At the same time, anthropologists documented the destructive effects of colonial rule: land alienation, forced labour, the disruption of exchange systems, and the spread of disease. After the Second World War, as empires dissolved, anthropologists turned the concept of imperialism back on their own discipline, questioning the categories and authority through which other peoples had been described.

Edward Said's *Culture and Imperialism* (1993) extended this critique to literature and culture, showing how novels, travel writing, and scholarship helped to naturalise imperial rule by representing colonised peoples as backward or in need of civilising. Said's work made cultural representation itself an object of anthropological analysis, and it encouraged attention to resistance — to the ways colonised peoples contested, adapted, and reworked imperial power rather than simply enduring it. From this perspective, imperialism is not only a matter of armies and markets but also of knowledge, language, and imagination.

## Key Debates

One enduring debate concerns the relationship between imperialism and colonialism. Colonialism is often treated as the practice of acquiring territory and settling it, while imperialism names the broader system of domination of which colonialism is one form. Another debate concerns whether imperialism ended with decolonisation. Scholars of neo-imperialism argue that political independence did not end economic dependence: former colonies remained tied to metropolitan economies through trade, debt, and investment, and new forms of power — development aid, structural adjustment, and cultural influence — replaced direct rule. Others argue that the term has been stretched so far that it loses analytical precision, and that it should be reserved for specific historical systems of territorial empire.

A further debate concerns the agency of colonised peoples. Older accounts often portrayed them as passive victims of overwhelming power. More recent work stresses negotiation, alliance, and resistance, and examines how local elites, traders, and ordinary people shaped the terms on which imperial power operated. Imperial encounters were rarely one-directional: they transformed metropolitan societies as well, through migration, the circulation of goods and ideas, and debates about race, nation, and citizenship at home.

## Contemporary Relevance

Imperialism remains a live concept in anthropology and the social sciences. It is invoked to analyse the legacies of empire in postcolonial states, the geopolitics of resource extraction, military intervention, and the global inequalities that persist long after formal empires have gone. Anthropologists study how imperial histories shape present-day identities, land disputes, and memories of violence, and how museums, monuments, and archives carry the traces of empire. The concept also frames debates about globalisation: some scholars see contemporary globalisation as a new phase of imperialism, while others argue that it has displaced imperialism with a more diffuse system of power. Either way, the vocabulary first forged to explain the empires of the early twentieth century continues to organise how anthropologists think about power across borders.
