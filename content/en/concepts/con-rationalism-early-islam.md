# Rationalism and the Mu'tazila in Early Islam

The **Mu'tazila** were the early school of Islamic theology (*kalam*) that gave reasoned argument a systematic public place in doctrine. Classical reports locate the origin in Basra late in the first century AH and early in the second (the early eighth century CE), around Wasil ibn 'Ata' (d. 131 AH / 748 CE) and 'Amr ibn 'Ubayd (d. 144 AH / 761 CE). Heresiographers say that Wasil withdrew (*i'tazala*) from the circle of al-Hasan al-Basri over the status of the grave sinner, and that the school's name comes from that withdrawal. The anecdote is old. It is also a later etiology. What is secure is that a Basran current, soon joined by a Baghdad current, argued about God and human acts with tools other than transmitted reports alone. Earlier disputes, including those of the Qadariyya and of Jahm ibn Safwan, already raised questions the Mu'tazila inherited. They were not the first Muslims to argue.

## The five principles

Kalam grew partly from disputation with non-Muslims and partly from disagreements among Muslims about God, acts, and leadership. Mu'tazili teachers held that reason and revelation do not conflict, and that reason can establish some truths about God and about moral responsibility before one interprets the ambiguous texts. They were not neutral inquirers. The inferences started from theological commitments, above all the denial of anything that would compromise divine unity or justice.

By the time of Qadi 'Abd al-Jabbar (d. 415 AH / 1025 CE), the school summarized its teaching in five principles (*al-usul al-khamsa*):

- **Unity (*tawhid*)**: God is one. Attributes are not distinct eternal things alongside the essence. Bodily descriptions in scripture are to be interpreted accordingly.
- **Justice (*'adl*)**: God does not do injustice, and God does not create the evil acts that God then punishes. Human beings perform their own acts and are responsible for them. This rejected compulsion (*jabr*).
- **The promise and the threat (*al-wa'd wa-l-wa'id*)**: God carries out the reward and the punishment announced in revelation. Intercession does not cancel the threat against the grave sinner who dies unrepentant.
- **The intermediate position (*al-manzila bayn al-manzilatayn*)**: the grave sinner is neither a believer in the full sense nor an unbeliever.
- **Commanding right and forbidding wrong**.

A further doctrine, not one of the five headings but central in the public conflict, was that the Qur'an is created, not a second eternal reality beside God. The People of Hadith (*ahl al-hadith*) held that it is uncreated.

Abu al-Hudhayl al-'Allaf (d. c. 227 AH / 841 CE) gave Basran teaching much of its systematic shape and is traditionally credited with a formulated list of the five principles. A Baghdad branch is associated with Bishr ibn al-Mu'tamir (d. 210 AH / 825 CE). The branches differed on the imamate, on the scope of human power, and on other points.

## The mihna, and what survived it

The Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun made the created Qur'an a test for judges and scholars. The *mihna* began in 218 AH / 833 CE and continued under al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq. Ahmad ibn Hanbal was imprisoned and, in Hanbali accounts, flogged for refusing the doctrine. Al-Mutawakkil ended the test in 234 AH / 848–849 CE and restored the public standing of the hadith scholars. The Mu'tazila lost the state. They did not vanish. 'Abd al-Jabbar, chief judge in Rayy, wrote the large synthesis of the Basran Bahshami school. The book printed in 1965 as his *Sharh al-usul al-khamsa* is, as Daniel Gimaret showed, a commentary by his follower Mankdim Shashdiw (d. c. 425 AH / 1034 CE) on 'Abd al-Jabbar's own *Usul*. Al-Zamakhshari (d. 538 AH / 1144 CE) still wrote Mu'tazili exegesis. Zaydi scholars in the Caspian region and in Yemen copied and taught Mu'tazili books that most Sunni libraries did not keep.

The failure of the *mihna* discredited the Mu'tazila as an official creed and raised the prestige of Ahmad ibn Hanbal. It did not end rational theology among Sunnis. Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 324 AH / 935–936 CE), trained as a Mu'tazili, and later Ash'ari and Maturidi teachers continued kalam while rejecting Mu'tazili justice, the created Qur'an, and the intermediate position. They described their path as lying between Mu'tazili interpretation and the refusal of interpretation among hadith scholars. Imami theologians of the Buyid period, notably al-Shaykh al-Mufid (d. 413 AH / 1022 CE) and al-Sharif al-Murtada (d. 436 AH / 1044 CE), adapted Mu'tazili argument to a doctrine of the imamate. Zaydi theology stayed close to Mu'tazili teaching. Philosophy (*falsafa*) is a different discipline. It argued with the Mu'tazila. It did not simply continue them.

## Modern readings

Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd studied Mu'tazili interpretation, and in particular their theory of metaphor (*majaz*), in *al-Ittijah al-'aqli fi al-tafsir* (Beirut: Dar al-Tanwir, 1982). He treated that heritage as evidence that rational exegesis had classical precedents. Muhammad 'Abid al-Jabri, in *Takwin al-'aql al-'arabi* (Beirut, 1984), was harsher. He classed kalam with a polemical habit of mind rather than with demonstrative science. Both are modern arguments about the tradition. Neither is a report of what the Mu'tazila claimed for themselves.

Calling the school rationalist is accurate if it means that reasoned argument had a public role and was trusted to interpret ambiguous texts. It is misleading if it means that the conclusions were deduced from premises nobody disputed, or that their opponents offered no arguments. For about fifteen years, a theology proud of reason was also a test enforced by the caliph.
