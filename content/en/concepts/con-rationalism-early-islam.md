# Rationalism and the Mu'tazila in Early Islam

A rational theological current that emerged in the early second century AH (eighth century CE), represented primarily by the **Mu'tazila** school. It pushed reason and logical inference to a central place in establishing Islamic doctrine, using the tools of **kalam** (the science of rational, defensive disputation on the foundations of religion).

## The Rise of Kalam and the Mu'tazila

The emergence of kalam was tied to the need of Muslims to defend their beliefs against philosophical challenges from non-Muslims and against internal theological disputes. This gave rise to the art of rational disputation, which uses logic rather than the text alone. The **Mu'tazila**, who emerged in the early second century AH and became established in the second and third centuries, were the first to develop an organized rationalist theological method. Their adherents treated reason as the primary tool for interpreting religious texts, not merely as a subsidiary aid to them.

## The Five Principles

Mu'tazilite doctrine rested on five main principles: **divine unity** (*tawhid*), which denies divine attributes additional to the essence in order to avoid any suspicion of plurality or anthropomorphism; **divine justice** (*'adl*), the principle that all of God's acts are necessarily just, and that human beings are free in their acts and therefore fully morally responsible for them, in rejection of determinism (*jabr*); **the promise and the threat** (*al-wa'd wa-al-wa'id*), the necessary fulfillment of God's promise and threat on the Day of Resurrection, without intercession that would nullify justice; **the intermediate position between two positions** (*al-manzila bayn al-manzilatayn*), that the grave sinner is neither a complete believer nor an unbeliever, but occupies an intermediate status; and **enjoining good and forbidding evil**. Among their most prominent disputed doctrines was the claim that **the Quran is created**, that is, that it is created and not eternal and uncreated like God's essence, in contrast to the position of the People of Hadith, who held that it is eternal.

## The Inquisition and Decline

The Mu'tazila reached the height of their political influence under the Abbasid caliph **al-Ma'mun** (and after him al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq), who officially adopted their doctrine and, in the *mihna* (c. 833–848 CE), imposed on scholars a test of the createdness of the Quran. Those who refused, the most famous of whom was the imam Ahmad ibn Hanbal, were imprisoned. The caliph **al-Mutawakkil** abolished this policy in 848 CE and restored the standing of the People of Hadith, and the political influence of the Mu'tazila then declined gradually. Their intellectual influence continued in limited scholarly circles for centuries afterward, as in the case of al-Zamakhshari and the later Mu'tazila in Yemen.

## Impact and Intellectual Succession

Historians of Islamic thought argue that the failure of the Mu'tazila to establish their doctrine as a permanent official creed of the state weakened the position of rational kalam in the later Sunni tradition in general. It also opened the way for the rise of the **Ash'arite** school, which presented itself as a middle path between Mu'tazilite rationalism and the literalism of the People of Hadith. Nevertheless, the Mu'tazilite legacy remained strongly present in philosophy and in Zaydi and Imami Shi'i thought. Some contemporary Arab thinkers, such as Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd, who studied Mu'tazili Qur'anic interpretation in *al-Ittijah al-'Aqli fi al-Tafsir* (1982), have treated the Mu'tazilite heritage as a historical precedent for Islamic rationalism, whereas others, such as Muhammad Abid al-Jabiri, were more critical of kalam as an ideologically driven discipline.

## Caveats

Debate continues about the extent to which the actual "rationalism" of the Mu'tazila was consistent. Their theologians ultimately relied on prior doctrinal premises (such as the denial of anthropomorphism) before beginning rational inference, which leads some contemporary scholars to treat the description of them as "pure rationalism" with reservations.
