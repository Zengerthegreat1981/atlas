# Interdependence

## Meaning and Definition

Interdependence is an ethical concept describing the state of existential interconnection among beings, such that no individual, community or species is entirely independent of others in its existence or flourishing. Its good and ill are shaped within a network of reciprocal relationships that impose ethical obligations extending beyond the boundaries of the individual. It thus stands in opposition to the premise of the "atomistic self" (Atomistic Self), which builds ethics on the independent individual, the bearer of separate rights and interests.

## Roots in Islamic Thought

The idea of interdependence appears in the Islamic heritage in terms of social solidarity and the unity of the body, through a well-known prophetic image: "The believers, in their mutual affection, compassion and kindness, are like one body. When one of its limbs complains, the rest of the body responds with wakefulness and fever." Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah*, built his entire social theory on the principle that "human social organization is necessary," because a single individual is incapable of obtaining their sustenance alone and requires the cooperation of their fellow humans. Human civilization as a whole is therefore a network of functional exchange among crafts and trades. Jurists also drew on this meaning in the theory of "collective obligation" (*fard al-kifaya*), which holds that some duties cannot be correctly understood except as collective responsibilities distributed across the community, not as isolated individual obligations.

## In Western Philosophy

Contemporary environmental ethics has extended the concept to cover the relationship between humans and nature. The most prominent formulation is that of Aldo Leopold in *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), where he called for a "Land Ethic" (Land Ethic) based on the idea that the human being is a member of a broader "biotic community" that includes soil, water, plants and animals. On this view, any act is ethically right if it preserves the integrity, stability and beauty of this community. Care ethicists (Care Ethics), foremost among them Nel Noddings, also built their ethics on the premise that the human being is necessarily a relational creature, whose moral identity is formed through relationships of interdependence from childhood, rather than through a social contract among fully independent individuals. The concept is also invoked in critiques of individualistic liberalism from the perspective of communitarians (Communitarians), such as Alasdair MacIntyre, who argued in *Dependent Rational Animals* (1999) that human beings are vulnerable and dependent animals whose flourishing requires networks of giving and receiving, and that virtues themselves can only be acquired within a community that teaches the individual, corrects them, and in turn needs them when they grow old.

## Applications and Uses

Interdependence features today in debates on climate justice, where the responsibility of industrialized states toward the poorer states is raised on the basis that the atmosphere is a single shared system. It also appears in the ethics of collective healthcare (such as herd immunity) and in the philosophy of disability, which shows that complete self-sufficiency is an illusion, and that every person passes through stages of complete dependence on others (childhood, illness and old age). A concrete example is the decision of states to share vaccines during pandemics, which is not an act of charity but a duty arising from the fact that the health of individuals is functionally interconnected across borders.
