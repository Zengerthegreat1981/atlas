# Morphological Typology

A classification of languages by how words are built from morphemes, rather than by historical kinship. Friedrich Schlegel (1808) contrasted inflectional languages with the rest; August Wilhelm Schlegel (1818) distinguished isolating, agglutinative, and inflectional types; Wilhelm von Humboldt added an incorporating type, continued in later work as polysynthesis. August Schleicher reused the types as stages on an evolutionary scale. That ranking is no longer accepted. The labels survive as descriptive poles.

## Isolating (analytic) languages

The words of these languages (such as Mandarin Chinese and Vietnamese) tend to consist of a single indivisible morpheme, and grammatical relations (such as tense and plurality) are expressed through separate independent words and strict grammatical word order, not through affixes attached to the word itself.

## Agglutinative languages

The words of these languages (such as Turkish, Japanese and Swahili) tend to be built from a stem to which a chain of affixes is attached. Each affix carries one clearly defined grammatical meaning (such as a plural suffix, another for case, and a third for tense), so that a word can usually be easily broken down into its separate morphological units.

## Fusional (inflectional) languages

The words of these languages (such as Latin, Russian and, partly, Arabic) merge several grammatical meanings into a single morpheme that cannot easily be separated (such as a single inflectional ending that carries gender, number and case together). It is therefore difficult to isolate each grammatical meaning from the others within the single form.

## Polysynthetic languages

In these languages a single word can carry much of what other languages express as a clause, by combining a verb root with subject and object markers and other grammatical material. Textbook cases include Inuktitut and Mohawk, and, in the Caucasus, Northwest Caucasian languages such as Abkhaz. "Polysynthetic" is not a single structural type: how much material is inside the verb differs from language to language.

## A descriptive, non-exclusive value

These four categories are used as poles on a descriptive continuum, not as separate exclusive boxes. Most actual languages combine features from more than one category in different areas of their morphological system, and historical transitions between these types (through grammaticalization and phonetic erosion) are well documented.

## Greenberg's synthesis index

Seeking to move beyond purely qualitative descriptive classification, Joseph Greenberg proposed in 1960 quantitative indices for measuring the morphological degree of a language. The most prominent is the "synthesis index": the average number of morphemes per word across a specific text sample. The higher this ratio, the closer the language is to the synthetic pole (agglutinative, fusional and polysynthetic), and the lower it is, the closer it is to the analytic/isolating pole. This quantitative index enabled more precise comparisons between languages, instead of merely placing them in rigid separate categories.

## Arabic between fusion and agglutination

Standard Arabic combines features of more than one morphological type at once. Its inflectional affixes are largely fusional, since a single ending or prefix often encodes person, gender and number together (for example the perfect suffix *-tu* "I" or the imperfect prefix *ya-* "he"), while its attached pronominal and conjunction clitics (such as *wa-* "and" and *-hu* "him/his") behave in a more agglutinative way. In addition, its root-and-pattern (non-concatenative or "introflexive") morphology does not easily fall within any of the four classical types: words are derived by combining an abstract consonantal root (typically of three consonants) with a vocalic template that specifies the general morphological and semantic shape of the word.
