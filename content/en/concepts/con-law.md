# Law

Law is the system of rules, institutions, and practices by which a society regulates conduct, resolves disputes, and exercises authority. In modern states, law takes the form of statutes, courts, and police; but law in the anthropological sense is far broader. Every society has recognised ways of deciding what is permitted and what is forbidden, of judging wrongdoing, and of enforcing decisions — even where there are no written codes, professional judges, or prisons. The anthropology of law asks how these systems work, whose interests they serve, and how they change when different legal traditions meet.

## Definition

Legal theorists have long debated what counts as law. One classic view, associated with the English jurist John Austin, defined law as the command of a sovereign backed by sanctions. H. L. A. Hart, writing in 1961, offered a subtler account: law is a union of primary rules, which tell people what to do, and secondary rules, which say how rules are made, changed, and applied. Anthropologists added a comparative challenge: if law requires a state, then societies without states have no law. Bronisław Malinowski argued in 1926 that even in stateless societies, such as the Trobriand Islanders he studied, there are binding obligations enforced by reciprocity and public opinion, and that these constitute a genuine legal order. Later scholars spoke of "legal pluralism" — the coexistence of multiple legal systems within one society, such as state law, religious law, and customary law operating side by side. Law, in this view, is not a single thing but a field of competing norms and authorities.

## Origins and Development

The earliest legal systems grew out of custom: unwritten rules transmitted through practice and enforced by kin groups, elders, and councils. The invention of writing allowed law to be codified, most famously in the Code of Hammurabi in ancient Babylon, around 1750 BCE, and later in Roman law, which became the foundation of legal systems across Europe and its colonies. Henry Maine argued in 1861 that legal history moved from status to contract, as societies shifted from rights based on birth and kinship to rights based on individual agreement. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw the spread of European legal systems through colonialism, often imposed over Indigenous and customary law. Meanwhile, anthropologists documented the legal life of societies without states: E. Adamson Hoebel's study of "primitive law" in 1954 showed that even the simplest societies have recognised procedures for deciding disputes and sanctioning offences. In the late twentieth century, scholars such as Sally Falk Moore showed that law is not a fixed code but a process — a continuous negotiation in which rules are made, bent, and remade in everyday life.

## Key Debates

One central debate is whether law must be tied to the state. Legal centralists hold that only state law is truly law; pluralists argue that this view is a colonial legacy that dismisses the legal orders of most of the world's peoples. A second debate concerns the relationship between law and morality: must law reflect moral principles, or is it simply whatever the authorities declare? A third debate concerns the functions of law: does law primarily maintain social order, or does it serve the interests of the powerful? Marxist scholars argue that law legitimises inequality, while functionalists emphasise its role in resolving conflict. A fourth debate concerns legal change: how do societies adapt their laws to new circumstances, and what happens when state law conflicts with custom, as in disputes over land, marriage, or inheritance? Finally, the rise of international law, human rights, and transnational regulation has raised the question of whether a genuinely global legal order is emerging — and who it serves.

## Contemporary Relevance

Law is more prominent in daily life than ever before. Constitutions, courts, and human rights instruments shape politics in nearly every country, while international tribunals prosecute war crimes and regulate trade. At the same time, many of the world's people still resolve many of their disputes through customary or religious forums rather than state courts. Legal anthropology documents this gap between "law on the books" and "law in action," showing how people navigate, resist, and remake legal systems. Current issues include the recognition of Indigenous law, the reform of criminal justice, the regulation of digital platforms, and the legal response to climate change. Understanding law as a social practice, not merely a set of rules, is essential to understanding how power works in the modern world.
