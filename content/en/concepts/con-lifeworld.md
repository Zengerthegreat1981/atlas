# Lifeworld (Lebenswelt): The Lived World Before the Objective

The lifeworld is the world as it is lived before and beneath theoretical or scientific reflection: the everyday world of shared experience, in which we are practically immersed and whose familiarity is taken for granted. The term *Lebenswelt* was already in use in German thought before Husserl, but he gave it central importance in his late work *Die Krisis der europäischen Wissenschaften und die transzendentale Phänomenologie* (*The Crisis of the European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology*, parts published in 1936, the full text posthumously in 1954). There he argues that modern science, by mathematizing nature, has lost sight of the lifeworld that is its own meaning-foundation.

## Philosophical Meaning

For Husserl the lifeworld is the pre-given ground from which every science and theory arises: the world of perceived things, practical purposes and shared meanings, in which things appear as useful, threatening or beautiful before they are treated as measurable physical objects. The crisis he diagnoses is a "forgetting" of this origin, so that the idealized constructs of science are taken for reality itself. Access to the lifeworld as a theme of philosophical inquiry requires a suspension (*epoché*) of the scientific and theoretical presuppositions. For Husserl, the lifeworld is thereby also a topic of transcendental phenomenology.

Heidegger's analysis of "being-in-the-world" (*In-der-Welt-sein*) in *Being and Time* (1927) is earlier than the *Crisis* and developed independently. It shares the emphasis on practical, pre-theoretical involvement in a world but rejects Husserl's transcendental subject and his method of reduction, so the two notions should not be equated. Maurice Merleau-Ponty, in *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945), draws on both and treats the lived body as the medium through which the world is experienced. Alfred Schutz transferred the concept into sociology, analysing the everyday life-world (*Alltagswelt*) as constituted in intersubjective interaction (see Schutz and Luckmann, *The Structures of the Life-World*, 1973-83). Jürgen Habermas, in *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981), uses "lifeworld" for the shared background of communicative action, set against the "systems" of economy and administration.

## Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

The concept has been used in phenomenological psychiatry and psychology to describe how experience is organized before it is objectified. Phenomenological psychiatrists such as Eugène Minkowski, Ludwig Binswanger, Wolfgang Blankenburg and, more recently, Thomas Fuchs describe changes in the experience of time, space, the body and other people in conditions such as depression and schizophrenia as alterations of the lifeworld, and not merely as lost functions. In the philosophy of medicine, Havi Carel (*Illness*, 2008) analyses how serious illness transforms the lifeworld of the patient. Roger Barker's ecological psychology (behavior settings) also studies behaviour in natural settings, but it is an empirical research programme that does not derive from Husserl. The concept is descriptive and does not by itself yield diagnostic criteria or treatment guidance.

## What Distinguishes It from Neighboring Concepts

The lifeworld differs from the **environment** as studied in behavioral psychology, a set of objective, measurable stimuli, whereas the lifeworld is a meaningful fabric of values, purposes and shared understandings. It differs from the **Umwelt** of Jakob von Uexküll's biology, the species-specific perceptual world of an organism, because the human lifeworld is historical, social and cultural.
