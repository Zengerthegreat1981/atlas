# Act vs Rule Utilitarianism

## Meaning and Definition

Utilitarianism (the ethics based on maximizing utility) divides into two distinct formulations regarding the object of ethical assessment. Act utilitarianism (Act Utilitarianism) judges each individual act according to the outcomes it produces in that particular situation, such that an act is right if its net balance of happiness is greater than that of any other alternative available at that moment. Rule utilitarianism (Rule Utilitarianism) judges general rules according to the outcomes that their widespread observance produces. An act is right if it conforms to a rule whose general adherence by people leads to the best possible results, even if the individual act in a particular case does not directly maximize utility.

## Roots in Islamic Ethical Thought

This dichotomy was not formulated in its modern terms in the Islamic heritage. However, the distinction between judging a particular act and judging a universal rule can be compared with themes in the principles of jurisprudence, particularly in al-Shatibi's theory of the objectives of Islamic law in *al-Muwafaqat*. Al-Shatibi held that the rulings of Islamic law were established to realize universal interests (the universals of religion, life, intellect, lineage and property), and that a general legal rule may be applied even in cases where it does not by itself realize the greatest particular benefit, because the stability and consistent application of the rule itself serves the universal interest over the long term. This has been compared, loosely, to the logic of rule utilitarianism. By contrast, some jurists of necessity (based on the legal maxim "necessities permit what is forbidden") adopted a logic closer to act utilitarianism, in which an act is evaluated according to what its specific necessity requires rather than according to the general rule. Al-Ghazali, in *al-Mustasfa*, also discussed the question of unrestricted public interest (*al-maslaha al-mursala*), distinguishing between a particular, immediate interest and a universal interest that the law measures by the welfare of the whole community.

## Historical Context in Western Philosophy

Modern utilitarianism arose at the hands of Jeremy Bentham in his *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), in which he formulated the principle of utility as a standard for calculating the pleasure and pain of each act separately, which is regarded as the first formulation of act utilitarianism. John Stuart Mill developed this principle in his book *Utilitarianism* (1861). However, some interpreters, notably J. O. Urmson in "The Interpretation of the Moral Philosophy of J. S. Mill" (1953), found in Mill's treatment of secondary principles and moral rules a foreshadowing of rule utilitarianism. Explicit formulations were developed in the mid-twentieth century, particularly by Richard Brandt (for example in "Toward a Credible Form of Utilitarianism," 1963) and later John Harsanyi ("Rule Utilitarianism and Decision Theory," 1977). They proposed that moral judgment should be directed at general rules rather than individual acts, in order to avoid paradoxes that arise from calculating utility case by case.

## Main Objections

A well-known objection to act utilitarianism is that it may justify acts that are manifestly unjust if, in a rare case, they produce a greater net benefit. For example, the killing of an innocent person could be justified to appease an angry mob if doing so prevents wider rioting. Rule utilitarianism attempts to avoid this objection by adhering to the rule "do not kill the innocent," because its general observance is more beneficial to society. However, rule utilitarianism faces in turn the "Collapse Objection" (Collapse Objection), formulated by David Lyons in *Forms and Limits of Utilitarianism* (1965): if the rule is permitted to include every exception that maximizes utility, it ends up coinciding with the verdicts of act utilitarianism itself, and rule utilitarianism thereby loses its theoretical independence.
