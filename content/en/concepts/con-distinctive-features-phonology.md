# Distinctive Features

## Definition

A central concept in functional phonology, holding that the phoneme is not an indivisible sound unit but a bundle of smaller sound properties, called distinctive features, which intersect to form the system of oppositions that distinguishes one meaning from another in a language. These features are usually formulated in binary form (+/−) or with multiple values, and they provide a systematic account of how phonemes are related to one another and of how phonological rules operate.

## Historical origins

The origin of the idea goes back to Nikolai Trubetzkoy and the Prague Linguistic Circle in the 1930s, when he proposed analyzing phonemes into distinctive oppositions (such as voiced versus voiceless). Roman Jakobson developed this idea with Morris Halle and Gunnar Fant in the mid-20th century, within a system of twelve universal binary sound features, presented in the report *Preliminaries to Speech Analysis* (1952). Noam Chomsky and Morris Halle transformed this framework in their book *The Sound Pattern of English* (1968) toward articulatory features more closely tied to the organs of speech. This became the basis adopted by most subsequent frameworks, including "Feature Geometry," which organized features into hierarchical structures instead of flat lists.

## Main types of features

- **Major class features**: such as [consonantal] and [sonorant], the latter distinguishing sounds produced with spontaneous voicing (vowels, nasals and liquids) from obstruents such as plosives and fricatives.
- **Place of articulation features**: such as [labial], [coronal/dental], [dorsal/velar], which describe the location of the obstruction in the vocal tract.
- **Manner of articulation features**: such as [nasal], [continuant], which distinguishes sounds with a continuous airflow (fricatives and vowels) from plosives, and [strident], for high-energy fricatives.
- **Laryngeal features**: such as [voice], which is marked by the vibration of the vocal folds, and [spread glottis], associated with breathiness (aspiration).

## Function in phonological theory

Distinctive features allow the definition of "natural classes," meaning groups of sounds that behave uniformly in the rules of a language (for example, all [+nasal] sounds being affected by a single rule). They also allow phonological rules and constraints (as in Optimality Theory) to be stated in more precise terms than the term "phoneme" alone. They are further used to explain phonological assimilation and to analyze the acquisition of the sound system in children.

## Representing a phoneme as a feature bundle

Each phoneme is represented as a bundle of values of these features taken together. The English sound /p/, for example, is described as [−voice, +labial, −continuant, +consonantal], while /b/ differs from it by only one value ([+voice]). This explains why the difference between them is regarded as a "minimal" contrast sufficient on its own to distinguish two different meanings (a minimal pair). This representation also makes it possible to describe contextual sound changes (such as the voiced sound becoming voiceless at the end of a syllable) as a change in the value of a single feature rather than a complete replacement of one sound by another.

## From Jakobson to Optimality Theory

After the formulation of the articulatory features by Chomsky and Halle, later frameworks such as Optimality Theory, developed by Prince and Smolensky (1993), used distinctive features within constraints ranked by priority rather than strict ordered rules. This made it possible to explain cross-linguistic variation as a difference in the ranking of the same constraints, rather than in separate rules.
