# Qiyas (Analogical Reasoning in Islamic Law)

**Qiyas** is, according to the majority of usuli scholars, the attachment of a case for which there is no text to a case for which a text states the ruling, on the basis that both share a single **'illa** (effective cause). It is counted as the fourth source of sharia proof according to the majority of the schools (after the Book, the Sunna and consensus), and it is the one that has provoked the most historical usuli debate.

## Its Four Pillars

The usuliyyun organized the pillars of qiyas into four (a systematization found, for example, in al-Ghazali's *al-Mustasfa*):

1. **The original case** (*asl*, the case compared against): the case in which the text states the ruling.
2. **The derived case** (*far'*, the case compared): the new case for which there is no text.
3. **The ruling** (*hukm*): the ruling established for the original case and sought to be established for the derived case.
4. **The 'illa** (effective cause): the shared descriptive attribute that unites the original and the derived case, and on which the ruling in the original case was based.

## The Debate over Its Authority

The **Zahiris** (the school of Dawud al-Zahiri, with Ibn Hazm as its major theorist) disagreed with the majority of usuli scholars. They rejected qiyas entirely as a legitimate sharia proof, holding fast to the apparent meaning of texts without seeking their causes or extending them to what has no text. This disagreement, over whether qiyas is authoritative or not, roughly (though not exactly) parallels the debate in modern legal theory about whether **gaps in the law may be filled by analogical reasoning** (reasoning by analogy) in systems that rely on judicial precedent or written texts.

## Conditions of the 'Illa and Types of Analogy

The usuliyyun stipulated that the 'illa be a descriptive attribute that is determinate, apparent, consistently present, and suitable for the ruling to be based upon it. They developed methods of establishing it, such as explicit text, allusion (*iyma'*), and examination and division (*sabr wa taqsim*). They divided analogy according to the strength of the 'illa into three types: **qiyas al-awla** (analogy from the stronger, where the 'illa is stronger in the derived case than in the original, such as the prohibition of striking one's parents being more forbidden than the prohibition of saying "uff" to them), **qiyas al-musawat** (analogy of equivalence, where the 'illa is equal in the derived and original cases, such as burning the property of an orphan by analogy with consuming it), and **qiyas al-adna** (analogy from the weaker, where the 'illa is weaker or less evident in the derived case than in the original). Usuliyyun also discussed, as a distinct category, **qiyas al-shabah** (analogy by resemblance, where the derived case is attached to one of two conflicting originals according to which it more closely resembles).

## Al-Shafi'i and the Place of Qiyas in al-Risala

**Al-Shafi'i** is credited with an early and influential systematic defence of qiyas as a source of proof in his *al-Risala*, in which he distinguished between explicit texts and independent reasoning by analogy in the absence of a text. In this way he laid the foundation on which later usuliyyun, chief among them al-Ghazali, built a complete methodological theory for regulating ijtihad and restricting its arbitrary expansion.

## Parallel with Analogical Reasoning in Western Law

Usuli qiyas parallels the phenomenon of **reasoning by analogy** in Western legal systems, especially the common law, where judges apply the principle of a precedent to new facts that are similar, without a text or direct precedent on the matter, relying on the existence of a shared "ratio" between the two cases. The essential difference, however, is that the source of obligation in jurisprudential qiyas is an authentic sharia text, whereas in common law its source is the ruling of a previous judge.
