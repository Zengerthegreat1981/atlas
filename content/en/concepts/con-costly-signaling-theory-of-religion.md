# Costly Signaling Theory of Religion

An explanatory framework from evolutionary anthropology holding that costly religious practices (long fasting, dietary taboos, painful rituals, large donations of resources) are not irrational burdens but **honest social signals** that are difficult to fake about the individual's genuine commitment to their group. The framework was first applied to religion by William Irons (`thk-william-irons`) and developed empirically by Richard Sosis (`thk-richard-sosis`), drawing on the general evolutionary theory of signaling; Scott Atran and Joseph Henrich later integrated it with the cognitive science of religion.

## The Logic: Why the Cost Itself Is the Message

The logic is borrowed from signaling theory in evolutionary biology (such as the peacock's costly-to-maintain tail as an honest signal of genetic fitness, following Zahavi's handicap principle). A signal that costs nothing is easy to fake (any hypocrite can claim faith for free), whereas a costly signal can only be borne by someone whose commitment is genuine, and so it becomes honest by economic necessity rather than by good intention alone. In this way religious groups solve the "free-rider problem": those who display their costly commitment are trusted more for cooperation than those who merely profess cheap verbal membership.

## Relation to the Cognitive Science of Religion

This framework complements the individual-cognitive explanations of Boyer and Barrett (why individuals find it easy to believe in invisible agents) with an explanation at the group level. Even if religious belief is a cognitive by-product, as Boyer argues, the costly ritual practices built around it are subject to cultural selection that favours groups that are more cohesive and have greater internal trust over groups that are less committed.

## Experimental Testing: Sosis and the Kibbutzim

Richard Sosis (`thk-richard-sosis`) tested the framework in two well-known studies. With Bradley Ruffle (`stu-kibbutz-religious-secular-cooperation-sosis-2003`, 2003) he used a common-pool resource game with members of Israeli religious and secular kibbutzim and found higher cooperation among members of religious kibbutzim, especially men who attended communal prayer frequently. With Eric Bressler (2003) he compared nineteenth-century American communes and found that religious communes survived longer than secular ones, and that the number of costly requirements imposed on members correlated with longevity among the religious communes. These results are widely read as support for the theory, though they are correlational.

## Criticism

It is noted that the theory explains the **persistence** and cultural-evolutionary success of costly practices better than it explains their original **emergence**. Moreover, like other functional explanations, it may overlook the lived meaning for the believer himself, since religious experience is reduced to a strategic calculation of social trust, even though the believer does not experience it that way.
