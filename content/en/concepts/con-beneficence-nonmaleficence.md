# Beneficence & Non-Maleficence

## Meaning and Definition

Beneficence and non-maleficence are two mutually connected principles in applied ethics, especially medical ethics. The first requires actually doing good for others and promoting their interests. The second requires refraining from inflicting harm on them. The second is often summed up in the Latin maxim *Primum non nocere* ("first, do no harm"), a later formula associated with the Hippocratic tradition rather than a phrase of the Oath itself. Although the two principles appear to be two sides of the same coin, many ethicists regard the duty of non-maleficence as more binding than the duty of beneficence. Refraining from harm is a general negative duty owed to all people, whereas positive beneficence may be limited by capacity and context.

## Roots in Islamic Moral Thought

In the Islamic juristic tradition, these two principles can be linked to legal maxims such as "harm must be removed" (one of the five major maxims) and "averting corruptions takes precedence over securing benefits." Ibn Nujaym (d. 1563), in *al-Ashbah wa-l-Naza'ir*, treats the removal of harm as a general principle on which many particular rulings are built, which corresponds closely to the modern principle of non-maleficence. The principle of beneficence is grounded in the Qur'anic verse "Indeed, Allah commands justice and beneficence." Al-Raghib al-Isfahani, in *al-Dhari'a ila Makarim al-Shari'a*, interprets beneficence as going beyond justice to doing good by grace rather than by entitlement, which makes it a higher degree than merely not wronging others.

## The Western Historical Context

An early grounding of these two principles is found in the Hippocratic tradition, in which the physician swears to benefit the sick and to keep them from harm and injustice. In the twentieth century, the two principles became two of the four pillars formulated by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress in their reference work *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (1979), alongside respect for autonomy and justice. These four pillars became the prevailing framework for teaching medical ethics around the world.

## Applications and Uses

The tension between the two principles is evident in chemotherapy decisions. Treatment may cause certain harm (nausea, hair loss, weakened immunity) in pursuit of a possible benefit (prolonged life or cure). This requires the physician to balance the two carefully, applying the principle of proportionality between the expected harm and the sought benefit. In jurisprudence this is known as the maxim "the more severe harm is removed by the lighter harm."
