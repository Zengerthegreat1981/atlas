# Judicial Review

The power of the courts to examine the legislation of the legislative branch or the decisions of the executive branch, and to strike down whatever conflicts with the constitution, which is regarded as the supreme law standing above any ordinary legislation.

## Foundation: Marbury v. Madison

Although the idea had earlier antecedents, including Alexander Hamilton's argument in *Federalist* No. 78 (1788), the classic foundation of judicial review in the United States is *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), in which the Supreme Court held section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 unconstitutional. Chief Justice **John Marshall** wrote the opinion of the Court. It held that the constitution is the supreme law, binding on the courts when they interpret and apply it, that any legislation conflicting with it is void, and that the courts, not Congress, are the body competent to resolve this conflict. In Marshall's words, it is the province of the judicial department "to say what the law is."

## Theoretical Justifications

Marshall and the supporters of judicial review relied on the idea that the will of the people, as expressed in the constitutional document, is superior to the will of their elected representatives in ordinary legislation. They also held that the constitution, as "law" rather than a mere declaration of political principles, requires a judicial mechanism for its enforcement, just like any other law adjudicated by the courts.

## Global Spread and Multiple Models

Judicial review developed worldwide in two main models. The **decentralized model** (such as the American one) gives every ordinary court the power of constitutional review. The **European centralized model** (first established in Austria's constitution of 1920, whose design Hans Kelsen influenced) entrusts constitutional review to a specialized constitutional court. Many democratic states adopted some form of judicial review after the Second World War (for example Italy and Germany, whose Federal Constitutional Court began work in 1951), often as a safeguard against a recurrence of authoritarian rule through legislative majorities. Some Commonwealth systems, such as the United Kingdom under the Human Rights Act 1998, use weaker forms of review in which courts may declare incompatibility without invalidating legislation.

## Limitation

- The debate over the **democratic legitimacy of judicial review** (the counter-majoritarian difficulty) continues: how can unelected judges be justified in striking down the decisions of elected representatives? This problem was articulated by Alexander Bickel in *The Least Dangerous Branch* (1962).
- The degree of actual judicial review varies widely among legal systems, from strict and comprehensive review to formal review of limited practical effect.

## Beyond the United States and Europe

Judicial review takes varied forms elsewhere. India's Constitution provides for review expressly, and in *Kesavananda Bharati* (1973) the Supreme Court held that Parliament's amending power cannot destroy the constitution's basic structure. Japan's 1947 Constitution gives the Supreme Court power to determine constitutionality, but the Court has invalidated legislation sparingly. Canada expanded review with the Charter of Rights and Freedoms in 1982, while allowing legislatures to override certain rights for renewable periods.

Several newer democracies chose specialized or strengthened courts. South Korea's Constitutional Court was established in 1988. South Africa's Constitutional Court, created for the post-apartheid order, struck down the death penalty in 1995. Colombia's Constitutional Court, created under the 1991 Constitution, is known for broad access through public actions and the *tutela* complaint. In the Arab world, Egypt's Supreme Constitutional Court, provided for in the 1971 Constitution and organized by a law of 1979, has exercised centralized review. In Latin America more broadly, mixed arrangements combine diffuse and concentrated review. Formal powers do not by themselves show independence, which also depends on appointments, jurisdiction and political conditions.
