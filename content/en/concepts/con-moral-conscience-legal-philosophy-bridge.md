# Religious Conscience and Legal Philosophy

Religious conscience intersects with legal philosophy at a specific question: what does a person do when the law commands what his conscience forbids him, and does the law have the right to hold someone accountable for what does not appear, namely intention? Classical Islamic jurisprudence addressed this through a precise distinction between the judicial ruling of this world (*qada'*) and the religious judgment of the afterlife (*diyana*): a contract may be valid in the judicial sense yet its party may be sinful before God.

## Religious judgment and judicial judgment in Islamic jurisprudence

Classical jurisprudence did not use the modern term "conscience" (*damir*), but it recognized what performs its function: "the reproaching self" (*al-nafs al-lawwama*) in the Qur'an (Sura al-Qiyama), the principle "ask your heart" (*istafti qalbak*) in the hadith narrated by Ahmad in the *Musnad*, and the hadith "righteousness is that with which the soul is at peace." Jurists drew a line between the judge's ruling according to what is apparent, that is, according to evidence (*qada'*), and the ruling of religion, that is, according to what lies between the servant and his Lord (*diyana*). Baber Johansen's *Contingency in a Sacred Law* (1999) discusses the relation between the legal and the ethical norms of Muslim fiqh, including the limits of what a court can judge. Al-Ghazali, in *Revival of the Religious Sciences* (*Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*), stressed the role of the inner state of the heart in the moral evaluation of acts; Frank Griffel's *Al-Ghazali's Philosophical Theology* (2009) is the standard study of his wider thought.

## Conscience and law in Western thought

Thomas Aquinas treated conscience as the judgment of practical reason on an act, and distinguished *synderesis*, the innate inclination toward the good. With the Protestant Reformation, the question of conscience became a question of freedom: when Luther, in 1521, defended himself before the Diet of Worms, he is reported to have said that his conscience was bound by the word of God and that he could not recant. Henry David Thoreau, in "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849, later titled "Civil Disobedience"), made individual conscience an authority that may override the law in matters of justice. John Rawls, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971, sections 55-59), treated "conscientious refusal" and civil disobedience as legitimate positions in a nearly just state.

## Contemporary debate

A famous debate took place between H. L. A. Hart and Lon Fuller in the *Harvard Law Review* (1958) on the separation of law and morality: Hart said that an unjust law remains law, while Fuller held that law has an inner morality. After the Second World War, Gustav Radbruch, in his article "Gesetzliches Unrecht und übergesetzliches Recht" (1946), put forward what is now called the Radbruch formula: statutory law that is intolerably unjust yields to justice. His position influenced postwar German courts. The Nuremberg Charter (1945) separately rejected obedience to superior orders as a defence, though it allowed it to be considered in mitigation.

In practice, conscience entered the law through "conscientious objection" to military service. Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (adopted 1966) protects freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, and in *Bayatyan v. Armenia* (Grand Chamber, 2011) the European Court of Human Rights held that Article 9 of the European Convention protects conscientious objection to military service. In *United States v. Seeger* (1965), the US Supreme Court read the statutory exemption for conscientious objectors to cover a sincere and meaningful belief that occupies in the life of its holder a place parallel to that of God in the life of a traditional believer.

## Limits of the connection

Religious conscience has a subjective character that the court can verify only through indications, so the law falls between two dangers: accepting every claim, or examining the inner self. The classical principle of judging by what is apparent protected individuals from inquisition into intentions, though it also left a gap between legal and religious accountability. Finally, conscience may be used to justify what harms others, which is why freedom of belief is restricted when it conflicts with the rights of others.
