# Cultural Relativism

Cultural relativism is the principle that beliefs and practices should be understood in the context of the culture in which they occur, rather than judged in advance by the standards of the observer's own society. In sociology and anthropology it serves mainly as a corrective to ethnocentrism, the term popularised by William Graham Sumner in *Folkways* (1906) for the habit of treating one's own group as the measure of all others.

## Origins

The principle is associated above all with Franz Boas and his students in American anthropology. Boas rejected schemes that ranked societies along a single evolutionary ladder and argued that cultural differences must be explained historically. Ruth Benedict's *Patterns of Culture* (1934) presented cultures as distinct configurations of values, and Melville Herskovits gave relativism a systematic statement in *Man and His Works* (1948). In 1947 the American Anthropological Association submitted a Statement on Human Rights, drafted largely by Herskovits, which cautioned against a universal declaration framed only in terms of Western values.

## Forms of Relativism

It is useful to separate several claims. Methodological relativism asks the researcher to suspend evaluation long enough to learn what a practice means to those who take part in it. Cognitive relativism holds that standards of truth or rationality vary between cultures. Moral relativism holds that there is no culture-independent basis for judging right and wrong. Accepting the first does not commit a researcher to the other two.

## Debates

Critics argue that a strong moral relativism would rule out criticism of oppressive practices, including criticism from members of the society itself, and that it treats cultures as bounded and internally uniform when they are mixed and contested. It is also argued that the demand to respect all cultural standards is itself a universal moral claim. Clifford Geertz, in "Anti Anti-Relativism" (1984), questioned the alarm surrounding relativism without simply endorsing it. Many researchers treat it as a rule of inquiry: understand first, and keep moral evaluation as a separate step that requires its own arguments.

## Sociological Relevance

The principle informs ethnography, comparative research, and studies of religion, family life, health and migration, where assumptions drawn from the researcher's own setting can easily distort description. It also raises practical questions in multicultural societies about how far public institutions should accommodate differing norms, and who is entitled to speak for a culture.
