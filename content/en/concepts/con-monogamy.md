# Monogamy

Monogamy is marriage to a single spouse at a time, the union of one man and one woman as exclusive partners. It is the only marriage form permitted by law in most of the world today, and it is often treated as the default against which other arrangements are measured. Yet anthropology shows that monogamy is neither the original nor the universal form of marriage. It is one option among several, including polygyny, polyandry, and serial monogamy, and its spread is a historical achievement rather than a natural fact. Understanding monogamy means asking why some societies made it the rule, how it is enforced, and what it does for those who practise it.

## Origins and Development

The comparative study of marriage began with Edward Westermarck's *The History of Human Marriage* (1891), the first systematic cross-cultural survey of the subject. Westermarck argued that monogamy was the original human condition, rooted in the prolonged dependence of human infants, which favoured a lasting pair bond. Later evolutionists disagreed, claiming that early humans were promiscuous and that monogamy arrived late, with private property and civilisation. George Peter Murdock's *Social Structure* (1949) settled the empirical question in one respect: using a sample of 250 societies, he found monogamy permitted everywhere but practised exclusively in only about a quarter of them, while polygyny was allowed in most. Monogamy was the most common actual arrangement, he argued, not because societies preferred it in principle but because most men could not afford more than one wife.

Jack Goody offered a different explanation in *The Development of the Family and Marriage in Europe* (1983). He traced the rise of monogamy in Europe to the early Christian Church, which prohibited divorce, remarriage, concubinage, and marriage to close kin, and thereby restricted the circulation of property through heirs. Monogamy, in this account, was not a natural stage but a strategy of the Church and of property-holding families, imposed over centuries on a population that had practised more flexible arrangements. In China, India, and the Islamic world, monogamy coexisted with concubinage and secondary wives, so that the ideal of one wife did not always match household reality.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns what monogamy is for. Functionalist accounts stress paternal certainty and investment: where men must provision children over many years, exclusive marriage secures their confidence in paternity and their willingness to invest. Joseph Henrich, Robert Boyd, and Peter Richerson revived this argument in "The Puzzle of Monogamous Marriage" (2012), proposing that socially imposed monogamy reduced male competition, lowered crime and violence, and increased savings and investment, thereby giving monogamous societies an advantage in building complex institutions. Their argument links marriage rules to the rise of large-scale cooperation and has been widely discussed and contested.

Critics reply that the functions attributed to monogamy can be served otherwise. Matrilineal societies invest in children through the mother's brother rather than the husband; many polygynous societies are stable and prosperous; and women's labour, not male provisioning, often sustains households. Feminist anthropologists add that monogamy has historically meant different things for men and women: male infidelity was tolerated or even expected in many monogamous societies, while female infidelity was punished severely, so that the rule was never symmetrical in practice. The double standard shows that monogamy is as much about the control of women's sexuality and reproduction as about pair bonding.

Another dimension is the distinction between social monogamy and sexual monogamy. Ethnographers document societies where marriage is formally monogamous yet extramarital relationships are common, tolerated, or ritualised, and others where serial monogamy, a succession of exclusive marriages, is the norm. Divorce rates, remarriage, and the acceptance of same-sex marriage have further loosened the link between monogamy, heterosexuality, and permanence. Anthropologists therefore distinguish the rule, the practice, and the ideal, which rarely coincide exactly.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Monogamy is now legally dominant but culturally contested. Same-sex marriage, legalised in a growing number of countries since the early 2000s, extended monogamy's privileges to couples it once excluded, while polyamory and other forms of consensual non-monogamy challenge the assumption that exclusivity is the only ethical form of committed love. At the same time, demographic research shows marriage itself declining in many wealthy countries, with cohabitation and single parenthood rising, so that monogamy increasingly describes an ideal that fewer people live by. Debates about marriage law, from divorce reform to the recognition of customary marriages, continue to treat the monogamous union as the unit of taxation, inheritance, and custody, with real consequences for those whose arrangements fall outside it.

Anthropology's contribution is to denaturalise monogamy without dismissing it. It is a human invention, enforced by law, religion, and custom, sustained by love, property, and the needs of children, and resisted or reshaped wherever those supports weaken. Studying monogamy comparatively reveals that the questions it answers, who may marry whom, who inherits, who cares for children, are asked in every society, but the answers vary. The monogamous marriage is one powerful answer among others, and its history is still being written.
