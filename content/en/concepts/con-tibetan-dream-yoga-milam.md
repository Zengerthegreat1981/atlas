# Tibetan Dream Yoga (Milam / rmi lam)

Dream yoga (in Tibetan *rmi lam*, pronounced approximately "Milam") is a systematic contemplative practice within Tibetan Buddhist traditions. It aims to attain a clear and controlled awareness during the dream itself. It is not the request for an instructive dream from an external source (as in Greek dream incubation or the Islamic istikhara prayer; see the concept of ritual dream incubation). It is the training of awareness to remain awake within the dream state itself, as a field of spiritual contemplation.

## The Difference from Dream Incubation

Whereas dream incubation rests on waiting for a message or vision given from an external sacred source (a god in a Greek temple, or divine guidance through the istikhara prayer), Tibetan dream yoga trains the practitioner himself in "lucidity": recognising that he is dreaming while he dreams. It then uses that state to train the recognition of the illusory nature of reality, in harmony with the broader Buddhist teachings on impermanence and illusion (maya).

## Academic Documentation

Serinity Young treated this tradition within her broader project on dreaming in Buddhist narrative, in her book *Dreaming in the Lotus* (1999). The book places dream yoga in its historical context within the development of Buddhist thought on dreams from India to Tibet, and relates it to other contemplative practices, such as meditation on emptiness (*shunyata*) and the completion-stage practices of Tibetan tantra. In the Tibetan Buddhist context dream yoga is one of the "six yogas of Naropa"; the Bon tradition has its own dream and sleep yogas.

## Relation to Other Comparative Traditions

The contrast becomes clear when dream yoga is compared with the Islamic istikhara prayer. The one seeking guidance asks God for a sign that directs his decision, through a dream given from outside himself, whereas the dream yoga practitioner asks nothing from any external source at all. Instead, he turns the dream itself into a laboratory for contemplating the nature of consciousness. This structural difference, between receiving a message and training self-directed attention, makes dream yoga methodologically closer to Buddhist mindfulness practices in general than to the traditions of prophetic guidance dreams found in the monotheistic religions.

## The Preparatory Structure of the Practice

Dream yoga is usually placed within a broader set of contemplative practices known as the yogas of night and sleep. It requires preliminary training in stabilising attention during waking life before attempting to carry it into the dream state. The practitioner first trains by repeating a mental intention before sleep ("I will recognise that I am dreaming"), and then by visualising a luminous image or seed syllable, often at the throat, as an anchor for awareness (details vary by lineage and teacher). This methodical gradation distinguishes dream yoga from mere "lucid dreaming" as a general psychological phenomenon, studied by modern sleep science apart from any contemplative spiritual aim.
