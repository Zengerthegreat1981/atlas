# Therapeutic Alliance

The **therapeutic alliance** (also known as the **working alliance**) is a central concept in psychotherapy research. It refers to the quality of the collaborative and emotional relationship between therapist and patient. It is one of the most stable variables in predicting treatment outcomes across different schools and techniques.

## Roots and Definition

The roots of the concept go back to **Sigmund Freud**, who noted in "On Beginning the Treatment" (1913) the importance of establishing a positive rapport and an attachment of the patient to the analyst as a condition for successful treatment, a theme later developed in the distinction between the transference and the "unobjectionable" positive part of the relationship, and in Zetzel's and Greenson's concepts of the therapeutic and working alliance. The most influential theoretical formulation, however, came from **Edward Bordin** in 1979. He redefined the therapeutic alliance as a concept **transcending schools of therapy** (pantheoretical), not confined to psychoanalysis, and analyzed it into three interrelated components:

1. **Agreement on goals**: the agreement of patient and therapist on what the therapy seeks to achieve.
2. **Agreement on tasks**: their agreement on the specific therapeutic activities and procedures required to reach those goals.
3. **Bond**: the trust, acceptance, and mutual emotional connection between the two parties.

## Measurement and Research

Bordin's model contributed to the development of standardized measurement tools, most notably the **Working Alliance Inventory** (WAI), constructed by Horvath and Greenberg (1989) from Bordin's model. Another is the **Penn Helping Alliance Questionnaire**, which Lester Luborsky and colleagues developed along a parallel research path, building on his earlier work on "helping alliances." Since the 1970s, the therapeutic alliance has become a central focus of research on **common factors** in psychotherapy. This research examines what effective therapies share across different schools (cognitive behavioral, psychodynamic, humanistic), rather than focusing only on the techniques of each school in isolation.

## Clinical Impact

Many meta-analyses agree that the strength of the early therapeutic alliance is modestly to moderately but consistently associated with improved treatment outcomes, across diverse diagnoses and therapeutic approaches, from cognitive behavioral therapy to psychodynamic therapy to family therapy. This consistency has led some researchers to describe the alliance as one of the strongest "common factors" explaining the effectiveness of psychotherapy; some researchers argue that it accounts for as much outcome variance as specific techniques, a claim that remains debated.

## Limits

The debate continues over the **direction of the causal relationship**. Does a strong alliance produce improvement, or does early improvement in the patient strengthen their perception of the quality of the alliance? In other words, the relationship may be reciprocal or partly reversed. The way the alliance is measured also varies with the tool and the source (patient assessment versus therapist assessment versus an external observer), which explains the notable variation in the size of the correlation across studies. Some research indicates that the importance of the alliance may vary with the severity of the disorder and the personality of the patient, so it cannot serve as a single, independent outcome indicator separate from the clinical context.
