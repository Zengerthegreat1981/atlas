# Ahimsa (The Principle of Non-Harm)

Ahimsa (Sanskrit *ahiṃsā*, "non-harming") is the principle of refraining from injury to living beings. It is central to Jainism, important in Buddhism and in many strands of Hinduism, and it is most rigorously worked out in Jain thought. The word is a negated form of *hiṃsā*, meaning harming, injuring or killing.

## Early Appearances

The term appears in the Upanishads (for example Chandogya Upanishad 8.15.1, which speaks of not harming any being), and the ideal is prominent in the *Mahabharata* and in the *Laws of Manu*, as well as in the edicts of the Mauryan emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE), who urged restraint in the killing of animals. Modern scholars debate how far non-harm emerged from the renouncer (*śramaṇa*) movements of the first millennium BCE, in which both Jainism and Buddhism arose, and how far from Vedic traditions.

## Dimensions

- **Bodily**: not killing or injuring living beings. Jain monastics observe this most strictly, for example by sweeping the ground before walking, straining drinking water, and not travelling at night in order to avoid harming small creatures.
- **Verbal**: not speaking in ways that wound. Jain texts treat harsh or deceitful speech as a form of violence.
- **Mental**: not harbouring hostile intentions. In Jain ethics the intention to harm already counts as violence, even when no act follows.
- **Karmic**: in Jain doctrine, karma is a subtle matter that binds to the soul (*jīva*) through passions and acts, and violence is a principal cause of bondage that hinders liberation.

## Comparison Across Traditions

- **Jainism**: ahimsa is the first and chief of the five great vows (*mahāvratas*) of monks and nuns, which the lay community follows in a modified form as the *aṇuvratas*. Jains regard Mahavira as the twenty-fourth *tirthankara* (a teacher in an ongoing lineage, not the founder), and modern scholars place him in roughly the fifth century BCE as an elder contemporary of the Buddha. Jain thought extends ahimsa to all life, including the smallest organisms, and links it to the doctrine of many-sidedness (*anekāntavāda*), which counsels respect for other viewpoints. Because many occupations cause harm, lay Jains have historically avoided those, such as farming, that involve large-scale killing of small creatures.
- **Buddhism**: refraining from taking life is the first of the five precepts (*pañcaśīla*) for the laity. Buddhist ethics grounds non-harm mainly in compassion and in the intention behind an act, rather than in the Jain view of karma as matter, and it is less absolute: lay Buddhists farm, and the focus falls on intentional killing.
- **Hinduism**: ahimsa is listed among the virtues in the *Bhagavad Gita* (for example 16.2 and 17.14), yet the *Gita* also presents Arjuna's duty as a warrior (*kṣatriya*) to fight, so non-harm there is weighed against *dharma*. Later Hindu traditions, particularly Vaishnavism, emphasise vegetarianism and non-violence. In Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras* (2.30) ahimsa is the first of the five *yamas*, or restraints.
- **Other traditions**: Comparisons are sometimes drawn with the Christian injunction to love one's enemies (Matthew 5:44), but they differ in scope and grounding. The extension of non-harm to all sentient life is characteristic of the Indian traditions.

## Modern Developments

Mohandas K. Gandhi reinterpreted ahimsa as the basis of *satyagraha* ("holding firmly to truth"), a method of nonviolent political resistance he developed in South Africa and India. He was influenced by the Jain thinker Shrimad Rajchandra and by Tolstoy, among others. Martin Luther King Jr. cited Gandhi as a model for nonviolent resistance in the American civil rights movement.

## Negative and Positive Senses

Ahimsa is usually defined negatively, as the avoidance of harm, but many interpreters stress a positive side of active benevolence, compassion and care for others. The balance between the two varies across traditions and thinkers.
