# Asabiyyah in Ibn Khaldun

The central concept in Ibn Khaldun's social and political theory: the strength of cohesion and collective solidarity arising from the bond of kinship, or what takes its place in loyalty and alliance. In his view it is the basic driving force behind the rise and fall of states.

## Content

Ibn Khaldun defined asabiyyah as a collective feeling of belonging and solidarity arising mainly from ties of kinship and lineage, though not confined to them, since something similar can arise from loyalty, alliance, and shared belonging to a cause or group. He held that asabiyyah is stronger and more vital in Bedouin societies, because of the harshness of life and the group's pressing need for the mutual support of its members in order to survive. It weakens in settled urban societies because the state guarantees protection, so individuals dispense with this direct collective cohesion. From this concept Ibn Khaldun derived a general law of the succession of states. A state arises when a group with strong asabiyyah prevails over others and founds a dominion. Asabiyyah then weakens gradually as power settles and the luxury of civilization grows, until the state collapses before a new, more cohesive Bedouin asabiyyah. Ibn Khaldun estimated the duration of this cycle at about three generations (about 120 years).

## Historical Context

Ibn Khaldun formulated this concept in his famous *Muqaddimah*, which he wrote in 1377 at the Qal'at Ibn Salama in Algeria. He drew on his direct observation of the history of the successive states of the Islamic Maghreb (the Almoravids, Almohads and Marinids), and on his wide personal experience of political and diplomatic work at several courts in al-Andalus and North Africa. This enabled him to observe the mechanisms of the rise and decline of states at a closeness rare among the thinkers of his age.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The concept of asabiyyah is part of Ibn Khaldun's broader project, the "science of human civilization" (ilm al-umran). He regarded it as an independent science with its own subject and method, based on comparative historical induction and not on transmission alone, as was the habit of many of the religious sciences of his time. Ibn Khaldun himself nevertheless remained a Maliki jurist and a judge who exercised judicial office in Mamluk Egypt. This blend of the historical inductive method with membership in the Islamic jurisprudential tradition is seen as a distinguishing mark of the originality of the Khaldunian project within classical Arab-Islamic thought.

## Impact

European Orientalists rediscovered this concept in the nineteenth century, and political anthropologists later used it, most notably Ernest Gellner, as an analytical framework for studying social organization in tribal societies of North Africa and the Middle East. The concept remains a subject of methodological debate about how far it can be generalized beyond the historical and geographical context in which it was originally formulated (the Islamic Maghreb in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries). In the twentieth century it was also revived in Arab attempts to ground sociology theoretically by returning to Ibn Khaldun as the "father of sociology," who preceded Auguste Comte by more than four centuries, though scholars differ over how accurate this title is and over how far his project actually continued in the development of modern science.

## Comparison with Later Theories of Solidarity

Asabiyyah is sometimes set beside Durkheim's "mechanical solidarity," since both describe cohesion in small, kin-based groups. The resemblance is thematic only. Ibn Khaldun's concept is tied to a political cycle of conquest, dynastic consolidation and decay, whereas Durkheim's is part of a typology of social integration under the division of labor. There is no documented line of influence between them, and the comparison should be treated as an analytical convenience rather than a historical connection.
