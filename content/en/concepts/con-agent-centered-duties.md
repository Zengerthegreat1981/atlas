# Agent-Centered Duties

## Meaning and Definition

Agent-centered duties (Agent-Centered/Agent-Relative Duties) are moral duties formulated from the perspective of the agent themselves, not from a perspective neutral with respect to agents. Such a duty gives a person a special reason that binds that very person to perform or refrain from an act, even if breaking it would produce a better outcome in terms of the total good in the world. A mother has a particular duty to care for her own child, not for every child in the world, even if another child is in greater need. This concept stands in contrast to what is called "agent-neutral duties", which bind every agent to promoting one general good regardless of their own position in relation to it, as utilitarianism holds.

## Roots in Islamic Ethical Thought

Muslim jurists and ethicists did not use this modern term. However, their jurisprudence of rights and duties was founded on a distinction close to it: the distinction between "the right of God" (*haqq Allah*) and "the right of the servant" (*haqq al-'abd*), and between general obligations (such as collective *jihad* and enjoining good) and particular obligations related to the agent's own person and kin, such as honoring one's parents, maintaining one's wife and relatives, and returning a trust to its very owner rather than to any other person in need. Jurists elaborated on the "individual obligations" (*fard al-ayn*) that bind each specific responsible person regardless of others. This implicitly affirms that the agent carries obligations specific to themselves that cannot be reduced to calculating the general interest alone, which approaches the idea of the centrality of the agent in duty.

## Roots in Western Philosophy and Contemporary Debate

In modern Western philosophy, Samuel Scheffler articulated this distinction precisely in his critique of utilitarianism. He showed that ordinary morality recognizes for every agent a right of "agent-centered prerogative" (Agent-Centered Prerogative) to give preference to their own interests and those of people close to them, and "agent-centered restrictions" (Agent-Centered Restrictions) that forbid them to commit an injustice even if this would prevent a greater injustice committed by someone else. Bernard Williams approached the same theme from a different angle in "A Critique of Utilitarianism" (1973), criticizing utilitarianism for dissolving the "integrity" of the moral agent into an impersonal calculation of outcomes. Kantian ethics is often cited as an ancestor of such views, since it grounds duty in the rational will of the agent rather than in the calculation of consequences. The terms "agent-relative" and "agent-neutral" themselves were popularized by Derek Parfit in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), building on Thomas Nagel's *The Possibility of Altruism* (1970).

## Objections and Example

Utilitarians object that this distinction justifies a disguised moral egoism: why should an agent be permitted to favor their own child over a stranger in greater need? Proponents of this trend reply that special relationships (such as fatherhood and friendship) are themselves a source of moral value, not an obstacle to it. An illustrative example: a doctor refuses to kill an innocent patient to harvest their organs and save five other patients, even though pure utilitarian calculation might favor the act. Here the agent-centered restriction appears clearly, since it forbids the doctor personally from being a killer, regardless of the overall outcome.
