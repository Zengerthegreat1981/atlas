# Institution

An institution is an established, enduring pattern of social behaviour, organised around a recognised purpose and sustained by norms, roles, and often formal organisations. The family, the state, the economy, religion, education, and law are the classic institutions of sociology. Each provides a stable framework within which people act: it tells them what to expect from others and what is permitted. Institutions outlast the people who occupy their roles, yet they exist only because people continue to enact them.

## Definition

Sociologists distinguish between institutions and organisations. An organisation is a concrete group with members, boundaries, and a hierarchy, such as a particular school or company. An institution is the broader pattern that organisations embody: education as a social institution includes schools, but also the norms, credentials, and expectations that make schooling meaningful. Émile Durkheim defined institutions broadly as all the beliefs and modes of behaviour established by the collectivity, while later writers narrowed the term to the major, enduring structures of social life.

Institutions have several features. They are normative, prescribing how people should behave; they are durable, persisting across generations; they are taken for granted, so that people follow their rules without reflecting on them; and they are interdependent, so that change in one, such as the economy, reverberates through others, such as the family. Institutions also distribute power: they decide who can do what, who gets what, and whose voice counts.

## Origins and Development

The concept was central to classical sociology. Durkheim, in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), treated social facts, including institutions, as external to individuals and constraining upon them. Max Weber, in *Economy and Society* (1922), analysed institutions as orders of legitimate domination, showing how bureaucracy, law, and the state organise action through rules and authority. Talcott Parsons, in *The Social System* (1951), gave institutions a functionalist frame, treating them as the structures that meet the needs of a social system and socialise individuals into its values.

Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann, in *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966), offered a different account of how institutions arise. They argued that institutions begin as habitual actions that become reciprocal and are then experienced as objective realities: people forget that they made the institution and treat it as a natural fact. This process, institutionalisation, explains both the stability of institutions and the possibility of change, since what was made can be remade. Douglass North, in *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990), brought the concept into economics, defining institutions as the rules of the game that shape incentives and arguing that differences in institutions explain differences in economic performance.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the relationship between institutions and agency. Functionalist and Durkheimian accounts emphasise constraint: institutions shape individuals, often without their awareness. Critics argue that this leaves too little room for agency, and that people also interpret, resist, and transform institutions. The new institutionalism, developed by Paul DiMaggio and Walter Powell in "The Iron Cage Revisited" (1983), sought a middle path, showing how organisations adopt similar forms not because they are efficient but because they seek legitimacy, copying peers and conforming to professional norms, a process they called isomorphism.

A second dimension is the distinction between formal and informal institutions. Formal institutions are codified in law and official rules; informal institutions are unwritten norms, customs, and conventions. The two often interact: a formal rule may be undermined by an informal norm, or an informal practice may be formalised into law. In many societies, informal institutions, such as kinship obligations or patronage networks, matter more for everyday life than formal ones.

A third debate concerns institutional change. Institutions are durable, but they do change, through crises, reforms, and slow drift. Some change is deliberate, as when a constitution is rewritten; some is unintended, as when a new technology makes an old practice obsolete.

## Empirical Illustrations

John Meyer and Brian Rowan's "Institutionalized Organizations: Formal Structure as Myth and Ceremony" (1977) argued that schools, hospitals, and firms often adopt formal structures because these are accepted as legitimate in their environment, not because they improve efficiency. Erving Goffman's *Asylums* (1961) examined the "total institution", in which residents of hospitals, prisons, and similar settings live under a single administrative regime. Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson's *Why Nations Fail* (2012) uses comparative cases to argue that political and economic institutions shape national prosperity, a thesis that remains debated.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Institutions are at the centre of contemporary debates about democracy, inequality, and development. Political scientists ask why some institutions, such as independent courts or free media, sustain democracy while others hollow it out. Economists debate whether inclusive institutions, which distribute power broadly, produce prosperity, while extractive institutions, which concentrate it, produce stagnation. The family as an institution is being reshaped by changing gender roles, same-sex marriage, and new reproductive technologies, while digital platforms are creating new institutional forms, such as the platform economy, whose rules are written in code rather than law.

Critics argue that the concept of institution is too broad, covering everything from marriage to the market, and that it can become a residual category for whatever is stable in social life. Others note that institutional analysis often neglects conflict, treating institutions as shared solutions to collective problems rather than as settlements imposed by the powerful. Feminist and postcolonial scholars have shown how institutions built by and for dominant groups systematically disadvantage others. These criticisms have made institutional analysis more attentive to power, history, and the lived experience of those who inhabit institutions.
