# Phenomenology of Ethics

"Phenomenology of ethics" (also called moral phenomenology) refers to the philosophical project that seeks to describe the living moral experience as it is actually lived in consciousness, before any theorizing or conceptual abstraction. The question is how a person perceives the other face to face, and how the sense of obligation, guilt and responsibility takes shape in the immediate moment, not as theoretical categories but as experiences actually lived. This approach stands in methodological contrast to analytic normative ethics, which begins by formulating abstract principles and standards and then applies them to cases.

## Echoes in Sufism and Islamic Thought

Islamic Sufi literature carries a descriptive tendency close to the spirit of phenomenology. Sufis such as Abu Hamid al-Ghazali were concerned with describing the inner "states" (*ahwal*), such as fear, hope, love and scrupulousness, in precise detail from within. They treated these as lived experiences of the heart rather than mere legal rulings. In *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, the quarters on "the destructive" (*muhlikat*) and "the saving" (*munjiyat*) qualities of the soul analyze such inner states in detail. Al-Raghib al-Isfahani, in *al-Dhari'a ila Makarim al-Shari'a*, likewise treated moral character as a matter of the soul's dispositions and not only of outward acts.

## Emmanuel Levinas: The Face of the Other

The French philosopher of Lithuanian origin, Emmanuel Levinas, is a leading figure in the development of phenomenological ethics in the strict sense. Drawing on Husserlian and Heideggerian phenomenology, he departed from it fundamentally. Instead of ethics being a branch of ontology (the theory of being), Levinas made ethics a "first philosophy." The encounter with "the face of the Other" (*le visage*) is the original event that demands from me an unconditional responsibility before any reflection or utilitarian calculation. According to Levinas, the face of the Other "commands" me by its very appearance, in the form of "you shall not kill." This call precedes consciousness itself and cannot be reduced to a logical proposition. Earlier, Max Scheler had developed a parallel attempt in *Formalism in Ethics*, drawing on Husserl's phenomenology. He made "values" (moral and aesthetic) objects grasped intuitively through feeling rather than through abstract reason, arranged in an objective hierarchy from the lowest (sensory pleasure) to the highest (the holy).

## Significance and Objections

The value of this approach lies in correcting the tendency of analytic ethics to abstract the moral act from its living context and personal relationships. It reminds us that the origin of moral obligation may precede any explicit rule. The approach is criticized, however, for the obscurity of its method and the difficulty of converting the phenomenological "description" into practical standards that can be applied to complex ethical disputes. How can we resolve a disagreement between two parties if all we have is the description of each one's individual experience?

*Illustrative example:* the scene of a mother who immediately rises to rescue her crying child in the night, without thinking about duty or calculating consequences. This is what the phenomenologist seeks to describe precisely, as an initial moral response that precedes any formulated ethical theory.
