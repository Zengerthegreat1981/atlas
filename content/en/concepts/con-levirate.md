# Levirate

Levirate is the custom by which a widow marries a brother of her deceased husband, often a younger brother (in many accounts the union is treated as a continuation of the first marriage). The term comes from the Latin *levir*, meaning a husband's brother, and the practice is best known from the Hebrew Bible, where Deuteronomy commands that a man marry his dead brother's widow so that the brother's name and inheritance are preserved. But levirate is far from a biblical curiosity: anthropologists have recorded it in societies across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, where it serves to keep a widow and her children within the husband's kin group, to secure inheritance, and to maintain alliances between families.

## Origins and Development

Early anthropologists treated levirate as one of a set of marriage customs that followed from the logic of kinship. If marriage was an alliance between groups rather than a contract between individuals, then the death of a husband did not end the alliance: the group that had received the wife still owed her protection and support, and the group that had given her still expected the marriage to continue. The levirate solved the problem by substituting a new husband from the same group. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's studies of the Nuer of South Sudan (*The Nuer*, 1940; *Kinship and Marriage among the Nuer*, 1951) gave a classic ethnographic account: among the Nuer, a widow remained the wife of her dead husband and was expected to live with one of his brothers or close kinsmen, and children born of that union counted as the dead man's children, preserving his lineage.

Claude Lévi-Strauss placed levirate within his theory of alliance in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949). For Lévi-Strauss, marriage was an exchange of women between groups, and the levirate was a mechanism for keeping that exchange in balance across generations. If a husband died childless, the alliance was threatened; the levirate restored it by providing a substitute husband from the same lineage. George Peter Murdock's comparative survey *Social Structure* (1949) recorded levirate as a widespread practice. It has often been linked to patrilineal descent and bridewealth, since a family that had paid for a wife expected to keep her and her children even after the husband's death.

Later scholars stressed the economic dimension. Jack Goody argued that levirate, like widow inheritance more generally, was connected to the control of property: in societies where land and cattle passed through men, allowing a widow to remarry outside her husband's group would carry property and children away from the lineage. The levirate prevented that loss. Feminist anthropologists added that the widow's own position was often constrained: she might have little choice about the match, and her labour and fertility remained under the control of her husband's kin.

## Key Debates

A central debate concerns the difference between levirate and widow inheritance. In the levirate in the strict anthropological sense, the widow's marriage to the dead man is treated as continuing: his brother or kinsman acts in his place, and the children count as the dead man's heirs. In widow inheritance, by contrast, a relative of the deceased takes the widow as his own wife, and later children are generally counted as his. Anthropologists debate whether these are variants of one institution or distinct customs with different meanings, and whether the biblical form should define the category or be treated as one case among many.

A second debate concerns the status of the children. In many leviratic systems, children born after the husband's death are legally his, not their biological father's. This separation of biological and social fatherhood is a classic anthropological finding: it shows that kinship is a social and legal institution, not simply a record of biology. The debate turns on how far this principle extends — whether the leviratic husband is a father in his own right, a stand-in, or something in between — and on what the arrangement means for the widow's children and their claims to inheritance.

A third debate concerns women's agency. The levirate is often described from the perspective of men and lineages, as a device for preserving names and property. But ethnography also records widows who welcomed the levirate because it guaranteed them a home, support, and standing, and others who resisted it, fled, or negotiated alternative arrangements. In contemporary Africa, where levirate persists in some communities, it has become a focus of legal reform and public-health debate, particularly in connection with HIV, inheritance rights, and the rights of widows to choose whether and whom to marry.

## Contemporary Relevance

Levirate remains a live issue. In parts of East and Southern Africa, customary levirate and widow inheritance continue to be practised, sometimes in tension with statutory law and constitutional guarantees of gender equality. Courts and legislatures have had to decide whether the custom is a protected cultural practice or a violation of women's rights, and women's organisations have campaigned both for its abolition and for its reform. The anthropological contribution to this debate is to show that the levirate is not a single, timeless custom but a flexible institution whose meaning depends on who practises it and why. In some settings it protects widows; in others it constrains them. Understanding it therefore requires attention to the particular history, economy, and politics of the communities where it is found, rather than a blanket judgement from outside.
