# Group: The Social Unit Between the Individual and Society

A **group** is a collection of people who interact with one another, share a sense of belonging, and recognize themselves — and are recognized by others — as a distinct social unit. A family, a work team, a religious congregation, and a nation are all groups, but they differ enormously in size, duration, and intensity of interaction. The group is the level at which much of social life actually happens: individuals rarely meet society directly; they meet it through the groups they belong to.

## Definition and Development

Charles Horton Cooley, in *Social Organization* (1909), drew the distinction that still organizes the study of groups: the **primary group**, characterized by intimate, face-to-face association and cooperation — the family, the neighbourhood, the circle of close friends — and the larger, more impersonal associations of modern life. In primary groups, Cooley argued, human nature itself is formed: we learn sympathy, loyalty, and a sense of self through the close, sustained contact of those who know us well. The primary group is not merely a small group; it is the nursery of the social self.

Georg Simmel, in his *Soziologie* (1908; the essay on group affiliations published in English in 1955 as "The Web of Group-Affiliations"), showed that modernity multiplies group memberships. In a traditional village, a person belongs to one or two groups that cover the whole of life; in a modern city, a person belongs to many groups — family, profession, club, party, church — each claiming only part of them. This web of affiliations is both liberating and fragmenting: it frees the individual from the total control of any single group, but it also creates conflicts of loyalty and a divided self.

## Key Contributions: Group Development and Identity

Bruce Tuckman, in "Developmental Sequence in Small Groups" (1965), proposed the most widely used model of how groups change over time. Reviewing fifty studies of small groups, he argued that groups pass through four stages: forming, when members orient themselves and test the boundaries; storming, when conflict over goals and leadership erupts; norming, when shared rules and cohesion develop; and performing, when the group can finally do its work. In 1977, Tuckman and Mary Ann Jensen added a fifth stage, adjourning, for the ending of a group. The model is descriptive rather than prescriptive — many groups skip stages or return to earlier ones — but it remains a standard frame for understanding why new teams struggle before they work well.

Henri Tajfel, in *Human Groups and Social Categories* (1981), showed how group membership shapes perception itself. In the minimal group experiments (Tajfel, Billig, Bundy and Flament, 1971) that the book summarises, people divided into groups on the most trivial basis — a preference for one painter over another — immediately began to favour their own group and discriminate against the other. Tajfel's point was that group identity does not require conflict over resources or a history of hostility; the mere act of categorization is enough to produce bias. His work became the foundation of social identity theory and of the study of prejudice and intergroup conflict.

## Examples

Groups are visible everywhere once you look for them. A hospital ward is a group with its own norms, hierarchies, and informal leaders; a new nurse learns its rules not from a manual but from watching who speaks, who decides, and who is listened to. A football crowd is a group whose members, strangers to one another, coordinate chants and movements with remarkable precision. A diaspora community is a group held together across distance by shared language, food, and memory. In each case, the group does something no individual can do alone: it sustains a shared reality.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

The study of groups has been criticized for treating the group as a thing rather than a process. Groups are not containers that people simply join; they are made and remade through interaction, and their boundaries are often contested — who counts as a member, who speaks for the group, and who is excluded. Tuckman's model, in particular, has been criticized for implying a fixed sequence that real groups rarely follow, and for drawing heavily on therapy and training groups rather than on the groups of everyday life.

The concept remains indispensable. It now frames research on online communities, where groups form without face-to-face contact and sustain themselves through text and image; on work teams distributed across continents; and on identity politics, where group membership becomes the basis of claims for recognition and rights. The group is still the unit in which individuals encounter society — and in which society, in turn, is made.

## Kin Groups in Comparative Perspective

Anthropology shows that the groups a person belongs to through kinship are organised very differently across societies. Where descent is traced through one line, lineages and clans can act as corporate bodies that hold land, regulate marriage and answer collectively for their members' conduct. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) described segmentary lineages in what is now South Sudan, in which segments opposed to one another at one level unite against a more distant segment at a higher level, so that the boundary of the effective group depends on the dispute. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown and Daryll Forde's *African Systems of Kinship and Marriage* (1950) surveyed patrilineal, matrilineal and other arrangements. Francis L. K. Hsu's *Clan, Caste, and Club* (1963) contrasted the Chinese clan, the Hindu caste and the American voluntary association as characteristic settings for meeting social needs. Later anthropologists, including Adam Kuper, questioned how far the lineage model described what people actually did, and David Schneider argued that Western assumptions about biological relatedness had shaped the study of kinship itself.
