# Confrontation with Western Thought

The modern Arab encounter with European thought began with an acute civilizational shock triggered by the French expedition to Egypt in 1798. In his chronicle *'Aja'ib al-athar fi al-tarajim wa-l-akhbar* (Marvels of Chronicles and Biographies), the Egyptian historian 'Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti recorded Napoleon Bonaparte's invasion with a complex mixture of scholarly astonishment and moral outrage: he praised the French military laboratory, scientific academy, and printing presses, while fiercely condemning the brutality and colonial subjugation of the invaders. This foundational shock generated the central question of the nineteenth-century Arab Renaissance (*al-Nahda*): why had Islamic civilization declined while Europe advanced, and how could modern European sciences and political institutions be adopted without sacrificing cultural and religious integrity?

## Nahda Reformers: Selective Appropriation and Constitutionalism

The early Nahda writers were selective. Rifa'a Rafi' al-Tahtawi, imam of the Egyptian student mission in Paris from 1826 to 1831, published *Takhlis al-ibriz fi talkhis Bariz* (An Imam in Paris, 1834). He described the French charter, civil rights, and public schooling as rational arrangements, and he looked for parallels in the Sharia rather than treating the two as opposed. In Tunis, Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi argued in *Aqwam al-masalik fi ma'rifat ahwal al-mamalik* (The Surest Path, 1867) that institutional limits on rulers served justice and public interest (*maslaha*), and that Muslims could adopt such institutions from Europe on that ground. The Lebanese writer Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq compared European and Arab manners, with satire, in *al-Wasita fi ma'rifat ahwal Malta wa-kashf al-mukhabba 'an funun Urubba* (Constantinople edition, 1881).

## The Intellectual Debates: Renan and Hanotaux

As colonial pressure mounted, Arab writers answered European polemics about Islam in the European and Arabic press. In 1883 Ernest Renan lectured at the Sorbonne, in "L'Islamisme et la science," that Islam and the Arabs were hostile to science and philosophy. Jamal al-Din al-Afghani replied in the *Journal des Débats* (18 May 1883). He argued that religions in general resist rational inquiry at first and later accommodate it, and he pointed to philosophy and science written in Islamic societies. In 1900 Muhammad Abduh, then Grand Mufti of Egypt, answered articles by Gabriel Hanotaux, a former French foreign minister, who had blamed Islamic theology, and in particular a doctrine of divine compulsion, for political stagnation. The exchange ran in the French and Egyptian press.

## Twentieth-Century Divergence and Postcolonial Critique

By the early twentieth century the responses had diverged, without forming three sealed schools.

1. **European civilizational standards.** Salama Musa, in *al-Yawm wa-l-ghad* (Today and Tomorrow, 1927), argued that Egypt should take modern Europe as its social and intellectual model. Taha Husayn's *Mustaqbal al-thaqafa fi Misr* (The Future of Culture in Egypt, 1938) is a related but narrower claim: Egypt, he argued, belongs to a Mediterranean world, and its schools should be organized on European lines.
2. **Reform within an Islamic reference.** A line from Muhammad Abduh onward wanted the natural sciences and constitutional government while keeping a reformed Islamic frame for law and education.
3. **Religious critique of cultural Westernization.** The Society of the Muslim Brothers, founded by Hasan al-Banna in 1928, treated much of Western social culture as a danger to Islamic morals, and it used modern organization, print, and schooling to say so.

From the 1960s the argument also became a critique of how Europe produced knowledge about the Arabs. Anouar Abdel-Malek's "Orientalism in Crisis" (1963) and Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) are the best-known statements. Hasan Hanafi's *Muqaddima fi 'ilm al-istighrab* (1991) proposed studying Europe as an object of knowledge rather than as the unquestioned measure of Arab culture. Hisham Sharabi's *Arab Intellectuals and the West* (1970) describes European thought, for the generation of 1875–1914, as a working language of Arab debate, not only as an external pressure.
