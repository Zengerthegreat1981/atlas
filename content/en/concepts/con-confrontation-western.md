# Confrontation with Western Thought

The Arab encounter with European thought in modern times began as a shock. 'Abd al-Rahman al-Jabarti, in *'Aja'ib al-Athar* (*Marvels of Biographies and Chronicles*), recorded the entry of the French into Egypt in 1798 with the eye of a historian who was astonished: he describes a printing press, a workshop and a scientific academy established by the French, while at the same time condemning their conduct and their invasion. From that shock arose the question of the Nahda (Renaissance): how did Muslims fall behind while others advanced, and how can one take from Europe without losing oneself?

## The First Positions

Al-Tahtawi took a position of selective admiration. In *Takhlis al-Ibriz* (*The Extraction of Gold*, 1834), he describes French freedom, constitutionalism and organisation, and relates some of them to the meanings of the Sharia, while criticising what he saw as moral corruption. Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi wrote in *Aqwam al-Masalik fi Ma'rifat Ahwal al-Mamalik* (*The Straightest Paths to Knowing the Conditions of Kingdoms*, 1867) that European reforms could be adopted so long as they did not contradict the Sharia. Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq, in *Kashf al-Mukhabba' 'an Funun Urubba* (*Uncovering the Arts of Europe*, 1860s), compared the two civilisations.

## The Replies to Renan and to Hanotaux

In 1883, Jamal al-Din al-Afghani wrote his reply to Ernest Renan, who had claimed in his lecture at the Sorbonne that Islam was an enemy of science. Al-Afghani responded that all religions had at their beginnings been obstacles to science, and that the Arabs had contributed to philosophy. In 1900, Muhammad Abduh replied to Gabriel Hanotaux, who had accused Islam of hindering progress; this was a direct confrontation in the press. Nikki Keddie published a translation of al-Afghani's reply in *An Islamic Response to Imperialism* (1968).

## Three Trends

At the beginning of the twentieth century three positions took shape. There was a call for full adoption, as in Salama Musa's *Al-Yawm wa-l-Ghad* (*Today and Tomorrow*, 1927). There was a call for selective adoption while holding fast to the original, as in Abduh's position. And there was a rejecting position that regarded the West as a threat to identity, which became prominent among Islamic movements from the 1930s onward. Taha Husayn, in *Mustaqbal al-Thaqafa fi Misr* (*The Future of Culture in Egypt*, 1938), argued that Egypt belongs to the Mediterranean civilisation and called for an educational reform that draws on Europe.

## Reversing the Gaze: Occidentalism and Orientalism

In the 1960s, Anouar Abdel-Malek criticised Orientalism in his article "Orientalism in Crisis" (1963), and then Edward Said came with his book *Orientalism* (1978). Hasan Hanafi responded with *Muqaddima fi 'Ilm al-Istighrab* (*Introduction to the Science of Occidentalism*, 1991), calling for the West to be made an object of study rather than a reference. Bernard Lewis, in *The Muslim Discovery of Europe* (1982), examined why Muslims showed little interest in Europe before the nineteenth century.

## Limits of the Dichotomy

The binary of "we" and "they" simplifies reality. The West itself is plural: France is not Britain, and colonialism is not philosophy. Arabs took from the West the tools of their own critique, from Marx to Foucault. Hisham Sharabi, in *Arab Intellectuals and the West* (1970), shows that the intellectuals themselves were mostly products of foreign schools, so the West was not "outside" them.
