# Choice and Constraint

The existential paradox is that we are "condemned to be free" (Sartre) yet radically constrained. We choose within limitations we did not choose: our body, historical moment, social position, past. The therapeutic work involves distinguishing between genuine constraints (which cannot be changed) and self-imposed constraints (which can).

## Definition

Choice and constraint name the two poles of the existential account of agency. Freedom, in this tradition, does not mean the ability to do anything one wishes. It means that one is always responsible for the stance one takes toward a situation, even when the situation itself is not chosen. Constraint, correspondingly, is not an excuse that cancels responsibility but the field within which responsibility is exercised.

## Facticity and Freedom

Heidegger named the givenness of existence "thrownness" (*Geworfenheit*), and Sartre called the corresponding aspect of human reality "facticity": what is simply given. We are born into a particular family, time, body, and social position. These are not chosen. Yet within facticity, freedom operates: our attitude toward these givens is ours to determine. Sartre went further and claimed that even in extreme situations one is free to give the situation meaning, a claim many readers find too strong. Frankl, writing from his experience of the concentration camps, similarly held that a last freedom remains, the choice of one's attitude, while taking care not to claim that this makes suffering acceptable.

Confusion arises when people either deny constraints (believing themselves utterly free) or deny freedom (believing themselves utterly determined). Emmy van Deurzen's four dimensions framework helps clarify this: physical constraints (body, health, mortality), social constraints (relationships, culture, systems), personal or psychological constraints (capacities, history), and spiritual constraints (values, meaning-systems). Authentic living recognizes real constraints while claiming what agency is possible within them. Rollo May, in *Love and Will* (1969), likewise treated freedom and destiny as intertwined.

## Therapeutic Implications

Many people in therapy discover that constraints are more negotiable than they believed. Someone believes they "have to" maintain a relationship, career, or lifestyle because they misidentify a choice (hard though it may be) as an inescapable fact. Yalom's emphasis on responsibility describes how clients often disown authorship of their situations. Conversely, others discover that what they took for limitless freedom operates within real constraints, such as economic, relational, or biological ones, that cannot be wished away.

The therapeutic stance is neither optimistic ("you can do anything") nor pessimistic ("your circumstances determine you") but realistic: What are the actual constraints here? Where does choice actually exist? What would authenticity require within these bounds?

## Criticisms and Limits

Critics, including Marxist and feminist thinkers, argue that existentialism's emphasis on individual choice can obscure structural forces such as poverty, discrimination, and oppression, which narrow the range of real options far more for some people than others. Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) is itself an existentialist analysis of how situations constrain freedom, and she distinguishes freedom from the material conditions needed to exercise it. Clinically, the language of "choice" must be used with care so that it does not blame people for circumstances, illness, or harm they did not choose.
