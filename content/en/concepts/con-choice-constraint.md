# Choice and Constraint

The existential paradox is that we are "condemned to be free" (Sartre) yet radically constrained. We choose within limitations we did not choose: our body, historical moment, social position, past. The therapeutic work involves distinguishing between genuine constraints (which cannot be changed) and self-imposed constraints (which can).

## Facticity and Freedom

Heidegger named the unchangeable aspects of existence "facticity"—what is simply given. We are born into a particular family, time, body, social position. These are not chosen. Yet within facticity, freedom operates: our attitude toward these givens is ours to choose.

The confusion arises when people either deny constraints (believing themselves utterly free) or deny freedom (believing themselves utterly determined). Van Deurzen's Four Dimensions framework helps clarify: physical constraints (body, health), psychological constraints (capacities, history), social constraints (relationships, systems), and spiritual constraints (meaning-systems, values). Authentic living recognizes real constraints while claiming what agency is possible within them.

## Therapeutic Implications

Many people in therapy discover constraints are more negotiable than they believed. Someone believes they "have to" maintain a relationship, career, or lifestyle because they misidentify a choice (hard though it may be) as an inescapable fact. Conversely, others discover that what they thought was limitless freedom actually operates within real constraints—economic, relational, biological—that cannot be wished away.

The therapeutic stance is neither optimistic ("you can do anything") nor pessimistic ("your circumstances determine you") but realistic: What are the actual constraints here? Where does choice actually exist? What would authenticity require within these bounds?
