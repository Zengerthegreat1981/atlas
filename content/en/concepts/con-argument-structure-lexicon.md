# Argument Structure

A central concept in lexical grammatical theories (such as LFG and HPSG) that describes the information each verb carries in the lexicon about the number and types of grammatical and semantic "arguments" it requires in order to form a well-formed sentence. For example, the verb "give" requires three arguments (the giver, the thing given, and the recipient), while the verb "sleep" requires one argument (the sleeper).

## Semantic Roles and the Grammatical Hierarchy

Argument structure links each argument to a specific semantic role (agent, patient, beneficiary) and orders these roles in a sequence of grammatical priority (the thematic hierarchy). This sequence determines which argument is expressed as the grammatical subject and which as the object. Grammatical relations on the surface are thus derived directly from the lexical information of the verb, rather than from transformational derivation rules separate from it.

## Effects on Grammatical Phenomena

Argument structure is used to explain several grammatical phenomena through a single lexical mechanism. These include alternations in sentence structure (such as turning a transitive verb into a passive construction), unaccusativity (some intransitive verbs behave grammatically like objects despite appearing with a subject form), and the constraints on control and raising verbs and auxiliaries.

## Argument Structure versus Transformational Derivation

The deeper theoretical stake of this concept lies in its position within a broader methodological dispute. Are grammatical relations (such as subjecthood and objecthood) derived from transformational operations applied to a unified deep structure, as in Chomskyan generative grammar? Or are they read directly from lexical information encoded in advance in the verb's lexical entry? LFG and HPSG adopt the second option. Every verb carries in its lexical entry a complete "argument structure" (also called predicate-argument structure), which specifies the number of arguments, their semantic roles, and the constraints on their grammatical expression. A well-formed sentence is then derived directly by matching this structure with the surface grammatical configuration, without any intermediate transformational derivation.

## Alternations in Argument Structure

The importance of this concept appears clearly in the study of "alternations." A single verb may appear with more than one regularly related argument structure. For example, a transitive verb may alternate with an intransitive form that omits its object (as with "eat") or expresses a change of state (as with "break"), or a verb may alternate between a double-object dative construction and a prepositional construction. Lexical theories seek to formulate general lexical alternation rules that derive these multiple forms from a single basic lexical representation, without duplicating the information.

## Argument Structure in Arabic

Traditional Arabic grammar, through the notion of transitivity and intransitivity (*ta'addi* and *luzum*), offers an early distinction that parallels modern argument structure. The intransitive verb is satisfied with a single subject, whereas the transitive verb requires one or more objects. Arab grammarians classified transitive verbs into those that take one object and those that take two (such as verbs of "considering" and their sisters, *zanna* and its counterparts). The classification records how many dependents a verb requires. It is not a theory of thematic roles or of a universal argument hierarchy, and it should not be read as an early statement of LFG or HPSG.
