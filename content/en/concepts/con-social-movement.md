# Social Movement

## Definition

A social movement is a sustained, collective effort, usually by people without routine access to institutional power, to bring about or resist social change through means that include protest, organisation, and the cultivation of shared identities and claims. Movements differ from interest groups, which lobby within established channels, and from fleeting crowds, by their duration, their networks of participants, and their challenge to prevailing arrangements. Charles Tilly, who made the study of movements a core field of political sociology, described them as sustained campaigns of claim-making that combine public displays of worthiness, unity, numbers, and commitment (WUNC) with a repertoire of actions such as demonstrations, petitions, strikes, and public meetings.

## Theoretical Approaches

Early theories, associated with Gustave Le Bon and later with the collective behaviour tradition (Herbert Blumer, Neil Smelser), tended to treat movements as products of crowd psychology, strain, or social breakdown. From the 1970s this view was challenged by resource mobilization theory. John McCarthy and Mayer Zald (1977) argued that grievances are widespread and constant, so that what explains movements is the availability of organisation, money, leadership, and networks. Political process theory, developed by Doug McAdam in *Political Process and the Development of Black Insurgency, 1930-1970* (1982) and by Charles Tilly in *From Mobilization to Revolution* (1978), emphasised political opportunities, such as shifts in elite alignments or state openness, that make challenges feasible.

A further strand, framing theory, drew on Erving Goffman. David Snow and colleagues (1986) analysed "frame alignment", the way movement organisers link their messages to the interpretive schemas of potential supporters. In Europe, the "new social movements" approach, associated with Alain Touraine and Alberto Melucci, argued that the peace, environmental, feminist, and student movements of the late twentieth century centred less on class and more on identity, autonomy, and the control of cultural meaning. Later work, notably by Jeff Goodwin and James Jasper, restored attention to emotions, culture, and the contingent choices of participants.

## Debates and Criticism

Debates concern whether movements are best explained by structures (opportunities, resources) or by culture and agency; whether the resource-mobilization emphasis on rational organisation understates the role of moral conviction and emotion; and how to measure success, since movements may achieve policy change, cultural change, or only the transformation of their participants. Critics of the early collective behaviour tradition argue that it pathologised protest. Critics of political process theory argue that it describes the movements of liberal democracies better than those under authoritarian rule.

## Examples

Frequently studied cases include the labour movement of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the US civil rights movement, women's suffrage and later feminist movements, anti-colonial and national independence movements, and environmental movements. Studies of the civil rights movement, for example, have highlighted the role of Black churches and colleges as organisational resources, alongside the opportunities created by shifts in national politics.
