# Herbalism

## Definition

Herbalism is the use of plants for healing — the knowledge of which plants treat which ailments, how they are prepared, and how they are administered — studied in anthropology as part of medical systems rather than as a collection of remedies. Herbal knowledge is practical, sensory, and often secret: it involves recognising plants, knowing where and when to gather them, and understanding the rituals that make them effective. A herbal remedy is rarely only a chemical: it is a substance embedded in a theory of the body, of illness, and of the relationship between people and the plant world.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of herbalism began with the recognition that healing systems are cultural systems. George Foster, in "Disease Etiologies in Non-Western Medical Systems" (1976), distinguished between personalistic theories, in which illness is caused by an agent — a spirit, a witch, an ancestor — and naturalistic theories, in which it results from imbalance in the body or the environment. Herbal remedies appear in both, but their role differs: in a personalistic system, the plant may be a vehicle for spiritual power; in a naturalistic one, it restores balance. Foster's framework showed that to understand a herbal remedy, one must understand the theory of illness in which it makes sense.

Richard Evans Schultes and Albert Hofmann's *Plants of the Gods* (1979) documented the ritual use of psychoactive plants, with much attention to the Americas — peyote, ayahuasca, psilocybin mushrooms — and showed how deeply plant knowledge was woven into religion. The plants were not drugs in the modern sense but sacred teachers and mediators, approached with fasting, prayer, and guidance. Schultes's work, grounded in long fieldwork in the Amazon, helped shape modern ethnobotany and documented the extraordinary botanical knowledge of Indigenous peoples: Amazonian healers routinely used hundreds of species, with detailed knowledge of their preparation and dosage.

Cecil Helman's *Culture, Health and Illness* (1984) brought herbalism into medical anthropology's core curriculum, showing how patients in every society — including modern Britain — combine biomedical treatment with herbal and folk remedies, and how healers and patients negotiate between systems. Nina Etkin, in "Ethnopharmacology: Biobehavioral Approaches in the Anthropological Study of Indigenous Medicines" (1988), argued for taking the pharmacology seriously: many herbal remedies contain active compounds, and the anthropologist's task is to understand both their biochemical effects and their cultural meanings, without reducing one to the other. Elisabeth Hsu's *The Transmission of Chinese Medicine* (1999) added a study of how herbal knowledge is learned and passed on, comparing secret, personal and standardised modes of learning in China and showing that the manner of transmission shapes what practitioners know — knowledge is not uniform even within a single tradition.

## Key Debates

The first debate concerns efficacy. Do herbal remedies work because of their chemistry, because of placebo and the healing relationship, or because of their ritual framing? The honest answer is that all three operate, and that separating them is itself a cultural act: biomedicine isolates the active ingredient, while most healing traditions treat the plant, the preparation, the healer, and the patient as a single therapeutic package. The World Health Organization's recognition of traditional medicine has made this debate practical as well as theoretical, since it shapes what gets funded, regulated, and taught.

A second debate concerns ownership. Herbal knowledge is increasingly valuable to pharmaceutical companies, which prospect for new drugs in Indigenous pharmacopoeias — a practice critics call biopiracy when the knowledge is taken without consent or compensation. The 1992 Convention on Biological Diversity and later agreements require benefit-sharing, but enforcement is uneven, and the question of who owns knowledge accumulated over generations remains unresolved. Anthropologists have documented both the exploitation and the cases in which communities have successfully asserted control over their plant knowledge.

A third debate concerns transmission and loss. Herbal knowledge is usually oral and apprenticeship-based, and it is disappearing rapidly as forests are cleared, languages are lost, and young people migrate to cities. Ethnobotanists race to document it, but documentation is not the same as living practice: a remedy written in a book is not the same as a remedy learned by gathering the plant with a grandparent.

## Contemporary Relevance

Herbalism is more visible today than it has been for a century. Herbal supplements are a multi-billion-dollar industry; traditional Chinese medicine and Ayurveda are practised worldwide; and the search for new drugs continues to draw on Indigenous knowledge. The COVID-19 pandemic saw a surge of interest in herbal remedies, and also in misinformation about them. Meanwhile, the ecological crisis gives herbalism a new urgency: the plants on which it depends are themselves threatened, and their loss is both a medical and a cultural catastrophe.

For anthropology, herbalism is a reminder that medicine is never only about the body. A plant remedy carries a theory of illness, a relationship with the landscape, a history of learning, and a politics of ownership — and to study it is to study all of these at once.
