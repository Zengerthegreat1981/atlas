# Sacred Space and Anthropological Place

## The Mosque and the Sanctuary: Limited and Transferable Sanctity

Islam provides a useful case for testing theories of sacred space. Some places have special inviolability: the sacred precinct of Mecca, whose boundaries forbid fighting and cutting trees, the Prophet's Mosque, and al-Aqsa Mosque, which together form the "three mosques" to which journeys are made. At the same time, there is the hadith "The earth has been made for me a place of prostration and a means of purification," so that every clean spot is suitable for prayer. This makes sanctity a function of use and orientation (the *qibla*) rather than an intrinsic property of the place. This duality shows that theories which presuppose a fixed sacred set against a profane one need revision in the face of traditions that rely on direction, boundary, and purity.

## Durkheim and Eliade

Émile Durkheim, in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912), regarded the sacred as absolutely separated from the profane, and held that the sanctity of a place expresses the power of the group and creates solidarity. Mircea Eliade, in *The Sacred and the Profane* (German edition 1957; English 1959), held that the sacred manifests itself in a place (hierophany), so that the homogeneity of space breaks and a center of the world (*axis mundi*) appears, linking heaven and earth; and that the religious person needs this center in order to orient himself in the cosmos. This model made the building of a temple a repetition of the creation of the world. Hence the idea of linking religious space with identity: the group defines its existence by a center around which it gathers.

## Jonathan Z. Smith and the Turners

Jonathan Z. Smith, in *To Take Place* (1987), criticized Eliade's model because it treats place as ready-made, and argued that sanctity is not manifested spontaneously but is **produced by human action**: people designate a spot and surround it with ritual and attention. The sacred is therefore a locative category and a construction project, not an essence. In the study of pilgrimage, Victor and Edith Turner, in *Image and Pilgrimage in Christian Culture* (1978), held that pilgrimage is a liminal journey far from everyday structure, in which pilgrims experience equality and communitas, and they described the pilgrimage shrine as a "center out there" at the periphery of the ordinary social world. Yi-Fu Tuan, in *Space and Place* (1977), distinguished abstract space from place, which acquires meaning through experience and attachment; this phenomenological approach helps in understanding a community's attachment to its sacred sites.

## Identity and Contestation

Many studies affirm that sacred places become arenas of identity conflict: Jerusalem, the Ibrahimi Sanctuary, and Hagia Sophia, which was converted from a church into a mosque in 1453, then into a museum in 1934, and returned to a mosque in 2020. These examples show that the same place may carry successive or competing sanctities, and that each community has its own narrative about it. Marc Augé, in *Non-lieux* (1992), adds a counter-dimension, describing modern transit sites (airports and shopping centers) as spaces without identity, which highlights the value of places with memory.

## Limits of the Approach

The three approaches assume that sacred place has a single structure, although the boundaries of the sacred differ between Islam, which relies on the *qibla* and ritual boundary; Christianity, which sanctifies the altar and the church; and Hinduism, which sanctifies rivers and mountains. Some studies also overlook the ability of believers themselves to distinguish between the sanctity of a place and the sanctity of God. Finally, the study of place requires field and archaeological data that is not always available.
