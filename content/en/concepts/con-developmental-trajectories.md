# Developmental Trajectories

## Definition

This concept studies how psychiatric disorders arise and change over the life span: from early biological and environmental vulnerabilities, to prominent symptoms in childhood, adolescence, or adulthood, and then to paths of persistence, recovery, or transition to another disorder. It is the core of what is called "developmental psychopathology," which rejects the separation of the normal from the pathological and explains both by the same laws of development.

## Theoretical Foundations

Rutter and Sroufe presented the concepts and problems of this field in *Development and Psychopathology* in 2000, including the point that a single child goes through transformations that produce either continuity or discontinuity. In 1996, in the same journal, Cicchetti and Rogosch introduced two concepts: "multifinality," meaning that a single risk factor may lead to different disorders, and "equifinality," meaning that different paths may end in the same disorder. Kessler and colleagues reported in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 2005, from a United States household survey, that half of all lifetime cases of DSM-IV disorders start by age fourteen and three-quarters by age twenty-four, which made adolescence a key window for research and intervention.

## Specific Trajectories

Terrie Moffitt presented in *Psychological Review* in 1993 a model of antisocial behavior on two paths: a "life-course-persistent" path, which begins early with neuropsychological deficit and harsh upbringing, and an "adolescence-limited" path, linked to social imitation. In schizophrenia, Murray and Lewis in the *British Medical Journal* in 1987, and Weinberger in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 1987, formulated the "neurodevelopmental hypothesis," which holds that the disorder begins with an early disturbance in brain development and appears upon maturity. Caspi and colleagues reported in *Science* in 2003 an interaction between a variant of the serotonin transporter gene and stressful life events in predicting depression, a finding that later meta-analyses and large studies have not consistently replicated. In *Clinical Psychological Science* in 2014, Caspi and colleagues proposed the "p factor," meaning that the overall severity of psychiatric illness follows a general factor.

## The Path in the Arab Medical Heritage

Medieval Arab medicine divided life into stages (childhood, youth, adulthood, old age, and senility) according to the dominance of temperaments, and linked mental illnesses such as melancholia to the balance of temperaments (humors) and to age. Equating this view with a modern developmental model is not valid, but it shows the presence of the idea of age and change in older understanding (see the entry on Temperament and Risk Factors).

## Therapeutic Implications

Understanding trajectories guides preventive intervention: early detection of anxiety and disturbed behavior, support for secure attachment, parenting programs (see the entry on Attachment Theory & Development), and early psychosis services. Individual prediction, however, is weak: most children with risk factors do not develop disorders, and most who do develop them show no clear signs in advance.

## Limits

The results depend on longitudinal studies, which are difficult to conduct, and on samples that are mainly Western. The molecular and social mechanisms mediating between early risk and later disorder remain incomplete.
