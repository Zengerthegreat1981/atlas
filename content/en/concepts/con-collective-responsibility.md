# Collective Responsibility

Collective responsibility is the attribution of blame or moral desert to a group of individuals (a nation, an institution, a generation, a party) as a single entity, rather than to each member individually on the basis of his own direct personal act. The idea raises a fundamental philosophical problem: can a group be a moral agent in its own right, with a will and consciousness for which it can be held accountable? Or is responsibility valid only for the rational, free individual?

In Islamic jurisprudence, the seeds of the distinction between individual and collective responsibility appear in the concept of communal obligation (*fard al-kifaya*), whereby the sin falls on the whole community if it is discharged by those who are sufficient for it. They also appear in the principle "no bearer of burdens shall bear the burden of another," which entrenches strict individual responsibility. This makes the Islamic heritage closer to rejecting the transfer of sin across a group, although it acknowledges a solidary responsibility in public affairs, such as enjoining good. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah*, approached the group (*asabiyya*, group solidarity) as a historical agent whose actions and outcomes are attributed to it as a whole, although he did not formulate this within a normative moral framework.

In contemporary Western philosophy, H. D. Lewis ("Collective Responsibility," 1948) defended a strictly individual view of responsibility that opposes any transfer of guilt. Peter French, by contrast, held that institutions, such as corporations, can be moral persons with an internal decision structure that makes them responsible in their own right, rather than being mere aggregates of individuals. Larry May developed the idea of "shared responsibility" based on participation in a social fabric that produces harm, as in cases of structural discrimination in which there is no single direct agent but the group is enabled to act or refrain from acting collectively. Hannah Arendt, in her essay "Collective Responsibility" (written 1968), distinguished between collective guilt, which she rejected as morally incoherent, and the collective political responsibility borne by everyone who belongs to a political community for the actions of its government, even if they did not participate in them directly.

The idea faces strong objections. The most prominent is that imposing on an individual a burden for an act he did not choose himself (such as blaming an entire generation for the crimes of its ancestors) violates the principle of individual justice and the principle that moral desert is conditional on will and choice. Its defenders reply that there is a difference between "blame," which remains individual, and "responsibility" in the remedial or compensatory sense, which can be collective. An example is a state's commitment to compensate the victims of a previous colonial rule without every current citizen having taken part in the act.

For example, when an entire company is held accountable for an environmental disaster resulting from accumulated administrative decisions, responsibility is attributed to the institutional entity itself. It is later translated into penalties and compensation that concern the group rather than any single individual.

The debate gains importance in intergenerational issues, such as responsibility for climate change, where earlier generations contributed emissions whose burden is borne by later generations. It also arises in transitional justice after civil wars or authoritarian regimes, where truth and reconciliation commissions are proposed as a middle formula. Such commissions acknowledge symbolic collective responsibility without assigning individual criminal guilt to each member. The question remains open as to whether traditional individual ethical frameworks can absorb these complex phenomena without dismantling the idea of the rational, responsible agent.
