# Ikhtilaf (Juristic Disagreement)

**Juristic disagreement** (*ikhtilaf*) is the multiplicity of rulings and opinions among jurists on the same question, resulting from their differences in understanding the text, in weighing the evidence, or in establishing whether the ruling's ground (*manat*) applies to the case. Disagreement is a structural phenomenon inherent in any legal system that relies on human juristic reasoning to derive its rulings from texts that admit more than one understanding. Muslim usulists developed a specific theory to understand this phenomenon and to define its legitimate limits.

## Is Every Mujtahid Correct?

The usulists were divided on a central theological question: does every juristic question have **one correct ruling in God's sight**, which some mujtahids miss and others hit (the position of the **"Mukhati'a"** or "those who hold that the mujtahid may err")? Or is **every mujtahid correct** according to his own reasoning, such that no single ruling is fixed in advance and the Sharia ruling takes shape in light of each mujtahid's reasoning (the position of the **"Musawwiba"** or "those who hold that every mujtahid is right")? Both sides nonetheless generally agreed that a qualified mujtahid who errs (on the supposition that error exists) bears no sin and has a reward for his effort, on the basis of a well-known prophetic report that a judge who strives and is correct has two rewards, and one if he errs. This practical agreement is what allowed the various juristic schools to coexist without mutual denunciation.

## The Etiquette of Disagreement and Its Legitimate Limits

Jurists developed what may be called "the etiquette of disagreement" (*adab al-ikhtilaf*): a set of criteria that distinguish acceptable disagreement, which falls within the recognized limits of juristic reasoning (such as the disagreement of the four schools on many subsidiary matters), from unacceptable departure from definitive consensus or from the contravention of an explicit text that admits no room for juristic reasoning. Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728/1328) wrote a dedicated treatise entitled *Raf' al-Malam 'an al-A'imma al-A'lam* ("Lifting Blame from the Great Imams"), in which he defended the breadth of this legitimate margin of disagreement. He explained that many of the imams who are followed may err in a juristic question without this removing them from the circle of uprightness and standing, so long as their reasoning has met its methodological conditions. Elsewhere Ibn Taymiyya distinguished disagreement of diversity (*ikhtilaf tanawwu'*), where both views are permissible, from disagreement of contradiction (*ikhtilaf tadadd*). The genre of comparative juristic disagreement is also well represented in classical literature, for example in al-Tabari's *Ikhtilaf al-Fuqaha'* and Ibn Rushd's *Bidayat al-Mujtahid wa-Nihayat al-Muqtasid* (d. 595/1198).

## Its Use in the Debate on Juristic Plurality and Contemporary Ijtihad

Modern reform thought invokes the theory of legitimate disagreement in the context of the debate on **ijtihad versus taqlid** (imitation). Some renewers argue that the historical recognition of the legitimacy of multiple juristic opinions opens the way for new contemporary juristic reasoning that responds to the questions of the age, provided it remains committed to the same usuli method and does not depart from it. From another direction, contemporary scholars such as Khaled Abou El Fadl employ the concept of disagreement in their critique of what he calls "authoritarian" interpretation of the sharia. He emphasizes that acknowledging a long history of legitimate juristic disagreement undermines any contemporary reading's claim to monopolize the one correct understanding of the text.

## Impact and Critique

Juristic disagreement is among the phenomena most indicative of the human, interpretive character of the process of deriving rulings in Islamic jurisprudence. It reflects this character rather than ambiguity or contradiction in the Sharia text itself, according to the classical understanding. Nonetheless, scholars caution that the saying "the disagreement of my community is a mercy," which is often cited in this context, is not established as a sound prophetic report according to most hadith scholars; it is often described as having no sound chain of transmission. The genuine usuli basis for the legitimacy of disagreement rests on other evidence (such as the authentic hadith on the reward of the mujtahid who errs), not on this popularly known hadith.

## Arguments and Attributions in the Taswib Debate

Fallibilists argued that contradictory verdicts, such as permission and prohibition of the same act in the same circumstances, cannot both express the truth; that scholarly argument would be pointless if nothing were there to be found; and that the report of two rewards and one presupposes that a judge can miss. They also cited the account of David and Solomon (Qur'an 21:78-79), in which understanding of a case is credited to Solomon.

Infallibilists answered that where no decisive evidence exists, God requires of each mujtahid only what appears most probable to him, so that this is the ruling for him. They noted that the same Qur'anic passage credits both prophets with judgment and knowledge, that the Companions tolerated one another's differing verdicts, and that error in the report may concern the facts or an overlooked text.

Infallibilism is associated especially with the theologian al-Baqillani, with al-Ghazali in *al-Mustasfa* for questions lacking a decisive text, and with many Basran Mu'tazilis such as Abu Ali al-Jubba'i and his son Abu Hashim; reports about al-Ash'ari are less uniform. Many jurists, including the Shafi'i Abu Ishaq al-Shirazi, held that only one answer is correct, as did Imami Shi'i usulists.
