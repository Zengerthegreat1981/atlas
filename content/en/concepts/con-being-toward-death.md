# Being-Toward-Death

*Being-toward-death* (German *Sein zum Tode*) is a central concept in Martin Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927), developed mainly in Division II, chapter 1 (sections 46-53). It holds that Dasein, the kind of being that each of us is, can understand itself as a whole, and live authentically, only by relating honestly to its own death as an ever-present possibility, not as a distant event that merely happens at the end of life.

## Death as a Possibility, Not an Event

Heidegger distinguishes between three things: *perishing* (*Verenden*), the biological ending of a living thing; *demise* (*Ableben*), the point at which Dasein ceases to be; and *dying* in the existential sense, the way Dasein relates to its end throughout its life. Being-toward-death concerns the third. Death, in his analysis, is not something that is simply "not yet" present, like an unpaid bill. It belongs to Dasein's being at every moment, because Dasein is always facing the possibility that it may no longer be.

He characterises death as Dasein's ownmost possibility, which is non-relational (no one can die my death for me), not to be outstripped, certain, and yet indefinite as to when. In a well-known phrase, it is "the possibility of the absolute impossibility of Dasein."

## Inauthentic and Authentic Relations to Death

In everyday life, Heidegger argues, people evade death by absorbing it into the anonymous "they" (*das Man*): "one dies," as an event that happens to others and will happen eventually. Idle talk and consolation tend to cover over the fact that death is mine alone. The authentic stance is not brooding or a wish to die, but what he calls *anticipation* (*Vorlaufen*), a lucid holding-oneself-open to death as one's own possibility. Anticipation is linked to anxiety (*Angst*), which discloses that one's existence is finite and without ultimate guarantee, and to resoluteness (*Entschlossenheit*), in which one takes up one's life as one's own. Heidegger holds that this awareness frees Dasein from dispersal in trivial concerns and lets it see its possibilities in their seriousness.

## How It Differs from Ordinary Fear of Death

It is not fear of a future event like other fears (see the entry on fear). For Heidegger, fear has a definite object in the world, whereas the anxiety connected with death has no particular object. Being-toward-death is a structure that shapes how every present moment is lived, not a feeling about an eventual end.

## Reception and Objections

Jean-Paul Sartre, in *Being and Nothingness* (1943), rejected Heidegger's idea, arguing that death is not "my ownmost possibility" but an external fact that cancels all my possibilities and so cannot give life its meaning. Emmanuel Levinas criticised the primacy of one's own death, arguing that the death of the other person is ethically more fundamental. Ernst Tugendhat and others asked whether Heidegger's account of authenticity has enough ethical content. Others question whether his analysis is a description of human existence or a normative ideal in disguise, and how far it depends on his wider ontology.

## Relation to Other Concepts in the Atlas

The concept has thematic links with later work on mortality, including existential psychotherapy (for example Irvin Yalom's treatment of death anxiety), and with the research tradition of terror management theory and measures such as the Death Attitude Profile-Revised. These are not applications of Heidegger: terror management theory draws mainly on Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death* (1973), and the empirical instruments measure attitudes, not Heideggerian structures. The link is a shared theme, not a derivation.
