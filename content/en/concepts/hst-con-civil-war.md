# Civil War and Internal Conflict

In classical Greek and Roman historiography, civil war and internal civic discord—designated as ***stasis*** in Greek and ***bellum civile*** in Latin—were conceptualized not simply as one military category among others, but as the supreme existential crisis, moral pathology, and structural dissolution of the political community (*polis* or *res publica*). Classical historians treated civil conflict with profound philosophical urgency, analyzing how factional extremism destroys ancestral laws, corrupts moral language, and perverts civic solidarity into suicidal fratricide.

## Thucydides and the Pathology of Greek *Stasis*

The foundational diagnosis of civil war in ancient literature appears in Thucydides' analysis of the revolution at Corcyra (427 BCE) in Book 3 (3.81–84) of his *History of the Peloponnesian War*. Thucydides portrayed *stasis* as an acute social fever that strips away the civilized veneer of civic life under the pressure of wartime hardship:
- **The Inversion of Moral Language**: Thucydides famously described how civil factionalism systematically subverts ethical vocabulary: reckless aggression became praised as courageous loyalty to party; prudent hesitation was branded as cowardly unmanliness; fanatical violence was viewed as the hallmark of a true man; and moderation was decried as a faint-hearted pretext for treason.
- **Faction over Family and Law**: Party loyalty (*hetaireia*) superseded familial bonds and statutory oaths. Factions formed not for the public benefit in accordance with established laws, but in rapacious greed to acquire power through violent constitutional overthrow.

Plato (*Republic* 5.470) drew a related distinction in classical political philosophy: while external conflict against foreign barbarians constituted legitimate war (*polemos*), violent struggle among fellow citizens was unnatural, diseased discord (*stasis*).

## Roman Historiography of *Bellum Civile*

Roman historical consciousness was profoundly traumatized by the century of catastrophic civil strife (133–31 BCE) that brought down the Roman Republic:
- **The Gracchi to Sulla**: Roman historians dated the collapse of civic harmony (*concordia*) to the tribunate of Tiberius Gracchus (133 BCE), when political disagreement was first settled with clubs and blood in the Forum. In *Bellum Catilinae* and *Bellum Iugurthinum*, Sallust diagnosed the socio-moral decay following the fall of Carthage (146 BCE), arguing that the removal of foreign threat (*metus hostilis*) unleashed unchecked aristocratic avarice and partisan ambition (*partes*), culminating in Sulla's bloody civil war and murderous proscriptions.
- **Caesar's *De Bello Civili***: Julius Caesar composed his own commentarii on the civil war of 49–48 BCE against Pompey and the senatorial majority. Caesar adopted a calculated, third-person pose of dispassionate legalism, asserting that he crossed the Rubicon with immense reluctance solely to protect his constitutional standing (*dignitas*) and defend the sacrosanct rights of the tribunes of the plebs against an intransigent senatorial faction (*factio paucorum*).
- **The Tragic Vision of Lucan and Appian**: In the early Empire, the poet-historian Lucan opened his epic *Pharsalia* (also titled *De Bello Civili*) with the famous lament for "wars worse than civil" (*bella per Emathios plus quam civilia campos*), depicting the struggle between Caesar and Pompey as the cosmic, self-inflicted suicide of the Roman world and the tragic demise of republican liberty (*libertas*). In the second century CE, Appian of Alexandria devoted five books of his *Roman History* (*Romaika*) to the Civil Wars, methodically tracing how small factional disputes progressively escalated into massive mercenary armies fighting across the Mediterranean.
- **Tacitus and the Year of the Four Emperors**: In the *Historiae*, Cornelius Tacitus anatomized the chaotic civil wars of 68–69 CE following the death of Nero, revealing the terrifying secret of empire (*arcanum imperii*): that emperors could be created outside of Rome by provincial legions driven by promises of plunder.

## Historiographical Significance

The ancient anatomy of civil conflict established lasting paradigms for analyzing political polarization, the militarization of domestic politics, the collapse of constitutional norms, and the weaponization of language during democratic breakdown.
