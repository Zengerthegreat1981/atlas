# Architecture

In anthropology, architecture is the study of the built environment as a cultural product: houses, temples, villages, cities, and monuments understood not merely as shelter or engineering, but as material expressions of social order, cosmology, power, and everyday life. Where art history often treats buildings as works of individual genius, anthropology asks how people make, inhabit, and give meaning to space — how a floor plan encodes kinship, how a doorway marks the boundary between the sacred and the profane, and how a city embodies a political vision. Architecture, in this sense, is a social fact made durable in stone, timber, mud, and concrete.

## Origins and Development

Anthropological interest in architecture began with the great nineteenth-century surveys of vernacular building. Lewis Henry Morgan, in *Houses and House-Life of the American Indians* (1881), argued that the longhouse and the pueblo were not primitive shelters but precise diagrams of kinship and communal life. The house, for Morgan, was the physical form of the social group that lived in it. A generation later, the French sociologist Émile Durkheim and his nephew Marcel Mauss showed in *Primitive Classification* (1903) that spatial organisation — the layout of a camp or a village — often mirrors the classification of the social world, a theme Mauss and Henri Beuchat pursued in their essay on seasonal variation among the Inuit (1904–05).

The field matured in the twentieth century when anthropologists began to study building as a process rather than a finished object. Amos Rapoport's *House Form and Culture* (1969) demonstrated that climate and materials explain far less about why houses look the way they do than do cultural values, religious beliefs, and family structure. Pierre Bourdieu's study of the Kabyle house in Algeria, first published as an essay around 1970 and taken up again in *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1977), showed how the interior of a dwelling maps a whole cosmology — light and dark, male and female, human and animal — and how moving through the house daily rehearses that cosmology in the body. By the 1990s, the anthropology of space had become a recognised subfield, consolidated in Setha Low and Denise Lawrence-Zúñiga's *The Anthropology of Space and Place* (2003), which treated place-making as a central human activity.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One enduring debate concerns determinism: does the built environment shape social life, or merely reflect it? Early functionalists tended to read buildings as reflections of society, while later scholars argued for a two-way relationship. Tim Ingold, in *The Perception of the Environment* (2000), rejected the idea of architecture as the imposition of mental design onto passive matter, arguing instead that building is a form of dwelling — an ongoing engagement between people and their surroundings. A house is never finished; it is continually repaired, extended, and reinterpreted.

A second dimension is power. Monumental architecture — pyramids, cathedrals, palaces, colonial administrative buildings — has long been read as a technology of domination, making hierarchy visible and permanent. Yet anthropologists also document how ordinary people resist imposed designs: squatters reshape planned housing, residents personalise uniform apartment blocks, and ritual practice reclaims spaces built for other purposes. The study of architecture thus connects the grand scale of the state with the intimate scale of the home.

A third dimension is gender and the body. Feminist anthropology has shown that domestic space is rarely neutral: who cooks where, who sleeps where, and who may enter which room are questions of authority as much as of convenience. The division of space within a household often reproduces, or quietly contests, wider inequalities of gender and generation.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Today the anthropology of architecture speaks directly to urbanisation, displacement, and climate change. More than half of humanity now lives in cities, and anthropologists study informal settlements, refugee camps, and gated communities as architectures of inequality and survival. Heritage debates — whose buildings are preserved, whose are demolished — reveal how the past is politically mobilised in the present. Meanwhile, the climate crisis has revived interest in vernacular building techniques: mud brick, timber framing, and passive cooling, long dismissed as backward, are now studied as sophisticated, sustainable knowledge.

Critics within the discipline warn against romanticising vernacular architecture or treating buildings as transparent texts that can simply be "read." Buildings are ambiguous, contested, and often built by people whose intentions are lost or multiple. The strongest contemporary work therefore combines ethnography of lived experience with attention to materials, labour, and political economy — asking not only what buildings mean, but who built them, at whose cost, and for whom.
