# Metaphor

Metaphor is the use of one thing to understand, describe, or think about another. When people speak of the "head" of a household, the "root" of a problem, or a "stormy" relationship, they borrow language from one domain — the body, plants, weather — and apply it to another. Metaphor is not decorative language added to plain speech. It is a basic way that human beings think: we grasp abstract or unfamiliar things through concrete and familiar ones. For anthropology, metaphor matters because it reveals how people in different cultures organise their experience of the world, what they take to be similar to what, and how those likenesses guide action, ritual, and social life.

## Definition

A metaphor links two domains: a source domain, which is relatively concrete, and a target domain, which is relatively abstract. In "argument is war", the source domain of combat supplies the vocabulary — attack, defend, win, lose — through which people think about argument. I. A. Richards, writing in 1936, called the two parts of a metaphor the tenor, the idea being expressed, and the vehicle, the image carrying it, and stressed that meaning emerges from their interaction rather than from simple substitution. Aristotle had already observed in the Poetics, composed around 335 BCE, that making good metaphors requires perceiving likeness in unlike things, a mark of intelligence. Modern cognitive linguistics, above all the work of George Lakoff and Mark Johnson, argues that metaphor is primarily conceptual rather than linguistic: people do not merely speak metaphorically, they think metaphorically, and entire systems of metaphor structure how a culture understands time, morality, emotion, and the mind itself.

## Origins and Development

For much of the twentieth century, metaphor was treated as a problem for literary criticism and rhetoric, a figure of speech to be classified and admired. Max Black's work in 1962 shifted the discussion by arguing that metaphor creates meaning rather than decorating it: calling a person a "wolf" does not merely substitute one word for another, it reorganises how we see the person. The cognitive turn of 1980, when Lakoff and Johnson published Metaphors We Live By, made metaphor a central topic in the human sciences. Anthropologists took up the idea with enthusiasm. James Fernandez, in his 1986 study of persuasion and performance, argued that metaphor is the core of religious and political rhetoric: leaders move people by offering compelling metaphors that make new social arrangements feel natural. Ethnographers documented metaphor systems in which kinship is understood through the body, the state through the family, and illness through invasion or imbalance. Metaphor became a window onto cultural worlds.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns universality and variation. Some metaphors appear widely — the body as a source for social organisation, up as good and down as bad — because they rest on shared bodily experience. Others are strikingly culture-specific, such as the metaphors of heat and coolness that organise moral life in parts of West Africa. A second debate concerns the difference between metaphor and metonymy: metaphor works by similarity, linking unlike things, while metonymy works by contiguity, substituting something associated with a thing for the thing itself. Roman Jakobson argued in 1956 that these are the two poles of language, with some styles and cultures favouring one over the other. A third debate concerns power. Metaphors are not neutral: to describe migrants as a "flood" or the market as a "natural force" shapes what responses seem reasonable. Who controls the dominant metaphors of a society controls, to a degree, how its problems are understood.

## Contemporary Relevance

Metaphor remains a live tool for anthropology because it connects language, thought, and social action. In medical anthropology, the metaphors patients and doctors use for illness — battle, journey, breakdown — shape diagnosis, treatment, and the experience of suffering. In political anthropology, metaphors of the nation as a family, a body, or a house reveal how belonging and exclusion are imagined. In the study of science and technology, metaphors of the brain as a computer or the genome as a code shape research agendas and public understanding. Contemporary research also examines how metaphors travel across languages and media, and how new technologies generate new metaphorical resources. Metaphor endures as a concept because it names something fundamental: human beings do not think in abstractions alone, but by carrying meaning from the familiar to the unfamiliar, and in doing so they build the worlds they inhabit.
