# Hope Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Hope ethics (Hope Ethics) is the conception that treats active hope for the improvement of conditions, combined with serious effort toward that improvement, as a moral virtue. It helps a person endure patiently, act, and avoid deadly despair or surrender to despondency. Hope thus serves as a motive for action rather than a substitute for it.

## Historical Context

In the Islamic tradition, hope appears above all in the concept of *raja'* (hope in God's mercy), one of the pillars on which the psychological balance of the believer rests. The Holy Qur'an warned against despairing of God's mercy in the words: "Do not despair of the mercy of God." Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, elaborated the dialectical relationship between fear and hope, regarding them as "two wings" with which the heart must fly together. If hope alone prevails, it leads to laxity and a sense of false security before God's plan. If fear alone prevails, it leads to despondency and despair. Moderation between them is the ideal state. Ibn al-Qayyim al-Jawziyya, in *Madarij al-Salikin*, distinguished true hope (*raja'*), which is accompanied by effort and obedience, from mere wishful thinking (*tamanni*).

In Western philosophy, the German philosopher Ernst Bloch treated hope in his book *The Principle of Hope* (*Das Prinzip Hoffnung*). He argued that hope is not merely a passive emotion but an anticipatory orientation toward a not-yet-realized better future, which he called the "utopian function," pushing people toward envisioning and working toward a better world. The French philosopher Gabriel Marcel, in works such as *Homo Viator* (1944), distinguished between superficial "optimism," which assumes an easy solution, and deep existential "hope," which endures even in the darkest circumstances without denying the difficulty of reality. He regarded hope as a spiritual virtue that shows itself most clearly in confronting suffering rather than in its absence.

## Objections and Example

It is objected that exaggerating hope may slide into an illusion that paralyzes real action or denies difficult facts. Most ethicists, however, distinguish between "active hope," which is coupled with work, and blameworthy "passive wishing." *Illustrative example:* a patient who faces a difficult diagnosis but maintains hope of recovery while committing to a treatment program, as opposed to someone who surrenders to despair and abandons treatment entirely. The difference between them shows the effect of practical hope in perseverance and action.

## Hope in the Face of Collective Adversity

The importance of hope as a virtue increases when it extends from the individual to the community, as in great crises, wars and disasters. There, preserving collective hope becomes a condition for the survival of society itself and for the continuation of its efforts toward reform. Bloch argued that hope for a better future is a recurrent driver of social movements and utopian imagination. This makes hope a historically effective force rather than merely an individual psychological state. At the same time, a warning is issued against confusing this authentic hope with propagandistic optimism, which is sometimes used to lull people into not confronting their real problems. The essential difference remains in whether virtuous hope is joined to serious work or to empty promises.
