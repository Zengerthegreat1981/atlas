# Culture as a Toolkit

The idea that culture is a varied repertoire of symbols, stories and rituals that individuals use with selective flexibility to build practical strategies of action, and not a coherent system of values executed literally in every situation.

## The Central Idea

In "Culture in Action" (1986), Ann Swidler put forward a critique of the traditional conception of culture as a coherent system of values that directly and deterministically guides individuals' behavior. In its place she proposed the conception of "culture as a toolkit": a varied and sometimes contradictory repertoire of symbols, stories, rituals and worldviews, which individuals use with selective flexibility to build practical "strategies of action" suited to their particular life situations. She distinguished between "settled lives" (or periods), in which culture is taken for granted and shapes action only indirectly through available habits and skills, and "unsettled lives," in which people are building new strategies of action (as in periods of social upheaval) and explicit ideologies play a larger, more direct role.

## Distinction from the Parsonian Model of Values

Swidler directed her critique squarely at the Parsonian model, which assumes that internalized values guide behavior almost automatically and consistently across all situations. She argued instead, drawing on existing ethnographic and historical studies, that individuals often hold apparently contradictory values (such as belief in individualism alongside belief in collective commitment) without this contradiction forming a practical obstacle. This is because individuals do not literally "execute" their values but "select" from their cultural repertoire what suits the present situation and justify their choice afterwards in the language of the values available to them.

## Impact

This framework became one of the most widely used conceptual tools in contemporary sociology of culture, and it deeply influenced studies of identity, social movements and religion. Later researchers extended it, such as Omar Lizardo, who distinguished explicit cultural knowledge that can be consciously verbalized from implicit, unconscious bodily skills, and Wendy Griswold, who placed it within her more comprehensive "cultural diamond" framework to locate cultural tools within the full cycle of the production and reception of meaning.

## Field Applications

Swidler herself later applied this framework in her interview study of love and marriage in America (*Talk of Love*, 2001), based on interviews with Americans in the San Francisco Bay Area. She showed how people draw on two cultural models of love, a "mythic" one (a single, all-encompassing and enduring bond) and a "prosaic-realistic" one (love as a practical relationship that takes work), using each as the situation demands without needing to reconcile them.

## Related Work on Taste

Richard Peterson and Roger Kern (1996) reported that high-status Americans' musical taste had shifted from exclusive "snob" preferences toward "omnivorous" tastes spanning highbrow and popular genres. This work is usually discussed alongside Swidler's because both treat cultural resources as a flexibly used repertoire rather than a fixed value system, though Peterson and Kern's argument concerns the patterning of taste and not the theory of action.
