# Cultural Competence in Psychiatry

## Definition

Cultural competence in psychiatry is the ability of the physician and the institution to assess and treat a patient with attention to his cultural, religious, and linguistic background, so that the expression of pain is not read by the standard of a single culture. It goes beyond providing an interpreter. It affects how the diagnosis is framed, the meaning of the illness for the patient, and the patient's expectations of treatment.

## An Ethical Origin in the Arab Medical Heritage

Medieval Arab medicine cared about the ethics of dealing with the patient. A well-known text on this is *Adab al-Tabib* (The Physician's Ethics) by Ishaq ibn Ali al-Ruhawi (ninth century), which Myron Levey translated and studied in *Transactions of the American Philosophical Society* in 1967. These works emphasize the physician's patience with the patient and attention to his condition, status, and creed. In the tenth century, Abu Bakr al-Razi wrote *Man la yahduruhu al-tabib* for travelers, the poor, and those who had no physician at hand, that is, a treatment suited to people's circumstances and the resources available to them. These are not "cultural competence" in the modern sense, but they show that sensitivity to the patient's context is an old professional value.

## Modern Formulation

The idea emerged in medical anthropology. Kleinman, Eisenberg, and Good published an article in *Annals of Internal Medicine* in 1978 on the clinical lessons of anthropology and cross-cultural research, in which they proposed asking the patient about his "explanatory model" of his illness (see the entry on Kleinman's Explanatory Models). Cross, Bazron, Dennis, and Isaacs issued in 1989, from the Georgetown Child Development Center, the document *Towards a Culturally Competent System of Care*, which provided the widely circulated institutional definition. Derald Wing Sue, Arredondo, and McDavis set out multicultural counseling competencies in the *Journal of Counseling & Development* in 1992, along three dimensions: awareness of one's own bias, knowledge of the client's worldview, and possession of appropriate skills. Stanley Sue, reviewing cultural competence in psychotherapy and counseling in *American Psychologist* in 1998, proposed scientific mindedness, dynamic-sizing skills, and culture-specific expertise as its ingredients. In 2013 the DSM-5 added the "Cultural Formulation Interview," whose guide was edited by Lewis-Fernández and others (American Psychiatric Publishing, 2016).

## Critique of the Concept and Its Alternative

In 1998 Tervalon and Murray-García criticized the idea of "competence" in the *Journal of Health Care for the Poor and Underserved* for implying a final body of knowledge that can be completed. They preferred "cultural humility": continuous self-reflection and a rebalancing of power relations between physician and patient. In 2012 Kirmayer reviewed the concept in *Transcultural Psychiatry*, warning against turning culture into fixed lists that reinforce stereotypes (such as the statement that "Arabs do such and such"), and calling for attention to internal variation, social conditions, and discrimination.

## In the Arab Context

Al-Krenawi and Graham studied culturally sensitive practice with Arab clients in *Health & Social Work* in 2000. They discussed the importance of the extended family and traditional healers, the frequent physical expression of distress, and how honor and family intersect with the decision to seek treatment, while cautioning against treating Arab clients as a single group. The physician needs to understand local expressions of distress, and the role of religion, which may be a resource for recovery and may also be a source of guilt (see the entry on Healing Traditions).

## Limits

Short training courses do not guarantee a change in practice, and there is no strong evidence that they consistently improve patient outcomes. Critics fear that cultural competence may turn into an administrative procedure while structural disparities in access to care remain unaddressed.
