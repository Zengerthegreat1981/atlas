# Justice in Healthcare

Justice in healthcare is a branch of applied moral philosophy. It examines how limited health resources (medicines, hospital beds, transplantable organs, and medical time) should be distributed fairly among members of society, within the limits of every person's right to access care regardless of their wealth or social standing.

## Meaning and Definition

The question of health justice arises when medical needs compete for resources that cannot satisfy everyone at once. Who is placed first on an organ transplant waiting list? Is the younger person treated first, the person in greatest need, or the person who arrived first? Should health spending be determined by calculating the greatest possible benefit to society as a whole, or by each individual's right to a minimum level of care?

## In the Islamic Tradition

Islamic jurisprudence rests on the preservation of life (*hifz al-nafs*), one of the five necessities (*darurriyyat*) that al-Shatibi set out in *al-Muwafaqat* as the foundation of legislation. From this it was derived that providing treatment to a person in need is an obligation of collective duty (*fard kifaya*), which falls on the community as a whole rather than on the individual alone. Jurists affirmed the principle of giving priority to the person in greatest need and entitlement when rights conflict, which modern writers call the "jurisprudence of priorities" (*fiqh al-awlawiyyat*), together with the legal maxim that a greater harm may be removed by a lesser one. Al-Ghazali, in *al-Mustasfa*, had earlier listed the preservation of life among the necessary objectives of the Shari'a, ranking it far above considerations of convenience.

## In Western Philosophy

John Rawls, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), offered a general framework of distributive justice based on the "difference principle," which permits inequality only if it benefits the worst-off. Norman Daniels extended this framework to healthcare in *Just Health Care* (1985) and *Just Health* (2008), arguing, as Rawls himself did not, that healthcare is a necessary condition for equality of opportunity, since a sick individual cannot compete for equal social opportunities with healthy people. In the principlist framework of Beauchamp and Childress, justice is one of four core principles of biomedical ethics, alongside autonomy, beneficence and nonmaleficence. By contrast, utilitarian approaches call for allocating health resources so as to maximize quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) at the level of society as a whole, even if this means denying some individuals an expensive treatment of limited collective benefit.

## Applications

The issue appears in practice in medical triage protocols during epidemics and disasters, in drug pricing and health insurance coverage policies, and in priority lists for organ transplantation, which usually combine criteria of medical need, the chance of success, and the length of the waiting period.

## Example

During a pandemic in which the number of critically ill patients exceeds the number of available ventilators, medical committees must set explicit criteria for priority (such as the likelihood of survival and the expected duration of need for the device) rather than relying solely on a "first come, first served" rule. This has provoked sharp ethical debate about the fairness of these criteria and their impact on the most vulnerable groups.

## Rawls, Daniels, and a Court on Scarce Treatment

Rawls's account in *A Theory of Justice* is not a health-care statute. Its second principle allows social and economic inequalities only when offices are open under fair equality of opportunity and when the inequalities work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. Daniels takes that opportunity idea into medicine. In *Just Health Care* and again in *Just Health*, he argues that disease and disability shrink the range of plans a person can pursue, so a fair system owes people services that protect or restore a normal opportunity range. The claim is limited by resources. It does not say that every desired treatment is owed.

Courts have had to draw a similar line in public systems. In *Soobramoney v Minister of Health, KwaZulu-Natal* (Constitutional Court of South Africa, 27 November 1997), Thiagraj Soobramoney, who had irreversible chronic renal failure and was not a transplant candidate, asked the state hospital for ongoing dialysis. The Court, in a judgment by Chaskalson, held that repeated dialysis to prolong life in a chronic illness was not emergency medical treatment under section 27(3) of the Constitution. Access to health-care services under section 27 is a duty of progressive realisation within available resources. Addington Hospital had few machines and a policy that favoured patients who could be cured or who were eligible for a transplant. The Court declined to order treatment for every person in Soobramoney's position, and it said it would be slow to upset rational, good-faith allocation choices of political and medical authorities. The appeal was dismissed.
