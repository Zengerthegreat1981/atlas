# Moral Externalism

Moral externalism is a position in the philosophy of moral psychology holding that believing an action to be right or wrong does not, in itself, entail having a motive to act in accordance with that belief. Motivation is a separate ("external") element from the moral belief, and it requires an independent desire in order for the action to arise. According to externalists, a person can therefore sincerely believe that helping the poor is a moral duty and feel no motive at all to do so, if the appropriate accompanying desire is absent. This is known as the possibility of the "amoralist."

This position loosely parallels the Islamic debate about the relationship between knowledge and action. Muslim scholars of ethics and Sufism, such as al-Ghazali in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, observed that knowing the legal ruling is not sufficient by itself for uprightness, and that many sinners know the wrongness of sin with certainty without refraining from it. He stressed the difference between knowledge that remains merely theoretical and knowledge that is firmly internalized and moves the heart (a contrast often discussed with the Quranic grades of certainty, *'ilm al-yaqin* and *haqq al-yaqin*). He sought to explain the gap between belief and action, a gap that contemporary externalists agree exists, although they differ on how to explain it philosophically.

In contemporary analytic philosophy, externalists stand against "moral internalism," defended by Michael Smith and others, which holds that there is a necessary conceptual link between a sincere moral judgment and motivation. On that view, whoever judges an action to be right without being moved to perform it is either insincere in their judgment or suffering from an exceptional mental disorder. David Brink defended externalism about moral motivation, relying on the common intuition that there are people (such as those with psychopathy) who understand moral concepts correctly without being moved by them in the slightest. This is used as an argument against internalism, although how far psychopaths really grasp moral concepts is itself disputed.

Externalists face a central objection: they empty moral judgment of its distinctive practical character and make it closer to a purely theoretical judgment, with no more connection to action than a mathematical judgment has. It seems obvious, however, that moral judgment has a particular "action-guiding" quality that distinguishes it from purely descriptive judgments. Externalists respond that this quality can be explained by a usual causal link (not a conceptual, necessary one) between belief and desire, which arises through sound social upbringing, but is not a logical connection that cannot be broken.

*Example:* a person with psychopathy who can distinguish wrong acts from right ones with precise theoretical understanding, yet feels no inner restraint against committing them, is the extreme case on which externalists rely to demonstrate that belief can be separated from motivation.

This disagreement has practical consequences for moral education. If externalism is correct, teaching children theoretical moral principles is not sufficient on its own. The appropriate desires and feelings must be cultivated in parallel, through example and habituation. This brings the externalist position close to the classical educational emphasis that virtue requires training the affections rather than the intellect alone. This resonates with the method of Ibn Miskawayh and al-Ghazali in refining the soul through practical discipline rather than through theoretical instruction alone.
