# Power and Dependence in Exchange Networks (Cook & Willer)

A precise empirical and mathematical extension of Blau's analysis of power in dyadic exchange, explaining how an actor's position within a multi-party exchange network determines its actual bargaining power.

## Content

Richard Emerson's power-dependence theory (1962), building on Peter Blau's analysis of power arising from imbalance in dyadic exchange, was extended to whole, interconnected multi-party exchange networks by Karen Cook and Emerson (1978) and, in a related but distinct programme, by David Willer and colleagues (Elementary Theory and Network Exchange Theory). The central idea: an actor's bargaining power is determined not only by its direct relationship with a single exchange partner, but by its overall position within the structure of the entire network, specifically by the number of "alternatives" available to it if exchange with a particular partner fails. An actor has greater power the more exchange alternatives it has (other substitute partners within the network to whom it can turn with relative ease), while its potential partners have relatively fewer alternatives, which makes them more "dependent" on it than the reverse. Cook, Emerson and their collaborators, and Willer's group, conducted laboratory experiments on artificial exchange networks with pre-specified geometric designs (linear chains of three or more nodes, star structures with one center and several ends, closed circles) in order to test quantitative predictions about how the "profit" produced by the exchange process is actually distributed among the different positions within each network structure. In negotiated-exchange experiments, some positions ("strong positions," such as the middle of a three-node chain) obtained a far larger share of the total profit despite having nominal individual capacities identical to those of the other actors, while positions in a closed circle of equivalent actors obtained roughly equal shares. The two programmes differ in their predictions and in their treatment of exclusion and of the structure of the network: Cook and Emerson stress dependence and alternatives, while Willer's Elementary Theory models power through graph-theoretic position (the "GPI" index).

## Impact

This framework provided more precise, testable predictions than the general qualitative description in Homans and Blau, and linked microscopic social exchange theory to the broader social network analysis methodology, which is more mathematically and empirically rigorous. It deeply influenced the study of organizational power within companies, economic supply chains, and political alliance networks, since it explains why a party that appears weak by absolute resource standards can hold great bargaining power if it occupies a strategically sensitive position within the overall network structure.
