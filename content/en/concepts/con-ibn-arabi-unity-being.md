# Ibn Arabi's Unity of Being

A Sufi metaphysical doctrine holding that true existence is one and does not multiply, namely the existence of God alone, and that all created beings are manifestations (*tajalli*) of this absolute existence rather than independent existences in themselves. The doctrine is attributed to the Andalusian Sufi **Muhyi al-Din Ibn Arabi** (1165–1240 CE).

## Textual origins and terminology

Recent studies note that Ibn Arabi himself did not use the term "unity of being" (*wahdat al-wujud*) literally in his well-known works. The term is associated with his school, above all his disciple and stepson **Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi**, and with later commentators such as Abd al-Razzaq al-Kashani, and it was prominent in the polemic of **Ibn Taymiyya** (d. 1328) against the school; it functions as a systematic summary of the content of his teachings rather than a literal quotation from him. The two most important sources for Ibn Arabi's thought are his books *Bezels of Wisdom* (*Fusus al-Hikam*) and *The Meccan Openings* (*al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya*).

## Philosophical content

Ibn Arabi distinguishes between **absolute existence** (the existence of God in Himself, self-subsistent and in need of nothing else) and **delimited existence** (the existence of creatures, which is an accidental existence granted by God at every moment, not an independent self-existence). In this sense the world is **truly real** (it is not an illusion, as is sometimes mistakenly understood), but its reality is borrowed from the absolute divine existence that bestows existence at every instant, and it is not a reality subsisting in itself. This is linked to his concept of the "fixed entities" (*al-a'yan al-thabita*), the eternal forms of things in the divine knowledge before they appear in external existence, and to the theory of "self-disclosure" (*tajalli*), by which he explains how absolute existence appears in the many forms of creatures without being divided or multiplied in its essence.

## The theological problem

The doctrine raises a theological question about the relationship between Creator and creature. Does saying "unity of being" mean that God and the world are one thing? This would approach pantheism or incarnation (*hulul*), which many theologians, including Ibn Taymiyya, rejected strongly. Or does it mean only an existential dependence of the creature upon the Creator, with the distinction between them remaining, as defenders of Ibn Arabi such as William Chittick and Michel Chodkiewicz explain it? This disagreement, between Ibn Arabi's supporters and opponents (especially Ibn Taymiyya and his students), has been among the most heated questions in the history of Islamic Sufi and theological thought.

## Impact

The doctrine of unity of being spread through the later Sufi orders in the Islamic East and West, and influenced major thinkers and poets (such as al-Qunawi, Abd al-Karim al-Jili and, in Persian and Indian Sufi thought, Jami), while Ibn Arabi came to be known in the tradition as "the Greatest Shaykh" (*al-Shaykh al-Akbar*). Critical responses also arose, such as Ahmad Sirhindi's alternative doctrine of "unity of witnessing" (*wahdat al-shuhud*). It has remained a subject of continuous theological dispute to this day, between Salafi currents that reject it and Sufi currents that adopt it with varying interpretations.

## Caveats

- The fact that Ibn Arabi himself did not use the literal term makes attributing a complete doctrine under this name to him a matter of historical interpretation rather than direct quotation.
- Contemporary readings vary between treating him as a thoroughgoing ontological monist and interpreting his thought as a relational dependence that leaves the distinction between Creator and creature intact.
