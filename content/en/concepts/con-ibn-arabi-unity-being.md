# Ibn Arabi's Unity of Being

## Definition

The **Unity of Being** (*wahdat al-wujud*) is a foundational metaphysical doctrine in Islamic mysticism (*tasawwuf*), historically associated with the Andalusian master Muhyi al-Din Ibn 'Arabi (560–638 AH / 1165–1240 CE), known as *al-Shaykh al-Akbar* (the Greatest Master). The doctrine posits that true, absolute existence (*wujud*) is singular and belongs to God alone. All created phenomena do not possess an autonomous, independent ontological ground; rather, they are contingent determinations, loci of manifestation (*mazahir*), and perpetual self-disclosures (*tajalliyat*) of the one divine reality.

## Textual Origins and Terminology

While modern scholarship and classical Islamic doxography uniformly link *wahdat al-wujud* to Ibn 'Arabi, the literal compound phrase does not occur as a technical term in his principal writings—notably *Fusus al-Hikam* (The Bezels of Wisdom, 627 AH / 1229–1230 CE) or *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya* (The Meccan Openings). The concept was systematized into a formalized philosophical lexicon by his immediate disciples and commentators, most notably his stepson and chief disciple Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi (606–673 AH / 1210–1274 CE), Sa'id al-Din al-Farghani (d. c. 700 AH / 1300 CE), and 'Abd al-Razzaq al-Kashani (d. 730 AH / 1329 CE). It was subsequently consolidated as an umbrella term in the critical polemics of the Hanbali theologian Taqi al-Din Ibn Taymiyya (661–728 AH / 1263–1328 CE).

## Core Metaphysical Architecture

Ibn 'Arabi's ontology operates through several dialectical principles:
- **Absolute vs. Delimited Existence**: God is Absolute Being (*al-wujud al-mutlaq*), completely transcendent (*tanzih*) and unconditioned by any creaturely limitation. The world, by contrast, is delimited being (*al-wujud al-muqayyad*). The universe is not an illusion, but its reality is entirely dependent, receiving existence renewed at every breath (*tajdid al-khalq fi al-an*).
- **The Immutable Entities (*al-a'yan al-thabita*)**: These are the objects of divine knowledge, fixed by the most holy effusion (*al-fayd al-aqdas*) and not, at that level, externally existent. Their appearance in the world is referred to a second gift, the holy effusion (*al-fayd al-muqaddas*).
- **Transcendence and Immanence (*al-Jam' bayna al-Tanzih wa-l-Tashbih*)**: Ibn 'Arabi rejects both unqualified transcendence (which empties God of relationality) and anthropomorphic immanence (which equates creation with God). Instead, he formulates the paradox of "He / not He" (*huwa la huwa*): creation is God insofar as it displays divine attributes, yet it is utterly not God in its contingent reality.
- **The Perfect Human (*al-insan al-kamil*)**: The cosmic microcosm who integrates all divine names and attributes in comprehensive equilibrium, serving as the bridge (*barzakh*) through which divine reality perceives its own reflection in creation.

## Theological Reception and Enduring Controversies

The doctrine ignited intense theological disputes across Islamic history:
- **Accusations of Pantheism and Incarnationism**: Opponents, led by Ibn Taymiyya and later Burhan al-Din al-Biqa'i (d. 885 AH / 1480 CE), condemned *wahdat al-wujud* as a veiled form of monistic pantheism, heresy, or incarnationism (*hulul* and *ittihad*), arguing that it obliterated the ontological gulf between Creator and creature and undermined moral accountability under the Sharia.
- **Scholastic Defenses**: Distinguished jurists and mystics defended Ibn 'Arabi against charges of heresy. Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti (849–911 AH / 1445–1505 CE) authored *Tanbih al-Ghabi bi-Tabri'at Ibn 'Arabi* (Warning the Dullard Concerning the Vindication of Ibn 'Arabi), and Syrian scholar 'Abd al-Ghani al-Nabulsi (1050–1143 AH / 1641–1731 CE) wrote extensive commentaries affirming the doctrine's orthodox alignment with scriptural monotheism (*tawhid*).
- **Unity of Witnessing (*wahdat al-shuhud*)**: In South Asia, Naqshbandi reformer Ahmad Sirhindi (971–1034 AH / 1564–1624 CE) proposed *wahdat al-shuhud* as a corrective, maintaining that the experience of unicity is subjective to the mystic's consciousness (*shuhud*) rather than an objective identity of being (*wujud*).
