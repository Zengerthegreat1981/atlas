# Business Virtue

## Meaning and Definition

Business virtue (*Business Virtue*) is the application of Aristotelian virtue ethics to the fields of commerce, economics, and management. Under this view, ethical commercial activity is assessed not only in terms of compliance with external rules (such as the law or a company charter), but also in terms of forming a stable character in the economic agent, one that practices honesty, justice, and moderation in its dealings as firm dispositions rather than situational obligations. This approach is distinguished from utilitarian approaches (maximizing profit or general welfare) and deontological approaches (adherence to strict rules) by its focus on the "character" of the businessperson and the institution, rather than on isolated acts.

## Roots in Islamic Thought

Islamic law contains a detailed ethical framework for commerce. Jurists regarded honesty and trustworthiness as essential conditions for the validity of a transaction. A hadith states: "The honest and trustworthy merchant is with the prophets, the truthful, and the martyrs," an explicit link between personal virtue and eschatological standing. Al-Mawardi and Ibn Khaldun also discussed earning and livelihood in their works. Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' Ulum al-Din* (The Revival of the Religious Sciences), devoted a full section to the "etiquette of earning and livelihood," in which he warned against fraud, hoarding, and usury, and called for leniency in selling and for granting time to the debtor who is in difficulty. In *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (The Refinement of Character), Ibn Miskawayh treated justice and liberality as virtues bound up with moderating the appetitive faculty, and greed as the corresponding vice, an outlook that can be applied to commercial conduct.

## Western Roots

This approach was recently inspired by the revival of virtue ethics led by Elizabeth Anscombe and Alasdair MacIntyre (*After Virtue*, 1981). Robert Solomon applied this framework directly to the business world in *Ethics and Excellence* (1992), calling for the building of a "community" within the firm in which the virtues of justice, integrity, and loyalty are practiced as part of a "practice" in the sense defined by MacIntyre, rather than as mere tools for maximizing profit. Joseph Heath, in *Morality, Competition, and the Firm* (2014), offers a contrasting "market failures" approach that treats the market as a cooperative framework whose rules should correct failures of competition, rather than building business ethics on virtue.

## Objections and Examples

Critics argue that competitive market logic sometimes rewards those who abandon virtue in pursuit of profit, which makes the call to virtue idealistic and impractical. Proponents of this approach reply that the long-term trust built by virtue (such as honesty in advertising) is itself a considerable form of commercial capital. For example, a trader who tells a customer about a hidden defect in his product, although he could have concealed it, loses an immediate gain but earns a lasting reputation. This is a practical embodiment of business virtue.

## Further Primary Sources

The classical starting points are Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, whose fifth book analyses justice in distribution and exchange, and the first book of his *Politics*, which distinguishes the acquisition of goods for household needs from the unlimited pursuit of money. Thomas Aquinas discussed fraud in buying and selling and the charging of interest in the *Summa Theologiae* (II-II, questions 77 and 78). Adam Smith's *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) treats prudence, justice, and beneficence as virtues, a useful counterpart to his economic analysis. In recent business ethics, Edwin Hartman's *Organizational Ethics and the Good Life* (1996) applies Aristotelian ideas to organizations, and Geoff Moore's "Corporate Character" (2005) uses MacIntyre's distinction between practices and institutions to describe the virtuous corporation. Deirdre McCloskey's *The Bourgeois Virtues* (2006) argues more broadly that commercial life can sustain the virtues.
