# Degrowth

**Degrowth** is a concept and an intellectual and social movement within ecological economics. The French term *décroissance* was in use by the early 1970s, notably in André Gorz's writing, in the wake of Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen's bioeconomics. The contemporary academic and activist network took shape in France, Spain and Italy and around the international degrowth conference in Paris in 2008. It calls for an organised and deliberate reduction in the size of wealthy economies specifically, rather than of the economies of the world equally. Its argument is that continuing to pursue aggregate growth, even in economies already materially saturated, aggravates the ecological crisis without improving actual well-being in proportion to its environmental cost.

## Distinction from the Steady-State Economy

The concept of degrowth goes further than the "steady-state economy" developed by Herman Daly. Where Daly calls for stabilisation at a certain level and then staying there, the degrowth movement calls for a deliberate, directed contraction in the size of wealthy economies specifically. Its basis is that these economies have already exceeded a level of material consumption that the planetary ecological boundaries can sustain. Its proponents argue that the continuation of their aggregate growth, even if slow, still consumes an unfair share of the planet's remaining resources and absorptive environmental capacity.

## Evidence from Happiness and Income Research

The degrowth current draws partly on the "economics of happiness" literature, beginning with Richard Easterlin's 1974 finding that average reported happiness did not rise with national income over time in wealthy countries. Later research has contested this "Easterlin paradox", and the evidence remains debated. From this it concludes that continuing to pursue further growth in these economies may not translate into a real, tangible improvement in the subjective well-being of their populations, while it continues to put pressure on ecological systems without a commensurate welfare benefit to justify it.

## Specific Policy Demands

Degrowth advocates propose a package of interlinked policies. These include reducing the working week (on the argument that this lowers total production and consumption while preserving employment); a combined minimum and maximum income; relocating production locally rather than relying on long, energy-intensive global supply chains; and transferring the collective ownership of shared resources instead of their full privatisation. These proposals go beyond partial economic reform to a broader structural rethinking of how the economy is organised.

## Criticism from Mainstream Economics and from the Global South

Degrowth faces criticism from mainstream growth economists, who argue that the technical decoupling of economic growth from material consumption (decoupling) is possible through innovation and energy efficiency without any need to abandon growth altogether. Degrowth advocates reply that evidence for the rapid, absolute decoupling that would be needed is weak. It also faces another critique from development thinkers in the Global South, who argue that the degrowth call, even if directed specifically at wealthy states, ignores the urgent need of many poor and developing countries for genuine economic growth to raise the basic living standards of their populations. This is a deep normative disagreement over the priority of the ecological limit versus the social limit in lower-income contexts.

## The social movement

Beyond journals, degrowth became a loose social movement in the 2000s. Further international conferences followed Paris, including Barcelona in 2010 and Leipzig in 2014, and the Research & Degrowth network helped link researchers and campaigners. Serge Latouche's *Petit traité de la décroissance sereine* (2007; English *Farewell to Growth*, 2009) carried the argument to a wider public. Campaigns associated with the movement have focused on shorter working time, defence of commons, and local production, alongside the academic case against growth in high-income countries. The movement remains politically diverse and has no single programme.

## Relation to Raworth's Compromise Model

The "doughnut economics" model developed by Kate Raworth is an attempt to reconcile the radical degrowth call with the need to guarantee a minimum level of social welfare. Raworth proposes that the goal is neither growth nor degrowth in themselves, but stability within a safe and just space between an internal social floor and an external ecological ceiling. Raworth describes her approach as agnostic about growth, which places it between Daly's steady-state stance and the more radical degrowth position.
