# Property Inheritance

Property inheritance is the transmission of rights in land, houses, livestock, valuables, and other assets from one generation to the next, usually at death but often through lifetime gifts, marriage settlements, and retirement arrangements. It is distinct from property itself, which concerns who holds rights in things; inheritance concerns how those rights move across generations, and therefore how inequality, kinship, and continuity are reproduced over time. Every society has rules about who inherits what — sons or daughters, eldest or youngest, kin or spouse — and those rules shape family structure, gender relations, and the distribution of wealth. Anthropologists study inheritance because it is where the abstract principles of kinship and property meet the concrete facts of death, grief, and the division of an estate.

## Definition

Inheritance is governed by norms that vary widely. In patrilineal systems, property typically passes through the male line, from father to son; in matrilineal systems, it passes through women, often from a man to his sister's son. Primogeniture gives the estate to the eldest child, usually the eldest son, preserving the holding intact; ultimogeniture favours the youngest; partible inheritance divides the estate among all heirs, which can fragment holdings over generations. Some systems distinguish between kinds of property: land may follow one rule, movable wealth another, and sacred or ancestral objects may be inalienable, passing only to a designated heir or remaining with the lineage as a whole. Inheritance is also gendered nearly everywhere. In many societies, daughters inherit less than sons, or inherit movable goods while sons inherit land — a pattern with lasting consequences for women's economic security. Disputes are common, revealing tensions between equality among children and the preservation of the estate, and between the claims of the widow and those of the lineage.

## Origins and Development

Jack Goody's comparative studies of Africa and Eurasia are central to the anthropology of inheritance. In *Death, Property and the Ancestors* (1962) and later *Production and Reproduction* (1976), Goody showed that in many African societies, property was held by corporate kin groups and passed laterally among kin, while in Eurasia, where intensive agriculture and private property predominated, inheritance was more often a direct transfer from parent to child, with a correspondingly greater emphasis on the conjugal family, marriage strategy, and the control of women's property. In Europe, the written will, the land registry, and the probate court became technologies of inheritance. Colonialism disrupted indigenous systems, imposing European models of private property and written title, and creating conflicts between customary and statutory inheritance that persist in many postcolonial states. In the twentieth century, the rise of the welfare state and of pension systems reduced the importance of inherited land for many families, while the growth of financial wealth — houses, savings, shares — made inheritance a central mechanism in the reproduction of inequality in rich countries as well.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns the relationship between inheritance rules and social structure. Goody argued that differences in inheritance reflect differences in the mode of production: where land is abundant and labour scarce, inheritance matters less; where land is scarce and valuable, the rules of transmission become elaborate and contested. Others emphasise the role of inheritance in shaping kinship itself: the pattern of who inherits from whom defines who counts as kin and what obligations they owe. A second debate concerns gender. Feminist anthropologists have shown that inheritance systems that favour men are a major source of women's economic disadvantage, and that women's struggles for equal inheritance rights — in South Asia, in Africa, and in Europe — are struggles over the material basis of equality. A third dimension is the tension between custom and law. In many societies, state law now guarantees daughters equal shares, while custom continues to favour sons; the gap between law and practice is itself a subject of study. Finally, inheritance is never only economic: the property of the dead carries memory and obligation, and funerary rites are part of the process by which the living take over from the dead.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Inheritance is at the centre of contemporary debates about inequality. Thomas Piketty's work on capital showed that inherited wealth is a major driver of inequality in rich countries, and that its importance is growing as economic growth slows — a finding that has revived political debate about inheritance taxation. In the Global South, disputes over land inheritance drive conflict within families and communities, particularly where land is scarce, titles are insecure, and customary and statutory systems overlap. Women's inheritance rights remain a live issue: in many countries, legal reform has outpaced social change, and daughters still surrender their claims under family pressure. Critics of the classical anthropology of inheritance note that it focused on the transmission of land and lineage property, and paid less attention to the inheritance of debts, obligations, and care — the burdens as well as the assets that pass between generations. The enduring insight is that inheritance is never a merely private matter: it is the mechanism by which societies reproduce themselves, and by which inequality is carried from one generation to the next.
