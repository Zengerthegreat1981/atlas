# Anti-Colonial Intellectualism

Arab anti-colonial thought developed from direct historical struggles into a sophisticated critique of imperial hegemony, knowledge production, and cultural dependency. The movement began with armed resistance to European invasion: Amir Abd al-Qadir al-Jaza'iri led Algerian resistance against the French occupation between 1832 and 1847; the Urabi uprising in Egypt culminated in the battle of Tel el-Kebir and the British occupation of 1882; Muhammad 'Abd al-Karim al-Khattabi waged the Rif War in Morocco between 1921 and 1926; and Italian colonial forces executed 'Umar al-Mukhtar in Libya in 1931. Across these encounters emerged an intellectual discourse that linked religious reform (*islah*), political sovereignty, and national liberation.

## Al-Afghani, Abduh, and al-Kawakibi

In the late nineteenth century, anti-colonial thought gained systemic expression through journalistic and political writing. Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh founded the short-lived but influential journal *al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa* in Paris in 1884. The publication rallied Muslims against British imperial dominance, urging political solidarity and internal revitalization. In his political treatise *Taba'i' al-istibdad wa-masari' al-isti'bad* (The Characteristics of Despotism and the Demise of Enslavement, 1900), 'Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi argued that domestic tyranny weakened Arab societies and left them exposed to external subjugation. In North Africa, the Association of Algerian Muslim Scholars, founded in 1931 under the leadership of 'Abd al-Hamid ibn Badis, articulated cultural resistance against French assimilationist policy through the slogan associated with it: "Islam is our religion, Arabic our language, Algeria our homeland."

## Malik Bennabi and Colonizability

The Algerian thinker Malik Bennabi (1905–1973) introduced a widely debated concept in modern Arab thought: "colonizability" (*al-qabiliyya li-al-isti'mar*). He set it out in *Les conditions de la renaissance* (1949; Arabic translation *Shurut al-nahda*, 1957). Bennabi argued that imperial conquest could not be explained merely by the military or economic superiority of the colonizer. On his account, it also required an internal moral, social, and psychological susceptibility that allowed colonization to take root. He contended that liberation required civilizational renewal and intellectual autonomy, not only a change of flag. Critics have replied that the concept risks shifting attention away from the violence of colonial rule. His focus on internal conditions nevertheless remained influential in later self-critical writing.

## Fanon, Memmi, and Said

From the 1950s onward, anti-colonial theory engaged with modern sociology, psychoanalysis, and literary theory. In *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), Frantz Fanon analyzed the dialectics of colonial violence and psychological alienation based on his work during the Algerian War of Independence. The Tunisian essayist Albert Memmi explored the reciprocal pathology of the imperial relationship in *The Colonizer and the Colonized* (1957). In Egypt, sociologist Anouar Abdel-Malek critiqued Western scholarship in his landmark paper "Orientalism in Crisis" (1963), identifying Orientalism as an ideological apparatus serving imperial hegemony. Edward Said systematized and globalized this critique in *Orientalism* (1978) and *Culture and Imperialism* (1993), analyzing how representations of the Orient legitimized imperial power. In *L'idéologie arabe contemporaine* (1967), Moroccan historian Abdallah Laroui applied historicist criticism to the dependency of modern Arab intellectual discourse.

## Limits and Internal Critiques

Anti-colonial intellectualism has faced critical reassessment from within Arab thought. Syrian philosopher Sadiq Jalal al-Azm published "Orientalism and Orientalism in Reverse" (1981) in *Khamsin*, warning that uncritical anti-colonial rhetoric could degenerate into nativist essentialism, mirroring the very stereotypes it sought to dismantle. Later critics noted that several early Nahda figures had combined resistance to foreign subjugation with genuine appreciation for European constitutional and scientific developments. While direct military occupation ended across most Arab states between 1943 and 1971, anti-colonial thought continues to animate debates over economic dependency, linguistic policy, educational reform, and cultural decolonization.
