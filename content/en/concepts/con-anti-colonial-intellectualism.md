# Anti-Colonial Intellectualism

Arab anti-colonial thought did not begin with a theory but with resistance. Amir Abd al-Qadir al-Jaza'iri led resistance to the French occupation between 1832 and 1847. The Urabi movement in Egypt ended with its defeat at Tel el-Kebir and the British occupation in 1882. 'Abd al-Karim al-Khattabi led the Rif War in 1921–1926, and the Italians executed 'Umar al-Mukhtar in Libya in 1931. From these events emerged a new political language that linked religious reform with national liberation.

## al-Afghani, Abduh and al-Kawakibi

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh issued the journal *al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa* in Paris in 1884. It called for the unity of Muslims and resistance to British influence. Nikki Keddie studied al-Afghani's political life in 1972. 'Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi wrote *Taba'i' al-Istibdad* (The Characteristics of Despotism), linking internal despotism to weakness in the face of external powers. In Algeria, the Association of Muslim Scholars (1931), led by 'Abd al-Hamid ibn Badis, adopted the slogan "Islam is our religion, Arabic our language, Algeria our homeland." Ali Merad studied the movement in *Le réformisme musulman en Algérie de 1925 à 1940* (1967).

## Malik Bennabi: The Colonizability

The Algerian Malik Bennabi (1905–1973) introduced the concept of "colonizability" (*al-qabiliyya li-al-isti'mar*). He argued that colonialism cannot be explained by the strength of the occupier alone, but also by a readiness within the colonized society that facilitates it. He called for addressing this readiness by reforming the individual and the idea. This view gains its weight from its insistence on internal responsibility, but in the eyes of critics it amounted to a softening of the crime of colonialism.

## Fanon, Memmi and Said

Frantz Fanon, in *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), offered a psychological and political analysis of colonial violence drawing on the Algerian experience. The Tunisian Albert Memmi, in *Portrait of the Colonized* (1957), showed how the colonial relationship distorts both parties. In Egypt, Anouar Abdel-Malek criticized Orientalism as a body of knowledge that serves domination (1963). Edward Said then formulated this critique in *Orientalism* (1978) and *Culture and Imperialism* (1993). Abdallah Laroui, in *The Contemporary Arab Ideology* (1967), linked intellectual dependency to historical structure.

## Limits

Some scholars have criticized the anti-colonial discourse when it turns into an all-encompassing explanation of the backwardness of Arab society that absolves the elites of responsibility. In this context, Sadiq Jalal al-Azm wrote "Orientalism and Orientalism in Reverse" (1981) in the journal *Khamsin*, criticizing what he saw as a mutual essentialism in Said and his opponents. Moreover, some pioneers of the Nahda combined resistance to occupation with admiration for European civilization, so a binary classification does not hold for everyone. Direct colonialism ended in most Arab countries between 1943 and 1971, but the questions it raised remained: economic dependency, language and education.
