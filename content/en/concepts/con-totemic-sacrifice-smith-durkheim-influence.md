# Robertson Smith's Totemic Sacrifice Theory and Its Influence on Durkheim

An account of the direct intellectual-historical connection between William Robertson Smith's theory of sacrifice and Émile Durkheim's theory of the sacred and the profane, covering the texts, dates and mechanism of this influence.

## From *Lectures on the Religion of the Semites* to *The Elementary Forms*

Émile Durkheim read William Robertson Smith's *Lectures on the Religion of the Semites* (1889) in the 1890s. That reading, documented by historians of social thought (especially Robert Alan Jones), was the turning point that moved Durkheim from a general sociological analysis to working directly on religion as a central social phenomenon. From Smith, Durkheim adopted two fundamental hypotheses, which he carried into his last work, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912). The first is that ritual (not belief) is the primary unit of analysis of religion. The second is that the collective totemic sacrifice, the communal feast that renews the bond between the group and its god, is the original model from which the whole sacred/profane dichotomy and collective effervescence can later be explained.

## The Durkheimian Transformation: From the Semitic Totem to the Australian Totem

The decisive difference is that Durkheim did not merely transfer Smith's thesis, but rebuilt it on a different ethnographic basis. He abandoned the scattered Semitic evidence that Smith had used (which was itself subjected to later critical revisions, as the entry on Smith documents), in favor of recent Australian ethnography from his own time (the Arunta tribes), regarding it as a purer "primitive" model. Evans-Pritchard, in his critical survey of theories of primitive religion, discusses this turn to Australian material, which is part of how a Semitic hypothesis of limited scope in Smith became, in Durkheim, a general sociological theory of the origin of both religion and society.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Robertson Smith, an Arabist as well as a biblical scholar, travelled in Arabia and built his account of early Semitic sacrifice partly on pre-Islamic and early Islamic Arabic sources, as in his earlier *Kinship and Marriage in Early Arabia* (1885). He also wrote on Arabia and related subjects for the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*. A key piece of evidence in the *Lectures* was the report of a camel sacrifice among Arab tribes by Nilus of Sinai, which he read as a communal meal uniting worshippers with the deity, and which he generalized to the Semites, including the ancient Hebrews. This reliance on pre-Islamic Arabic material is what makes the thread connecting Smith to Durkheim a thread that passes, though indirectly, through an orientalist use of classical Arab-Islamic sources. That use was later subjected to rigorous methodological critique for its selectivity and for projecting evolutionary hypotheses onto materials scattered in time and place.
