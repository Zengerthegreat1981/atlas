# Prescriptivism

## Meaning and Definition

Prescriptivism is a theory in the philosophy of moral language holding that moral judgments are not descriptions of objective facts in the world that can be true or false. Rather, they are in essence general commands or directives for action, similar in form to imperatives but applicable to all relevantly similar cases. When someone says "lying is wrong," he is not describing a property inherent in the act. He is issuing an implicit directive to himself and to others, "do not lie," and is committed to applying the same judgment to every similar situation without exception, including to himself.

## Roots in Islamic Ethical Thought

Prescriptivism was not formulated in its contemporary analytic form in the Islamic tradition, but the debate of the jurists (*usuliyyun*) on the nature of the "legal ruling" (*hukm shar'i*) carries a close echo of it. The jurists defined the ruling as "the address of God related to the actions of the legally responsible, either as a demand or as an option." In essence, it is a form of direction and command, not a descriptive statement about an event. This comes close to the idea of "direction" on which prescriptivists rely. Legal theorists also distinguished between a statement (*khabar*), which admits of truth and falsehood, and a performative or directive utterance (*insha'*), which does not, and classed commands and prohibitions with the latter, which corresponds, for contemporary prescriptivists, to the distinction between a moral judgment and a descriptive statement. The essential difference, however, is that for the jurists this utterance proceeds from the absolute will of God, not from a general rational human judgment as in the case of Hare.

## The Theory of R. M. Hare

The British philosopher Richard Mervyn Hare formulated this theory in his book *The Language of Morals* (1952), in response to emotivism, which reduced moral judgment to the mere expression of a subjective emotion without internal logic. Hare held that moral judgments combine two functions: a prescriptive function, which guides action, and a function of universalizability, which obliges the speaker to apply his judgment to every similar case without bias toward his own particular position. Thus, whoever says that a certain person's theft from him is wrong is logically committed to holding that his own theft from others is also wrong. Hare later developed this idea in *Moral Thinking: Its Levels, Method, and Point* (1981), in which he combined prescriptivism with a two-level (intuitive and critical) form of utilitarianism.

## Major Objections

A well-known objection to prescriptivism is the "problem of the fanatic." A racist fanatic can universalize his racist judgment in a logically consistent way, for example by accepting that he himself should be treated in the same manner if he belonged to the persecuted group. He would then satisfy the formal condition of universality without his judgment being morally sound. This reveals that logical consistency alone does not guarantee the correctness of moral content. Moral realists have also objected that prescriptivism empties moral disagreement of its true meaning, by making it a disagreement about commands rather than about facts. Yet disputants actually feel that they are contending over the correctness of a proposition, not over a personal preference.

## Illustrative Example

When someone says, "one should not discriminate in hiring on the basis of sex," he is, according to Hare, issuing a general directive that he is committed to applying in every relevantly similar case, including cases in which he is the one affected. He is not merely expressing displeasure at a particular case.

## Further Primary Sources

An earlier suggestion that norms are disguised commands appears in Rudolf Carnap's *Philosophy and Logical Syntax* (1935). Hare began working out the logic of commands in "Imperative Sentences" (1949), and *Freedom and Reason* (1963) developed universalizability into a method of moral argument, including the discussion of the fanatic. His late book *Sorting Out Ethics* (1997) places universal prescriptivism within a classification of ethical theories. The main critical texts include Philippa Foot's "Moral Beliefs" (1958-1959), which argued that not just anything can intelligibly be counted as a moral good, since moral terms have conditions of application tied to human benefit and harm. P. T. Geach's "Assertion" (1965) raised what is often called the Frege-Geach problem: moral sentences occur in unasserted contexts, such as the antecedents of conditionals, where they prescribe nothing, yet they appear to keep the same meaning in valid inferences. John Searle's "How to Derive 'Ought' from 'Is'" (1964) challenged the separation of description from prescription on which the theory relies.
