# Moral Subjectivism

## Meaning and Definition

Moral subjectivism is a metaethical position holding that moral judgments ("this is wrong," "that is a duty") do not describe objective facts that exist independently of the judging subject. Instead, they express the personal attitudes, feelings or preferences of the individual. The truth of a moral proposition is therefore relative to each judge. This differs from "cultural relativism," which makes moral truth relative to society rather than to the individual. It also differs from expressive non-realism (such as emotivism), which denies that moral judgments are propositions capable of being true or false at all. The subjectivist usually maintains that the statement "X is wrong to me" is indeed true or false, but that its content is restricted to the judge's own standpoint.

## Attitudes Toward the Idea in Islamic Thought

Most Muslim theologians, both Mu'tazila and Ash'ari, rejected any pure form of moral subjectivism. They agreed that actions possess a stable quality of goodness or badness that does not change with shifting desires, although they differed about the source by which this quality is perceived: reason for the Mu'tazila, revelation for the Ash'aris. Some Sufi writings that stress the inner taste (*dhawq*) of the seeker may sound close to subjectivism, though they are not usually presented as such. Mainstream jurists and theologians, however, regarded the outward rulings as binding on all and resisted any subordination of morality to the changing desires of individuals.

## Historical Context in Western Philosophy

The roots of moral subjectivism go back to the Greek Sophists, particularly Protagoras and his famous saying "Man is the measure of all things." Some of them applied this to ethics, making good and evil dependent on each individual's perception. In the modern period, David Hume developed an important philosophical basis when he argued that "reason is the slave of the passions," and that moral judgment is not a rational inference but an expression of approval or disapproval grounded in feeling. Hume was not an individualist subjectivist, however, because he linked these feelings to shared human sentiments such as sympathy.

In the twentieth century, the emotivists, such as A. J. Ayer and C. L. Stevenson, came close to subjectivism. They argued that a moral judgment is merely an emotional expression and not a descriptive proposition at all. Their position, however, is technically classified as non-cognitivist rather than subjectivist in the strict sense.

## Objections and Applications

A strong logical objection to subjectivism is often put as the problem of disagreement. If Zaid says "abortion is permissible" and Hind says "abortion is wrong," subjectivism makes both statements true together (each according to its own speaker), even though the disagreement appears to be a genuine contradiction. This empties moral debate of its meaning, since neither party can actually be mistaken. A further objection holds that subjectivism makes it difficult to explain moral progress in history, such as the abolition of slavery. If a moral judgment were relative to each individual or era, it would not be legitimate to say that the defenders of slavery in the past were truly wrong.

An applied example: when a person says, "I believe that white lies are acceptable," subjectivism makes this statement true for that person alone. This does not mean that someone who holds the opposite view is mistaken. Consequently, a genuine moral dialogue between the two sides becomes logically impossible.

## Further Primary Sources

The saying of Protagoras is examined in Plato's *Theaetetus*, which asks whether it can be maintained without refuting itself. Sextus Empiricus's *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* collects disagreements about good and bad as grounds for withholding judgment, a sceptical rather than subjectivist conclusion. G. E. Moore's *Ethics* (1912) pressed the disagreement objection against the view that calling an act right reports the speaker's own feeling. Edward Westermarck's *Ethical Relativity* (1932) argued that moral judgments rest on emotions and lack objective validity. Bertrand Russell's *Religion and Science* (1935) treated questions of value as lying outside knowledge. C. L. Stevenson's article "The Emotive Meaning of Ethical Terms" (1937) explained how moral language influences attitudes. J. L. Mackie's *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977) offers a contrasting error theory, on which moral judgments aim at objective truth and fail. Later discussions of speaker relativism include Gilbert Harman's "Moral Relativism Defended" (1975), a relativism based on agreements, and James Dreier's "Internalism and Speaker Relativism" (1990).
