# Dowry

Dowry is property — money, gold and jewellery, furniture, clothing or land — that a bride brings from her own family into her husband's household at marriage. It must be distinguished from two related transfers that move in the opposite direction: bridewealth (or bride price), which the groom's family pays to the bride's family, and the Islamic *mahr*, which the groom himself pays to the bride as her own legal right. Dowry is not the bride's protected property in the way *mahr* is; it typically passes under the control of the husband and his family, above all his mother. It therefore reflects a different economic and familial structure from bridewealth: one in which a daughter is transferred between patriarchal households, and property moves with her as the price and instrument of that transfer.

## Definition and Historical Origins

Dowry is an ancient practice, documented in classical Greece and the Roman Empire, in India, and across the Middle East. Dorothy Stein argued in her study of 1988 that dowry reflects a patriarchal model of property: the father marries off his daughter and transfers authority over her to her husband, and the dowry is the material expression of that transfer from one family to another. In the Indian context, dowry (*dahej*) developed out of a property system in which women did not inherit land; instead of a share of the family estate, a daughter received her portion in the form of a dowry at marriage.

Jack Goody and Stanley Tambiah, in *Bridewealth and Dowry* (1973), placed the practice in comparative perspective. They argued that dowry is characteristic of societies with intensive agriculture, private property in land and social stratification — Eurasian societies, broadly — where marriage is a strategy for preserving and transmitting status and property. Bridewealth, by contrast, predominates where land is abundant, labour is scarce and property is held corporately. The contrast explains why dowry and bridewealth rarely coexist in the same system: each belongs to a different organisation of property, labour and inheritance.

## Geographical and Cultural Forms

In India, among Hindus, Sikhs and Muslims, dowry commonly includes large sums of cash, gold and jewellery, furniture, and clothing for the bride and groom. Meenakshi Thapan, writing on contemporary India in 2009, observed that dowry has ceased to be an economic necessity and has become a symbol of family honour: a poor family may go into debt to provide a lavish dowry to demonstrate its respectability. The size of the dowry signals the family's standing.

In parts of the Levant, Iraq and the Caucasus, dowry includes fine furniture, upholstered seating, handmade carpets and copper and silver household goods. In some communities a rich dowry is read as a sign of a father's love and generosity — proof that he wishes his daughter comfort in her new home. In medieval and early modern Europe, dowry was a legal precondition of marriage: without a dowry a woman could not marry, and the property passed to the husband to use as he wished. Sherrill Cohen, in her study of women's asylums in Renaissance Italy (1992), showed how dowry pressures shaped women's lives: a woman without an adequate dowry faced exclusion from marriage and, often, confinement in an institution, since the dowry was the material condition of an honourable married life.

## Social and Economic Mechanisms

First, dowry gives the husband's family economic power over the bride. If a wife attempts to leave, she may lose her dowry or be denied maintenance; a mother-in-law may use it as a weapon, reminding the bride that her property now belongs to them. Ethnographic research has shown that dowry creates a lasting sense of debt: the bride feels she "owes" her husband's family.

Second, dowry transfers patriarchal control. Before marriage the bride is under the authority of her father and her natal family; after marriage she is under the authority of her husband's family, and the dowry is the mechanism that eases that transfer — the husband, in effect, acquires rights over the wife.

Third, dowry reproduces poverty across generations. A poor family borrows to pay a dowry, the debt prevents investment in education or enterprise, and the next generation inherits both poverty and debt. Barbara Miller argued in *The Endangered Sex* (1981) that dowry is an unbreakable cycle of poverty and exclusion, linked to the neglect of daughters in rural North India: where daughters are an economic burden, families invest less in their health and schooling.

## Dowry-Related Violence and Reform

In India alone, thousands of deaths each year are attributed to dowry: bride burning, where a wife is killed because her family paid too little; suicide under pressure; and physical violence for "bringing an insufficient dowry". India outlawed dowry in the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961 and strengthened penalties in 1984 and 2005, but enforcement remains weak; Bangladesh prohibited it in 1974 and Pakistan in 1961, with similar problems of implementation. Women who bring a small dowry also suffer verbal abuse, medical neglect and poorer nutrition within the household.

Resistance takes several forms. Feminist movements campaign against dowry and support its victims; some families reduce the dowry to a symbolic sum; others give the dowry directly to the bride rather than to the groom's family; and some women choose not to marry rather than endure its humiliation. Open questions remain: whether dowry can be reformed without dismantling the patriarchal structure itself, how its effects vary by class, caste and religion, and what role older women — especially mothers-in-law — play in sustaining the practice. The dowry system endures because it is woven into property, honour and kinship at once; understanding it requires seeing all three threads together.
