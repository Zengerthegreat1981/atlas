# Multiple Attestation and Source Corroboration

In historical epistemology and source criticism, multiple attestation refers to the principle that a reported event, statement, or condition is substantially more likely to be authentic if it is independently attested by two or more unrelated sources. Across classical antiquity and medieval historiography, historians recognized that source corroboration provided the strongest defense against partisan bias, personal fabulation, and memory failure.

## Definition and Epistemological Core

The criterion of multiple attestation rests upon the logical improbability that independent observers in different contexts, writing with different biases, would invent identical narrative details. However, methodology distinguishes between genuine independent attestation and secondary consensus:
1. Independent corroboration: distinct sources deriving information from separate eyewitnesses or unrelated archival streams.
2. Derivative repetition: multiple later authors merely copying a single unverified authority—the fallacy modern stemmatics terms common-source dependence.

## Practice in Classical Greek and Roman Historiography

Explicit source cross-examination is prominent in Thucydides (c. 460–c. 400 BCE). In Book I of his *History of the Peloponnesian War* (1.22), Thucydides articulated his rule for reconstructing events: he refused to rely on casual informants or personal impressions, testing every report through cross-examination of multiple eyewitnesses. He observed that witnesses to the same engagement gave contradictory testimonies, colored by partiality or imperfect memory, requiring the historian to triangulate between competing accounts.

In the second century CE, Flavius Arrianus (Arrian) offers a well-known example of source comparison in the Preface to his *Anabasis of Alexander*. Confronted by sensationalized Hellenistic accounts, Arrian chose as his main authorities two participants in the campaign: Ptolemy son of Lagus and Aristobulus. His stated rule was that where Ptolemy and Aristobulus agreed he set down their account as true; where they differed, he selected the version he found more credible and more worth telling.

Roman historians applied multiple attestation when navigating conflicting annalistic traditions. In *Ab Urbe Condita*, Livy compared earlier annalists, expressing skepticism whenever Valerius Antias claimed enormous casualty numbers uncorroborated by earlier writers. Similarly, Tacitus often notes where his authorities disagree and names them (for example at *Annals* 13.20), sometimes declining to decide between versions.

## Arabic-Islamic Historiography: The Theory of Mutawatir

A highly developed formalization of multiple attestation appears in classical Islamic jurisprudence (*usūl al-fiqh*) and Hadith criticism through the classification of reports into *mutawātir* (mass-transmitted) and *āhād* (solitary). 

A *khabar mutawātir* was defined as a report transmitted by such a large multitude of independent transmitters at every link of the transmission chain (*isnād*) that collusion upon a falsehood (*tawātu' 'alā al-kidhb*) was impossible. Scholars such as al-Ghazali (450–505 AH / 1058–1111 CE) held that *tawātur* produced necessary, certain knowledge (*'ilm ḍarūrī*), for example of the existence of distant lands. Ibn Khaldun (732–808 AH / 1332–1406 CE), for his part, warned in the *Muqaddimah* that historians must not simply repeat widely circulated reports without testing them against the nature of society.

## Modern Historiographical Critique

In modern historical method, as formulated by Ernst Bernheim and Marc Bloch, multiple attestation remains a foundational criterion of probability. Modern historians emphasize that attestation must be screened for stemmatic independence. Furthermore, multiple attestation cannot eliminate widespread cultural rumors, shared literary tropes, or coordinated state propaganda, which can be reported independently by sincere witnesses without being factually true.
