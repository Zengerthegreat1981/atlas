# Hayek's Knowledge Problem

**Hayek's knowledge problem** is a central concept formulated by **Friedrich Hayek** in his influential article "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), after earlier statements in "Economics and Knowledge" (1937). It points to the impossibility of gathering in a single central body the information necessary for rational economic allocation of resources, because this information is in fact dispersed among millions of individuals in the form of local, unsystematised fragments of knowledge about particular circumstances of time and place.

## Redefining the economic problem

This concept challenges the standard formulation of the economic problem in textbooks, which assumes that the problem is the allocation of resources of known quantity among known uses according to uniform rational criteria. Hayek argues that this formulation implicitly assumes the solution to the harder problem: how the information needed for such allocation can be obtained at all, when this information is itself not fully given in advance to any individual or agency.

## Two kinds of knowledge

Hayek distinguishes between general scientific knowledge that can be verbally stated and collected in central books and references (such as the laws of physics), and local, practical knowledge (what Hayek called knowledge of the particular circumstances of time and place, about specific circumstances, fleeting opportunities, and precise personal preferences), which is difficult or impossible to express fully in a form transferable to a single centre. Knowledge of the second kind, although less "scientific" in form, is the most practically important for running a real economy efficiently. It is precisely the type that cannot be gathered centrally given its dispersed and constantly changing nature. Hayek's term is not identical to the "tacit knowledge" of the philosopher Michael Polanyi (*Personal Knowledge*, 1958; *The Tacit Dimension*, 1966), although the two ideas are related and scholars debate the extent of their connection.

## Prices as a solution, not merely a computational tool

Hayek presents the price system of a competitive market as a practical solution to this predicament. When the price of a commodity changes in response to changed conditions anywhere, each person receives a concentrated signal sufficient to adjust his behaviour without needing to know the full reasons behind the change. This makes the market a decentralized information-processing system that outperforms any central computational apparatus, because the original problem is not computational but structural and epistemic.

## Impact on the socialist calculation debate

This concept formed Hayek's central argument in the **socialist calculation debate** against economists such as Oskar Lange, who proposed that a central planner could simulate the market arithmetically through hypothetical accounting prices adjusted by trial and error. Hayek argued that this proposal ignores the essence of the problem: not the computational capacity to solve equilibrium equations, but the impossibility of obtaining the necessary information in the first place with the speed and accuracy sufficient to keep pace with continuous changes in actual conditions on the ground.

## Distinction from Mises's calculation problem

This concept is related to the economic calculation problem that Ludwig von Mises raised in 1920, a quarter-century earlier, but it differs from it in focus. Mises concentrated on the absence of real prices for means of production under socialism due to the absence of private ownership, while Hayek concentrated on the nature of knowledge itself and its structural distribution, partly independently of the system of ownership. This made Hayek's argument wider in scope and applicable even to forms of central organization that do not entirely abolish private ownership but attempt to coordinate complex economic decisions centrally.

## An influence beyond economics

The concept was later drawn upon in debates on the decentralized design of complex systems in general, from distributed computer networks to the theory of administrative organization. It has been regarded as an early contribution to what later became the broader field of information economics, although that field developed later with mathematical tools fundamentally different from the descriptive method Hayek himself used.
