# Perinatal Depression

A major depressive episode that occurs during pregnancy or in the weeks and months following childbirth. The term "perinatal" covers both antepartum (prenatal) depression and postpartum depression. The DSM-5 recognizes this period through the specifier "with peripartum onset," which is added to the diagnosis of major depressive episode when symptoms begin during pregnancy or within four weeks after delivery.

## Prevalence and Diagnosis

The prevalence of perinatal depression is estimated at roughly 10% to 20% of women, with wide variation depending on the measurement instruments and the cultural and economic context. The most widely used screening tool is the **Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)**, developed by Cox and colleagues in 1987, which has relatively high sensitivity and specificity compared with structured diagnostic interviews based on the DSM. It must be distinguished from the **"baby blues"**, a mild and transient condition affecting a large proportion of mothers in the first days after delivery that usually resolves on its own within about two weeks, and from **postpartum psychosis**, a rare and severe condition that is a psychiatric emergency.

## Risk Factors

Documented risk factors include a personal or family history of depression, pregnancy or delivery complications, weak social support, an unplanned pregnancy, domestic violence, and financial difficulties. Potential biological foundations are also being studied, particularly the sharp hormonal changes (the drop in estrogen and progesterone after delivery) and their effect on neurotransmitter systems; the precise causal role of these hormones is not settled.

## Clinical Impact

Perinatal depression has consequences that extend beyond the mother to the infant and the family: possible difficulties in maternal bonding, associations with adverse child cognitive and emotional outcomes, and an increased risk of suicide; in some high-income countries, mental health conditions including suicide are counted among the leading causes of pregnancy-associated deaths. Professional bodies, including the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (2023), recommend screening for depression in adults including pregnant and postpartum people.

## Treatment

Treatment options described in guidelines include psychotherapy (particularly cognitive behavioral therapy and interpersonal therapy), antidepressants (with individual weighing of risks and benefits in pregnancy and breastfeeding), and specialized interventions in severe cases. In 2019 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved **brexanolone**, the first drug designed specifically to treat postpartum depression, which is given by intravenous infusion and acts through GABA-A receptors; its maker stopped marketing it at the end of 2024 and the FDA withdrew the approval in 2025. It was later followed by **zuranolone**, an oral formulation approved in 2023. This entry is educational and does not constitute clinical advice.
