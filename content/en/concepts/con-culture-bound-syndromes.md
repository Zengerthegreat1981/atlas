# Culture-Bound Syndromes

## Definition

Culture-bound syndromes is a term used in comparative psychiatry for behavioral and emotional disorders recognized within specific cultural groups that do not fit Western diagnostic categories. Examples include *amok* in Malaysia, *latah* in Southeast Asia, *koro* (the fear that the genitals are retracting into the body) in China and Southeast Asia, *susto* in Latin America, and *ataque de nervios* among Latino populations, especially from the Caribbean. In the Arab world they take forms linked to zar, spirit possession, and the evil eye.

## History of the Concept

Emil Kraepelin traveled to Java in 1904 and wrote the article "Comparative Psychiatry" in the *Zentralblatt für Nervenheilkunde und Psychiatrie*, believing that the underlying diseases were the same, with their manifestations changing according to the people. Pow Meng Yap presented an article on "mental illnesses peculiar to certain cultures" in the *Journal of Mental Science* in 1951, then in the 1960s proposed the formulation of "culture-bound reactive syndromes." Ronald C. Simons and Charles C. Hughes edited *The Culture-Bound Syndromes* (D. Reidel, 1985), which became a standard collection. The fourth edition of the diagnostic manual (DSM-IV, 1994) included an appendix titled "Culture-Bound Syndromes." DSM-5 (2013) then dropped the phrase and replaced it with "cultural concepts of distress," with three domains: cultural syndromes, cultural idioms of distress, and cultural explanations (perceived causes).

## Arab and Islamic Examples

Zar, a ritual of spirit possession found in Sudan, Egypt, the Gulf, and Ethiopia, is a prominent case. Janice Boddy studied it in northern Sudan in *Wombs and Alien Spirits* (University of Wisconsin Press, 1989), presenting it as a language through which women express social suffering (see the entry on zar). I. M. Lewis, in *Ecstatic Religion* (Penguin, 1971), examined the idea that possession may be a means by which the deprived express themselves and negotiate. The notion of "possession" (*mass*) by jinn is a broad Quranic-folk concept, and Dols showed in his book on the madman that medieval Muslim physicians at times tried to explain madness medically and at other times accepted a jinn cause.

## Critique of the Concept

Guarnaccia and Rogler argued in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 1999 for new directions in research on these syndromes. The label suggests that only non-Western cultures are "culture-bound," while Western disorders (such as anorexia nervosa) are considered universal, a paradox that critics including Kleinman (*Rethinking Psychiatry*, Free Press, 1988) have emphasized. Moreover, a number of these conditions overlap with recognized diagnoses such as panic, depression, and conversion, and some are very rare or historical. Researchers also look for Western conditions that could be labeled by the same criterion, such as eating disorders.

## Clinical Use

The concept helps alert the clinician to the meaning of distress for the patient and to when a behavior is culturally acceptable, without sliding into reifying the patient or reducing every complaint to "culture." For this reason specialists recommend combining diagnosis with cultural explanation and an understanding of the social context (see the entry on cultural competence).
