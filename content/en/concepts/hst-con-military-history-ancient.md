# Military History in Ancient Historiography

Military conflict constituted the primary subject matter of ancient historiography. In both the Greek and Roman traditions, history (*historia* or *res gestae*) was conceived essentially as the narrative of wars, interstate diplomacy, and the deeds of political leaders. Far from being armchair observers, many ancient historians were active military commanders or officers whose narrative techniques reflected both practical experience and contemporary rhetorical conventions.

## Definition and the Commander-Historian Tradition

In classical antiquity, military history was not a specialized subdiscipline but the core of historical inquiry. The authority of the ancient historian rested largely on *autopsia* (eyewitness experience), which was nowhere more valued than on the battlefield. Many ancient historians held military commands or office:
- Thucydides served as an Athenian general (*strategos*) operating in Thrace during the Peloponnesian War.
- Xenophon was one of the generals who led the retreat of the Ten Thousand Greek mercenaries, which he chronicled in the *Anabasis*.
- Polybius was a cavalry commander (*hipparchos*) of the Achaean League before his deportation to Rome, later advising Scipio Aemilianus at the siege of Carthage.
- Julius Caesar composed his *Commentarii* (*De Bello Gallico* and *De Bello Civili*) directly from the perspective of supreme field commander.
- Flavius Arrianus (Arrian) served as Roman governor of Cappadocia (c. 131–137 CE), where he organized the defence against an Alan incursion, and also wrote the *Anabasis of Alexander*.
- Ammianus Marcellinus participated as a staff officer under Emperor Julian during the Roman invasion of the Sasanian Empire in 363 CE.

## Structural Conventions of the Ancient Battle Piece

Ancient military narratives adhered to sophisticated literary and tactical schemas. Rather than presenting chaotic, fragmented skirmishes, historians organized accounts around the stylized "battle piece":
1. Topographical orientation: surveying the terrain, river crossings, marshes, and defiles that conditioned tactical deployment.
2. Pre-battle speeches: the commander's harangue (*paraklēsis* in Greek, *cohortatio* in Latin), designed to articulate the strategic stakes, remind troops of ancestral glory, and reveal the general's moral character.
3. Order of battle (*taxis* or *acies*): describing the disposition of heavy infantry, skirmishers, and cavalry wings.
4. The decisive turning point: identifying the critical breakthrough, ambush, flank collapse, or tactical stratagem (*stratēgēma*).
5. Casualties, pursuit, and aftermath: recording the distribution of spoils, burial of the dead, and political consequences.

Military history also functioned didactically as a manual of statecraft and tactical education. Polybius explicitly stated that history existed to instruct future statesmen and commanders on how to endure reversals of fortune and execute military strategy.

## Cross-Cultural Parallels: Islamic Maghazi and Futuh

A parallel centrality of military narrative characterized early Arabic-Islamic historiography. The earliest formal historical works developed as *maghāzī* (accounts of the Prophet Muhammad's military campaigns), compiled by scholars such as Musa ibn 'Uqba (d. 141 AH / 758 CE) and al-Waqidi (130–207 AH / 747–823 CE). This genre expanded into *futūh* literature (the history of the early Islamic conquests), exemplified by al-Baladhuri's *Futūh al-Buldān*. Like classical historians, early Islamic chroniclers meticulously documented military logistics, troop allocations (*diwān al-jund*), treaty terms, and commanders' tactical decisions.

## Modern Historiographical Critique

Modern military historiography, reshaped by John Keegan's *The Face of Battle* (1976), has encouraged scholars of antiquity (such as Victor Davis Hanson) to ask what combat was like for ordinary soldiers. Many argue that ancient historians frequently imposed rhetorical topoi, formulaic troop movements, and idealized commander speeches onto what were chaotic, terrifying, and sensorially overwhelming experiences for common soldiers. Modern analysis therefore distinguishes between the stylized rhetorical form of ancient battle accounts and their underlying technical, logistical, and political insights.
