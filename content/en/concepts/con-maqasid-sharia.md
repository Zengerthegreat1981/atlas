# Maqasid al-Sharia

The maqasid of the Sharia are the aims and wisdoms that the Lawgiver intended behind its rulings. They became an independent discipline within the principles of jurisprudence (*usul al-fiqh*). Their story begins centuries before al-Shatibi and extends after him into the reform projects of the twentieth century.

## The first foundations

Al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi (d. c. 295–320 AH / 908–932 CE; the year is disputed), in *al-Salat wa-maqasiduha* (Prayer and Its Purposes), used "maqasid" for the inner aims of acts of worship. The idea then developed in the reasoning of jurists. Al-Juwayni (d. 478 AH / 1085 CE), in *al-Burhan*, developed the division of interests and arranged them into the levels of the necessary, the needed and the embellishing, and he said that whoever does not perceive that the aims are present in the commands and prohibitions has no insight into how the Sharia was established. Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH / 1111 CE), in *al-Mustasfa*, set out the enumeration of the necessities as five: the preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny and property, and called what preserves them a *maslaha* (benefit).

## Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam and al-Qarafi

Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam (d. 660 AH / 1262 CE) built his book *Rules of Rulings Concerning the Benefits of Mankind* (*Qawa'id al-Ahkam fi Masalih al-Anam*) on the premise that the whole Sharia consists of benefits that avert harms and secure advantages. Al-Qarafi (d. 684 AH / 1285 CE) added a distinction between the Prophet's actions as conveyor of the message, as issuer of legal opinions, as judge and as imam. The classical statement of this line is *al-Muwafaqat* of Abu Ishaq al-Shatibi (d. 790 AH / 1388 CE). He treated the maqasid as known with certainty by an inductive survey of the law, rather than by any single text.

## Modern renewal

Muhammad al-Tahir ibn Ashur (1879–1973) returned to the discipline in *Maqasid al-Shari'a al-Islamiyya* (Tunis, 1946). He described the general aim of the Sharia as the preservation of the community's order and the continuity of its welfare, and he discussed freedom (*hurriyya*), equality (*musawah*), and magnanimity or ease (*samaha*), together with the human disposition (*fitra*), among the law's wider characteristics. Ahmad al-Raysuni later wrote a study of al-Shatibi's theory (Beirut, 1992). Jasser Auda, in *Maqasid al-Shariah as Philosophy of Islamic Law* (2008), reread the maqasid through systems theory and argued for shifting the emphasis from preservation of the individual goods toward development and rights.

## Dispute and limits

The maqasid have not received full acceptance. The Zahiris deny ratiocination by benefit outside the text, and critics warn that appeal to the maqasid could become a means of setting the text aside in the name of benefit. Najm al-Din al-Tufi (d. 716 AH / 1316 CE) provoked one such dispute in his comment on the hadith "no harm and no reciprocating harm." He argued that, in transactions rather than in acts of worship, a preponderant benefit may outweigh a particular text. The argument stayed outside the mainstream of *usul al-fiqh*. Wael Hallaq includes it in his history of legal theories. Later writers disagree over how far al-Tufi meant that priority to extend. The five necessities are themselves open to question: they rest on an induction that may be incomplete, and the classical list does not name freedom or justice. Ibn Ashur and Auda discussed further aims of that kind. They did not claim that the fivefold list had already contained them.
