# Maqasid al-Sharia

The maqasid of the Sharia are the aims and wisdoms that the Lawgiver intended behind its rulings. They became an independent discipline within the principles of jurisprudence (*usul al-fiqh*). Their story begins centuries before al-Shatibi and extends after him into the reform projects of the twentieth century.

## The first foundations

Al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi (d. c. 295 AH / 908 CE), in *Prayer and Its Purposes* (*al-Salat wa-maqasiduha*), used the word "maqasid" in the sense of the secrets of acts of worship. The idea then developed in the reasoning of jurists. Al-Juwayni (d. 478 AH / 1085 CE), in *al-Burhan*, established the division of interests and arranged them into the levels of the necessary, the needed and the embellishing, and he said that whoever does not perceive that the aims are present in the commands and prohibitions has no insight into how the Sharia was established. Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH / 1111 CE), in *al-Mustasfa*, completed the enumeration of the necessities as five: the preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny and property, and called what preserves them a *maslaha* (benefit).

## Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam and al-Qarafi

Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam (d. 660 AH / 1262 CE) built his book *Rules of Rulings Concerning the Benefits of Mankind* (*Qawa'id al-Ahkam fi Masalih al-Anam*) on the premise that the whole Sharia consists of benefits that avert harms and secure advantages. Al-Qarafi (d. 684 AH / 1285 CE) added a distinction between the Prophet's actions as conveyor of the message, as issuer of legal opinions, as judge and as imam. This edifice reached its peak in al-Shatibi's *al-Muwafaqat* (d. 790 AH / 1388 CE), which made the maqasid a definitive principle established by induction rather than by any single text.

## Modern renewal

Muhammad al-Tahir ibn Ashur (d. 1973) revived this discipline in *The Objectives of Islamic Law* (*Maqasid al-Shari'a al-Islamiyya*). He made the general aim of the Sharia the preservation of the order of the community and the continuity of its wellbeing, and added the aims of freedom, equality and tolerance. Later, Ahmad al-Raysuni wrote his study of al-Shatibi, and Jasser Auda, in *Maqasid al-Shariah as Philosophy of Islamic Law* (2008), rebuilt the maqasid on systems theory, moving them from the preservation of individuals toward development and human rights.

## Dispute and limits

The maqasid have not received full acceptance. The Zahiris deny ratiocination by benefit outside the text, and critics warn that the maqasid could become a means of suspending the text in the name of benefit. The dispute that Najm al-Din al-Tufi (d. 716 AH / 1316 CE) provoked by giving benefit precedence over the text in transactions is a case in point, and Wael Hallaq discussed his position, showing how narrow what Tufi actually said was. The five necessities themselves are subject to critique: they rest on an induction that may be incomplete, and they do not explicitly mention freedom or justice, which Ibn Ashur and Auda sought to remedy by adding them.
