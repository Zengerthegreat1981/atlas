# Magic and Religion in Frazer

A central concept in Sir James George Frazer's encyclopaedic book *The Golden Bough*, which first appeared in 1890 in two volumes, then gradually expanded (second edition, three volumes, 1900; third edition, twelve volumes, 1906–1915), and was followed by a one-volume abridged edition in 1922. In it Frazer presents an evolutionary theory describing the history of human thought as a passage through three successive stages: **magic**, then **religion**, then **science**.

## Theoretical Structure

Frazer distinguishes between magic and religion by the criterion of the relationship with hidden forces. **Magic** is an attempt at **direct control** of nature through technical actions that presuppose automatic causal laws (false in Frazer's view cognitively, but "scientific" in its logical structure, since it assumes that action (a) automatically produces result (b)). **Religion**, by contrast, is the propitiation and appeasement of conscious supernatural beings (gods, spirits) presumed to have free will that may or may not respond, that is, a negotiated relationship rather than a mechanical technique. Frazer divides magic itself into two related types: **homoeopathic or imitative magic**, based on the principle that "like produces like" (such as making a doll representing an enemy in order to harm him), and **contagious magic**, based on the principle that things that were once in contact remain causally linked even after separation (such as using a person's hair or nail to affect them).

## The Three-Stage Evolutionary Theory

Frazer held that humanity passed historically through three stages of thought: the age of **magic**, in which humans believed they could control nature directly through symbolic techniques; then, when these techniques failed to produce consistent results, the age of **religion**, which delegates control over nature to supernatural beings whose favour must be won; and finally the age of **science**, in which humans return to assuming automatic causal laws as magic does, but this time through a precise experimental method open to correction, instead of rigid superstitious belief. This means that Frazer saw a structural similarity between magic and science (both assume automatic causality) in contrast to religion (which assumes conscious will), although magic and science stand at opposite poles in terms of cognitive validity.

## The "King of the Wood" Myth and Fertility Rites

The central narrative thread of the book is the explanation of an obscure ritual at Lake Nemi in ancient Italy: the priest of the temple of Diana there bore the title "King of the Wood" (*Rex Nemorensis*), and could only be succeeded in office by one who killed him after plucking the "golden bough" from a sacred tree. Frazer uses this puzzle as a starting point for a vast comparison between hundreds of rituals and myths around the world about "god-kings" who are ritually killed to ensure the fertility of the land and the renewal of the seasons, linking these rituals to the cycle of death and rebirth of fertility deities (Adonis, Osiris, Attis) in a cross-cultural religious comparison.

## Impact

The book was one of the most influential works in Western literary and intellectual culture in the early twentieth century. It directly influenced the "myth and ritual" school in the comparative study of religion, and major literary works: T. S. Eliot explicitly referred to it in his poem *The Waste Land*, acknowledging its debt to the book. It also influenced psychoanalysis (Freud and Jung) and comparative mythological studies (Joseph Campbell).

## Limitations

Frazer's framework was subjected to decisive criticism by later field anthropology. The most prominent criticisms are as follows. First, his total reliance on "armchair anthropology": Frazer himself conducted no fieldwork, and gathered his material from the reports of missionaries, colonial administrators and travellers, a method Bronisław Malinowski explicitly attacked as unreliable. Second, its unilinear evolutionary character, which ranks cultures on a single scale from "primitive" to "European civilisation", an assumption later rejected by Franz Boas and the American historical culture school as ethnocentric and not supported by sufficient empirical evidence. Third, E. E. Evans-Pritchard and others criticised Frazer for cutting ritual and mythical elements out of their specific social context in order to compare them superficially across cultures separated greatly in time and space, thereby depriving them of their actual local meaning.

## Cases from the Third Edition

The third edition arranged its material in parts: *The Magic Art and the Evolution of Kings*, *Taboo and the Perils of the Soul*, *The Dying God*, *Adonis, Attis, Osiris*, *Spirits of the Corn and of the Wild*, *The Scapegoat*, and *Balder the Beautiful*, followed by a bibliography and index volume. Each part gathers cases around one theme. For imitative magic Frazer drew, among much else, on Baldwin Spencer and F. J. Gillen's accounts of Central Australian ceremonies intended to multiply the plants and animals associated with particular totemic groups. For the killing of the divine king, a prominent case was the Shilluk of the Upper Nile, whose kings, according to information supplied by C. G. Seligman, were put to death when their vigour failed. Later scholars, including Evans-Pritchard, questioned whether such killings actually took place as described.

Other volumes assemble European harvest customs concerning the last sheaf, often called the Corn Mother or Maiden, relying heavily on Wilhelm Mannhardt; the Ainu bear ceremony of northern Japan; rites for expelling evils by means of a scapegoat; and the European fire festivals of Lent, Easter, May Day, and Midsummer.
