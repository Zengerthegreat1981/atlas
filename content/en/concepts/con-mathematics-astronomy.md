# Mathematics & Astronomy

Arabic mathematical science began with the translation of Euclid's *Elements* and Ptolemy's *Almagest* by al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf ibn Matar, Ishaq ibn Hunayn, and Thabit ibn Qurra. It quickly went beyond translation. Roshdi Rashed, in *The Development of Arabic Mathematics*, treats the emergence of algebra as an independent discipline as a central development of that science.

## Algebra and Arithmetic

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (d. c. 232 AH/847 CE) wrote *al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr wa-l-Muqabala* (The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing). In it he solved quadratic equations systematically, according to their six types, with geometric proofs. The word "algebra" (*al-jabr*) derives from it. Then al-Karaji (d. c. 420 AH / 1029 CE) wrote *al-Fakhri*, which developed an algebra of polynomials. Al-Samaw'al al-Maghribi (d. c. 570–576 AH / 1175–1180 CE) carried this direction further in *al-Bahir fi al-Jabr*. 'Umar al-Khayyam (d. c. 526 AH/1131 CE) treated cubic equations by the intersection of conic sections in his treatise on algebra. As for Ghiyath al-Din al-Kashani (d. 832 AH/1429 CE), he calculated the approximate ratio of the circumference to the diameter to sixteen decimal places in *al-Risala al-Muhitiyya*.

## Astronomy

Astronomers compiled many astronomical tables (*zijes*). Among them is the *Zij al-Sabi'* of al-Battani (d. 317 AH/929 CE), which corrected Ptolemy's values. Ibn al-Haytham, in *al-Shukuk 'ala Batlamyus* (Doubts concerning Ptolemy), criticized the astronomical models for failing to agree with a coherent physics of the spheres. At the Maragha observatory, founded in 657 AH / 1259 CE under the Ilkhanid ruler Hulagu, with Nasir al-Din al-Tusi as its guiding figure, astronomers formulated the "Tusi couple" (*zawj al-Tusi*), a geometric device that produces a linear oscillation from two circular motions. Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi and Ibn al-Shatir al-Dimashqi (d. 777 AH / 1375 CE) developed lunar and planetary models that did not use Ptolemy's equant. The models of Ibn al-Shatir match, in their geometry, devices that Copernicus later used. George Saliba has argued that the match makes transmission likely. No direct evidence of Copernicus's access to these models has been documented, and other historians leave open the possibility of independent convergence.

## Institutions

Patronage and institutions sustained the work. In the reign of al-Ma'mun, observers measured a degree of the meridian on the plain of Sinjar. Ulugh Beg built an observatory at Samarkand in the 1420s. In Istanbul, Taqi al-Din Muhammad ibn Ma'ruf (d. 993 AH / 1585 CE) directed an observatory founded in 1577 and closed within a few years. E. S. Kennedy, in *A Survey of Islamic Astronomical Tables* (1956), catalogued the surviving astronomical handbooks (*zijes*).

## Limits of the Picture

One may exaggerate by saying that the Arabs "invented" everything. The accurate view is that the numerals are of Indian origin, and that algebra drew on earlier Babylonian, Greek and Indian traditions of problem solving. The similarity between late Islamic planetary models and Copernicus is not, by itself, proof of transmission. It is also a mistake to say that scientific work stopped after al-Ghazali. The observatories and teaching traditions of Maragha, Samarkand, and Istanbul belong to the centuries after his death.
