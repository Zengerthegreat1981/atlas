# Mathematics & Astronomy

Arabic mathematical science began with the translation of Euclid's *Elements* and Ptolemy's *Almagest* by al-Hajjaj ibn Yusuf ibn Matar, Ishaq ibn Hunayn and Thabit ibn Qurra. It quickly went beyond translation. Roshdi Rashed, in his study *The Development of Arabic Mathematics*, identifies the greatest achievement as the emergence of algebra as an independent discipline.

## Algebra and Arithmetic

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi (d. c. 232 AH/847 CE) wrote *al-Kitab al-Mukhtasar fi Hisab al-Jabr wa-l-Muqabala* (The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing). In it he solved quadratic equations systematically, according to their six types, with geometric proofs. The word "algebra" (*al-jabr*) derives from it. Then al-Karaji (d. c. 410 AH/1019 CE) wrote *al-Fakhri*, which introduced the calculus of polynomials. Al-Samaw'al al-Maghribi (d. 570 AH/1175 CE) carried this direction further in *al-Bahir*. 'Umar al-Khayyam (d. c. 526 AH/1131 CE) treated cubic equations by the intersection of conic sections in his treatise on algebra. As for Ghiyath al-Din al-Kashani (d. 832 AH/1429 CE), he calculated the approximate ratio of the circumference to the diameter to sixteen decimal places in *al-Risala al-Muhitiyya*.

## Astronomy

Astronomers compiled many astronomical tables (*zijes*). Among them is the *Zij al-Sabi'* of al-Battani (d. 317 AH/929 CE), which corrected Ptolemy's values. Ibn al-Haytham, in *al-Shukuk 'ala Batlamyus* (Doubts on Ptolemy), again criticized the astronomical model for failing to match physics. At the Maragha observatory established under the Ilkhanid ruler Hulagu, with Nasir al-Din al-Tusi as its guiding figure, in 657 AH/1259 CE, the "Tusi couple" (*zawj al-Tusi*) was formulated, a geometric device that converts circular motion into linear motion. Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi and Ibn al-Shatir al-Dimashqi (d. c. 777 AH/1375 CE) developed models that did not require the equant. George Saliba has shown that some of these models match mathematically what Copernicus later used, which makes it likely that they were transmitted by some route, although the route has not been proven.

## Institutions

Science was not an individual hobby. In the reign of al-Ma'mun, a degree of the meridian line was measured in the Sinjar desert, and in Samarkand Ulugh Beg built his observatory around 1420 CE. E. S. Kennedy, in *A Survey of Islamic Astronomical Tables* (1956), demonstrated how many of these zijes there were.

## Limits of the Picture

One may exaggerate by saying that the Arabs "invented" everything. The accurate view is that the numerals are of Indian origin, and that algebra inherited Babylonian and Indian computation. Moreover, what is called a transmission to Copernicus is a matter of debate: some hold that a convergence in instruments does not prove transmission. Nor is it valid to speak of science "stopping" after al-Ghazali, since the schools of Maragha, Samarkand and Istanbul continued for centuries afterward.
