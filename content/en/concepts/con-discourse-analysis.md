# Discourse Analysis

Discourse analysis is the study of language in use: how people talk and write, how texts are structured, and how language does social work — persuading, blaming, justifying, including, and excluding. It starts from the premise that language does not merely describe a world that already exists; it helps to construct that world. To call a group of people "migrants" rather than "refugees," or to describe a war as a "surgical operation," is not a neutral choice of words but a move that shapes what can be thought and done. Discourse analysis studies those moves, in everyday conversation, in the media, in political speeches, and in institutional documents, and it asks what versions of reality they produce and whose interests they serve.

## Definition

The term "discourse" is used in several senses. In linguistics it refers to language beyond the sentence: connected stretches of talk or text. In sociology and cultural studies, following Michel Foucault, it refers to a broader system of statements, practices, and institutions that produces knowledge about a subject and sets the limits of what can be said. A medical discourse, for example, is not only the words doctors use but the hospitals, training programmes, journals, and legal rules through which medical knowledge is authorised. Discourse analysis therefore operates at two levels. At the level of the text, it examines vocabulary, grammar, metaphor, narrative structure, and rhetorical strategies. At the level of the social, it examines how discourses are produced, circulated, and contested, and how they are tied to power. The analyst asks who is speaking, from what institutional position, to whom, using which taken-for-granted assumptions, and with what consequences for who is believed, who is blamed, and who is silenced.

## Origins and Development

Discourse analysis emerged from several traditions that converged in the 1960s and 1970s. In linguistics, the study of conversation and text moved beyond the sentence to the structure of whole interactions. In philosophy, the later Wittgenstein and the ordinary language philosophers argued that meaning lies in use, and that language games are woven into forms of life. Foucault's *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969) gave the sociological tradition its central text, analysing how discourses define their objects — madness, sexuality, criminality — and regulate who may speak about them. In Britain, Jonathan Potter and Margaret Wetherell's *Discourse and Social Psychology* (1987) developed a distinctive approach that treated attitudes and identities not as inner states but as things people construct in talk, using interpretative repertoires and rhetorical devices. Norman Fairclough's *Language and Power* (1989) founded critical discourse analysis, which explicitly connects the fine detail of texts to the workings of ideology and social power, and analyses how discourses sustain or challenge relations of domination. Teun van Dijk's work, including *Discourse and Power* (2008), extended this programme to the study of racism, news, and political discourse.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

Several approaches coexist under the name of discourse analysis, and their differences matter. Conversation analysis, developed by Harvey Sacks and his colleagues, studies the fine-grained organisation of talk — turn-taking, pauses, repairs — and insists on grounding claims in what participants themselves display in interaction, resisting the analyst's importation of external categories. Critical discourse analysis, by contrast, starts from social theory and asks how texts reproduce ideology and inequality, which its critics say risks reading the analyst's politics into the data rather than discovering them there. A related debate concerns the status of reality: if discourse constructs the world, does anything exist outside discourse? Most discourse analysts accept that a material world exists but argue that our access to it is always mediated by language, and that the sociologically interesting question is which mediations prevail and why. Another dimension is the scale of analysis. Some studies examine a single conversation in detail; others trace a discourse across decades of newspaper coverage or parliamentary debate. The choice of scale determines what the analysis can show, and the best work is explicit about it.

## Contemporary Relevance

Discourse analysis is widely used across the social sciences today because so much of social life is now conducted through language and text. Researchers analyse political speeches and social media to understand how populist leaders construct "the people" and its enemies; they study medical consultations to see how doctors and patients negotiate diagnoses; they examine corporate sustainability reports to see how companies present themselves as responsible while continuing harmful practices; and they study courtroom talk to see how credibility is established and undermined. The digital age has multiplied both the material available for analysis and the stakes. Online discourse spreads rapidly, is archived permanently, and is shaped by algorithms that amplify some voices and suppress others. The study of misinformation, hate speech, and conspiracy theories has become a major application of the approach. At the same time, the rise of automated text analysis has created a productive tension: computational methods can process discourse at a scale no human analyst can match, but they cannot easily capture irony, context, and the subtle work of implication that discourse analysis was designed to reveal. The two approaches are increasingly combined.
