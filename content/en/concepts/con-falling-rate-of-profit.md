# The Falling Rate of Profit

The **falling rate of profit** is a law developed by **Karl Marx** in volume three of *Capital*, drafted in the 1860s and published posthumously by Friedrich Engels in 1894. It describes a structural tendency Marx attributes to capitalism that pushes the aggregate rate of profit toward decline over time, even though capitalists continue to pursue profit by every means available to them.

## The organic composition of capital

Marx builds this law on his concept of the **organic composition of capital**: the ratio between "constant" capital (machinery and raw materials) and "variable" capital (wages paid to workers). Marx assumes that competition among capitalists constantly pushes them to replace living labour with machines in pursuit of a competitive advantage (lower costs, higher productivity), which gradually raises the organic composition of capital across the economy as a whole.

## The basic logic of the law

Since living labour — not machines — is for Marx the sole source of surplus value, a falling share of living labour in the total capital invested means, mathematically, that the ratio of surplus value to total invested capital (the rate of profit) tends to decline, even if the rate of exploitation of workers (the ratio of surplus value to wages) remains constant or rises.

## Counteracting factors and the crisis cycle

Marx himself acknowledged the existence of "counteracting factors" that may slow or temporarily reverse this tendency: intensified exploitation of labour (lengthening or intensifying the working day), the depression of wages below the value of labour power, the cheapening of the elements of constant capital through technical progress itself, the relative surplus population, and foreign trade. For this reason Marx describes the law as a **tendency** rather than a mechanical inevitability operating without interruption: a structural force that interacts with counter-forces, producing recurring cyclical crises rather than an immediate and permanent final collapse.

## Later influence on the analysis of capitalist crisis

This law became one of the most important analytical tools used by later Marxists to explain the cyclical crises of capitalism. It faced sharp technical and methodological criticism from later economists, however, including the Japanese economist Nobuo Okishio, who showed in 1961 that, if the real wage is held constant, a cost-reducing technique adopted by capitalists cannot lower the general rate of profit. This (the Okishio theorem) casts doubt on the law's inevitability without additional assumptions, such as rising real wages; defenders and critics have debated the point since. The French Regulation School later addressed the question of capitalism's stability and crises from an alternative institutional angle, relying less on this law alone.
