# Organ Transplant Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Organ transplant ethics is a branch of applied medical ethics that examines the legitimacy of transferring an organ from one body to another, the conditions for obtaining it from a living or deceased donor, and the justice of distributing scarce organs among patients awaiting them. It intersects with questions about the limits of a person's ownership of their own body, the definition of death itself (brain death versus cessation of the heart), and the permissibility of financial compensation in exchange for donation.

## The Position in Islamic Thought

Contemporary jurists addressed the matter in light of the hadith that breaking the bone of a dead person is like breaking it while he is alive, reported in collections including Abu Dawud and Ibn Majah. Some jurists therefore favored absolutely prohibiting mutilation of the corpse. However, the International Islamic Fiqh Academy of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation, in its resolution of 1988 (fourth session, Jeddah), permitted transplanting an organ from a dead person when it is essential to keep the recipient alive or restores a primary bodily function, with authorization from the deceased before death, from the heirs, or from the competent authority. Such permission is commonly justified by necessity and the preservation of life, one of the overarching objectives of Islamic law according to al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi, on the view that preserving the life of the living takes precedence over the sanctity of the corpse when the two cannot be reconciled. For living donors, the resolution permitted giving another person an organ that renews itself, such as blood or skin, from a legally competent donor. It forbade removing a vital organ, or one whose loss would deprive the donor of a primary bodily function, ruled out the sale of organs, and left cases it did not cover for further study. As for ruling brain death acceptable as a legal criterion of death, this has remained a matter of disagreement between those who regard it as actual death, to which the rulings of death apply (among them most contemporary jurisprudential councils), and those who are cautious about it and hold to the traditional criterion of cessation of the heart.

## The Position in Western Philosophy

In Western traditions, the debate has been divided between the school of "self-ownership", influenced by John Locke, which regards the body as the property of the person, who may dispose of it by sale or donation with complete freedom, and Kant's position, which fundamentally prohibited treating parts of the body as commodities because this corrupts the dignity of the person and treats the human being as a means rather than an end. Some contemporary Kantians have, however, exempted free donation not made in exchange for payment. Contemporary bioethics also discusses brain-death criteria (such as "whole-brain death"), which grew out of the 1968 report of the Harvard Medical School ad hoc committee on irreversible coma, a landmark in the definition of brain death. Brain-death criteria made it possible to widen the source of organs to include people declared brain-dead whose hearts continue to beat with the aid of life-support machines.

## Questions of Distributive Justice

The acute shortage of available organs relative to the number of people waiting raises the question of justice in distribution. Should an organ be given to whoever registered first, to whoever has the better medical chance of a successful graft, or to whoever is most in need and closest to death? Most medical systems today adopt a mixture of these criteria through centralized allocation algorithms. The sale of organs, as it actually occurs in some poor countries, is rejected morally by the majority of jurists and philosophers alike, because it exploits the need of the poor and turns the body into a commodity in an unequal market.

## Illustrative Example

When one kidney is available for three patients (a child in urgent need, an elderly person who has been first on the waiting list for years, and a person expected to live the longest after the transplant), the ethical trade-off between criteria of priority, need and expected benefit emerges. Medical ethics committees attempt to resolve this trade-off through published protocols and criteria subject to review.

## Further Primary Sources

Law and policy documents are central sources in this field. In the United States, the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act (1968) provided a framework for donating organs after death, the Uniform Determination of Death Act (1981) recognized irreversible cessation either of circulatory and respiratory functions or of all functions of the entire brain, and the National Organ Transplant Act (1984) prohibited buying and selling organs for transplantation. The President's Commission report *Defining Death* (1981) set out the reasoning for a whole-brain standard, and the President's Council on Bioethics revisited the controversy in 2008. Internationally, the World Health Organization's Guiding Principles on Human Cell, Tissue and Organ Transplantation (endorsed in 2010) address consent and non-commercialization, and the Declaration of Istanbul (2008) condemns organ trafficking and transplant tourism. The Council of Europe adopted an Additional Protocol on transplantation in 2002. Among ethical works, Richard Titmuss's *The Gift Relationship* (1970), a study of blood donation, shaped arguments for voluntary, unpaid giving.
