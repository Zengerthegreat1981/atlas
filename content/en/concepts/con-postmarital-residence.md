# Postmarital Residence: Where a New Couple Goes to Live

**Postmarital residence** is the rule or pattern governing where a married couple lives: with or near the husband's kin, the wife's kin, the husband's mother's brother, or in a new household of their own. The question seems small — where does the couple sleep? — but anthropologists have treated it as one of the keys to social organisation. Where a couple lives determines whose kin are near at hand, who controls land and labour, who cares for children and the old, and how political alliances are built. Residence shapes kinship, and kinship shapes residence, in a circle that links the intimate decision of where to live to the largest structures of society.

## Definition and Development

The comparative study of residence was shaped by George Peter Murdock's *Social Structure* (1949), a large cross-cultural survey of kinship. Murdock classified the world's societies by their residence rule: patrilocal or virilocal residence, in which the couple lives with or near the husband's kin; matrilocal or uxorilocal residence, in which they live with or near the wife's kin; avunculocal residence, in which they live with the husband's mother's brother; bilocal residence, in which they may live with either side; and neolocal residence, in which they set up a new household independent of both. Murdock's survey showed that patrilocal residence is the most common pattern worldwide, found in a majority of societies in most samples, while matrilocal residence is the next most common, found in a minority. Neolocal residence, the pattern familiar in modern industrial societies, is rare outside them.

Murdock also proposed explanations. He argued that residence follows the division of labour: the sex that contributes most to subsistence keeps its kin close, so that where men do most of the work, residence is patrilocal, and where women do most, it is matrilocal. He also argued that residence follows warfare and migration: prolonged male absence, depopulation, and the need to break up male kin groups can all push a society toward matrilocality. These hypotheses launched half a century of cross-cultural testing, in which anthropologists used the Human Relations Area Files and statistical samples to test one explanation against another.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Melvin Ember and Carol R. Ember, in "The Conditions Favoring Matrilocal Versus Patrilocal Residence" (1971), tested Murdock's division-of-labour hypothesis and found it wanting: worldwide, the sex that contributes most to subsistence does not reliably predict the residence rule. What did predict it was warfare. Patrilocal residence is favoured by internal warfare — fighting within a society, between its own communities — because keeping brothers together provides a ready-made fighting force. Matrilocal residence is favoured by purely external warfare — fighting only against outsiders, because the dispersal of men among their wives' villages prevents the formation of the fraternal interest groups that fight internal wars. Residence, in this account, is a political arrangement, shaped by the demands of war and peace.

William Divale, in "Migration, External Warfare, and Matrilocal Residence" (1974), added migration to the story. Matrilocal residence, he argued, often develops when a patrilocal society migrates into an already inhabited region: the newcomers face external warfare with the indigenous population, and breaking up their fraternal groups by dispersing men among their wives' villages is an adaptive response. Divale tested the argument cross-culturally and reported support for it. Later work by Carol Ember and others refined these findings, showing that the predictors of residence differ between world regions and between subsistence systems, and that no single factor explains the pattern everywhere.

## Ethnographic Examples

Ethnography shows how residence is lived. Among the Iroquois of North America, matrilocal residence made women the anchors of the longhouse: a man moved into his wife's household, his sisters' sons inherited through the female line, and clan mothers held the power to appoint and depose chiefs. Among the Trobriand Islanders, descent is matrilineal: a boy typically grows up in his father's village, and later usually moves to live in his mother's brother's village, where his own lineage holds land (avunculocal residence), although some sons, notably of high-ranking men, remain with their fathers. Lineage and inheritance run through the mother's brother while childhood is spent with the father, a reminder that residence and descent are separate principles. Among the Nuer of South Sudan, patrilocal residence kept brothers together in cattle camps, providing the solidarity needed for feuding and defence.

Residence is also a matter of negotiation and change. A couple may begin married life with one set of kin and move later; a husband may live matrilocally for a period of bride service and then take his wife to his own village. In many societies, the rule is a preference rather than a command, and the actual pattern is a compromise between the rule, the availability of land and housing, and the personal wishes of the couple and their kin.

## Contemporary Debates

Postmarital residence remains a live topic, now connected to new questions. One debate concerns its consequences for women's status: matrilocal residence is often associated with higher female status, because a woman surrounded by her own kin is better protected and better able to control resources — but the association is not automatic.

A second debate concerns the present. Urbanisation, wage labour, and housing markets are making neolocal residence the norm in much of the world, even in societies where patrilocal or matrilocal rules once governed. Anthropologists ask what is lost and gained in the change: neolocal residence gives couples independence, but it also removes them from the daily support of kin — the childcare, the shared meals, the help in hard times — that residence with kin provided.
