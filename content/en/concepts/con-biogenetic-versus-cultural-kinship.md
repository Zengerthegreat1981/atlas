# Biogenetic versus Cultural Kinship

A critical concept formulated by the anthropologist David Schneider to show that the prevailing assumption in the study of kinship is an ethnocentric Western premise rather than a universal truth. That assumption holds that "biological" relations (procreation, blood, genes) are the universal basis on which all human kinship systems are built, and that cultural differences among them are merely "interpretations" or "social arrangements" of this shared natural foundation.

## Core Argument

In *American Kinship: A Cultural Account* (1968), Schneider analyzed the kinship system of white middle-class Americans as **a cultural system of symbols and meanings**, not a network of functional social roles. He showed that Americans themselves distinguish between two types of relation that together make up kinship for them: the relation of "nature" or "biological fact" (blood), and the relation of "law" or "conduct" (law/code for conduct, such as marriage, adoption, and close friendship). Most importantly, both types, including what is presumed to be "purely biological," are in fact **cultural categories**. That "blood" is the basis of kinship is not a natural truth to be discovered, but a culturally constructed American belief. This is shown by the fact that other cultures (and even other groups within American society itself, such as adoptive and spiritual kinship) do not give the biological tie this central rank.

Schneider developed this argument radically in *A Critique of the Study of Kinship* (1984). There he argued that the concept of "kinship" itself, as an anthropological analytical category that is universal and comparable across cultures, is in turn a projection of a Western category (based on the assumption of the universality of the sexual-reproductive tie) onto ethnographic materials that may not distinguish at all between the "biological" and the "social." Those materials may build relations that anthropologists translate as "kinship" on entirely different foundations (sharing food, living together, care, or land).

## Supporting Arguments from Ethnographic Studies

Schneider and his successors drew on ethnographic cases showing that "biological truth" is not always the decisive reference. In some Melanesian and Amazonian societies, it is believed that the fetus is formed through repeated bodily work (repeated intercourse or sharing food) rather than from a single "moment of conception." In systems of **adoption** and spiritual kinship (such as Christian godparenthood, or milk kinship in Islamic jurisprudence, where nursing creates full marriage prohibition without any blood tie), non-biological ties are treated as "real" kinship in terms of rights and duties. This undermines the idea that "blood" is the decisive criterion universally.

## Impact

This concept produced a revolution in the study of anthropological kinship. The field shifted from searching for universal laws of descent and alliance (as in Morgan, Radcliffe-Brown, or Lévi-Strauss) to studying how each culture builds its own meaning of "kinship" as a local symbolic system. This approach paved the way for a later wave of **new kinship studies**, which addressed transnational adoption, kinship based on assisted reproductive technologies (artificial insemination, surrogacy), queer and same-sex kinship, and multispecies kinship. All of these start from Schneider's premise that what counts as "natural" in kinship is itself a cultural construction that can change and be negotiated.

## Limits

Several anthropologists, including Janet Carsten, objected that Schneider's critique, though powerful, may tend to deny any importance to the body and procreation in the lived experience of kinship. Later studies show that people themselves often flexibly mix "biological" and "social" considerations in understanding their relationships, rather than separating them sharply as the theoretical framing of Schneider might suggest. Other researchers noted that generalizing Schneider's conclusions, drawn mainly from interviews with white middle-class Americans in the 1960s, to "the West" as a whole requires the same methodological caution that he himself directed at Morgan's generalizations.
