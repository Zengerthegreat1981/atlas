# Justice

Justice is the idea that people should receive what they are due, and that wrongs should be put right. It is at once a moral ideal, a set of institutions, and a language in which people make claims on one another. Every society has some notion of fairness — who deserves what, what counts as a wrong, and who may judge — but the answers differ widely. Anthropologists study justice not as a single universal standard but as a lived practice: how people argue about what is fair, settle disputes, punish offences, and distribute goods in ways their own communities recognise as right.

## Definition

In its broadest sense, justice concerns the fair treatment of persons and the fair distribution of goods, burdens, and honours. Philosophers traditionally distinguish several kinds. Distributive justice asks how resources and opportunities should be shared. Retributive justice concerns punishment for wrongdoing. Restorative justice focuses on repairing harm and restoring relationships rather than inflicting suffering on the offender. Procedural justice asks whether the processes by which decisions are made are themselves fair, whatever the outcome. Anthropology adds a comparative dimension: in small-scale societies without courts or written law, justice is often achieved through negotiation, compensation, mediation by elders, and public pressure rather than through formal trials. What counts as a just outcome is therefore inseparable from local ideas about persons, kinship, and the social order.

## Origins and Development

Reflection on justice is as old as recorded thought. In ancient Greece, Aristotle distinguished between distributive justice, the proportional allocation of goods according to merit, and corrective justice, the rectification of unfair transactions. Roman law developed these ideas into a systematic legal order, and medieval Christian and Islamic thinkers wove them into theologies of divine justice. The modern period transformed the question: the Enlightenment made justice a matter of universal rights rather than of status or divine decree, and the great political revolutions of the eighteenth century wrote equality before the law into constitutions. In the twentieth century, John Rawls reframed the debate by asking what principles of distribution free and equal persons would choose if they did not know their own position in society — his famous "veil of ignorance." Meanwhile, anthropologists working in Africa, Melanesia, and elsewhere documented dispute settlement systems that operated without states, showing that justice could be achieved through consensus and compensation rather than coercion.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns whether justice is universal or culturally relative. Universalists argue that certain principles, such as the prohibition of arbitrary killing or the right to a hearing, hold everywhere. Relativists reply that notions of fairness are embedded in particular ways of life, and that imposing one society's standards on another is itself unjust. A second debate opposes formal equality — treating everyone identically — to substantive equality, which recognises that people start from unequal positions and may need unequal treatment to reach a fair outcome. A third debate concerns punishment: should the state punish to deter, to exact retribution, or to rehabilitate? The rise of restorative justice, drawing partly on Indigenous practices such as Māori conferencing in New Zealand and community courts in Africa, has challenged the dominance of punitive models. Finally, feminist and postcolonial critics have argued that classical theories of justice ignored gender, race, and empire, treating as universal principles that were in fact the product of a narrow historical experience.

## Contemporary Relevance

Justice remains one of the most contested words in public life. Debates over inequality, taxation, climate responsibility, reparations for slavery and colonialism, and the reform of criminal justice systems all turn on rival conceptions of what is fair. Transitional justice — the attempt to reckon with mass atrocity through truth commissions, trials, and reparations — has become a major field of practice and scholarship. The same questions arise in debates over algorithmic fairness, where decisions about credit, hiring, and policing are increasingly made by automated systems whose outcomes must still be judged fair. Anthropologists contribute by showing how global ideals of human rights are translated, resisted, and remade in local settings, and by documenting the informal justice systems that most of the world's people actually use. The study of justice thus connects abstract philosophy to the everyday work of resolving disputes and living together.
