# Functionalism in Philosophy of Mind

The dominant theory in analytic philosophy of mind since the 1960s, holding that mental states are defined by their function, that is, by the causal relations among inputs, outputs, and other mental states, not by the matter that constitutes them. This entry deals with functionalism in general and its branches, while its first founding formulation (machine functionalism in Putnam, 1967) is confined to a separate entry.

## Roots

Some scholars hold that the idea goes back to Aristotle, who defined the soul in *De Anima* (II, 1) as "the first actuality of a natural organized body," that is, its form and function, not its matter. Putnam and Nussbaum (1992) proposed a functional reading of Aristotle, but others objected that Aristotle did not separate function from its living matter. In the Islamic tradition Averroes (Ibn Rushd) follows Aristotle in treating the soul as the form of the body (though he held that the material intellect is separate), whereas Avicenna (Ibn Sina) argues that the rational soul is a separable substance (as in his "flying man" thought experiment); the first position is sometimes regarded as closer to the spirit of functionalism.

## Types

- Machine functionalism (Putnam, "Minds and Machines" 1960 and "Psychological Predicates" 1967): mental states are computational states of a machine (a probabilistic automaton), and "pain" is not a particular physical state (firing of C-fibers) but the state that is caused by bodily damage and causes withdrawal and groaning.
- Analytic or folk functionalism (David Lewis 1972, and Armstrong 1968): defines mental states by the causal roles determined by commonsense folk wisdom about the mind.
- Psychofunctionalism (Fodor 1968, *Psychological Explanation*): the roles are determined by a mature theory of cognitive psychology, not by common sense.
- Homuncular functionalism (Dennett, Lycan): breaks function down into simpler functions performed by little "dwarfs" until it ends in mechanical operations.
- Teleological functionalism (Millikan 1984): function is what a thing was selected for evolutionarily.

## Arguments in Favor

- It solves the problem of "multiple realizability": pain does not belong to the human brain alone, but may exist in silicon or alien beings.
- It links philosophy of mind with cognitive science and neuroscience.
- It avoids the problems of behaviorism and identity theory together.

## Criticism

- The problem of absent and inverted qualia (Block and Fodor 1972), the "China brain" thought experiment, and the problem of chauvinism versus liberalism (Block 1978): functionalism may grant a mind to what has none or deprive of it what has one.
- Searle in "Minds, Brains, and Programs" (1980): function alone does not give real "understanding."
- The knowledge argument and the zombie argument (Jackson 1982, Chalmers 1996): function does not explain the subjective quality of consciousness.
- Putnam's retreat (1988) because of arguments from wide content and triviality.

## Limits and Open Questions

Whether functionalism implies that artificial systems, including contemporary large language models, could have minds is disputed, and depends on the level of abstraction at which function is defined. Another open question is whether functional organisation can account for conscious experience.
