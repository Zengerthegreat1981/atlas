# Functionalism in Philosophy of Mind

Functionalism has been the dominant theory of mental states in analytic philosophy of mind and cognitive science since the 1960s. It holds that mental states are defined by their functional roles—that is, by their characteristic causal relations to sensory inputs, behavioral outputs, and other mental states—rather than by the underlying physical material (such as biological neurons or silicon chips) that realizes them.

## Historical Roots and Classical Precursors

While functionalism developed in mid-twentieth-century analytic philosophy, scholars often identify historical antecedents in Aristotle's psychology. In *De Anima* (Book II, Ch. 1), Aristotle defined the soul (*psyche*) as the "first actuality of a natural organized body," conceptualizing life and mind in terms of form, organization, and capacities rather than pure material constituents. Modern interpreters, notably Martha Nussbaum and Hilary Putnam (1992), proposed a functionalist reading of Aristotle, although critics like Myles Burnyeat countered that Aristotelian forms cannot be abstracted from the specific organic matter of living creatures.

In the medieval Islamic tradition, Averroes (Ibn Rushd) followed Aristotle in treating the soul as the form of the living body, whereas Avicenna (Ibn Sina) defended the substantial independence of the rational soul through his "floating man" thought experiment. The Aristotelian and Averroistic emphasis on formal organization over sheer substrate is frequently viewed as foreshadowing the core intuition of functionalism.

## Major Varieties of Functionalism

Functionalism developed into several distinct theoretical programs:

- **Machine Functionalism**: Pioneered by Hilary Putnam ("Minds and Machines", 1960; "Psychological Predicates", 1967), this view models the mind on a Turing machine or probabilistic automaton. A mental state is identified with an internal computational state defined by its transition table, linking perceptual inputs, internal transitions, and behavioral outputs.
- **Analytic (Folk-Psychological) Functionalism**: Developed by David Lewis (1972) and D. M. Armstrong (1968), this approach uses Ramsey-Lewis sentence reduction to define mental state terms via the causal roles implicit in everyday commonsense psychology.
- **Psychofunctionalism**: Articulated by Jerry Fodor (*Psychological Explanation*, 1968), this version rejects folk concepts in favor of empirical discovery, defining functional roles through the empirical theories of mature cognitive psychology.
- **Homuncular Functionalism**: Associated with Daniel Dennett (*Brainstorms*, 1978) and William Lycan, this approach explains complex mental operations by decomposing them into sub-capacities managed by simpler subsystems ("homunculi"), cascading downward until operations are realized by basic neurocomputational mechanisms.
- **Teleological Functionalism**: Formulated by Ruth Garrett Millikan (*Language, Thought, and Other Biological Categories*, 1984), this view defines functional roles through evolutionary etiology—what a cognitive mechanism was selected by natural evolution to do.

## Core Advantages

Functionalism gained widespread acceptance because:
1. **Multiple Realizability**: It accommodates the intuition that diverse physical systems (humans, cephalopods, or extraterrestrial beings) can share the same mental state (e.g., pain) despite radically different neurochemistries.
2. **Avoiding Reductive Pitfalls**: It overcomes both the eliminative behaviorism of the mid-twentieth century (by admitting genuine internal mental states) and mind-brain type identity theory (by denying that mental types are identical to specific biological neural types).
3. **Integration with Cognitive Science**: It provides a philosophical foundation for computer science, artificial intelligence, and cognitive psychology.

## Criticisms and Controversies

Functionalism has faced influential objections:
- **Qualia and Phenomenal Consciousness**: Critics argue that functionalism leaves out the subjective "what it is like" of experience. Ned Block and Jerry Fodor (1972) introduced the absent qualia and inverted qualia arguments. Block (1978) formulated the "China brain" (or China body) thought experiment, arguing that a nation of individuals simulating the functional organization of a brain would satisfy functional criteria without possessing a single unified conscious experience, exposing functionalism to the charge of "liberalism" (attributing mentality to entities that lack it).
- **The Chinese Room Argument**: John Searle (1980) argued that purely formal or functional manipulation of symbols does not generate semantic understanding or intentionality.
- **Epistemic and Metaphysical Challenges**: Frank Jackson's knowledge argument (1982) and David Chalmers's zombie argument (1996) contend that functional descriptions leave an unbridgeable explanatory gap regarding phenomenal experience.
- **Putnam's Reversal**: Putnam himself later retreated from machine functionalism in *Representation and Reality* (1988), arguing that mental contents depend on external physical and linguistic environments (semantic externalism) and cannot be captured by internal computational states alone.
