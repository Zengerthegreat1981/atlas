# Phratry

A phratry is a grouping of several clans that recognise a common, usually remote, ancestry and a shared identity, without being able to trace the exact genealogical links between them. The term comes from the Greek *phratria*, meaning brotherhood, and it was borrowed by anthropologists from the study of ancient Greece, where the phratry was a subdivision of the tribe. In anthropological usage, the phratry sits between the clan and the tribe or moiety in scale: clans are its constituent units, and several phratries may together make up a tribe. It is distinct from the moiety, which divides a society into exactly two halves, and from the clan, which is a single descent group whose members claim a common ancestor. The phratry is a looser, more inclusive grouping, held together by a shared name, shared totems, shared ritual obligations and a sense of common origin.

## Origins and Development

The concept of the phratry emerged from the comparative study of kinship in the nineteenth century. Lewis Henry Morgan described phratries among the Iroquois, where the eight clans of the Seneca were grouped into two phratries, each with its own name, and where the phratry played a role in council deliberations, in mourning and in the condolence ceremonies that installed new chiefs. Lorimer Fison and Alfred Howitt described large exogamous divisions in Australia, which some writers of the period also called phratries, although where there are only two they are now usually termed moieties. Robert Lowie, in *Primitive Society*, treated the phratry as one of the standard units of social organisation, intermediate between the clan and the tribe, and noted that its functions varied widely: in some societies it regulated marriage, in others it organised warfare or ritual, and in many it did little more than provide a shared identity and a forum for cooperation.

Phratries typically function in three ways. First, they provide a framework for exogamy: in some societies marriage is forbidden not only within the clan but within the whole phratry, so the phratry is the effective unit of marriage regulation. Second, they organise collective action: phratries may field warriors together, contribute to feasts and ceremonies, or act as units in games and contests. Third, they serve as a level of political organisation: phratry leaders may sit on tribal councils, mediate disputes between their member clans, and represent the phratry in dealings with other phratries. Among the Iroquois, for example, Morgan reported that in the condolence ceremony the phratry not in mourning consoled the bereaved side, as part of the process of raising up a new chief to replace one who had died. In ancient Greece, the phratry was the group that validated citizenship: a child was presented to the phratry, and membership confirmed the child's status as a member of the polis.

## Key Debates

A central debate concerns whether the phratry is a genuine social unit or merely a classificatory convenience. Because phratry members often cannot trace their exact genealogical connections, some anthropologists have argued that the phratry is a fiction — a claim of common ancestry without demonstrable descent — and that its importance has been exaggerated by theorists eager to find neat hierarchies of clan, phratry and tribe. Others reply that the inability to trace descent is precisely the point: the phratry is a grouping based on putative rather than demonstrated ancestry, and its social reality lies in the actions its members take together, not in their genealogy. A related debate asks how phratries relate to moieties: some societies have both, with phratries nested inside moieties, while others have phratries without moieties, suggesting that the two are independent principles of organisation that can be combined in different ways. A third debate concerns the phratry's political significance: in societies without centralised government, the phratry may be the largest unit that acts together, and its strength or weakness shapes the capacity for collective action.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The phratry is less prominent in contemporary anthropology than the clan or the moiety, partly because the classic kinship studies that documented it have given way to studies of practice, identity and politics. But the concept still matters. In Indigenous North America, clan and phratry identities remain central to political organisation, to the revival of chiefly installation ceremonies, and to claims over land and heritage. In the study of ancient Greece, the phratry remains a key institution for understanding citizenship and political participation. More broadly, the phratry illustrates a general anthropological insight: that human societies organise themselves through nested, overlapping groups whose boundaries are defined as much by shared stories, names and rituals as by blood — and that belonging is often a matter of recognised affiliation rather than demonstrable descent.
