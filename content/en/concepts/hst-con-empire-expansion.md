# Empire-Building and Imperial Expansion

The dynamics, justification, and internal consequences of imperial expansion constituted one of the central concerns of ancient Greco-Roman historiography. Rather than treating territorial conquest merely as a sequence of triumphant campaigns, classical historians systematically scrutinized why empires expand, how imperial rule is maintained or corrupted, and what ethical and institutional costs are borne by the conquerors as well as the conquered.

## Classical Greek Perspectives: Hubris and Realpolitik

The analysis of imperial overreach originated in Herodotus's *Histories*, which framed Persian expansion under Cyrus, Cambyses, Darius, and Xerxes through the theological-moral paradigm of *hubris* and *nemesis*. Herodotus depicted imperial ambition as an inherent drive to transgress natural and geographical boundaries—such as bridging the Hellespont or invading Scythia and Greece. In Herodotus's historical vision, unbounded imperial expansion inevitably provokes divine retribution and brings about catastrophic reversal.

In contrast, Thucydides examined imperial power (*arche*) through the lens of political realism in his *History of the Peloponnesian War*. During the debate at Sparta (1.75–76), Athenian envoys articulated the fundamental motives that drive imperial expansion: fear (*deos*), honor (*time*), and self-interest or profit (*opheleia*). Thucydides has the Athenians argue that once established, an empire cannot easily be surrendered without existential risk. In the famous Melian Dialogue (5.85–113), Athenian ambassadors expressed the unrestrained ethos of imperial domination, declaring that in human affairs justice is only debated between equals in power, whereas the strong do what they have the power to do and the weak suffer what they must.

## Polybius and the Universal Rise of Rome

In Hellenistic historiography, Polybius made Rome's rapid subjugation of the Mediterranean the central subject of his work. In the prologue to his *Histories*, Polybius posed his governing question: how and under what system of polity Rome succeeded in less than fifty-three years (220–167 BCE) in bringing almost the entire inhabited world (*oikoumene*) under its solitary rule.

Unlike earlier Greek historians who attributed geopolitical shifts to capricious fortune (*tyche*), Polybius argued that Rome's imperial ascendancy was the calculated result of institutional excellence, exceptional military discipline, and a resilient mixed constitution. Polybius also examined the broader consequences of universal empire, noting that imperial hegemony brought peace but simultaneously removed external competitors, setting the stage for future internal instability.

## Roman Historiography: Just War and Moral Decline

Roman historians developed complex ideological justifications for imperial conquest while paradoxically diagnosing empire-building as the principal cause of domestic moral decay. In republican political thought, as articulated by Cicero in *De Re Publica* and *De Officiis*, Roman conquests were legitimized through the doctrine of the "just war" (*bellum iustum*), asserting that Rome waged war only to honor treaties, defend allies (*socii*), or retaliate against unprovoked aggression. Livy echoed these themes in *Ab Urbe Condita*, framing Rome's expansion as a destiny sanctioned by the gods and earned through ancestral piety and bravery.

Conversely, Sallust offered an influential critique of imperial success in *Bellum Catilinae* and *Bellum Iugurthinum*. Sallust argued that the elimination of external threats—symbolized by the destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE—unleashed unbounded greed (*avaritia*) and ambition (*ambitio*), transforming an empire built on virtue into an engine of factional strife and tyranny.

Under the Principate, Tacitus interrogated the dark reality of imperial dominion. In *Agricola* (ch. 30), Tacitus placed a scathing denunciation of Roman imperialism in the mouth of the Caledonian chieftain Calgacus: "To robbery, slaughter, and plunder they give the false name of empire; they create a desolation and call it peace" (*solitudinem faciunt, pacem appellant*). Through such speeches, Tacitus exposed the violent coercion and hypocrisy underpinning Roman imperial glory.
