# Narrative Identity in Religious Context

A concept used in narrative theology and comparative religious studies. The term "narrative identity" itself was developed in philosophy by Paul Ricoeur (*Time and Narrative*, 1983-85; *Oneself as Another*, 1990), and the idea of tradition-constituted narratives also appears in Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981). It describes how the narrative structures of religious texts and sacred stories define the identity of religious practitioners and communities.

## The Basic Definition

Religious narrative identity is not merely a set of "beliefs" held by the individual (such as "I believe that God exists"). It is a historical and collective way of understanding oneself within a great religious story. The basic question, "Who am I as a Christian, Muslim or Buddhist?", is not answered by a list of doctrines, but by: "I am a version of a character or a people that exists within a particular religious story."

## The Biblical Example

In Christianity, the narrative identity of the believer is linked to:
- The story of Adam and the Fall: "I am a sinner, corrupted."
- The story of Jesus and redemption: "I am saved through Christ's death and resurrection."
- The story of the Church and the eschatological promise: "I am a member of the body of Christ, and I will share in the coming kingdom."

These are not isolated beliefs but chapters in one continuous story that determines who I am now and who I will become.

## The Difference Between Narrative Identity and Traditional Doctrine

| Traditional doctrine | Narrative identity |
|---|---|
| "I believe that God exists" (a proposition) | "I am a person within a story God created" (a position within a narrative) |
| Focus on intellectual assent | Focus on position and role within a story |
| Can be separated from its narrative context | Deeply interwoven with the narrative context |

## Comparative Significance

Narrative identity differs radically according to the religious tradition:

- **Christianity**: identity is often framed through a story centred on Christ and the biblical narrative from creation to consummation.
- **Islam**: identity is anchored in the Qur'an, whose stories of earlier prophets are retold as models, together with the exemplary life of Muhammad (the *sira* and the *sunna*).
- **Buddhism**: identity is shaped by the life story of the Buddha and the Jataka tales of his previous lives, which serve as models of the path.

These are broad generalisations: each tradition contains many internal differences, and not every scholar accepts that narrative is the primary vehicle of religious identity (George Lindbeck's cultural-linguistic account, for instance, emphasises doctrine as the grammar of a community's language).

## Practical Implications

Understanding narrative identity explains the visible differences between religious practices:
- Why do Christians focus on the life of Jesus and imitation of him?
- Why do Muslims place such weight on the recitation and memorisation of the Qur'an and on the example of the Prophet?
- Why do Buddhists make use of the life story of the Buddha and the Jataka tales in teaching?
