# Polygyny

Polygyny is the marriage of one man to two or more wives at the same time. It is a form of polygamy — marriage involving more than two spouses — and the counterpart of polyandry, in which one woman has several husbands. Cross-cultural surveys have long shown polygyny to be the most common form of plural marriage in the ethnographic record: in George Peter Murdock's *Social Structure* (1949), the great majority of societies surveyed permitted polygyny, even though in most of them only a minority of men actually had more than one wife. Polygyny is therefore best understood not as a universal practice but as a socially permitted arrangement, governed by rules about wealth, status, seniority, and the rights of co-wives.

## Origins and Development

The comparative study of polygyny began with the early surveys of marriage systems. Murdock's cross-cultural data established that monogamy, though dominant in Europe and its settler societies, was a minority rule worldwide, and that polygyny was the preferred or permitted form in most of the societies then known to anthropology. The question then became why: what social and economic conditions favour plural wives, and what work does the institution do?

One influential answer came from the economist Ester Boserup, who in *Woman's Role in Economic Development* (1970) linked polygyny to hoe agriculture in sub-Saharan Africa, where women do much of the farming and additional wives mean additional cultivators and children. Jack Goody, in "Polygyny, Economy and the Role of Women" (in *The Character of Kinship*, 1973), tested and qualified this link, arguing that women's farm labour alone does not explain the distribution of polygyny and drawing attention to the later age at which men marry. In *Production and Reproduction* (1976) he contrasted African systems with Eurasian ones, where intensive agriculture, scarce land, and the transmission of property to children of both sexes, including through dowry, tend to favour monogamy.

Douglas White and Michael Burton, in their article "Causes of Polygyny: Ecology, Economy, Kinship, and Warfare" (1988), tested these claims statistically across a worldwide sample. They found that polygyny is associated with several factors at once, including fraternal interest groups, warfare involving plunder or the capture of women, and environmental conditions allowing expansion, while women's contribution to subsistence explained less than often assumed. No single cause suffices.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns women's experience. Early accounts, often written by male ethnographers, presented polygyny from the husband's point of view — as prestige, wealth, and sexual privilege. Feminist anthropology has complicated that picture. Co-wife relations are often marked by competition over resources and affection, but also by cooperation: co-wives may share childcare, domestic labour, and companionship, and senior wives frequently hold considerable authority in the household. Ethnography from West Africa shows that many women value polygyny when it brings labour, security, and social standing, and resent it when it brings neglect or inequality. The institution cannot be reduced to either female oppression or female advantage; its meaning depends on position within it.

A second dimension is the household economy. Polygynous households are typically organised around separate units — each wife with her own house, fields, and children — and the husband circulates among them. This arrangement creates distinctive patterns of inheritance, as children of different mothers form competing lines, and of authority, as the senior wife often mediates between the husband and junior wives. Anthropologists study these arrangements to understand how inequality is produced within families, not only between them.

A third dimension is the distinction between polygyny and serial monogamy. In societies where divorce and remarriage are common, a man may have several wives over a lifetime but only one at a time. Anthropologists insist on keeping these forms apart analytically, since simultaneous polygyny creates a distinctive social structure — a household of co-wives — that serial marriage does not.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Polygyny remains a contested institution. It is legally recognised in many African and Middle Eastern states, restricted or banned in others, and the subject of recurring debate about gender equality, children's welfare, and religious freedom. In South Africa, the Recognition of Customary Marriages Act (1998) gave legal standing to polygynous customary marriages while requiring a husband who wishes to marry again to apply to a court to approve a written contract governing the property regime of his marriages, with existing wives joined in the proceedings. In other settings, HIV/AIDS research in the 2000s raised questions about whether polygynous networks affect the spread of infection, with findings that remain disputed and highly dependent on local sexual and social practices.

Critics argue that anthropological attention to the "functions" of polygyny has sometimes obscured its costs, particularly for junior wives and for women with little bargaining power. Defenders reply that blanket condemnation repeats the colonial error of judging African marriage by European standards, and that reform should begin from the perspectives of the women involved. The most persuasive recent work does both: it documents the constraints polygyny imposes while taking seriously the reasons women themselves give for entering, remaining in, or leaving such marriages.
