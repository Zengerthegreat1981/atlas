# Epistemology in Islamic Thought

Islamic culture addressed the question of knowledge: what its sources are, what degrees of certainty it has, and what the relation is between reason, transmitted text and taste (*dhawq*). There is no single science called "epistemology" in its heritage. The answer is distributed across kalam, logic, the principles of jurisprudence and Sufism. Franz Rosenthal, in *Knowledge Triumphant* (1970), gathered the meaning of the term *'ilm* (knowledge) in Islamic civilization, and found it broader than the European notion of "knowledge", encompassing action, conduct and the attainment of salvation.

## Among the Theologians: The Causes of Knowledge

The Ash'aris and the Maturidis identified three causes of knowledge: sound senses, true report (*khabar*) and reason, and some added inspiration. Al-Baqillani (d. 403 AH/1013 CE) defined knowledge as "knowing the known as it is". The Mu'tazila distinguished between necessary knowledge (*al-'ilm al-daruri*) and acquired knowledge gained through reflection (*nazar*), and they made reflection obligatory for the responsible person.

## Among the Philosophers: Reason and Demonstration

Al-Farabi and then Ibn Sina tied knowledge to the theory of the Active Intellect. The soul is united with a separate intellect from which it receives intelligible forms, and this takes place through intuition (*hads*) in prophets and the intelligent. In *al-Isharat*, Ibn Sina treated intuition as a faculty that yields the middle term all at once. As for Ibn Rushd, he reaffirmed demonstration in *Fasl al-maqal* and held that the Sharia calls people by rhetoric, dialectic and demonstration according to their natures.

## Al-Ghazali: The Crisis of Certainty

In *al-Munqidh min al-dalal* (Deliverance from Error), al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH/1111 CE) recounts that he doubted both the senses and reason, until God restored his confidence through "a light cast into the breast". He counted four paths to knowledge: the theologians, the Batiniyya, the philosophers and the Sufis, and he favoured the path of taste (*dhawq*). W. Montgomery Watt studied his life in *The Faith and Practice of al-Ghazali* (1953).

## Ibn Taymiyya and al-Jabiri

Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728 AH/1328 CE), in *al-Radd 'ala al-mantiqiyyin* (Refutation of the Logicians), answered the philosophers and logicians by making innate nature (*fitra*), the senses and true report the sufficient foundations. In modern times Muhammad 'Abid al-Jabiri divided the Arab mind into *bayan* (explanation), *irfan* (gnosis) and *burhan* (demonstration) in his *Critique of Arab Reason* series, in *Takwin al-'aql al-'arabi* (The Formation of Arab Reason, 1984) and especially *Bunyat al-'aql al-'arabi* (The Structure of Arab Reason, 1986). This division is controversial in its attribution to the culture as a whole.

## Limits of Classification

Dividing systems of knowledge into *bayan*, *irfan* and *burhan* is useful, but it may freeze diversity. Ibn Sina, for instance, combines demonstration and gnosis, and al-Ghazali employs logic in the principles of jurisprudence. The reasoning "reason versus transmission" does not describe the position of each school precisely, since most scholars agreed that reason is a condition for understanding transmission.
