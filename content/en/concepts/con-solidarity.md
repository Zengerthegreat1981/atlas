# Solidarity: Social Cohesion and Mutual Obligation

**Solidarity** is the feeling and practice of shared fate that binds the members of a group together: the sense that each person bears part of the others' burdens and shares in their fortunes, not for private advantage but because membership itself demands it. It differs from charity, which is an individual gift from the fortunate to the unfortunate, and from self-interest, which calculates returns. Solidarity is collective and institutional as well as emotional: it appears in mutual aid, in trade unions, in welfare states, and in movements that defend people its members may never meet. It is one of sociology's oldest concepts, because it names the very thing that holds societies together.

## Islamic and Arab Perspectives

In the Islamic tradition, solidarity is expressed through the principle of *takaful* — mutual guarantee — institutionalised in the systems of *zakat* (obligatory almsgiving) and *waqf* (charitable endowment), and in the Qur'anic command to "cooperate in righteousness and piety." The Prophet Muhammad described the community of believers as a single body: when one part suffers, the whole body responds with sleeplessness and fever. Jurists also developed the category of *fard kifaya*, the collective duty, which binds the community as a whole until enough of its members discharge it; relief of those in dire need is commonly counted among such duties. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah* (1377), analysed a related bond, *asabiyya* — group feeling or social cohesion — as the force that binds tribes together, founds dynasties, and eventually dissolves as urban luxury weakens it. *Asabiyya* is broader than moral solidarity, since it can arise from kinship rather than justice, but it remains the classical Arabic analysis of what holds a society together.

## Key Contributions: Durkheim's Two Solidarities

The modern sociological concept was established by Émile Durkheim in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893), his doctoral thesis. Durkheim distinguished two forms. **Mechanical solidarity** characterises simple societies, where cohesion rests on likeness: people share the same beliefs, rituals, and way of life, and the collective conscience is strong. Offences against shared norms are punished severely, because they threaten the unity of the group. **Organic solidarity** characterises modern societies, where cohesion rests on difference: the division of labour makes people dependent on one another — the farmer needs the teacher, the teacher needs the doctor — and solidarity arises from interdependence rather than similarity. Law in such societies is less punitive and more restitutive, aiming to restore cooperation rather than to avenge the collective.

Durkheim's distinction explained how modern societies could be both more individualistic and more cohesive than traditional ones: individuality and interdependence grow together. It also gave sociology a way to study the moral basis of social order — not as a matter of force, but as a matter of shared norms and mutual dependence.

## Political Solidarity

Solidarity also became a political concept. In the European labour movement, it named the practice of workers supporting one another across trades and nations — striking in sympathy with workers they did not know, because their interests were shared. The Polish trade union *Solidarnosc* (Solidarity), founded in 1980, turned the word into a political programme: a movement that joined workers and intellectuals, with support from the Catholic Church, against an authoritarian regime, and that became a model for democratic opposition across Eastern Europe.

Catholic social teaching adopted solidarity as a virtue opposed to excessive individualism, and Richard Rorty, in *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989), recast it for a secular age. Rorty argued that solidarity should not rest on a metaphysical claim about a shared human essence but on the practical work of widening the circle of those we count as "us" — attending to the suffering of strangers until they become fellow sufferers. Solidarity, in this account, is made, not found: it grows through stories, journalism, and literature that make distant pain vivid.

## Examples

A strike in support of dismissed colleagues who are not one's own workmates is solidarity in its classic form: action taken at personal cost for people whose fate one shares. A crowdfunding campaign to pay a stranger's medical bill is a contemporary, digital form. At the institutional level, the welfare state is solidarity made durable: contributions from the healthy support the sick, from the employed support the unemployed, and from the young support the old, through systems that no individual could sustain alone. During the Covid-19 pandemic, mutual aid groups that delivered food and medicine to neighbours showed how quickly solidarity can be improvised when formal institutions falter.

## Critique and Contemporary Questions

Solidarity has its critics. Liberal theorists warn that it can harden into tribalism — loyalty to one's own group against outsiders — and that authoritarian regimes have invoked solidarity to suppress dissent in the name of unity. Feminist and postcolonial scholars note that appeals to solidarity have often assumed a universal "we" that in practice excluded women, minorities, and the colonised. The ethical challenge is therefore to distinguish an open solidarity, which widens the circle of concern toward humanity as a whole, from a closed solidarity, which defines itself against an enemy.

Contemporary questions concern the scale of solidarity: can it extend beyond the nation, to refugees, to future generations, or to the non-human world? Can digital networks sustain it, or do they fragment it into echo chambers? And can the welfare state survive austerity and demographic change? Solidarity remains, for sociology, both an empirical fact — the bonds that hold societies together — and a normative question: what do we owe one another, and how far does the "we" extend?

## Solidarity in Transnational Movements

Solidarity across borders has a long record, including campaigns against slavery and, in the twentieth century, international opposition to apartheid. Margaret Keck and Kathryn Sikkink's *Activists beyond Borders* (1998) described transnational advocacy networks in which groups blocked by their own government seek allies abroad who can bring pressure from outside, a pattern they called the boomerang. Sidney Tarrow stressed that many transnational activists remain rooted in national settings, drawing resources and opportunities from them. Such ties can join people with unequal money, safety and voice, so that supporters far away may reshape the aims of those they back.

## The Welfare State under Austerity

Paul Pierson argued that retrenching welfare programmes follows a different politics from building them, because established programmes create constituencies and governments seek to avoid blame; change therefore often comes gradually. Peter Taylor-Gooby (2013) described a double pressure: long-term demands from ageing and care, joined to cuts in public expenditure after the 2008 financial crisis, in a setting where support for helping some groups had weakened. Research on deservingness, such as Wim van Oorschot's, finds that publics tend to favour older and sick people over the unemployed and migrants, which shows how solidarity can be graded. Effects differ between countries and programmes.
