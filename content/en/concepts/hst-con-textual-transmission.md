# Textual Transmission and Manuscript Corruption

Textual transmission encompasses the material, scribal, and editorial processes through which ancient historical works were copied, preserved, and altered across centuries. Because no original authorial manuscripts (autographs) survive from classical antiquity, modern knowledge of ancient history depends entirely on copies of copies made on perishable media, each vulnerable to mechanical error, deliberate interpolation, and catastrophic physical loss.

## Definition and Core Concept

The survival of ancient texts was determined by material constraints and institutional continuities. Transmission involves the passage of a text through successive copying events—from authorial drafts written on wax tablets or papyrus rolls (*volumina*), through library copies in antiquity, to medieval monastic and Byzantine scriptoria, and ultimately to Renaissance humanists and modern printed critical editions. Along this trajectory, scribal corruption (*corruptela*) accumulated inevitably through transcription errors, glosses mistakenly incorporated from the margins into the main text, lacunae (losses of text caused by damaged leaves), and well-intentioned but flawed editorial emendations.

## Historical Transmission Bottlenecks

The historical preservation of Greek and Roman historians was conditioned by several critical technological and cultural bottlenecks:

1. **The Roll-to-Codex Transition (Second to Fourth Centuries CE):** The replacement of the fragile papyrus scroll by the parchment notebook (*codex*) represented a major sift of ancient literature. Works that were not transcribed into codex format were frequently abandoned to decay or discarded as libraries were reorganized.
2. **The Late Antique Library Dispersals:** Fires, invasions, and religious transformations in major centers—such as the Library of Alexandria, the Serapeum, and the imperial libraries of Rome—severely reduced the textual corpus.
3. **The Epitomizing Trend:** Readers in late antiquity increasingly preferred abridged digests over massive narrative histories. Extensive multi-volume works suffered heavily: of Livy's 142 books of *Ab Urbe Condita*, only 35 survive in full; the lost books are known mainly through the later summaries (*Periochae*).
4. **The Script Transitions (Ninth Century CE):** In both the Byzantine East and the Latin West, texts underwent a transition from majuscule (uncial) scripts to minuscule cursive hands (Byzantine minuscule and Carolingian minuscule). Texts deemed obscure or unread were often never transliterated, causing the majuscule exemplars to be scrapped or reused as palimpsests.

## Ancient Awareness of Textual Corruption

Ancient scholars and historians were keenly aware of the vulnerability of texts. Hellenistic philologists at the Library of Alexandria, led by Zenodotus, Aristophanes of Byzantium, and Aristarchus of Samothrace, developed systematic critical symbols (such as the *obelos* to denote spurious lines and the *asteriskos* for duplicated lines) to identify corruptions and interpolations in Homeric and classical literature. Polybius, for example, commented on the archaic language of the early Roman-Carthaginian treaty (3.22), while the physician Galen wrote *On My Own Books* specifically to counter widespread fraudulent attributions and corrupted commercial copies of his treatises circulating in Roman book markets.

## Stemmatics and Modern Historical Method

The systematic study of manuscript transmission was transformed in the nineteenth century through the establishment of the genealogical method (stemmatics), associated with Karl Lachmann and refined by Paul Maas. By collating surviving manuscripts, identifying shared errors (*errores significativi*), and reconstructing a genealogical tree (*stemma codicum*), classical philologists reconstruct the lost common ancestor (the *archetype*) from which all surviving copies descend. For modern historians, understanding the textual transmission of an ancient author is indispensable: it prevents building historical arguments upon scribal slips, late interpolations, or corrupted numerical data.
