# Political Authority in Islam

Political authority in Islam encompasses the complex body of jurisprudential (*fiqh*), theological (*kalam*), and philosophical theories formulated to define legitimate political leadership, the qualifications and duties of the ruler, the mechanisms of investiture, and the moral boundaries of civic obedience following the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632 CE (11 AH). Beginning with the establishment of the historical caliphate (*khilafa*), classical Islamic thought developed sophisticated debates concerning the relationship between sacred revelation (*shar'*) and political coercive power (*sultan*).

## Foundational Origins and Sunni Juristic Theory

The historical departure point for Islamic political governance is the assembly at the Saqifa of Banu Sa'ida, where leaders of the Ansar and the Muhajirun disputed the succession and a pledge of allegiance (*bay'a*) was given to Abu Bakr as "successor to the Messenger of God" (*khalifat rasul Allah*). Sunni political jurisprudence later read this event as the precedent for an elective, contractual caliphate:
- **Al-Mawardi's Institutional Blueprint**: Abu al-Hasan al-Mawardi (364–450 AH / 974–1058 CE) formulated the most influential classical Sunni theory in *Al-Ahkam al-sultaniyya* (*The Ordinances of Government*). Writing during the Buyid tutelage over the Abbasid caliphate, al-Mawardi sought to reaffirm the normative authority of the caliph against military usurpers. He defined the imamate as an office instituted to succeed prophethood in defending the faith and managing worldly affairs. He stipulated strict qualifications for the imam (justice, religious knowledge, sensory and physical fitness, prudence, courage, and descent from the Quraysh) and recognized two legitimate modes of investiture: election by the qualified electors (*ahl al-hall wa-l-'aqd*, "those who loose and bind") or designation (*'ahd*) by the preceding ruler.
- **The Pragmatic Accommodation of Power**: As military power shifted to warlords and sultanates, Sunni theorists adjusted their doctrines to limit civil war (*fitna*). Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (450–505 AH / 1058–1111 CE), especially in the imamate section of *Al-Iqtisad fi al-i'tiqad*, treated recognition of the de facto sultan as a necessity when the alternative was the collapse of legal order. He did not call tyranny a good. A later proverb, that a tyrant is better than lasting strife, circulates in political literature and should not be attributed to him as a maxim he established. Badr al-Din Ibn Jama'a (d. 733 AH / 1333 CE), in *Tahrir al-ahkam fi tadbir ahl al-Islam*, accepted rule by seizure (*istila'*) when the ruler could enforce order and protect the law.
- **Sharia Governance (*Al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya*)**: Ahmad ibn Taymiyya (661–728 AH / 1263–1328 CE) re-oriented political theory in *Al-Siyasa al-shar'iyya*, deemphasizing the formal, idealized caliphal apparatus in favor of functional Islamic governance. Drawing on Qur'anic verses (notably Surah an-Nisa 4:58), Ibn Taymiyya argued that legitimate rule requires the distribution of public trusts (*amanat*) to competent individuals and judgment according to justice, encapsulated in the twin virtues of strength (*quwwa*) and trustworthiness (*amana*).

## Shi'i and Social-Historical Alternatives

- **The Shi'i Doctrine of the Imamate**: Twelver and Ismaili theology, unlike the Sunni contractual account, treat rightful leadership as a matter of doctrine. The Imam is designated by explicit appointment (*nass*) from the Prophet through 'Ali ibn Abi Talib and a specified line of descendants, and is held to be protected from error (*'isma*) and to possess authoritative knowledge (*'ilm*). Zaydi doctrine differs: the imam is a qualified descendant who rises to rule, and infallibility is not claimed in the same way. After the Greater Occultation (*al-ghayba al-kubra*) of the Twelfth Imam, conventionally dated to 329 AH / 941 CE, many Twelver jurists counseled caution toward claims of sovereign power. Debate over the jurist's authority is older than the twentieth century. Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini's *wilayat al-faqih* (guardianship of the jurisprudent), set out in lectures of 1970 and published as *Hukumat-i Islami* (*Islamic Government*), argued for a comprehensive political guardianship and became a constitutional doctrine of the Islamic Republic of Iran.
- **Ibn Khaldun's Sociological Realism**: In the *Muqaddima*, a first draft of which was completed in 1377 CE, Ibn Khaldun (732–808 AH / 1332–1406 CE) analyzed power as a historical pattern rather than only as a juristic ideal. He argued that rule depends on group solidarity (*'asabiyya*), and he described a passage from the religion-guided caliphate (*khilafa*) to dynastic kingship (*mulk*). The cycle is his theory, not a law demonstrated for every dynasty.

## Modern Controversies: Abolition and Secular Reform

The Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the Ottoman sultanate in November 1922 and the caliphate on 3 March 1924. Muhammad Rashid Rida answered the first crisis in articles of 1922–1923, issued as *Al-Khilafa aw al-imama al-'uzma* (Cairo: Matba'at al-Manar, 1923). He defended a caliphate with political authority, not a merely spiritual office on a papal model. After the 1924 abolition, 'Ali 'Abd al-Raziq (1888–1966), an Azhar-trained judge, published *Al-Islam wa-usul al-hukm* (Cairo: Matba'at Misr, 1925). He argued that the Prophet's mission was a religious message, that the historical caliphate was a form of government rather than a doctrine of the faith, and that revelation does not prescribe one constitution. The Council of Senior Ulama at al-Azhar condemned the book, and he was removed from the judiciary. The dispute remains a reference point for arguments over civil government and religious authority.
