# Political Authority in Islam

The body of jurisprudential and theological theories that addressed the foundations of legitimate rule in Islam after the death of the Prophet Muhammad (632 CE), the conditions for who holds it, and the limits of obedience to it. It begins with the institution of the **caliphate** (*khilafat al-nubuwwa*, "succession to prophethood"), which arose immediately after his death, and continues through its systematic jurisprudential theorization in the Abbasid period.

## The Origins of the Caliphate

After the Prophet's death, the assembly at the Saqifa of Banu Sa'ida took place, where Muslims pledged allegiance (*bay'a*) to Abu Bakr al-Siddiq as "Caliph of the Messenger of God." This established the precedent of **bay'a** (the contract between ruler and community) as the fundamental legitimate mechanism of rule in Sunni thought. The Shi'i position is different: it holds that a divine designation (*nass*) of 'Ali ibn Abi Talib and his descendants is the basis of the imamate, not the choice of the community.

## Classical Juristic Theory: al-Mawardi

The Shafi'i jurist **Abu al-Hasan al-Mawardi** (974–1058 CE) produced the most comprehensive systematic Sunni theory of political authority, in his foundational book *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* (The Ordinances of Government). In it he specified the conditions of the imam (such as Qurashi descent, justice, knowledge and competence); the ways in which the imamate is validly established (the choice of the people of loosing and binding, *ahl al-hall wa-l-'aqd*, or the designation of the previous imam); the limits of his authority (restricted by the Sharia, not absolute); and the grounds on which an imam forfeits office, such as the loss of justice or of the capacity to rule. Al-Mawardi formulated his theory in a specific historical context, in which the actual power of the Abbasid caliph had declined before the amirs of the Buyids and the Seljuks. His work was therefore an attempt to reconcile the religious legitimacy of the caliphate with the political reality of multiple centres of actual power.

## Contested Questions

Jurists and theologians disagreed about several questions. Is the imamate obligatory by reason or by revealed law alone? Is the Qurashi lineage condition absolute, or is it constrained by historical circumstances? What are the limits of obeying an unjust ruler? On the last point there was sharp disagreement between those who required patience under his tyranny to avert strife, and those who permitted rebellion against him under certain conditions. These questions were raised again by later thinkers such as Ibn Taymiyya, who in *al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya* offered a practical model that stressed strength and trustworthiness (*quwwa* and *amana*) in office-holders, and Ibn Khaldun, who analyzed kingship and the caliphate by a social-historical method in the *Muqaddima*.

## Modern Influence and Contemporary Use

These classical theories remained a living reference in modern debates in Islamic political thought about the relationship between religion and the state. They were invoked both by advocates of reviving the caliphate and by reformist thinkers who attempted to reconcile this heritage with modern state models. An example of the latter is 'Ali 'Abd al-Raziq, whose controversial book *al-Islam wa-Usul al-Hukm* (Islam and the Foundations of Rule, 1925) questioned the religious basis of the caliphate institution itself.

## The Shi'i Doctrine of the Imamate

In Twelver Shi'ism the imamate is a divinely appointed office: the Imams, beginning with 'Ali, are designated by the Prophet and the preceding Imam, and are held to be infallible (*ma'sum*) and the authoritative interpreters of religion. The Twelver tradition holds that the twelfth Imam is in occultation (*ghayba*), so that rule in his absence was long treated as provisional; the later doctrine of the guardianship of the jurist (*wilayat al-faqih*) is a modern development of this question. Ismaili and Zaydi Shi'a hold differing views on the line of succession and on the qualifications of the Imam.

## Caveats

- The distinction between the caliphate as an actual historical institution and the caliphate as an idealized normative model is often confused in contemporary literature.
