# Prebisch-Singer Hypothesis

The **Prebisch-Singer hypothesis** is an economic thesis put forward independently in 1950 by the Argentine economist **Raúl Prebisch** and the German-born British economist **Hans Singer**. It holds that the terms of trade (the ratio of export prices to import prices) tend to deteriorate over the long run for countries that export primary commodities, relative to countries that export manufactured goods.

## An overturning of the classical expectation

The hypothesis ran against an expectation, associated with John Stuart Mill and other classical writers, that diminishing returns in primary production and technical progress in manufacturing would move the terms of trade in favour of primary exporters. Comparative advantage does not itself imply that the gains from trade are shared equally. Prebisch and Singer argued that the historical record for primary exporters showed a long-run deterioration in the terms of trade, rather than the improvement that line of classical reasoning had suggested.

## Two proposed explanatory mechanisms

The two authors offered two main mechanisms to explain this deterioration. First, demand for primary commodities is less responsive to income than demand for manufactured goods: as world income rises, people spend an increasing share of it on diverse manufactured goods rather than on basic primary commodities such as food and raw metals. Second, trade unions and monopolistic firms in industrial countries tend to retain the gains from productivity growth in the form of higher wages and profits, while the productivity gains in agriculture and extraction in less industrialized countries pass to global consumers through relatively lower prices, owing to the weak bargaining power of workers there.

## The policy recommendation: protective industrialization

The hypothesis was used as a central theoretical justification for the policy of "import-substituting industrialization" adopted by many governments in Latin America in the 1950s and 1960s. Since reliance on exporting primary commodities is subject to a long-term structural deterioration in the terms of trade, the solution lies in protecting emerging domestic industries with customs tariffs, rather than continuing the primary export specialization that keeps these economies permanently in a dependent position.

## Empirical criticism and mixed evidence

The hypothesis faced empirical criticism from economists who tested it statistically over longer periods and with different econometric methods, and reached mixed results. Some studies, such as Grilli and Yang (1988), found a long-run decline in real primary commodity prices over the twentieth century, while others questioned whether this trend is stable across all periods and commodities, arguing that sharp short-term fluctuations in commodity prices make measuring the long-term "trend" with full precision a statistically highly sensitive matter, depending on the choice of starting and ending points.

## Lasting influence on dependency theory

The hypothesis was a starting point for more radical later developments within dependency theory, which went beyond the limited empirical diagnosis to assert that unequal exchange is not a transient phenomenon correctable by technical means, but a permanent structural feature of the position of the peripheries within the global capitalist system itself.
