# Childhood Anxiety

## Definition

Childhood anxiety disorders include separation anxiety, specific phobia, social anxiety, generalized anxiety, and selective mutism. They are among the most common psychiatric disorders in children and are often accompanied by depression. This entry covers what is specific to childhood and early adolescence, and what differs from adult anxiety in its manifestations, course, and treatment.

## From Little Hans to Epidemiological Studies

In 1909 Freud studied the case of "Little Hans," a boy who was afraid of horses, and read it in the light of the Oedipal conflict. In 1920 Watson and Rayner responded in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology* with the "Little Albert" experiment, which presented fear as something learned through conditioning. In 1924 Mary Cover Jones showed that a child's fear could be reduced through gradual counterconditioning (pairing the feared object with food) in the case of "Peter." The epidemiological shift came with the Great Smoky Mountains study, whose results were published by Costello and colleagues in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 2003. It found that roughly one-third of children had met criteria for at least one psychiatric disorder by age sixteen.

## Temperament and Development

Jerome Kagan and colleagues described "behavioral inhibition" in *Science* in 1988 as a biological temperament that makes children highly cautious in the face of new things. Hirshfeld and colleagues linked it in *JAACAP* in 1992 with a higher risk of anxiety disorders later (see the entry on Temperament and Risk Factors). Beesdo, Knappe, and Pine reviewed the developmental courses of anxiety in *Psychiatric Clinics of North America* in 2009. They noted that separation anxiety and specific phobias tend to begin earlier than social anxiety, and that childhood anxiety disorders raise the risk of later depression. Onset is influenced by parenting style: overprotection and parental control feed avoidance (see the entry on Attachment Theory & Development).

## Treatment

Philip Kendall developed the "Coping Cat" program in cognitive behavioral therapy for children and published his controlled trial in *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* in 1994. In the large CAMS study, published by Walkup and colleagues in *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2008, in children aged 7 to 17, cognitive behavioral therapy and sertraline each achieved better improvement than placebo, and the combination had a higher response rate than either treatment alone. For adolescent depression, the Treatment for Adolescents with Depression Study (TADS), published by March and colleagues in *JAMA* in 2004, found that the combination of fluoxetine and cognitive behavioral therapy outperformed either treatment alone on the primary outcome. The boxed warning issued in the United States in 2004 about suicidal thinking in young people taking antidepressants, however, called for close monitoring (see the entry on Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors).

## Physical Symptoms and Cultural Context

Children often express anxiety through abdominal pain, headaches, and school refusal rather than in words about fear. The assessment of pathological behavior varies by culture: a child's shyness and obedience may be considered a virtue in one environment and read as symptoms in another. This calls for cultural competence in assessment (see the entry on Cultural Competence in Psychiatry).

## Limits

The distinction between normal developmental anxiety and pathological anxiety depends on distress and functional impact. Agreement among child, parent, and teacher reports varies, so studies require multiple sources. Studies of childhood in the Arab world remain fewer than their Western counterparts.
