# Moral Facts

Moral facts are states of affairs that are presumed to exist in the world regardless of whether any individual or culture believes in them, and that make statements such as "torture is wrong" true in a sense similar to the truth of scientific or mathematical statements. The central question here is whether there are objective moral properties in the universe (such as wrongness, rightness, justice and injustice) on the model of physical properties, or whether everything called "moral truth" is merely a projection of our subjective attitudes onto the world.

In Islamic thought, the question of *husn* and *qubh* (the goodness and ugliness of acts) was the most prominent arena of dispute on this question. The Mu'tazila affirmed the existence of objective moral facts independent of revelation, which reason apprehends by itself in the way it apprehends the truths of logic and mathematics: injustice is ugly in itself, whether or not a prohibition of it has been revealed. The Ash'aris denied this, holding that goodness and ugliness are purely legal attributes that exist only through divine address, such that if God were to command lying, lying would become good. This position is closer to the "Divine Command Theory" in Western philosophy. Other thinkers sought middle positions; for example, later usul al-fiqh discussions of public interest (*maslaha*) allow that reason perceives many benefits and harms while treating revelation as the authoritative source of rulings.

In contemporary analytic philosophy, moral realists (such as David Brink and Russ Shafer-Landau) stand against non-realists in their various forms. On one side are the expressivists (Ayer and Stevenson), who hold that moral judgments are emotional expressions rather than descriptions of facts. On the other side is error theory (J. L. Mackie), which accepts that moral language claims to describe objective facts but denies that those facts actually exist, so that all positive moral judgments are systematically false. Mackie used the "Argument from Queerness" to argue that moral facts, if they existed, would be entities very strange within the fabric of the natural universe, requiring a special cognitive capacity (a moral intuition) with no counterpart in any other field of knowledge.

Naturalist realists (such as Richard Boyd and Peter Railton) reply that moral facts can be reduced to complex natural properties (such as human flourishing or the satisfaction of basic needs) that are amenable to scientific study, thereby dissolving their alleged strangeness. Non-naturalist realists (such as Derek Parfit in *On What Matters*) insist, however, that moral facts are normative facts that are irreducible to natural facts and hold independently of what anyone believes, on the model of mathematical truths.

Example: the statement "killing innocent people is unjust" is believed by realists to be true regardless of who believes it, just as "2 + 2 = 4" is true, whereas non-realists regard it as merely a strong expression of a shared human rejection. The decisive test between the two positions is how we conceive the case of deep moral disagreement. If there is one objective moral truth, then one of the two sides of any deep moral disagreement must be mistaken. Realists accept this conclusion with relative comfort, while non-realists find it philosophically troubling and call for an alternative explanation of persistent moral disagreement across cultures. The debate remains alive because each position appears to explain a genuine aspect of the phenomenon of human moral experience without fully encompassing it.
