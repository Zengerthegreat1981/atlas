# The Three Premises of Symbolic Interactionism in Blumer

## Definition

The condensed methodological formulation that the American sociologist Herbert Blumer offered to summarize the essence of the symbolic interactionist perspective in sociology, published in its final form in *Symbolic Interactionism: Perspective and Method* (1969).

## Historical Context

Blumer was a direct student of George Herbert Mead at the University of Chicago, and it was he who coined the term "symbolic interactionism" itself in 1937 to describe Mead's philosophical and social-psychological legacy. Only after decades of teaching and continuous reflection on its foundations did Blumer crystallize this legacy in a complete sociological methodological form. His final formulation of the three premises came at a time when American sociology was moving strongly toward quantitative functionalist approaches (as in Talcott Parsons), and Blumer wanted to defend the originality of an alternative perspective that gives priority to subjective meaning formed in live interaction between individuals.

## Main Ideas or Method

Blumer summarized the symbolic interactionist perspective in three interrelated premises. **The first premise**: human beings act toward things (including physical objects, other people, institutions, ideals, and even the activities and actions of others) on the basis of the meanings these things have for them, and not on the basis of their "objective" properties independent of this meaning. **The second premise**: these meanings do not stem from the thing itself, nor from a prior internal individual psychological structure, but arise and take shape through the process of social interaction that the individual engages in with others. **The third premise**: these meanings are not applied automatically and fixedly but are handled and modified continually through an internal interpretive process carried out by the individual himself as he deals with the things he encounters in each new situation. From these three premises Blumer drew a methodological rejection of standardized quantitative approaches with pre-set categories, and a defense of a flexible qualitative method that stays close to the perspective of the actors themselves as they build actual meaning.

## Impact and Critique

These three premises became the accepted reference theoretical foundation of American qualitative sociology throughout the second half of the twentieth century. They paved the way for many later developments within the interactionist tradition, most notably Sheldon Stryker's structural symbolic interactionism. In contrast to Blumer's wariness of explaining conduct from pre-existing structure, it tried to integrate some elements of structural analysis while preserving the core focus on meaning formed interactively. Some critics objected to Blumer's reluctance to treat social structure as determining conduct, holding that this stance may make it hard to explain stable patterns of structural inequality (such as class and race) that go beyond momentary individual interaction. Objection was also raised to the vagueness of some of his methodological concepts (such as "sensitizing concepts") and the difficulty of applying them with strict procedural precision. Nevertheless, these three premises are still widely taught and cited as an introduction to symbolic interactionism.
