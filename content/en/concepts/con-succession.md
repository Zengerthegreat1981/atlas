# Succession

Succession is the transfer of an office, a position of authority, or a social role from one holder to the next. Where inheritance concerns what a person receives, succession concerns who a person becomes: the new chief, the new priest, the new head of the household, the new king. Every society needs rules for replacing its office-holders, because offices outlive the people who hold them, and the moment of transfer is one of the most dangerous moments in political life. A disputed succession can split a kingdom, a lineage, or a congregation; a smooth one confirms that the social order endures beyond any individual life. Anthropologists study succession because it reveals how authority is legitimated, how continuity is maintained, and how the ambitions of rivals are contained.

## Definition

Succession must be distinguished from inheritance, though the two often overlap. Inheritance transfers things — land, wealth, goods — while succession transfers a position and the rights and duties attached to it. The same person may inherit property and succeed to office, but the rules are often different: property may be divided among all children, while an office can pass to only one person. Succession systems vary in how they choose the next holder. Hereditary succession passes office within a family or lineage, by rules of primogeniture, seniority, or election among eligible kin. Elective succession chooses the holder by vote or consensus, as in the election of a pope or a village headman. Appointment passes the choice to a superior authority. Some systems combine these: a council may elect among the sons of the dead chief, or an oracle may confirm a choice made by elders. Succession may be immediate, with the new holder installed at once, or delayed by a period of mourning or ritual preparation. In others, the successor is not known until the holder dies, and the interval is managed by regents, councils, or ritual specialists.

## Origins and Development

The comparative study of succession was founded by Jack Goody, who edited the volume *Succession to High Office* in 1966, bringing together case studies of succession among African peoples — the Lozi, the Baganda, the Nyamwezi, and the Gonja — and analysing the problem each system solves and the dilemmas it creates. Goody showed that succession systems must balance two competing needs: continuity, so that authority does not lapse, and legitimacy, so that the new holder is accepted. Systems that designate an heir in advance gain continuity but risk rivalry between holder and heir; systems that leave the choice open gain flexibility but risk conflict after the death. Max Weber's analysis of authority, published posthumously in 1922, provided the theoretical frame: succession is the problem of how charismatic authority — the personal authority of a founder or prophet — is routinised into an office that can be passed on, whether by heredity, election, or designation. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's study of the Nuer (1940) showed a society that managed without centralised succession at all: leadership was situational and segmentary, emerging in feuds and rituals rather than passing through offices. Lucy Mair's *Primitive Government* (1962) compared how different African political systems handled the transfer of power, from the highly ritualised kingship of Buganda to the acephalous politics of stateless peoples.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns the relationship between succession rules and political stability. Do strict hereditary rules produce stability by removing doubt, or do they produce weak rulers and succession crises when the heir is incompetent or a minor? Do elective systems produce better rulers, or do they invite factional struggle? Ethnographic evidence suggests that no system eliminates the problem: every succession rule creates its own characteristic form of conflict, whether between brothers, between the heir and the council, or between rival lineages. A second debate concerns the role of ritual. Succession is almost always marked by ceremony — coronations, installations, funerals of the old holder — and anthropologists argue that ritual does not merely celebrate the transfer but effects it: the new holder becomes the office-holder through the rite, and the community's witnessing of the rite is what makes the claim stick. A third dimension is gender. In most recorded systems, succession to high office has been restricted to men, but there are important exceptions — queen mothers, female chiefs, and priestesses — and feminist anthropology has shown how women have exercised power through control over succession even when they could not succeed themselves.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Succession remains a live political issue. In postcolonial Africa, conflicts between customary succession and state law continue: a chief may be chosen by a traditional council and recognised, or not recognised, by the state, and rival claimants may appeal to different authorities. In corporations, religious organisations, and political parties, succession planning has become a formal discipline, and the anthropology of succession offers a useful lens: the problems of the heir apparent, the regency, and the disputed election are not new. Critics note that the classical literature focused on succession to high office in African kingdoms, and paid less attention to succession in egalitarian societies, in women's organisations, and in modern institutions. The enduring insight is that succession is never a merely administrative matter: it is the moment when a society must show that its institutions survive the death of individuals, and the rules it uses to do so reveal its deepest assumptions about legitimacy, kinship, and power.
