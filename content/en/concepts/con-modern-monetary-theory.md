# Modern Monetary Theory

**Modern monetary theory** is a macroeconomic analytical framework that developed gradually from the mid-1990s onward, through economists associated with the post-Keynesian tradition (among the most prominent are Warren Mosler, L. Randall Wray and Stephanie Kelton). It draws on earlier intellectual roots in Abba Lerner's "functional finance" and in Georg Friedrich Knapp's *Staatliche Theorie des Geldes* (1905; English translation *The State Theory of Money*, 1924), a source of "chartalism", to offer an alternative view of the nature of money and the real constraints on government spending.

## Monetary sovereignty: a state does not "run out" of its currency

The theory starts from a central distinction between a state with **full monetary sovereignty** (one that issues its own currency, does not undertake to convert it into gold or a foreign currency at a fixed rate, and does not borrow in a currency other than its own) and other states that lack this sovereignty (such as the individual member states of the eurozone, or states that borrow in dollars). In the first case, the theory argues that the government cannot technically "run out" of its own currency in the sense that a household runs out of income, because it is the sole issuer of that currency.

## The real constraint: resources and inflation, not "money"

The theory concludes that the real constraint on government spending for a state with full monetary sovereignty is not the availability of "funds" in the traditional accounting sense, but the availability of actual unused productive resources in the economy (idle labour and productive capacity). If the government spends on available, idle resources, this produces little or no inflation. If spending exceeds the maximum productive capacity of the economy (that is, when the economy approaches full employment), inflation becomes the real constraint to watch, not the size of the budget deficit in itself.

## Taxes for a functional purpose rather than pure financing

The theory also redefines the function of taxation. According to this view, taxes are not the means by which the government "finances" its spending, since the government issues its currency before collecting taxes. They are instead a tool for regulating aggregate demand and restraining inflation (by withdrawing purchasing power from the economy), for creating a basic demand for the currency itself (because citizens need the national currency to pay their tax obligations), and not an end in themselves independent of these functions.

## A broadly contested reception

The theory provoked wide academic and political debate, especially after the 2008 global financial crisis, when the discussion of acceptable limits on government fiscal deficits intensified, and later in debates over emergency government spending during the COVID-19 pandemic. Leading economists from several schools, including some post-Keynesians themselves, have criticized some of its simplified popular formulations. They argue that these sometimes underestimate the practical risks of inflation and the complexity of implementing bold fiscal policies in open economies exposed to international trade and cross-border capital flows.
