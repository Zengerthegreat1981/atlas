# Kula Exchange

A complex economic and social system first documented by Bronisław Malinowski among the Trobriand Islanders in Papua New Guinea. The kula is a cycle of exchange linking many islands through the circulation of valuable armshells and necklaces, governed by strict rules of etiquette and social obligation.

## Ethnographic Definition

The kula is not **a form of spontaneous gift-giving** but a **specialized economic system** that operates according to defined rules:

- **White armshells** (*mwali*): circulate in one fixed direction around the ring
- **Red necklaces** (*soulava*): circulate in the opposite direction
- Kula gifts are distinguished from *gimwali*, ordinary barter of useful goods, which is carried on alongside the kula and is bargained over, whereas kula gifts are not
- The aim is **not to possess the objects but to circulate them**: a man who keeps armshells without exchanging them is regarded as shameful

## The Social Structure of the Kula

The kula is neither **primitive** nor a **pre-monetary economy** but a **specialized** economic system parallel to the market:

1. **Geographic network**: it links scattered islands across long distances, requiring dangerous sea voyages
2. **Social network**: each person has specific **exchange partners**, and these relationships last across generations
3. **Personal identity of objects**: each armshell or necklace has a **name and a history** that define its value and social reputation
4. **Long-term obligations**: the exchange of an object creates a **permanent** obligation to return an equivalent: the transaction never ends

## Difference from the Potlatch

Unlike the potlatch (an exchange among the peoples of the North American Northwest Coast):
- The kula is a **continuous cycle**: the objects always circulate and are never kept permanently
- The kula is **more balanced**: the return is expected with greater precision
- The potlatch is **competitive**: the chief gains prestige by giving away and destroying wealth; the kula is **cooperative**, its aim being long-term continuity

## Theoretical Importance

The kula became the **basic model** to which **Marcel Mauss** applied his theory of the gift:
- It demonstrates that gift-giving and reciprocity form an economic system **entirely independent** of the market and money
- It breaks the traditional Western dichotomy between the economy and "social gifts"
- It shows that non-capitalist societies have **sophisticated economic systems** and are not primitive
