# Consumer Surplus

**Consumer surplus** is an economic measure popularised by Alfred Marshall in his *Principles of Economics* (1890), building on the earlier work of the French engineer Jules Dupuit (1844) on the utility of public works. It measures the difference between the maximum price a consumer is willing to pay for a good and the price actually paid in the market. It serves as a rough quantitative indicator of the net utility consumers gain from the process of commercial exchange.

## Graphical Representation Under the Demand Curve

Consumer surplus is represented graphically by the area below the demand curve and above the prevailing market price. Because the demand curve reflects the maximum price consumers are prepared to pay for each unit of quantity, every unit that consumers bought at a price lower than their maximum willingness to pay gives them a surplus. The sum of these surpluses across all units consumed is the total consumer surplus in that market.

## A Tool for Measuring the Effects of Public Policy

This concept has become an essential tool in the cost-benefit analysis of government policies. When the government imposes a tax on a good, the reduction in consumer surplus resulting from its higher price can be measured and compared with the tax revenue the government collects, and with the producer surplus that is also affected. This allows an estimate of the total net effect of the policy on the welfare of society as a whole, rather than only on the government's budget.

## The Connection to Welfare Economics

Arthur Cecil Pigou, Marshall's direct student, worked in the same Marshallian tradition of welfare analysis in *The Economics of Welfare* (1920), in which the gap between private and social costs and benefits is central. Together these two measures allow the calculation of the full net effect of any policy or intervention on the welfare of both sides of the market, consumers and producers, rather than limiting the calculation to its effect on only one of them.

## Producer Surplus: The Mirror Image

A similar concept on the supply side mirrors consumer surplus: **producer surplus**. It is the difference between the price at which the producer actually sold and the lowest price it was prepared to accept for selling that unit (which usually reflects the marginal cost of producing it). The two concepts are used together, consumer surplus and producer surplus, to calculate the "total welfare surplus" achieved by a competitive market. Economists use this measure to compare the efficiency of different market arrangements, or to assess the net loss caused by a particular government intervention, such as a price ceiling or an import quota.

## An Approximate Value, Not a Precise Absolute Measure

Consumer surplus remains an approximate monetary measure of utility, not an exact absolute measure of happiness or of complete subjective well-being. It implicitly assumes that the marginal utility of income is roughly constant, so that a unit of money is worth the same at different income levels. This simplifying assumption may not hold precisely for goods that take a large share of income. John Hicks later developed the exact measures of compensating and equivalent variation, and Robert Willig (1976) showed that for goods with small income effects the Marshallian measure is a close approximation. Nonetheless it remains the most widely used practical tool in applied economic analysis of policies in many parts of the world, from evaluating public infrastructure projects to analysing the effects of customs duties on domestic consumers.
