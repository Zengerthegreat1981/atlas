# Hybrid Ethics

Hybrid ethics refers to metaethical theories that attempt to combine two elements that seem to be at odds in moral judgments: the element of cognitive description (cognitivism), which holds that moral judgments carry content that can be true or false, and the element of emotional expression (expressivism), which holds that they express an emotional attitude or a motive for action. These theories seek to explain why the statement "injustice is wrong" appears, on the one hand, to describe a fact, and on the other, to be a psychological motive that binds one to act, without fully siding with either party.

This problem was not formulated in its contemporary analytic form in the Islamic tradition, but the debate over good and evil (*al-husn wa-l-qubh*) between the Mu'tazila and the Ash'ari carries the seeds of the same tension. The Mu'tazila came close to a cognitive realist position, in which goodness is an intrinsic property of an action perceived by reason. Sufi writers who linked moral perception to the state of the heart (*dhawq*, "taste") foreshadow, at a distance, the role of affect in moral judgment, though the theological context remains fundamentally different from the modern analytic one.

In Western philosophy, Michael Smith formulated the "moral problem": how can a moral judgment be a cognitive belief (as realism requires) and at the same time a self-motivating impulse to act (as psychological common sense requires), when beliefs usually do not motivate on their own but need an accompanying desire? David Wiggins and John McDowell developed "sensibility theory," on which values are properties that depend on, and are apprehended through, our human sensibilities, so that perception and response are intertwined; it is often discussed as a neighboring view, though it is a form of cognitivism. Explicitly hybrid theories include Michael Ridge's "ecumenical expressivism" and David Copp's "realist-expressivism," which hold that a moral judgment involves both a descriptive belief and an expressed attitude. Allan Gibbard's "norm-expressivism," which explains moral judgment as the expression of acceptance of a system of norms, is a sophisticated expressivism that allows moral talk to look descriptive.

Hybrid approaches face the objection of theoretical instability. Critics argue that they try to "have one's cake and eat it too," that is, to gain the benefits of realism (the objectivity of truth and falsity) and the benefits of expressivism (the explanation of self-motivation) without a coherent metaphysical account of how the two can coexist in a single mental state. The proponents of these theories reply that actual moral life displays this duality every day, and that any one-dimensional theory misses a genuine aspect of moral experience.

An example: when someone says "lying is wrong," he describes a state of affairs he believes to be correct (a cognitive dimension), and at the same time he feels a spontaneous aversion to lying that moves him to avoid it (an emotional dimension). Hybrid ethics seeks to explain both together.

The importance of this direction lies in its attempt to move beyond the sharp polarization that dominated metaethical debate throughout the twentieth century, between the camp of strict realism and the camp of explicit non-realism. It offers models that are closer to the actual linguistic and psychological experience of moral judgment, which makes them the subject of growing interest in the contemporary philosophy of moral language. Yet demonstrating their internal consistency remains an ongoing research challenge for every theory that attempts to combine two requirements that appear to conflict, especially when it is asked to specify which of the two elements, the cognitive or the emotional, is the basis to which the other is explained.
