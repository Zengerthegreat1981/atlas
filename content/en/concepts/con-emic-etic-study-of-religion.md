# Emic/Etic Perspectives in the Study of Religion

A methodological distinction that scholars of religion borrowed from anthropological linguistics (Kenneth Pike, 1954, modeled on phonemic/phonetic), which distinguishes between two possible positions for the researcher toward a religious phenomenon: the **emic** position, which describes a belief or practice in the terms of the believers themselves and according to their internal standards of meaning; and the **etic** position, which analyzes the same phenomenon with external analytic categories that its practitioners do not necessarily accept as true, and which they may not even know.

## The Emic Position in the Study of Religion

The phenomenology of religion (for example Otto, van der Leeuw and Eliade) tends toward the emic position through the technique of "bracketing" (*epoché*) borrowed from Husserl, though the extent of its fidelity to Husserl is debated: judgment on whether a belief is true or false is suspended, and the belief is described as the believer lives it, in an effort to understand its meaning "from within" before any causal explanation.

## The Etic Position in the Study of Religion

The cognitive science of religion and reductive social approaches adopt a thoroughly etic position: they explain the ritual or belief by categories (a cognitive mechanism, an evolutionary function, a social function) that the believer himself does not know and does not need to know for the validity of his religious practice.

## The Effect of the Distinction on the Reductionism Debate

This distinction parallels, without exactly matching, the disagreement between reductionist and non-reductionist approaches. The emic position is a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for the non-reductionist stance: a researcher can describe from within (emically) and then explain what he has described by external factors (etically) in a later step. This is precisely what proponents of non-reductionism criticize, seeing in the move from emic description to etic explanation a betrayal of the particularity of the religious phenomenon.

## Pike and Harris

Pike's emic/etic contrast was a linguistic analogy applied to behaviour in general: emic units are those meaningful within a particular system, etic units are cross-culturally comparable descriptive categories. Marvin Harris, in *Cultural Materialism* (1979), gave the pair a different sense, treating emic accounts as the participants' own and etic accounts as the observer's scientific categories. Later scholars of religion usually follow this second, observer-versus-participant sense, which is looser than Pike's.

## The Origin of the Term

Pike borrowed the two terms from the phonetic distinction in linguistics between "phonemic" (the sound unit that carries meaning within the system of a particular language) and "phonetic" (the purely physical description of sound apart from any particular linguistic system). Pike applied this distinction to the study of human behavior in general, and scholars of religion later adopted it as a tool for characterizing the researcher's position toward the religious belief he studies.

## Russell McCutcheon's Critique

In *Manufacturing Religion* (1997), Russell McCutcheon argues that many practitioners of religious phenomenology use the emic position as a pretext for avoiding any critical explanation of religion, turning the "respect for the believer's perspective" into an implicit protection of religion from the causal analysis to which all other human phenomena are subjected. McCutcheon regards this as a retreat from the explanatory standards used elsewhere in the humanities and social sciences; defenders of the emic approach reply that understanding precedes explanation.
