# Knowledge by Presence and Self-Intuition (Knowledge by Presence)

The founding epistemological concept in Illuminationist philosophy and Transcendent Wisdom; it distinguishes between "acquired knowledge" (ilm husuli: indirect knowledge acquired by the imprinting of the thing's form in the mind, such as logical definitions) and "knowledge by presence" (ilm huduri: direct intuitive knowledge in which the reality of the thing and its concrete existence are present to the knower, such as the self's knowledge of itself, its pain and its inner feeling, without a formal intermediary).

The concept was founded by Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi in his Illuminationist school, and later developed by Mulla Sadra al-Shirazi within his Transcendent Wisdom. This direct knowledge of the self by itself has been compared by some modern scholars with discussions of qualia and first-person knowledge in contemporary philosophy of mind, and bears on the debate between dualism and physicalism over knowledge of mental states.

## Historical Context

Ibn Sina prepared the ground for the idea with the argument of the "floating man": if a complete human being were created suspended in space, sensing nothing of his body, he would remain aware of himself, which indicates that the self's awareness does not pass through the senses or through a form. Suhrawardi in "Hikmat al-Ishraq" (1186) took this idea and made it a principle: the human being's awareness of himself is not by an acquired form but by the presence of his self to his self, and it is an "illuminative knowledge by presence," and he extended it to the claim that universals and knowledge of the abstract lights take place in the same mode. Suhrawardi criticized the Aristotelian theory of definitions and limits because it yields acquired knowledge of things that does not dispense with intuitive witnessing.

## The Sadrian Formulation and Later Thinkers

Mulla Sadra al-Shirazi generalized the concept, holding that a cause's knowledge of its effect is by presence, that the soul knows by presence the imaginal and intellectual forms that it itself produces, and that God's knowledge of things is by presence. In the twentieth century Allama Tabataba'i, in "Usul al-Falsafa wa al-Manhaj al-Waqi'i" (1953 onward), made it a basis for establishing realism and confronting skepticism, because knowledge by presence is not subject to error: error occurs in acquired knowledge when the form is attributed to something outside it. Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr employed it in "Our Philosophy" (Falsafatuna, 1959) in criticizing the materialist theory of knowledge.

## Comparison with Phenomenology

Knowledge by presence is often compared with the pre-reflective self-awareness discussed in phenomenology, for example in Husserl's analysis of inner time-consciousness and in Sartre's notion of the pre-reflective cogito, where consciousness is aware of itself without making itself an object. The comparison is suggestive, but the traditions differ: in Suhrawardi and Sadra the doctrine is part of a metaphysics of light and existence, and extends to the soul's knowledge of its own acts and to God's knowledge, while phenomenology describes the structure of experience and brackets metaphysical commitments.

## Critique and Limits

Critics objected that knowledge by presence is difficult to test by a general criterion, because it is personal and not transmissible. Others asked about the boundary between direct presence and a form projected onto consciousness, and what guarantee there is of its immunity from error. The commentators of Sadrianism held that presence does not concern propositions that admit truth and falsity, but is the existence of the knower himself in his own presence to himself.
