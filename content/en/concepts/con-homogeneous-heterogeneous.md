# Homogeneous and Heterogeneous: Sameness, Difference, and Social Life

**Homogeneous** and **heterogeneous** describe the composition of a social group or a society: a homogeneous population is made up of people who are alike in the respects that matter — language, religion, descent, occupation, or way of life — while a heterogeneous population is made up of people who differ in those respects. The pair is deceptively simple. No society is ever entirely one or the other, and sameness in one dimension often hides difference in another: a village may share a single language yet be divided by clan, caste, or wealth, while a great city may contain a hundred languages yet share a common civic life. Anthropologists use the two terms to ask how sameness and difference are made, maintained, and managed, and what each makes possible or impossible in social life.

## Definition and Development

The classic statement of the problem comes from Émile Durkheim in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893, English edition 1933). Durkheim distinguished two kinds of social solidarity. Mechanical solidarity binds together people who are alike: in small, homogeneous societies, everyone performs similar tasks, shares the same beliefs, and is held together by a strong collective conscience that punishes deviation. Organic solidarity, by contrast, binds together people who are different: in complex, heterogeneous societies, the division of labour makes people depend on one another precisely because they do different things, and solidarity rests on interdependence rather than likeness. Durkheim's point was that heterogeneity is not the absence of social order but a different kind of order, one built on difference itself.

Later anthropology complicated the picture. Fredrik Barth, in *Ethnic Groups and Boundaries* (1969), showed that heterogeneity is not a property of populations but of the boundaries drawn between them: what matters is not how different people objectively are, but which differences are treated as socially significant. Two groups may be nearly identical in language and livelihood yet insist on their distinctness, while groups that differ sharply may live together without making those differences matter. Homogeneity and heterogeneity are therefore achievements of social practice — the result of boundary work, not of nature.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Ulf Hannerz, in *Cultural Complexity* (1992), argued that heterogeneity is the normal condition of modern life. In cities, in diasporas, and in the flows of global media, people constantly encounter cultural difference, and they manage it through a repertoire of roles, codes, and competences: the same person may speak one language at home, another at work, and a third with friends. Culture, in this view, is not a single shared package but a distribution of meanings across a population, unevenly shared and constantly negotiated. Arjun Appadurai, in *Modernity at Large* (1996), added that global flows of people, media, money, and ideas produce new forms of heterogeneity — and new forms of sameness, as the same films, brands, and styles appear everywhere, only to be remade locally.

Steven Vertovec, in "Super-diversity and Its Implications" (2007), gave a name to the heterogeneity of contemporary migration: not just many ethnic groups in one place, but diversity within each group — of legal status, age, gender, education, and migration history — so that no single label captures who anyone is. Vertovec's point was that older models, which treated a city as a mosaic of homogeneous communities, no longer fit. The same insight applies in reverse: apparent homogeneity is often an illusion produced by looking from too far away, or by the power of a dominant group to define what counts as normal.

## Ethnographic Examples

Ethnography shows both terms at work. In highland New Guinea, neighbouring valleys may speak mutually unintelligible languages, yet trade, marry, and share ritual forms — heterogeneity of speech coexisting with dense social connection. In the caste-ordered villages of India, people share a language, a religion, and a village, yet are divided by rules of purity, commensality, and occupation that make daily life a constant negotiation of difference. In the great port cities of the past and present — Zanzibar, Singapore, New York — heterogeneity has been the ordinary condition, managed through markets, neighbourhoods, and cosmopolitan etiquettes that allow strangers to live side by side.

Homogeneity, too, is ethnographically visible, but usually as a project rather than a fact. Nation-states have worked hard to produce homogeneous populations through schooling in a single language, conscription, and national holidays — and those projects have repeatedly generated resistance from people whose difference they sought to erase.

## Contemporary Debates

The pair is now central to debates about multiculturalism, migration, and inequality. One debate concerns whether heterogeneity threatens social trust: some political scientists argue that diverse societies have lower levels of trust and cooperation, while anthropologists reply that trust depends less on sameness than on institutions, shared projects, and everyday contact — and that homogeneous societies are often riven by hidden divisions of class, gender, and generation. A second debate concerns the politics of recognition: should states treat citizens as members of homogeneous cultural communities, or as individuals whose identities are multiple and shifting? The answer matters for policies on language, schooling, and the funding of cultural life.

A third debate concerns method. Anthropology once studied supposedly homogeneous small societies — "the Nuer," "the Trobrianders" — as if each were a single culture. Contemporary anthropology treats heterogeneity as the starting point: every society contains internal differences, and every culture is a field of contest. The question has shifted from whether a society is homogeneous or heterogeneous to how sameness and difference are produced, experienced, and turned into resources for solidarity or into grounds for exclusion.
