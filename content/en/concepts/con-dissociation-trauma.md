# Dissociation & Trauma

**Dissociation** is a partial or complete disruption of the normal integration of consciousness, memory, identity, and sensory perception of the environment or of the body. It appears especially as a psychological response to acute or chronic **psychological trauma**, particularly in early childhood.

## Earlier Formulations

Pierre Janet used *désagrégation* for a splitting of mental functions and set out the idea in *L'automatisme psychologique* (1889), linking it to trauma and to narrowed consciousness. Early twentieth-century psychiatry often folded such states into hysteria. DSM-III (1980) separated a group of dissociative disorders from conversion and from the older hysteria category. The trauma link was debated again in the 1990s, when some clinicians treated dissociation as almost always the product of severe early abuse and others warned that suggestion, media models, and expectation could shape reported symptoms. Both the trauma association and the risk of iatrogenic elaboration remain part of the literature.

## Dissociation as a Response to Trauma

Clinically, dissociation is often described as an automatic response when a threat cannot be escaped or mastered. It is sometimes compared with a "freeze" response, but it is not the same thing as tonic immobility. Consciousness, memory, or the sense of the body fails to stay integrated with an experience that cannot be absorbed at the time. This produces symptoms such as **depersonalization** (a feeling of detachment from oneself or one's body, as if observed from outside) and **derealization** (a feeling that the surroundings are unreal or hazy), or gaps in memory: **dissociative amnesia** for traumatic events.

## The Dissociative Subtype of PTSD

DSM-5 (2013) formally added a **dissociative symptoms specifier** to PTSD ("with dissociative symptoms"), commonly called the dissociative subtype. It applies to people who meet the full criteria for PTSD and who also have persistent or recurrent symptoms of depersonalization or derealization. Research indicates that this subtype, which represents a minority of PTSD cases, is associated with a higher rate of childhood abuse history. In the model proposed by Lanius and colleagues (2010), people with the typical form show emotional under-modulation (hyperarousal, amygdala overactivity, and reduced medial prefrontal inhibition), whereas those with the dissociative subtype show the opposite pattern of **heightened prefrontal inhibition and over-modulation** of limbic activity. This is interpreted as a more extreme defensive response than emotional flooding, though the neuroimaging evidence comes from relatively small studies.

## Separate Dissociative Disorders

DSM-5-TR distinguishes between the dissociative subtype of PTSD and the **separate dissociative disorders**. The most severe of these is **dissociative identity disorder**, which in DSM-5-TR involves a disruption of identity marked by two or more distinct personality states (in some cultures described as an experience of possession), together with recurrent gaps in recall of everyday events, important personal information, or traumatic events that are inconsistent with ordinary forgetting. Depersonalization/derealization disorder and dissociative amnesia are the other principal dissociative disorders. This diagnosis remains a subject of ongoing scientific debate about how common it actually is and about the precise causal relationship between the severity of early childhood trauma (especially repeated physical and sexual abuse) and its emergence.

## Therapeutic Relevance

Clinicians have argued that recognizing the dissociative pattern may call for adapting standard **exposure-based treatments** for PTSD, since intense exposure without adequate preparation may in some patients escalate dissociation. Specialized guidelines (such as the ISSTD guidelines) describe a **phase-oriented** approach that begins with stabilization, emotion regulation skills, and grounding before trauma processing. The evidence base for these adaptations is still limited.
