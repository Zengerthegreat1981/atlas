# Generalized Other (Mead)

A pivotal concept in George Herbert Mead's theory of the emergence of the self, describing the capacity to take on the attitude of society as a whole, rather than of a particular individual, and to use it as an internal standard for evaluating and guiding one's own action.

## Content

In *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934), a book assembled by Charles W. Morris from students' lecture notes after Mead's death in 1931, Mead distinguished two stages in the development of the child's capacity to adopt the perspectives of others: "play," in which the child takes on one specific role at a time (playing the role of the mother, then the role of the doctor, in alternation with no coordination between them); and the "organized game," in which the child must call up at once the positions and roles of several simultaneous players organized by shared rules (as a player on a baseball team, Mead's own example, must know the position of every teammate and opponent in order to decide his next action). From this second, more complex capacity arises the capacity to form the "generalized other": an internal representation not of one individual but of the attitude of society or the group as a whole, organized by its shared general rules and standards. The mature individual uses this "generalized other" as a permanent internal reference for evaluating and regulating his actions, even in the absence of any particular individual actually watching him.

## Later Applications

Mead connected the generalized other with the community's role in giving the individual a unified self: the person's conduct becomes social because it is guided by the attitudes of the organized community. The idea is sometimes compared with Freud's concept of the superego, though Mead explained the origins of conscience through social interaction and role-taking rather than instinctual drives.

## Impact

This concept, together with Mead's distinction between the "I" (the spontaneous, unpredictable dimension of the self) and the "Me" (the internalized image of the expectations of others and of society), formed part of the theoretical basis on which Herbert Blumer, who coined the term in 1937, built symbolic interactionism. The concept is regarded as a more abstract and complex theoretical extension of the earlier idea of the "looking-glass self" in Charles Horton Cooley, since it moves the analysis from the individual's imagining of the judgment of specific others to the representation of a broader collective normative system.
