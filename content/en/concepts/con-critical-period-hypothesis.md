# Critical Period Hypothesis for Language Acquisition

A biological hypothesis formulated by Eric Lenneberg in 1967. It holds that the ability to acquire a first language easily and completely is constrained by a specific window of development, extending roughly from early childhood until near puberty. The window is linked to a particular neural plasticity of the developing brain.

## Evidence

Lenneberg relied on several lines of evidence. Patterns of recovery from language-related brain injuries (aphasia) differ between children and adults. Language development in children with certain intellectual disabilities (such as Down syndrome) was reported to plateau around puberty. Rare cases of severe language deprivation have also been cited. The child known as Genie was removed from extreme isolation in 1970, at the age of thirteen, after very little ordinary linguistic interaction. Susan Curtiss's study reports that Genie acquired a substantial vocabulary, while her grammatical development remained far from that of typical native speakers. A single case, with many confounding deprivations, cannot by itself confirm a biological cutoff.

## Ongoing Debate

The precise boundaries of the critical period remain open research topics with no final consensus. So do its specific neural causes (whether they are the specialization of the language-dominant hemisphere or broader cognitive factors), and whether the hypothesis applies to second language acquisition in the same way.

## The Presumed Neural Basis

Lenneberg linked the critical period to the gradual "lateralization" of language functions, usually to the left hemisphere, which is roughly complete by puberty. He proposed that the plasticity of the developing brain, before this specialization is complete, allows full language acquisition even after major brain injuries, since the other hemisphere can take over language functions compensatorily in children. This plasticity gradually loses effectiveness after puberty. Later research questioned the precision of tying lateralization exclusively to the end of the critical period. Evidence suggests that lateralization begins very early in childhood and is not complete only at puberty, as Lenneberg originally assumed.

## Application to Second Language Acquisition

Later researchers extended the question to second languages. Jacqueline Johnson and Elissa Newport (1989) tested grammaticality judgments by learners of English and reported a strong relationship between age of arrival and accuracy, with an advantage for those whose exposure began in childhood. That study measured morphosyntactic judgments, not pronunciation. David Birdsong and others have documented adult learners who reach near-native proficiency on some measures. Those results weigh against a single window that closes absolutely at puberty, and they are compatible with a gradual decline, often called a sensitive period, whose boundaries and causes remain disputed.

## Impact on Educational Policy

The hypothesis was reflected in practical educational debates about the optimal time to begin teaching foreign languages in schools, and in the establishment of early immersion programs. These rest on the assumption that the greatest benefit from childhood neural plasticity requires exposure to a second language before the critical period ends or its plasticity diminishes. Conclusively verifying this effect experimentally remains difficult, however, because of social and motivational factors that accompany the age at which language learning begins.
