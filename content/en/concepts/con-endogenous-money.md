# Endogenous Money

The theory of **endogenous money** offers a view of money supply that differs from the traditional quantity-theory school. Instead of the central bank fixing the quantity of money in advance (as an independent "external" variable), to which the economy then responds through price adjustment, this theory holds that money is created essentially inside the banking system in response to demand for loans from households and firms, and that the central bank accommodates this demand by setting the interest rate rather than controlling the quantity directly.

## Origins among post-Keynesian economists

Nicholas Kaldor developed this idea in his critiques of monetarist theory in the 1970s and early 1980s (collected in *The Scourge of Monetarism*, 1982). Basil Moore later crystallized it in his book *Horizontalists and Verticalists: The Macroeconomics of Credit Money* (1988), arguing that commercial banks first extend loans in response to profitable credit demand and then seek the reserves they need (from the central bank or the interbank market). Since the central bank is in practice obliged to supply these reserves to preserve the stability of the payments system, the money supply is effectively determined by the demand for credit, not by an independent decision of the monetary authority.

## The confrontation with the quantity theory

This view directly contradicts the monetarist assumption that inflation results from excessive money growth freely determined by the central bank. If money is endogenous, the analytical focus shifts to interest rates, credit conditions and the confidence of the banking system, rather than to the "money stock" as a variable directly controlled. This debate between the two schools has continued for decades.

## Implications for explaining crises

The theory is used to explain how credit expands rapidly in boom periods (banks lend generously in response to market optimism) and contracts sharply in recessions (lending standards tighten), regardless of the central bank's nominal policy. This intersects with Hyman Minsky's analysis of cycles of financial fragility.

## Relevance to economic policy

The view is invoked in the modern monetary theory debate about the ability of a sovereign currency issuer to finance its spending without a conventional financing constraint. Its core description of money creation through bank lending has also been endorsed in central-bank publications, notably the Bank of England's 2014 explanation that banks create deposits when they make loans.

## Horizontalists versus verticalists

Basil Moore coined the labels "horizontalists" and "verticalists". Horizontalists hold that the money supply curve is horizontal at the interest rate set by the central bank, which fully and automatically accommodates the demand for reserves. "Verticalists" are those who treat the money supply as exogenously fixed by the central bank, as monetarists do. Within the post-Keynesian camp itself, a further distinction is drawn between "accommodationists", who follow the horizontalist view, and "structuralists" (such as Robert Pollin and Thomas Palley), who hold that the central bank accommodates only partially and that banks' liquidity preference and balance-sheet conditions also shape credit supply.
