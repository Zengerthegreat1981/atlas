# Social Learning Theory

The founding theory formulated by Neal Miller and John Dollard in their book *Social Learning and Imitation* (1941), seeking to explain human behavior, including "social" behavior and psychological disorders, within a strictly behavioral framework, while framing psychoanalytic concepts (repression, conflict) as measurable variables.

## Definition

Social Learning Theory in its original form holds that social behavior arises from the interaction of four basic factors:
- **Drive**: a biological or psychological need (such as hunger, anxiety, belonging).
- **Cue**: a signaling environmental stimulus that alerts behavior (for example: the presence of food = a cue to eat).
- **Response**: the actual behavior.
- **Reward**: what follows the response and reinforces it.

## The Four Elements

Miller and Dollard described learning in terms of the sequence drive, cue, response and reward: a motivated organism responds to cues, and the response that is followed by reward (drive reduction) is strengthened. This is a conceptual scheme, not a mathematical equation.

## Major Contributions

1. **Explaining anxiety as an acquired drive**: anxiety is not an "instinct" but a drive the individual learned through association with a real threat (Dollard & Miller, 1950).
2. **Conflict as approach-avoidance conflict**: the model of "contested behavior", an individual approaching a goal that is desired but at the same time threatening (for example: dreaming of speaking before an audience but fearing embarrassment).
3. **The frustration-aggression theory (1939)**: frustration generates aggression (the Frustration-Aggression Hypothesis); this hypothesis became one of the most cited pieces of research in social psychology.
4. **Imitation learning**: complex behavior arises by imitating reinforced models, a later basis for Bandura.

## Bandura's Expansion

The original theory grew out of Hullian learning theory: imitation was explained as a response that is itself reinforced. In the 1960s Albert Bandura, with Richard Walters (*Social Learning and Personality Development*, 1963), argued that people can learn by observing models without direct reinforcement of their own, as in the Bobo doll experiments (1961). He later developed *Social Learning Theory* (1977) and "Social Cognitive Theory" (SCT, 1986), introducing:
- Cognitive factors (self-efficacy, expectations).
- Symbolic representations.
- Self-regulation.

The original Miller-Dollard theory is now regarded as the "first edition" of what would become SCT.

## Contemporary Clinical Significance

- **Anxiety**: the approach-avoidance conflict model (developed by Miller) is conceptually related to exposure therapy, in which repeated exposure reduces avoidance.
- **School programs**: reinforcing positive social behavior through modeling and clear consequences.
