# Marginal Utility

**Marginal utility** is the additional satisfaction, or utility, that an individual obtains from consuming one more unit of a particular good or service. It is the central concept of the marginal revolution in economics, formulated independently by **William Stanley Jevons** and **Carl Menger** (both 1871) and **Léon Walras** (1874).

## The law of diminishing marginal utility

The basic principle associated with this concept states that as an individual consumes additional units of a good, the utility provided by the last added unit gradually diminishes. The first glass of water for a person who is extremely thirsty gives an enormous satisfaction, while the tenth glass gives much less. This principle addresses the "paradox of value" that troubled classical economists such as Adam Smith: why is water, essential to life, cheap, while diamonds, a luxury, are expensive? The answer is that the abundance of water keeps the marginal utility of an additional unit low, while the scarcity of diamonds keeps the marginal utility of their last unit high. The price is therefore determined by marginal utility, not by total utility.

## From the labour value to the subjective value of the consumer

This concept represented a radical methodological reversal of the classical labour theory of value held by Smith, Ricardo and Marx. Instead of determining the value of a commodity by the quantity of labour needed to produce it, marginal economics links the value of a commodity to the subjective utility it gives the consumer at a particular moment. This shifts the unit of analysis from the supply side (production and labour) to the demand side (the individual consumer's decision).

## The condition of consumer equilibrium

Economists use the concept of marginal utility to formulate a precise mathematical equilibrium condition. A rational consumer distributes a limited income among different goods so that the marginal utility of the last unit spent on each good, divided by its price, is equal across all goods. If the utility of the last pound spent on good "A" is higher than its utility spent on good "B", the rational consumer will redistribute spending toward "A" until the relative marginal utilities are equal.

## From consumption to production and distribution

The concept was later extended to the factors of production themselves, not consumption alone. **John Bates Clark**, in *The Distribution of Wealth* (1899), developed the theory of **marginal productivity**, arguing that in competitive static equilibrium the wage equals the marginal product of labour and interest equals the marginal product of capital. Later neoclassical theory treated this, together with marginal utility on the demand side, as a general account of the pricing of goods and of the services of labour, capital and land. Pure profit, in that static picture, is competed away. It remains a basic tool of introductory microeconomics.

## Cardinal and ordinal utility

Jevons assumed that utility could be measured quantitatively and, to some extent, compared across persons. Later economists, notably Vilfredo Pareto, Hicks and Allen, showed that consumer theory needs only an ordinal ranking of preferences, so the equilibrium conditions can be stated in terms of marginal rates of substitution without absolute measurement of utility.
