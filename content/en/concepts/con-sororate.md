# Sororate

The sororate is the custom by which a widower marries his deceased wife's sister, or, more broadly, by which a widower takes another woman from his late wife's family. The term comes from the Latin *soror*, sister. The sororate is the counterpart of the levirate, in which a widow marries her dead husband's brother. Both customs belong to the wider system of marriage alliance: they keep the bond between two families alive after a death, protect the children of the first marriage, and preserve the bridewealth or other exchanges that the marriage created. The sororate is found in many parts of the world, from Native North America and the Amazon to Africa and the Pacific.

## Origins and Development

The sororate was first described systematically in the comparative literature on marriage. Edward Westermarck discussed it at length in *The History of Human Marriage* (1891), gathering cases from many societies and arguing that it expressed the idea that marriage was a contract between families rather than between individuals: if one wife died, the family that had given her owed a replacement. James Frazer treated the sororate in *Totemism and Exogamy* (1910) as part of the system of exogamy and alliance, a rule that regulated who could marry whom and kept groups bound together across generations.

A. R. Radcliffe-Brown gave the sororate its place in the theory of kinship in *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952). He argued that the sororate, like the levirate, must be understood through the structure of the social system: where marriage creates a durable alliance between groups, the death of a spouse threatens that alliance, and the sororate repairs it by substituting a sister for the dead wife. It also keeps the dead woman's children with their mother's sister. The Royal Anthropological Institute's *Notes and Queries on Anthropology* (1951) codified the sororate for fieldworkers, listing it among the standard forms of secondary marriage to be recorded in any ethnographic survey.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the sororate's function. The alliance explanation stresses continuity between groups: the marriage bond is renewed rather than broken, and bridewealth need not be returned. A second explanation stresses the welfare of children and of the widower himself, who gains a wife already known to the household and to the children. A third stresses the interests of the wife-giving family, which retains its claim on the husband's household and on the children. These explanations are not rivals: in practice the sororate serves all these ends at once, and ethnographers find that its meaning shifts with the circumstances, welcomed in some cases and resented in others.

A second dimension is the sororate's relationship to the levirate and to other forms of replacement marriage. The two are often paired in the literature, but they are not symmetrical. The levirate keeps children within the husband's lineage, which matters in patrilineal societies, while the sororate keeps the wife's family connected to the husband's household. In matrilineal societies the logic reverses again. Jack Goody's work on the classification of descent systems (1961) showed that the significance of the sororate depends on the wider rules of descent, residence, and inheritance in which it is embedded, and that the same custom can have different meanings in different systems.

A third dimension is consent and compulsion. Early accounts often described the sororate as an obligation, imposed on the widower and the sister alike. Later ethnography showed that it is usually a right rather than a duty: the widower may claim the sister, and the sister's family may offer her, but refusal is often possible, and the arrangement is negotiated like any marriage. The sister's own wishes, her age, and her prospects all enter into the negotiation. The sororate therefore illustrates a general point about kinship rules: they frame choices rather than dictating them, and people work within them strategically.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The sororate has declined sharply under the influence of state law, Christian and Islamic teaching, and changing ideas of marriage. Where it survives, it is often practised informally, without legal recognition, and it is sometimes condemned as a violation of the widow's and the sister's freedom to choose. Feminist anthropology has asked whether the sororate treats women as replaceable, one sister standing in for another, and whether it subordinates the sister's interests to those of the families. Others reply that the custom also protected women, giving a widowed sister's family a continuing claim on support and giving the sister herself a marriage already embedded in kinship and familiarity.

The sororate's analytical value endures even as the practice fades. It shows that marriage, in many societies, is not a private contract between two individuals but a public bond between groups, and that death does not end it but transforms it. It also shows how kinship rules manage continuity across the rupture of death. In a world where marriage is increasingly defined as a bond between individuals, the sororate stands as a reminder of an older and once widespread understanding, in which marriage joined families and outlived the people who made it.
