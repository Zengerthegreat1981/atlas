# Ontology

Ontology is the study of what exists — of the kinds of beings that populate the world and the relations among them. In philosophy the term is ancient, but in anthropology it has acquired a distinctive meaning: not a catalogue of abstract entities, but an inquiry into how different peoples understand what is real. Do animals have souls? Are mountains persons? Is a spirit a belief about the world, or a being in it? Since the early 2000s, the so-called "ontological turn" has argued that such questions should be taken seriously as claims about reality rather than dismissed as cultural beliefs about a single natural world. Ontology, in this anthropological sense, asks what worlds people live in, not merely what they think about the world.

## Origins and Development

The ontological turn grew out of dissatisfaction with the classic distinction between nature and culture. For much of the twentieth century, anthropologists assumed a shared natural reality interpreted differently by different cultures. Philippe Descola, in *Beyond Nature and Culture* (2013, first published in French in 2005), argued that the nature–culture divide is itself a historically specific, Western ontology, and that other peoples organise reality differently: some treat animals as persons with social lives, others treat spirits as masters of the forest, and still others treat the world as animated by forces that cross the boundary between human and non-human entirely. Descola proposed four basic ontologies — animism, totemism, naturalism, and analogism — as a comparative framework for mapping this diversity.

A parallel strand came from Amazonian ethnography. Eduardo Viveiros de Castro, in his essay "Exchanging Perspectives" (1998) and later in *Cannibal Metaphysics* (2014), developed the concept of perspectivism: for many Amazonian peoples, animals and spirits see themselves as human, and see humans as animals or spirits. What differs between species is not culture but the body — the point of view that a body affords. A jaguar sees blood as manioc beer; what humans call a mud puddle, a tapir experiences as a ceremonial house. Perspectivism thus inverts the familiar formula: one culture, many natures, rather than one nature, many cultures.

Roy Wagner's *The Invention of Culture* (1975) anticipated this move by arguing that anthropologists do not simply describe other cultures; their concepts are themselves inventions shaped by the encounter. Marilyn Strathern's *The Gender of the Gift* (1988) similarly showed that Melanesian persons are not bounded individuals but composite beings made of relations — a conception of personhood that unsettles Western assumptions about where a person ends and the world begins.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns the status of ontological claims. Supporters argue that treating Indigenous statements about spirits, animals, and ancestors as real — rather than as beliefs to be explained away — is an act of intellectual respect, a way of taking other peoples seriously as thinkers. Critics reply that the turn risks a new exoticism: attributing radical alterity to Indigenous peoples while ignoring the practical, material, and political contexts in which they live. If a hunter says a peccary is a person, is he making an ontological claim, or negotiating a hunting ethic, or both?

A second debate concerns method. Martin Holbraad and Morten Axel Pedersen, in *The Ontological Turn* (2017), argue that ontology is not a theory about the world but a methodology: a way of allowing ethnographic material to challenge and transform the anthropologist's own concepts. On this view, the point is not to catalogue exotic worldviews but to let the fieldwork reshape what the analyst means by "person," "thing," or "relation."

A third dimension is political. Ontological arguments have been taken up in debates about the rights of nature, where rivers and forests are granted legal personhood, and in Indigenous land claims, where the presence of ancestors and spirits in a landscape is invoked as evidence of belonging. Ontology thus connects the most abstract philosophical questions to concrete struggles over land, law, and recognition.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The ontological turn has reshaped anthropology's relations with neighbouring disciplines — philosophy, science studies, and environmental humanities — and has influenced debates about climate change and the Anthropocene, where the agency of non-human beings can no longer be treated as metaphor. Its influence is also felt in archaeology and museum practice, where objects once classified as "artefacts" are now discussed as persons, ancestors, or relations.

The sharpest critique remains that ontology can float free of history and power. Critics such as those writing in the wake of the turn insist that ontologies are not timeless worldviews but products of colonial encounter, missionisation, and state formation — and that any account of what exists must also ask who has the power to say so. The strongest contemporary work therefore pairs ontological attention with political economy, showing how ways of being are made, defended, and transformed under pressure.
