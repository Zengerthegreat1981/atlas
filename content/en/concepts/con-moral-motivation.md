# Moral Motivation

## Meaning and Definition

Moral motivation is the philosophical question of what moves a person to act in accordance with what he believes to be right. It divides into two parts. The first is descriptive: what actually drives people to commit to moral conduct? The second is metaethical and concerns the question of internalism versus externalism. Does the mere judgment that an action is morally obligatory necessarily motivate one to perform it? Or can a person judge that an action is obligatory and yet find in himself no inclination to perform it?

## The Problem of Motivation in Islamic Ethics

Muslim moral scholars addressed this question through the concepts of love, hope, and fear as the actual psychological drivers of conduct, not merely the cognitive recognition of a legal ruling. Al-Ghazali, in *Revival of the Religious Sciences*, distinguished between one who knows virtue theoretically and one in whom virtue has become a firmly established disposition. He emphasized that knowledge alone is not enough to move one to act. What is required is the discipline of the soul (*riyadat al-nafs*), which converts knowledge into effective will. This explains for him the phenomenon of the "learned sinner," who knows the truth but does not act on it because his motivation is weak despite his complete knowledge. Ibn Miskawayh held that the true motive of virtue is love of moral beauty for its own sake, once it has become a firmly established habit, and not fear of punishment or hope of reward. This makes the virtuous act proceed from a settled character rather than from an external calculation. The Ash'aris linked motivation to God's creation of the will in the heart, in comparison with His creation of power in the limbs. For them, motivation is ultimately a divine act accompanied by the servant's acquisition (*kasb*).

## In Western Philosophy

This question was the focus of the disagreement between David Hume and Immanuel Kant. Hume held that "reason is the slave of the passions" and cannot move the will by itself. There must be a prior emotional inclination, such as sympathy, into which the moral judgment is translated to become an actual motive. This is the core of motivational Humeanism. Kant, by contrast, held that respect for the moral law itself, not emotional inclination, is the only moral motive of genuine moral worth. He held that an action driven by mere emotional inclination (such as pity) lacks full moral worth, even if its outward appearance matches duty. Michael Smith formulated the "moral motivation problem," explaining the difficulty of reconciling the view that moral judgments are objective beliefs (as realists hold) with the fact that they are at the same time intrinsically motivating to action (as internalism requires).

## Illustrative Example

A person may theoretically acknowledge that giving to the poor is a moral obligation, yet feel no inner motive to do so. Externalists take this as evidence that judgment and motive can come apart. Internalists interpret it differently: this person does not truly hold the judgment as a complete genuine belief, but merely repeats it verbally.
