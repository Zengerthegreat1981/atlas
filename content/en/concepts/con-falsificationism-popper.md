# The Falsifiability Criterion and the Epistemic Boundary (Falsificationism)

The distinguishing mark of empirical science is the capacity of a theory to make predictions that can be refuted by observation, not its capacity to be verified or confirmed. It is a criterion of demarcation between science and non-science, and a method for the advancement of knowledge based on risking hypotheses and then trying to refute them.

## The Original Formulation in Popper

Karl Popper (1902–1994) formulated this criterion in his book *Logik der Forschung* (1934, dated 1935 on the title page; English translation, *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1959), in reply to Hume's problem of induction and to the verification principle adopted by the positivist Vienna Circle. For him, a scientific theory (such as Einstein's general theory of relativity) is distinguished from non-scientific claims (such as psychoanalysis, Adler's individual psychology or Marxist historical theory, in his critique of them) in that it lays down definite conditions under which empirical observation could refute it if it were false. The 1919 eclipse experiment, which tested Einstein's prediction of the bending of light, was for him the model of risk: a bold prediction that could have been refuted. In *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963) he extended the idea into a general method of critical rationalism.

## The Logical Basis

The criterion rests on a logical asymmetry: no set of confirming observations proves a universal proposition, but a single contrary observation suffices to refute it, according to the valid form of inference (denying the consequent). Popper therefore replaced confirmation with the concept of "corroboration", which is an assessment of a theory's resistance to severe tests, not of its probability of being true.

## The Debate with Logical Positivism

This criterion is the second party in the founding debate in the philosophy of science between the verification principle of Schlick and Carnap (the Vienna Circle) and Popper's principle of falsification, a debate that began in effect with the publication of Popper's 1934 book and is still a reference in teaching the philosophy of science today. Popper declared that he did not intend his criterion to distinguish meaning from meaninglessness as the positivists did, but to distinguish the scientific from the non-scientific.

## Arab Parallels

Some historians of science hold that Ibn al-Haytham (d. 1040), in the *Book of Optics* and in *Doubts Concerning Ptolemy*, followed a method based on testing earlier theories by experiment and objecting to them, though that is closer to reliance on testing than to the Popperian logical formulation of falsification.

## Influence and Limits

The falsification criterion was later used as a tool for drawing the boundary between science and non-science (the demarcation problem), but it was subjected to criticism by later philosophers of science. Pierre Duhem and Quine showed that hypotheses are not tested singly but together with auxiliary hypotheses, so a conflict with observation does not identify which part is to be rejected. Thomas Kuhn in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) showed that scientists rarely abandon their theories as soon as an anomaly appears, and Imre Lakatos (1970) put forward "scientific research programmes" as an alternative, arguing that what is appraised is a series of theories with a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, not an isolated theory. Others hold that the criterion does not account for probabilistic hypotheses and does not guarantee that a theory that has survived will always survive.
