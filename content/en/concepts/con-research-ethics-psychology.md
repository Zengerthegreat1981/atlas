# Research Ethics in Psychology

Research ethics is the set of principles and procedures that govern how researchers may treat the people who take part in studies. Psychology studies thoughts, emotions and behaviour, sometimes under stress or with incomplete disclosure, so the ethical questions are persistent: what may be asked of participants, what must they be told, and how are their interests protected against the researcher's wish to learn something?

## Historical background

Modern research ethics grew from revelations of abuse. The Nuremberg Code of 1947, written after the trial of Nazi physicians, put voluntary consent at the centre of acceptable research. The Declaration of Helsinki, first adopted by the World Medical Association in 1964, extended and revised these principles. In the United States, public exposure of the Tuskegee syphilis study, in which treatment was withheld from Black men for decades, led to the National Research Act of 1974 and the Belmont Report of 1979. Behavioural science supplied its own controversies. Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments of the early 1960s caused many participants acute distress. Philip Zimbardo's 1971 Stanford prison simulation has been widely criticised on ethical grounds, and also on scientific grounds, because of the role of the experimenters in shaping participants' behaviour. The Little Albert study by John Watson and Rosalie Rayner in 1920 conditioned fear in an infant with no apparent attempt to remove it afterwards. Such cases are routinely cited when the rules are explained.

## Core principles

The Belmont Report named three principles. **Respect for persons** requires that participants be treated as autonomous agents and that those with diminished autonomy, such as children, receive added protection; in practice this means informed consent. **Beneficence** requires minimising possible harm and maximising benefit. **Justice** asks that the burdens and benefits of research be distributed fairly, so that no group is used merely because it is convenient or vulnerable. The American Psychological Association's Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct, first issued in 1953 and revised several times, sets out comparable general principles and specific standards, and other national societies have their own codes.

## Informed consent

Participants should receive enough information about purpose, procedures, risks, benefits and their right to withdraw to decide freely whether to take part, and consent should be voluntary. Consent is treated as a process rather than a signature. Special arrangements apply to children, who give assent alongside parental permission, to people with cognitive impairment, and to people in dependent relationships with researchers such as students or prisoners. Participants may withdraw without penalty, and confidentiality or anonymity of data must be protected and explained.

## Deception and debriefing

Some psychological questions are hard to study if participants know the purpose, since awareness changes behaviour. Ethics codes therefore allow deception only in limited circumstances: when the study has significant value, when no non-deceptive alternative is workable, and when the deception does not conceal expected physical pain or severe distress. Participants must be debriefed as soon as feasible, with the deception explained and an opportunity to withdraw their data. Critics, including Diana Baumrind in a well-known 1964 response to Milgram, argue that deception undermines trust and may cause lasting harm. Some disciplines, such as experimental economics, avoid deception altogether.

## Oversight

In many countries research involving people must be reviewed by an independent committee before it begins, called an institutional review board in the United States and a research ethics committee elsewhere. Reviewers weigh risk against benefit, examine consent materials and consider protection of vulnerable groups. Defenders see review as essential protection. Critics argue that it can be inconsistent between institutions, can grow bureaucratic, and sometimes protects the institution more than participants.

## Wider issues

Ethics extends beyond participant welfare. Scientific integrity matters: fabricating or falsifying data, as in the Diederik Stapel case exposed in 2011, harms the field and the public. Questionable analytic practices raise ethical as well as statistical concerns, because they waste participants' effort. Newer topics include data privacy, research using online platforms and social media, consent for large datasets, cultural differences in what counts as respectful research, and ensuring that findings are not used to stigmatise groups.

## Limits

Codes give principles but not algorithms; applying them requires judgement, and reasonable people disagree about, for example, how much distress is acceptable. Compliance with a code is not the same as moral adequacy, and rules lag behind new methods.
