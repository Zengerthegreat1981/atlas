# Prejudice: Attitudes, Stereotypes, and Discrimination

**Prejudice** is a negative attitude toward the members of a group, held because they belong to that group rather than because of anything they have done as individuals. It differs from a stereotype, which is a belief about a group, and from discrimination, which is behaviour toward a group; prejudice is the attitude that links the two, turning a belief into a readiness to exclude, devalue, or harm. It is one of sociology's oldest topics, because it names the psychological and social mechanism through which inequality is justified and reproduced.

## Definition and Development

Prejudice is usually understood as having three components: a cognitive component (stereotyped beliefs about the group), an affective component (feelings of dislike, fear, or contempt), and a behavioural disposition (a readiness to avoid, exclude, or discriminate). A person can hold a stereotype without prejudice — believing, for example, that a group is musical — but prejudice adds a negative evaluation and a sense of threat or inferiority. Prejudice can be overt, expressed openly, or subtle, expressed through discomfort, avoidance, or the language of cultural difference rather than of race.

The study of prejudice began in earnest after the Second World War, when scholars sought to explain the Holocaust and American racial segregation. Early work treated prejudice as a personality problem — the product of authoritarian upbringing or psychological frustration, as in Adorno and colleagues' *The Authoritarian Personality* (1950). Later work shifted the explanation to social structure: prejudice is learned in families, schools, and media, and it serves the interests of dominant groups by justifying inequality. Contemporary research emphasizes that prejudice is often implicit — operating without conscious awareness — and that it is shaped by institutions as much as by individuals.

## Key Contributions

**Gordon W. Allport** (1897–1967) set an agenda for the modern study of prejudice in *The Nature of Prejudice* (1954). Allport defined ethnic prejudice as "an antipathy based upon a faulty and inflexible generalization," and distinguished between prejudiced attitudes and discriminatory acts. His most influential idea was the contact hypothesis: prejudice between groups can be reduced by contact, but only under certain conditions — equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support. Mere contact, he warned, can deepen prejudice as easily as reduce it.

**Henri Tajfel** (1919–1982) showed, in the minimal group experiments of the early 1970s (Tajfel and colleagues, 1971) and in the synthesis of *Human Groups and Social Categories* (1981), that prejudice does not require a history of conflict or even real differences between groups. In his minimal group experiments, people assigned to groups on trivial grounds, such as a stated preference for one painter over another (a later variant by Billig and Tajfel in 1973 assigned them openly at random), tended to favour their own group over the other. Tajfel's social identity theory, developed with John Turner (1979), explained this: people derive self-esteem from their group memberships, and they enhance that esteem by viewing their own group favourably and others unfavourably. Prejudice, in this account, is a by-product of the ordinary human need for a positive social identity.

**Thomas F. Pettigrew** (born 1931) refined the contact hypothesis in "Intergroup Contact Theory" (*Annual Review of Psychology*, 1998), reviewing decades of research to show that contact reduces prejudice most reliably when it involves friendship — sustained, personal, cross-group relationships — rather than brief or superficial encounters. Pettigrew also emphasized that contact works through several mechanisms: learning about the other group, changing behaviour, building emotional bonds, and reappraising one's own group. His synthesis turned Allport's conditions from a list into a theory of how contact changes minds.

**Rupert Brown** synthesized the social psychology of prejudice in *Prejudice: Its Social Psychology* (1995), bringing together research on stereotypes, intergroup relations, and the cognitive processes — categorization, illusory correlation, attribution — that sustain prejudice. Brown's work showed that prejudice is not a single phenomenon but a family of processes, and that reducing it requires interventions at several levels: individual attitudes, group relations, and the social structures that make prejudice useful.

## Examples

Prejudice appears in many domains. In hiring, studies using identical résumés with different names have repeatedly shown that applicants with minority-sounding names receive fewer callbacks. In housing, estate agents steer minority buyers away from white neighbourhoods. In schools, teachers' expectations of pupils vary with the pupils' ethnicity and class, shaping outcomes in ways that confirm the original prejudice. Prejudice also operates within groups: colourism — preference for lighter skin within a racial group — shows that prejudice can be internalized and turned inward.

Yet prejudice is not destiny. Desegregation, intergroup friendship, and changes in law and norms have reduced overt prejudice in many societies, even as subtler forms persist. The history of prejudice is therefore also a history of its contestation.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics argue that the psychological study of prejudice individualizes what is fundamentally a structural problem: focusing on attitudes can obscure the institutions — labour markets, housing systems, policing — that produce inequality regardless of what anyone feels. Others note that prejudice research has focused on race and ethnicity while neglecting prejudice based on class, disability, age, and sexuality, and that attention to intersectionality — the interaction of multiple prejudices — came later to this literature. Today, prejudice is increasingly expressed and amplified online, and encoded in algorithms that reproduce historical biases in hiring, lending, and policing. The concept remains essential, but its study now spans psychology, sociology, and the analysis of institutions and technologies.

## Intersectionality

Intersectionality, a term introduced by the legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989, holds that positions such as race and gender combine to produce experiences that cannot be read off from either one separately. Crenshaw showed that anti-discrimination law could fail Black women when it asked whether they had been treated like Black men or like white women. Patricia Hill Collins, in *Black Feminist Thought* (1990), described interlocking oppressions organised in a matrix of domination. For prejudice research the implication is that stereotypes of a group often rest on a presumed typical member. Valerie Purdie-Vaughns and Richard Eibach (2008) proposed that people with several subordinate identities may suffer a distinctive invisibility, because they fit the prototype of none of their groups. Social dominance theorists, by contrast, have argued that men of subordinated groups are often the main targets of direct, severe discrimination. These claims can both be examined by asking which harm, in which setting, falls on whom, and they caution against simply adding separate measures of prejudice together.
