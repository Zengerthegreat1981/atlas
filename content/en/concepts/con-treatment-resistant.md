# Treatment-Resistant Illness

## Definition

Treatment resistance refers to cases that do not respond sufficiently to two or more adequate treatments, in dose and duration. There is no single agreed definition. In depression, the most common definition is the failure of two different antidepressants. In schizophrenia, it is the failure of two antipsychotics given at adequate dose and duration, with adherence confirmed. "Apparent resistance" must first be excluded: misdiagnosis, insufficient dosing, non-adherence, comorbid illness, substance use, and persistent stressors.

## Roots in Arab Medicine

Muslim physicians took care to record cases in which treatment failed. Abu Bakr al-Razi preserved documented clinical observations of his patients and their outcomes in *al-Hawi*, and he wrote a book of doubts about Galen in which he challenged the authority of his predecessor when observation contradicted it (see the entry on al-Razi). The story in *Chahar Maqala* by Nizami 'Arudi of Samarqand recounts how Ibn Sina diagnosed a case of lovesickness in the prince of Jurjan by taking his pulse while naming places and people, and then treated him by arranging his marriage. It is an entertaining story and not an accurate record. This does not mean that classical medicine knew "treatment resistance" in the modern sense.

## Treatment-Resistant Depression

The STAR*D study, whose results Rush and colleagues published in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 2006, found that roughly 30% of patients reached remission after the first step (citalopram). The reported cumulative remission rate after up to four steps was about 67%, although this figure was calculated under assumptions that later reanalyses have criticized, and relapse rose as the number of steps increased. McIntyre and colleagues reviewed the definitions of treatment-resistant depression, along with the evidence and an algorithmic approach, in the *Journal of Affective Disorders* in 2014. The options include augmenting the drug with lithium or a second-generation antipsychotic; transcranial magnetic stimulation (O'Reardon and colleagues, *Biological Psychiatry*, 2007); electroconvulsive therapy, whose effectiveness was confirmed by the UK ECT Review Group in *The Lancet* in 2003; ketamine, which Zarate and colleagues showed to act rapidly in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 2006; and intranasal esketamine (Popova and colleagues, *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 2019). Mayberg and colleagues tested deep brain stimulation in *Neuron* in 2005 (see the entry on fMRI and PET findings).

## Treatment-Resistant Schizophrenia

Clozapine is the antipsychotic with the best-established evidence in treatment-resistant schizophrenia, as Kane and colleagues showed in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 1988. The TRRIP group (Howes and colleagues, *American Journal of Psychiatry*, 2017) proposed unified criteria. Some patients remain resistant even to clozapine (see the entry on antipsychotic treatment response), and outcomes improve with psychosocial rehabilitation (see the entry on recovery from psychosis).

## Limits and Meaning

The term is criticized because it attributes the failure to the patient ("resistant") rather than to the treatment, and alternative labels such as "difficult to treat" have been proposed. Definitions also vary between studies, which makes comparison difficult, and the evidence for the effectiveness of later treatment steps is weaker. Clinicians generally stress a comprehensive review of the diagnosis and of social factors, not merely a series of drugs (see the entry on polypharmacy and drug interactions). This entry is educational and descriptive, not clinical advice.
