# Thinking Substance and Extended Substance (Res Cogitans & Res Extensa)

The substance dualism of René Descartes (1596–1650), canonically articulated in the *Meditations on First Philosophy* (*Meditationes de prima philosophia*, 1641), divides created reality into two ontologically distinct, mutually exclusive substances: immaterial thinking substance (*res cogitans*) and material extended substance (*res extensa*). This radical cleavage eliminated the Aristotelian-Scholastic apparatus of substantial forms and vegetative/animal souls, reducing the physical cosmos to homogeneous matter and mechanistic motion governed by mathematical laws.

## Textual Evolution and Conceptual Analysis

Descartes developed the vocabulary and logical mechanics of dualism across several major works:

1. **The French Formulations (*Discourse on Method*, 1637)**: In Part IV of the *Discourse*, Descartes formulated his famous *je pense, donc je suis* (later known in Latin as *cogito, ergo sum*) and identified the soul as "a substance whose whole essence or nature is simply to think" (*une substance dont toute l'essence ou la nature n'est que de penser*), possessing existence independent of any material place or physical body.
2. **The Latin Formulation (*Meditations*, 1641)**: In the Second Meditation, having deployed radical doubt to bracket the physical senses and the external world, Descartes asks *Quid igitur sum?* (What then am I?), answering: "A thing that thinks. What is that? A thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, is willing, is unwilling, and also imagines and has sensory perceptions." In the Sixth Meditation, he establishes the **real distinction** (*distinctio realis*): because he clearly and distinctly understands himself as a thinking, non-extended thing, and clearly understands body as an extended, non-thinking thing, God is capable of creating them apart; hence the mind is completely distinct from the body.
3. **Systematization in the *Principles of Philosophy* (1644)**: In *Principia Philosophiae* (Part I, §§51–54), Descartes provides strict ontological definitions:
   - **Substance (*Substantia*)**: An entity that exists in such a way as to depend on nothing else for its existence (§51). Strictly speaking, only God meets this definition; created substances depend only on God's ordinary concurrence.
   - **Principal Attribute (*Attributum Principale*)**: Each substance possesses one sovereign property that constitutes its nature and essence (§53). For mind, this is **thought** (*cogitatio*); for matter, it is **extension** in length, breadth, and depth (*extensio in longum, latum, et profundum*).
   - **Modes (*Modi*)**: Variable determinations of the principal attribute (§56). Doubting, willing, and judging are modes of thought; shape (*figura*), motion (*motus*), and spatial location are modes of extension.

## The Interaction Problem and the Mind-Body Union

The complete ontological separation of the two substances generated the classical mind-body problem: how can an unextended, non-spatial mental substance causally affect, or be affected by, an extended physical mechanism?

- **Princess Elisabeth's Critique (1643)**: In their renowned correspondence, Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed Descartes to explain how a soul could impel animal spirits to produce voluntary bodily motion without physical contact or surface. Descartes conceded the difficulty, distinguishing three primitive notions: the notion of the soul, the notion of the body, and the notion of the substantial union (*l'union substantielle*) between them, which is experienced directly in ordinary life rather than deduced by pure intellect.
- **The Pineal Gland (*Les Passions de l'âme*, 1649)**: To account for physiological interaction, Descartes situated the primary seat of the soul in the conarium or pineal gland at the center of the brain (Articles 31–34), where animal spirits could interact with mental intentions.
- **Subsequent Metaphysical Solutions**: Later 17th-century rationalists found Descartes's interactionism untenable: Nicolas Malebranche developed **occasionalism** (God is the sole true causal agent on the occasion of mental or physical events), Baruch Spinoza asserted **substance monism** (mind and extension are two attributes of one infinite Substance), and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz formulated **pre-established harmony** (mind and body are non-interacting substances synchronized by God).

## Comparison with Ibn Sina's Floating Man

Scholars frequently compare Descartes's *cogito* and dualism with the "floating man" or "suspended man" thought experiment (*al-insān al-mu'allaq fī al-hawā'*) proposed by Ibn Sina (Avicenna) in *al-Shifā'* (*De Anima*) and *al-Ishārāt wa-l-Tanbīhāt*. Ibn Sina asks the reader to imagine being created fully mature, suspended in a void with no sensory perception of limbs or external environment; even in this total sensory deprivation, one remains immediately conscious of one's own existence (*dhāt*). 

However, while Ibn Sina uses this thought experiment to prove that self-consciousness is immaterial and distinct from the bodily organs, he retains a modified Aristotelian framework wherein the soul is the entelechy and perfection (*kamāl awwal*) of a natural organic body. Ibn Sina does not reduce the physical universe to dead Cartesian mechanism, maintaining a vitalistic hierarchy of vegetative and animal faculties that Descartes abolished.
