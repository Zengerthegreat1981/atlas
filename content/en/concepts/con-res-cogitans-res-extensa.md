# Thinking Substance and Extended Substance (Res Cogitans & Res Extensa)

The dualism of René Descartes, formulated in the *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641): the soul is an immaterial, free, thinking substance (res cogitans), and matter is an extended, geometric, mechanical, unconscious substance (res extensa). The distinction between the two substances is the metaphysical pillar of his philosophy, and by it he ends what he inherited from Scholasticism of "substantial forms" and vegetative and animal souls in nature, so that nature as a whole becomes extension and motion subject to mathematical laws.

## The Epistemic and Analytic Dimension

The dualism rests on the argument from "clear and distinct conception" in the Sixth Meditation: I can conceive of myself clearly as a thinking, non-extended thing, and conceive of the body clearly as an extended, non-thinking thing, and whatever can be conceived as separate can exist separately by God's power, so the soul is truly distinct from the body. This is a result grounded in the cogito ("I think, therefore I am"), which establishes the existence of the thinking self before any knowledge of the body. As for extension, for Descartes it is the essence of matter, and by it all its primary qualities are explained; he set this out in detail in the *Principles of Philosophy* (1644). This dualism is the cornerstone of the Cartesian school.

## The Problem of Interaction

The complete separation of the two substances generates the mind-body problem: how can a non-extended substance interact causally with an extended substance? Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia put it to Descartes in her letter of 1643, and he acknowledged that the substantial union of soul and body is a self-evident experience that pure reason does not understand, then proposed in the *Passions of the Soul* (1649) that the pineal gland is the point of contact. Later reactions came to attempt a solution: occasionalism in Malebranche, the unity of substance in Spinoza, and pre-established harmony in Leibniz.

## Link to the Islamic Heritage

Scholars compare Descartes's argument from conception with the argument of the "flying man" (or the man suspended in the air) in Ibn Sina in *al-Shifa'* and *al-Isharat*, where one imagines oneself existing without sensation of one's body and thereby establishes self-awareness apart from the body. But Ibn Sina does not draw, as Descartes does, a division between a thinking soul and a mechanical nature; he remains within a modified Aristotelian framework that defines the soul as the first perfection of a natural body, so the parallel between the two arguments is partial.

## Use and Influence in Contemporary Thought

The dualism formed a starting point for the critique of modern philosophy of mind, including Gilbert Ryle's view, which described it as the "ghost in the machine" in *The Concept of Mind* (1949), and for discussions of neural reductionism, since the question of the relation between consciousness and the brain remains a direct echo of the split between the extended and the non-extended. Critics from the traditions of phenomenology (Merleau-Ponty) and embodiment hold that Descartes neglected the experience of the lived body, while contemporary defenders of "property dualism" try to formulate a lighter conception without reviving the two substances.

## Selected Quotations

"I am, then, in the strict sense only a thing that thinks; that is, I am a mind, or intelligence, or intellect, or reason" — Second Meditation (trans. Cottingham, Stoothoff and Murdoch).
