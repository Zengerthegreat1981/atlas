# Involuntary Commitment & Civil Rights

The legal-medical procedure by which a person is confined to a psychiatric institution to receive treatment without their personal consent. It is based on a clinical and legal assessment that the person poses a risk to themselves or others, or is severely incapable of caring for themselves. It is one of the most controversial medical procedures, because it directly intersects with the constitutional right to personal liberty.

## The Dangerousness Standard

The **dangerousness criterion** became prominent in the United States in the late 1960s and 1970s (for example in California's Lanterman-Petris-Short Act of 1967 and in federal court decisions such as *Lessard v. Schmidt*, 1972), as an attempt to restrict the power of involuntary commitment. Earlier commitment statutes had rested broadly on the existence of mental illness and a perceived need for treatment (the state's *parens patriae* power), with limited procedural protection. In *O'Connor v. Donaldson* (1975) the US Supreme Court held that a state may not confine a non-dangerous person who is capable of surviving safely in freedom. Under this standard, a person may not be committed involuntarily unless they pose a direct danger to themselves (suicidal tendencies) or to others (aggressive tendencies), or are "gravely disabled," meaning unable to provide for their basic needs because of their mental condition. The precise wording and application of this standard vary between legal jurisdictions within the United States and between countries.

## Constitutional and Procedural Safeguards

Under the principle of **due process** in the Fourteenth Amendment of the US Constitution, people subject to involuntary commitment retain basic rights. In *Addington v. Texas* (1979) the Supreme Court held that civil commitment requires at least "clear and convincing" evidence. State statutes typically add rights to notice, a hearing, counsel, and periodic review, and emergency holds are time-limited (often a matter of days, with the length varying by state). Other countries have comparable but different rules. For example, in England and Wales the Mental Health Act 1983 allows detention for assessment (section 2, up to 28 days) or treatment (section 3, initially up to six months) on grounds of mental disorder and risk to the person's health or safety or to others, with a right of appeal to a tribunal. The Mental Health Act 2025 amends these provisions, among other things introducing a serious-harm test and shortening the initial section 3 period to three months; its amendments apply only once brought into force.

## The Central Ethical Tension

Involuntary commitment presents a structural tension between two principles. The **principle of beneficence** is the duty to protect the patient and those around them from serious potential harm. The **principle of autonomy** is the individual's right to refuse medical treatment, even if this decision appears irrational from an outside perspective. Patients' rights movements (sometimes linked with the "anti-psychiatry" movement) have raised wide criticism of historical involuntary commitment practices, demanding that it be limited to the strictly necessary minimum with rigorous procedural safeguards.

## Limits

- Involuntary commitment criteria and durations vary widely between countries and legal jurisdictions. This makes accurate generalization about "the law" in this field difficult without specifying the jurisdiction concerned.
- The ethical debate over the optimal balance between protecting the safety of the individual and society, on the one hand, and respecting personal autonomy, on the other, remains open, without a final consensus solution.
