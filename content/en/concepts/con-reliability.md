# Reliability

Reliability is the consistency of a measure: the degree to which repeated measurements of the same thing produce the same result. A bathroom scale that gives a different reading every time you step on it is unreliable, however accurate any single reading might be. In sociology, reliability is a foundational criterion of measurement quality. Surveys, scales, interviews, observations, and content analyses all depend on it: if a measure is unreliable, its results are noise, and no substantive conclusion can be trusted.

## Definition

Reliability is usually distinguished from validity. Validity asks whether a measure captures what it claims to capture; reliability asks only whether it does so consistently. A measure can be reliable without being valid — a scale that always reads five kilos too heavy is consistent but wrong — but it cannot be valid without being reliable, since inconsistent results cannot consistently hit the target. This asymmetry makes reliability the first, necessary test of any instrument.

Sociologists distinguish several forms. Test-retest reliability is stability over time: the same respondents, measured twice, should give similar answers. Internal consistency is agreement among the items of a scale: questions meant to measure the same attitude should correlate with one another. Inter-rater reliability is agreement between observers: two coders classifying the same interview material, or two observers rating the same behaviour, should reach similar judgements. Each form addresses a different source of error — the instability of the respondent, the incoherence of the instrument, or the subjectivity of the observer — and a thorough study reports more than one.

## Origins and Development

Reliability entered the social sciences from psychometrics, the psychology of mental measurement. Its key technical tool is Cronbach's alpha, introduced by Lee Cronbach in 1951, a coefficient that summarises the internal consistency of a scale from the correlations among its items. Alpha became the standard report in survey research: a scale with an alpha above 0.70 is conventionally treated as acceptably reliable, though the threshold is a rule of thumb rather than a law.

The broader framework came from classical test theory, developed through the mid-twentieth century and codified in works such as Jum Nunnally's *Psychometric Theory* (1967; second edition 1978), which taught generations of researchers to think of every observed score as a true score plus error, and to design instruments that minimise the error component. Donald Campbell and Donald Fiske's paper on the multitrait-multimethod matrix (1959) extended the idea: reliability and validity should be assessed by measuring several traits with several methods and examining the pattern of correlations, so that the researcher can separate what is being measured from how it is being measured. Edward Carmines and Richard Zeller's *Reliability and Validity Assessment* (1979) brought these ideas to sociologists in an accessible form, while Klaus Krippendorff's work on content analysis (1980) developed a reliability coefficient suited to coded textual data, where agreement must be corrected for chance.

## Key Debates

A first debate concerns the trade-off between reliability and richness. Highly standardised instruments — closed survey questions, strict coding schemes — are reliable precisely because they restrict what can be recorded. Qualitative researchers argue that this restriction loses the meaning of social action: an interview that follows a rigid script may be reliable but shallow. The debate is not about whether reliability matters, but about what kind of consistency is appropriate for the material at hand. For ethnographic observation, consistency may mean that another researcher, given the same field notes, would reach a similar interpretation — a standard of transparency rather than of numerical agreement.

A second debate concerns the limits of statistical fixes. A high alpha can be produced by a scale with many redundant items, or by items that share a method rather than a meaning. Reliability coefficients describe the instrument, not the world: a perfectly reliable measure of a concept that does not exist is still useless. This is why reliability must always be reported alongside validity evidence, and why the numbers never substitute for theoretical judgement about what is being measured.

A third debate is about the human observer. Inter-rater reliability studies repeatedly show that agreement is hardest where judgement is most consequential — coding open-ended responses, rating classroom interaction, assessing interview quality. Training, clear coding manuals, and double-coding with adjudication improve agreement, but they also reveal a deeper point: measurement in sociology is a social process, and its reliability depends on shared understandings among researchers, not only on technical design.

## Contemporary Relevance

Reliability has gained new urgency in the age of big data and machine learning. Automated measures — sentiment scores, topic models, algorithmic classifications — are often treated as objective, yet their reliability is rarely tested: the same model, run on slightly different data or with a different random seed, can produce different results. The replication crisis in the social sciences has likewise put measurement under scrutiny, as findings built on unreliable scales fail to reproduce. At the same time, new tools offer new solutions: preregistration, open data, and shared code make it possible to check the consistency of a measure in ways earlier generations could not. The lesson of reliability endures: before asking what the data mean, ask whether the data would say the same thing twice.
