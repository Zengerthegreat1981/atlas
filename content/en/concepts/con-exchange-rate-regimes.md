# Exchange Rate Regimes

**Exchange rate regimes** are the institutional arrangements that determine how the value of a country's currency is set against the currencies of other countries. They range along a spectrum from free floating to strict fixed pegging.

## The spectrum from floating to pegging

Under a **free float**, the exchange rate is left to be determined entirely by the forces of supply and demand in the foreign exchange market, without direct government intervention. By contrast, a **fixed peg** fixes the value of the local currency at a specified level against a reference currency (usually the dollar), and the monetary authority commits to defending this level through direct intervention in the market or through more rigid mechanisms such as a currency board. Between the two extremes lie several intermediate regimes, described below.

## Intermediate regimes

A **managed float** leaves the rate to the market while the central bank intervenes, without announcing a parity, to limit swings. A **crawling peg** adjusts a declared parity in small, frequent steps; John Williamson analysed this class of rule in his work on exchange-rate policy. A **band** or target zone, such as the European Exchange Rate Mechanism before 1999, commits the authority to keep the rate inside announced margins. A **currency board** is a hard peg in which the monetary base is backed by foreign reserves at a fixed rate; Hong Kong has operated one since 1983. These categories are ideal types. The International Monetary Fund's *de facto* classification, and the historical reconstruction of Reinhart and Rogoff (2004), often differ from the regime a government officially declares.

## Friedman's case for floating

In his article "The Case for Flexible Exchange Rates" (1953), **Milton Friedman** defended free floating, arguing that it allows the economy to absorb external shocks automatically through adjustment of the exchange rate, rather than bearing internal recession or imported inflation. It also frees domestic monetary policy to serve internal goals such as employment and inflation, instead of permanently defending a pegged level.

## The Bretton Woods system as historical background

The **Bretton Woods Conference (1944)** established the framework that governed the international monetary system after the Second World War. It fixed the currencies of member states (within narrow bands) against the dollar, which in turn was convertible into gold at 35 dollars an ounce. The arrangement collapsed after the Nixon shock of August 1971 (suspension of dollar convertibility) and the failure of the Smithsonian Agreement, and by 1973 the major currencies were floating. Classification of actual regimes is not always straightforward: the International Monetary Fund's *de facto* classification of intermediate regimes often differs from what countries officially declare.

## The Arab case: Gulf pegs

The Arab Gulf states offer a prominent example of long-lasting fixed pegs. Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar, Bahrain and Oman have pegged their currencies to the US dollar for decades, while Kuwait has pegged its dinar to an undisclosed basket of currencies since 2007. This choice suits oil economies whose exports are priced in dollars to begin with and which import a large share of their needs. The peg reduces the risk of exchange-rate volatility for trade and investment, at the cost of giving up independence in monetary policy. Egypt and Lebanon, by contrast, illustrate the risks of defending an exchange rate that comes under pressure: Egypt devalued and floated several times (notably in 2016 and 2022–2024), and Lebanon's long-standing peg collapsed from 2019 as reserves were depleted.

## Trade-offs and limits

No regime is absolutely ideal. A peg provides stability and confidence, but exposes the economy to speculative attacks if it appears unsustainable. Floating provides flexibility, but increases the volatility of trade and investment. This trade-off is precisely formulated by the concept of the impossible trinity.
