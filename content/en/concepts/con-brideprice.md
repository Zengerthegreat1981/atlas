# Brideprice

Brideprice — also called bridewealth — is the transfer of goods, livestock, money, or labour from the groom's kin to the bride's kin at marriage. It is one of the most widespread marriage transactions in the ethnographic record, documented across sub-Saharan Africa, Melanesia, and parts of Asia, and it must be distinguished from dowry, in which wealth flows in the opposite direction, from the bride's family to the couple. Anthropologists treat brideprice not as a purchase price, but as a social institution: it creates and confirms relationships between groups, compensates the bride's family for the loss of a member's labour and fertility, and gives the marriage public legitimacy. The payment is typically negotiated, paid in instalments, and remembered for years.

## Origins and Development

The comparative study of brideprice began with the classic ethnographies of African kinship. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) described how cattle transferred at marriage tied lineages together and how disputes over outstanding payments could sustain or sever alliances for a generation. Meyer Fortes's *The Web of Kinship Among the Tallensi* (1949) showed a similar pattern in northern Ghana, where brideprice payments were woven into a long-term cycle of debt and reciprocity between intermarrying lineages. In both cases, marriage was never a private contract between two individuals; it was an alliance between groups, and brideprice was its material signature.

Jack Goody's comparative work gave the institution a global frame. In *Bridewealth and Dowry* (1973), co-edited with S. J. Tambiah, Goody argued that bridewealth predominates in societies where land is abundant and labour is scarce, and where children — and therefore women's reproductive capacity — are the key resource. Dowry, by contrast, predominates where land is scarce and must be transmitted to heirs. Goody's thesis linked marriage payments to systems of property, inheritance, and production, moving the debate beyond the moral language of "buying a wife" toward a materialist explanation of why different societies pay in different directions.

John Comaroff's edited volume *The Meaning of Marriage Payments* (1980) deepened the analysis by insisting that payments are polysemic: the same cattle or cash can simultaneously mean compensation, alliance, prestige, and obligation, depending on who is speaking and when. Meaning, Comaroff argued, is not fixed in the goods themselves but negotiated in the politics of marriage.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The most persistent debate concerns commodification: does brideprice turn women into commodities? Early colonial observers and missionaries often condemned it as wife-purchase, and some feminist anthropologists have argued that it institutionalises male control over women's fertility and labour. Others reply that the transfer is better understood as a gift that creates obligation and recognition, and that women themselves often defend the institution as a guarantee of their status, a claim on their husband's kin, and a resource they can mobilise in disputes. Ethnography shows both dynamics at work, and the balance depends on local histories of gender, property, and power.

A second dimension is change. Brideprice has not disappeared with monetisation; in many places it has expanded. Cash, consumer goods, and even bank transfers have joined cattle and shell valuables, and the amounts demanded have risen sharply in some urban settings, leading to complaints that marriage has become unaffordable for young men. Anthropologists link this inflation to wage labour, migration, and the rising cost of education, showing how a "traditional" institution is continually remade by the modern economy.

A third dimension is the distinction between brideprice and other transactions with which it is often confused. It is not a payment for the bride herself, nor is it the same as the "bride service" found in Amazonia and elsewhere, where the groom works for his in-laws instead of paying goods. Careful comparison of these forms has been central to kinship theory, because each form implies a different structure of obligation and alliance.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Brideprice remains a live political issue. In Uganda, Kenya, and South Africa, activists and courts have debated whether brideprice should be refundable on divorce, whether it contributes to domestic violence, and whether it should be capped by law. In 2015, Uganda's Supreme Court ruled that the refund of brideprice could not be a condition for dissolving a marriage, a decision welcomed by women's rights advocates and contested by others who saw the payment as essential to cultural identity. Anthropologists contribute to these debates by documenting how the institution actually works — who pays, who receives, who benefits, and who is harmed — rather than treating it as a timeless custom.

The contemporary critique also turns on method: scholars caution against reducing brideprice to a single function, whether economic, political, or symbolic. A payment that looks like compensation to an economist may look like respect to a participant, and like oppression to a critic. The most persuasive recent ethnography holds these perspectives together, showing brideprice as a contested field in which kinship, gender, and money are continually renegotiated.
