# Speeches in Historical Narrative

The inclusion of formal speeches delivered in direct discourse (*oratio recta*) was one of the central conventions of classical Greek and Roman historiography. Rather than serving as decorative rhetorical exercises, inserted speeches—encompassing political debates in civic assemblies, senatorial deliberations, diplomatic embassies, and battlefield harangues (*cohortationes*)—functioned as primary vehicles for political analysis, moral reflection, and the dissection of historical causation.

## The Classical Methodological Dilemma: Thucydides and Oratorical Truth

Because ancient historians lacked verbatim transcripts, stenography, or official recording apparatuses (with partial exceptions in the late Roman Republic and Principate where shorthand was occasionally recorded), historical speeches were authorial literary compositions.

Thucydides provided the foundational methodological statement on historical speech composition in Book 1.22 of the *History of the Peloponnesian War*. Recognizing the practical impossibility of remembering the exact words (*akribeia autē*) spoken either before or during the conflict, Thucydides established a dual criterion: he composed what, in his judgment, was demanded by each historical situation (*ta deonta malist' eipein*), while adhering as closely as possible to the general sense and intention (*xympasa gnome*) of what was actually said.

This formulation created a durable historiographical convention: ancient speeches were expected to possess rhetorical verisimilitude. A composed speech had to be appropriate to the historical speaker's character (*ethos*), suitable to the urgent political moment (*kairos*), and faithful to the substantive arguments advanced in historical reality.

## Structural and Analytical Functions of Speeches

Across classical and Hellenistic historiography, speeches performed distinct structural functions:

Debate and Deliberation: Thucydides regularly paired opposing speeches (*dissoi logoi*) to lay bare the competing strategic and moral dilemmas confronting states. In the Mytilenean debate (Book 3), the speeches of Cleon and Diodotus contrasted naked imperial retribution with calculated political self-interest; in the Melian Dialogue (Book 5), the exchange between Athenian envoys and the Melian council dramatized the uncompromising clash between imperial power politics and appeals to divine justice.

Pragmatic Instruction: Polybius mounted a vigorous defense of historical speeches in Book 12 of his *Histories*, while fiercely criticizing historians such as Timaeus and Phylarchus who invented melodramatic, implausible orations. Polybius insisted that an authentic historical speech must record the actual arguments that persuaded historical actors, thereby providing political education for future statesmen.

Characterization and Moral Evaluation: Roman historians used speeches to evaluate the moral psychology of political figures. In *Bellum Catilinae*, Sallust composed contrasting speeches for Julius Caesar and Cato the Younger during the senatorial debate over the execution of the Catilinarian conspirators, using the oratorical confrontation to crystallize two competing models of aristocratic virtue and constitutional philosophy.

Subversive Critique: Under the autocratic constraints of the Roman Principate, speeches provided historians with a medium to express dissenting political critiques. Tacitus frequently placed trenchant indictments of Roman imperial exploitation into the mouths of foreign resistance leaders, such as the Caledonian chieftain Calgacus in the *Agricola* (30–32) and Queen Boudica in the *Annales* (14.35). By using enemy orators to articulate critiques of Roman rule, Tacitus explored the coercive realities of empire.

## Historiographical Significance and Demise of the Convention

The practice of composing historical speeches remained a central element of Western historical writing from antiquity through the Renaissance, utilized by humanists such as Leonardo Bruni, Niccolò Machiavelli, and Francesco Guicciardini. It was only with the nineteenth-century development of modern source criticism and positivist archival history (championed by Leopold von Ranke and Barthold Georg Niebuhr) that the composed speech was largely abandoned in favor of literal documentary quotation.
