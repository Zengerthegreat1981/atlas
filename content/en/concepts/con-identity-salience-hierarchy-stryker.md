# Identity Theory and Salience Hierarchy in Stryker

A theoretical framework formulated by Sheldon Stryker that explains the multiplicity of an individual's social identities, their hierarchical internal organization, and which of them is actually activated in a given situation.

## The Content

In an article of 1968 and then in *Symbolic Interactionism: A Social Structural Version* (1980), Stryker assumed that the individual does not possess a single unified "self" but a set of multiple "identities," each tied to one of his multiple social roles within the structure of society (father, employee, friend, member of a sports club, politically active citizen). These multiple identities are not equal in importance or presence in the individual's consciousness. They are organized in a "salience hierarchy": an internal ordering that determines which identity is most likely to be invoked and activated and to guide behavior in varied situations, including situations not directly tied to any particular role. Stryker formulated a central explanatory principle for determining this ordering: the greater the "commitment" to a given identity, measured by the number of important social relationships built on performing that specific identity and by the depth of these relationships and their emotional importance to the individual, the greater that identity's salience in the hierarchy, and the greater the likelihood of its activation even in ambiguous situations that do not clearly call for it.

## Impact

This framework offered an explanatory tool open to quantitative measurement and empirical testing for a phenomenon that had previously been handled with purely descriptive interpretive flexibility in Blumer. This allowed symbolic interactionism to be linked with the quantitative research methodology of social psychology. This work directly paved the way for Peter Burke's later development of "identity verification theory," which added a precise cybernetic explanatory mechanism for how identity works psychologically during immediate moment-to-moment interaction, complementing the broader structural framework laid down by Stryker.

## Relation to Social Structure

Stryker's framework distinguishes itself by calling itself "Structural Symbolic Interactionism," since it explicitly links the macro social structure (the distribution of roles, opportunities and resources in society) with the individual's internal psychological processes (the salience and activation of identities). This is unlike Blumer's older symbolic interactionism, which focused on the flexibility of immediate individual action without linking it clearly enough to the relatively stable social structure that limits the choices available to the individual in forming his multiple identities in the first place.

## Applied Examples

The hierarchy can be illustrated with a practical example. The "parent role" may occupy the highest position in the salience hierarchy of identities for a given person if his family relationships are deeply committed emotionally and socially. This person is then likely to activate the "parent" identity in many ambiguous situations (such as career decisions concerning the time available for the family). By contrast, the "employee role" may occupy the highest position for another person who built most of his important social network around his career path, and he is likely to activate that identity in exactly similar situations, despite the objective external situation being identical in the two cases.
