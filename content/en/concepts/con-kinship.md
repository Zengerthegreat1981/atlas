# Kinship

Kinship is the system of relationships that connects people as relatives: parents and children, siblings, spouses, cousins, ancestors and descendants. It is one of anthropology's oldest and most central topics, because in many societies kinship is not one institution among others but the organising principle of social life itself — it determines who you can marry, where you can live, what you inherit, whom you owe loyalty to, and who owes loyalty to you. Even in modern industrial societies, where kinship has lost much of its public power, it remains the language in which the most intimate obligations are expressed. This entry treats kinship as the general system of relatedness; the family as a domestic group and the lineage as a descent group are treated in their own entries.

## Definition

Kinship is built from a small set of relationships — descent, the link between parent and child; alliance, the link created by marriage; and siblinghood — combined according to rules that vary enormously. Descent may be traced through the father's line (patrilineal), the mother's line (matrilineal), both lines (bilateral), or through a combination that changes with the situation. Marriage rules determine who is a possible spouse and who is forbidden: some societies prescribe marriage with a particular category of cousin, others forbid it, and the rules of exogamy — marrying outside one's own group — create alliances between groups that last for generations. Kinship is not only a matter of blood. Adoption, fostering, godparenthood, blood brotherhood, and milk kinship — the bond created when children are nursed by the same woman — all create relatives where biology alone would not. Anthropologists therefore distinguish between kinship as a biological fact and kinship as a social relationship: what matters is not who is genetically related to whom, but who is recognised as a relative, and what that recognition entails.

## Origins and Development

The study of kinship founded modern anthropology. Lewis Henry Morgan, in *Ancient Society* (1877), collected kinship terminologies from around the world and argued that they revealed stages in the evolution of the family, from promiscuity through group marriage to monogamy. In the twentieth century, kinship became the centre of British social anthropology. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, in *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952), treated kinship as a system of rights and duties that held society together. Claude Lévi-Strauss, in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949), argued that the fundamental fact of kinship was not descent but alliance: the exchange of women in marriage between groups created the bonds that made society possible, and the incest taboo was the rule that forced groups to exchange rather than keep their own. David Schneider's *American Kinship* (1968) turned the field in a new direction, showing that American kinship was organised around two cultural symbols — blood and love — rather than around biological facts, and arguing that anthropologists had been imposing their own folk model of kinship on other societies. In *A Critique of the Study of Kinship* (1984), Schneider argued that kinship was not a universal human reality but a cultural category invented by anthropologists, and that the study of kinship should be replaced by the study of how different peoples understand relatedness. Janet Carsten's edited volume *Cultures of Relatedness* (2000) took up this challenge, showing how relatedness is created through shared substance, shared food, shared residence, and shared experience, not only through descent and marriage.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns the relationship between kinship and biology. Is kinship grounded in the facts of reproduction, as Morgan and Radcliffe-Brown assumed, or is it a cultural construction, as Schneider argued? Most anthropologists now take a middle position: biology provides the raw material, but societies decide which biological links count, and they create kinship where biology is absent. A second debate concerns the relative importance of descent and alliance. Descent theorists, following Fortes and Radcliffe-Brown, emphasised unilineal descent groups — lineages and clans — as the building blocks of society; alliance theorists, following Lévi-Strauss, emphasised marriage exchange. A third debate concerns gender. Feminist anthropologists showed that classical kinship theory was written from a male point of view: women appeared as objects exchanged between groups, not as agents who make kinship. Later work showed how women create kinship through childbirth, feeding, and care, and how kinship systems constrain and empower them in different ways. A fourth dimension is the question of change: kinship systems are not timeless. Migration, urbanisation, new reproductive technologies, and same-sex marriage have all transformed what kinship means, and anthropologists study how people improvise new forms of relatedness — chosen families, donor siblings, co-parenting arrangements — when old ones do not fit.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Kinship remains central to contemporary life, in new forms. Assisted reproduction — in vitro fertilisation, egg and sperm donation, surrogacy — has created children with multiple genetic, gestational, and social parents, and has forced courts, clinics, and families to decide who counts as a parent. Migration has created transnational families, held together by remittances, phone calls, and video links, and the stub for this entry flags digital kinship — relationships maintained and created online — as a gap for future work. Same-sex marriage and adoption have challenged the assumption that kinship requires a mother and a father. The enduring insight is that kinship is not a given but an achievement: relatedness is made, maintained, and sometimes unmade, through the daily work of living together, caring for one another, and remembering who belongs to whom.
