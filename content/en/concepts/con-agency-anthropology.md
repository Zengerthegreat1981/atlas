# Agency

Agency is the capacity of individuals and groups to act intentionally, make choices, and thereby make a difference to the course of events, rather than merely being carried along by circumstances. In sociology the concept is almost always discussed together with structure, the patterned social arrangements, rules, and resources that constrain and enable action. The relation between the two, often called the structure-agency problem, is one of the discipline's oldest and most persistent theoretical questions. The concept is used in a similar way in anthropology, where it sits at the centre of theories of practice.

## Classical Roots

The question is already present in the classical tradition. Karl Marx's remark in *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852) that people make their own history, but not under conditions they choose, is the most quoted compact statement of it. Max Weber defined sociology as the interpretive understanding of social action, action to which actors attach subjective meaning, so that agents' motives are part of any explanation. Talcott Parsons's *The Structure of Social Action* (1937) developed a voluntaristic theory of action in which actors pursue ends within normative limits. Against this, Emile Durkheim's emphasis on social facts external to the individual stood for the strongly structural pole of the debate.

## Major Theoretical Positions

Interpretive traditions foreground agency. Symbolic interactionism, following George Herbert Mead and Herbert Blumer, treats people as interpreting and negotiating meanings in interaction, and ethnomethodology, following Harold Garfinkel's *Studies in Ethnomethodology* (1967), studies how members actively produce social order in everyday practice. Rational choice theory models agents as pursuing their goals under constraints.

Later theorists tried to overcome the opposition between structure and agency. Pierre Bourdieu's *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1977) introduced habitus, durable dispositions that are shaped by social position and yet generate improvised practice. Anthony Giddens's *The Constitution of Society* (1984) proposed the duality of structure, in which structures are both the medium and the outcome of the practices they organise. William Sewell Jr. (1992) revised this account by stressing that structures are made of resources as well as rules. Margaret Archer argued instead for analytical dualism, holding that structure and agency should be separated analytically because structure precedes the actions that later reproduce or transform it. Mustafa Emirbayer and Ann Mische's "What Is Agency?" (1998) defined agency as a temporally embedded process combining habitual repetition, imagination of future possibilities, and practical judgment in the present. Margaret Archer's *Realist Social Theory* (1995) set analytical dualism out as a morphogenetic approach, and her later work treated reflexive internal conversation as the way people mediate structural conditions. Sewell tied agency to the ability to transpose cultural schemas and to mobilise resources in new settings. Giddens used the term for the capability to have acted differently.

## Anthropology and Wider Applications

Anthropology took up agency through practice theory. Sherry Ortner's review "Theory in Anthropology since the Sixties" (1984) traced the shift from structuralism and functionalism toward questions of practice and power, and her later *Anthropology and Social Theory* (2006) examined the acting subject within unequal social worlds. Saba Mahmood's *Politics of Piety* (2005), an ethnography of women in the Egyptian mosque movement, argued that agency should not be equated with resistance to domination, since it can also take the form of cultivating norms. Alfred Gell's *Art and Agency* (1998) and Bruno Latour's actor-network theory extended the term to objects and nonhuman actors, a move many sociologists dispute. In psychology, Albert Bandura (2001) treated human agency as intentionality, forethought, self-regulation, and self-reflection.

## Empirical studies

Paul Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977) followed working-class boys who actively rejected school culture in ways that helped lead them into manual jobs, a case in which agency contributes to reproducing structure. Glen Elder's life-course research, beginning with *Children of the Great Depression* (1974), examined how families and individuals responded to economic hardship. Agency became one of the principles of that perspective.

## Critiques and Debates

Critics make several objections. Structural and Marxist critics argue that emphasis on agency can mask the force of class, race, and gender constraints; scholars working in the interpretive tradition reply that structural accounts treat people as passive. Others hold that the concept is used inconsistently, sometimes meaning intention, sometimes choice, sometimes the mere capacity to cause effects. Feminist and postcolonial writers ask whose agency is recognised and warn against assuming a liberal model of the autonomous chooser. Because agency is hard to observe directly, empirical work typically studies proxies such as choices, strategies, and narratives of efficacy, and the results depend heavily on the concept's definition. Those indicators do not measure the same thing: a reported sense of control may exist where real options are few. Stressing agency can also shift responsibility onto people whose circumstances tightly limit what they can do.
