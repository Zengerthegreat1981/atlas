# Mental Illness in Non-Western Contexts

## Definition

This concept examines how mental illness emerges, takes its course, is interpreted, and is treated in societies outside Europe and North America. It asks whether Western diagnostic categories (schizophrenia, depression, and anxiety) are valid globally, or carry cultural assumptions. It differs from the entry on ethnopsychiatry, which addresses the colonial history of the field, and deals here with epidemiology, symptoms, course, and the expression of distress.

## Depression and Somatization

Arthur Kleinman, in *Social Science & Medicine* in 1977, offered a critique of the "new cross-cultural psychiatry." In his book *Social Origins of Distress and Disease* (Yale University Press, 1986), he studied patients with "neurasthenia" in China, arguing that many physical complaints express social and psychological distress, and that treating them as an incomplete "somatization" rests on a Western dualism between body and mind. Racy, in the *British Journal of Psychiatry* in 1980, in his study of somatization among Saudi women, suggested that physical complaints in an Arab environment may be a socially acceptable language for psychological pain. This suggests that the patient should be asked about the meaning of their complaint (see the entry on Kleinman's explanatory models school).

## Schizophrenia and Its Course

The World Health Organization's International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia in the 1970s was followed by the study of determinants of outcome in severe mental disorders (DOSMeD). Jablensky and colleagues published its results in *Psychological Medicine* in 1992 (special supplement). The study found similarity in core symptoms across cultures and a better course in developing countries, which has often been attributed to family and social support, though this explanation is debated. The finding was later criticized for differences in samples. Hopper and colleagues (eds.), in *Recovery from Schizophrenia: An International Perspective* (Oxford University Press, 2007), reviewed this debate. Luhrmann and colleagues, in the *British Journal of Psychiatry* in 2015, found that patients in India and Ghana tend to describe friendly voices and relationships with them more than patients in the United States do, and that culture shapes the experience of hallucination.

## Local Concepts of Distress

Many societies explain psychological disturbance through spirits, sorcery, the evil eye, envy, and possession, and may distinguish between "madness" and "being possessed" when seeking treatment. In Arab societies, healing often happens first with a folk healer or sheikh, and then at a clinic (see the entry on cultural healing traditions). Ahmed Okasha, in *Clinical Psychology Review* in 1999, gave an overview of mental health in the Middle East from an Egyptian perspective, noting the role of family and religion, traditional healing, and the shortage of specialists.

## Trauma and Critique

Derek Summerfield, in the *BMJ* in 2001, wrote a critique of exporting the diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder to war zones, warning against converting collective suffering into an individual illness. Ethan Watters, in *Crazy Like Us* (Free Press, 2010), argued, drawing on clinicians such as Sing Lee, that exporting Western categories can change how distress is expressed, using the changing presentation of anorexia nervosa in Hong Kong as an example. The book is journalistic synthesis, not a primary epidemiological study. On the other hand, denying the existence of severe psychotic or depressive disorders in non-Western societies on the grounds of "cultural difference" deprives patients of care.

## Limits

The distinction between "global health" and "local culture" is not a strict dichotomy. Core symptoms may be shared, while their labels, meanings, and responses vary. Field reviews in a number of Arab countries remain few in number and heterogeneous.
