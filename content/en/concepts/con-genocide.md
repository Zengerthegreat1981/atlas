# Genocide

Genocide is the deliberate destruction of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group, in whole or in part, through killing, inflicting serious harm, imposing conditions of life calculated to bring about the group's destruction, preventing births, or forcibly transferring children. The term names both a crime under international law and a social process studied by anthropologists, sociologists, and historians, who ask how ordinary people, institutions, and states come to participate in mass murder — and how societies recover, or fail to recover, afterwards.

## Origins and Development

The word "genocide" was coined by the Polish-Jewish lawyer Raphael Lemkin, who combined the Greek *genos* (race or tribe) with the Latin *cide* (killing). Lemkin introduced the term in *Axis Rule in Occupied Europe* (1944), his study of Nazi policies of extermination, arguing that the destruction of a people was a distinct crime that existing law did not capture. His campaign led to the United Nations Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (1948), which defined genocide in legal terms and obliged signatory states to prevent and punish it. The Convention's definition — centred on the intent to destroy a group as such — remains the legal standard, though scholars have long debated its limits, notably its exclusion of political groups and of cultural destruction.

The Holocaust is the paradigmatic case against which the concept was formed, and it has shaped the study of genocide ever since. Hannah Arendt, reporting on the trial of Adolf Eichmann in *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963), argued that the Holocaust was carried out not by monsters but by ordinary bureaucrats — a claim she captured in the phrase "the banality of evil." Arendt's argument provoked fierce debate, but it set a lasting question for the social sciences: how do modern states organise mass killing through the division of labour, euphemistic language, and the displacement of responsibility?

## The Sociology and Anthropology of Genocide

Sociologists and anthropologists have moved beyond the study of individual perpetrators to examine genocide as a social process. Helen Fein, in *Accounting for Genocide* (1979), examined why Jewish victimisation varied between European states under Nazi domination, and argued that genocide occurs when a state defines a group as outside the "universe of obligation" — the circle of people whose lives are considered to matter — and when war or crisis provides the opportunity to act on that exclusion. Fein's analysis emphasised that genocide is not a sudden eruption of ancient hatreds but a political project, planned and carried out by states.

Frank Chalk and Kurt Jonassohn, in *The History and Sociology of Genocide* (1990), broadened the field historically, documenting genocides from antiquity to the present and showing that mass killing has taken many forms: settler colonialism, religious persecution, ideological revolution, and the destruction of indigenous peoples. Their work challenged the view of genocide as a uniquely modern or uniquely European phenomenon. Alexander Hinton's edited volume *Annihilating Difference: The Anthropology of Genocide* (2002) brought anthropological perspectives to the fore, examining how cultural categories, rituals of dehumanisation, and local histories of violence shape the ways genocide is imagined, organised, and experienced by victims and perpetrators alike.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns the role of ideology versus circumstance. Some scholars argue that genocide requires a radical ideology that dehumanises the victim group; others argue that ideology is often a justification adopted after the killing has begun, and that local conflicts over land, resources, and power are more important drivers. Another debate concerns the boundaries of the concept: whether the term should be extended to cover cultural destruction, environmental destruction, or mass killing that lacks the specific intent required by the Convention. Scholars who favour a broad definition argue that a narrow legal definition excludes many victims; those who favour a narrow one argue that stretching the concept weakens its moral and analytical force.

A further debate concerns prevention and responsibility. The doctrine of the "responsibility to protect," adopted by the United Nations in 2005, holds that the international community has a responsibility to act, through collective measures, when a state manifestly fails to protect its own population from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity. But intervention raises its own problems: who decides, by what authority, and with what consequences. Anthropologists have also documented how the language of genocide is itself used politically — invoked to mobilise intervention, or denied to avoid obligation — making the naming of genocide a matter of power as well as of fact.

## Contemporary Relevance

Genocide remains a central concern of anthropology and the social sciences. The genocide in Rwanda (1994), the killings at Srebrenica in Bosnia (1995), which international courts have judged to be genocide, and contested debates over whether the term applies to other cases, such as the treatment of the Rohingya in Myanmar, have kept the concept at the centre of public argument. Anthropologists study the aftermath of genocide: how survivors rebuild lives and communities, how memory is preserved or suppressed, how justice is pursued through international tribunals and local courts, and how the children and grandchildren of victims and perpetrators inherit the past. The study of genocide is thus not only a study of destruction but of survival, testimony, and the fragile work of living together after the worst has happened.
