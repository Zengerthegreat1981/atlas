# Intersectionality in History

An analytical method in historical writing that examines how multiple systems of oppression and privilege (gender, race, class, sexuality, legal status and colonial status) intersect to shape the experience of historical individuals and groups, rejecting the treatment of each of these axes in isolation from the others.

## Origins of the Concept

The American lawyer and scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw coined the term "intersectionality" in 1989, in her seminal article "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex." She diagnosed the inadequacy of legal and theoretical frameworks that treated discrimination against Black women either as separate "racial discrimination" or "gender discrimination," ignoring that their actual experience arises from the intersection of the two together, in a way that cannot be reduced to a simple sum of each axis taken separately. In developing this argument Crenshaw drew on an earlier intellectual and activist heritage extending to the 1851 speech of the activist Sojourner Truth, and on the works of Angela Davis and Audre Lorde, which criticized the exclusion of Black women from both the mainstream feminist movement and the anti-racist movement.

## Applying the Concept to Historical Writing

Historians have carried this analytical framework, which originally arose in a contemporary legal and theoretical context, over into the study of earlier societies and historical periods. They use it to examine how the experiences of specific groups were shaped at the intersection of several oppressive axes at once (for example enslaved women in the Americas, immigrant women workers in industrial Europe, or colonial women under colonial rule). This replaces studying "women's history," "the history of slavery" or "the history of colonialism" as separate frameworks, each of which implicitly assumes a unified subject (women "in general," or the "slave" in general) that hides internal inequalities among the different intersecting groups.

## Crenshaw's Three Dimensions

In developing the concept, Crenshaw distinguished three dimensions that historians can apply: the "structural" dimension, which examines how legal, economic and social institutions were themselves designed in ways that produce intersecting inequalities (for example labor laws that historically excluded sectors dominated by Black or female workers); the "political" dimension, which studies how social movements sometimes fail to represent those who stand at the intersection of more than one cause; and the "representational" dimension, which examines how cultural, media and historiographical images themselves produce reductive representations that ignore this complexity.

## Value and Criticism

This method has allowed historians to explain fine-grained inequalities that are hard to account for with single-axis analytical frameworks (such as the wage gap between white and Black women in the same industrial context). It also faces methodological criticism concerning the difficulty of determining which analytical axes should be included in the "intersection" and which should be excluded, and the risk that historical analysis fragments into categories so fine that it becomes hard to draw broader explanatory patterns that apply to larger groups.
