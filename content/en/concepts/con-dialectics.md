# Dialectic (Dialektik)

Dialectic (Greek *dialektikē*, from *dialegesthai*, "to converse") is a method of reasoning and of analysing reality through the confrontation of opposed positions. Its meaning changes radically from one thinker to another: for Socrates and Plato it is a method of reaching truth through question and answer; for Aristotle a technique of reasoning from reputable opinions rather than from certain premises; for Kant the logic of illusion; and for Hegel and Marx a movement of thought and of history through contradiction.

## Historical Stages

### 1. Zeno, the Sophists and Socrates (5th century BCE)

Aristotle, according to Diogenes Laertius, called Zeno of Elea the inventor of dialectic, because of his arguments that expose contradictions in his opponents' assumptions. The Sophists, such as Protagoras and Gorgias, are associated with the art of argument and persuasion, and Plato often contrasts their contentious debating (eristic) with true dialectic. Socrates practised a method of cross-examination (*elenchus*): asking "what is X?", as in the *Euthyphro* on piety, in order to expose contradictions in the interlocutor's beliefs. In the *Theaetetus* he compares his questioning to midwifery (maieutics).

### 2. Plato (4th century BCE)

In the *Republic* (Books VI-VII) dialectic is the highest form of knowledge, an ascent from hypotheses to the "unhypothetical first principle" (*anhypotheton*). The Divided Line distinguishes imagination (*eikasia*), belief (*pistis*), thought (*dianoia*) and understanding (*noesis*), and dialectic belongs to the last. In the *Parmenides* Plato subjects his own theory of Forms to a demanding dialectical exercise, and in the *Sophist* dialectic is described as the science of dividing and combining kinds.

### 3. Aristotle (4th century BCE)

In the *Topics* he defines dialectic as reasoning from generally accepted opinions (*endoxa*), in contrast with demonstration (*apodeixis*) from true and primary premises, as in the *Posterior Analytics*. Dialectic is useful for debate, for training, and for examining first principles, but it does not itself demonstrate them.

### 4. Islamic Philosophy

In the Arabic tradition, Aristotle's *Topics* was translated as *Jadal* (dialectic) and belonged to the logical corpus (*Organon*). Al-Farabi and Ibn Sina (Avicenna) distinguished demonstrative, dialectical, rhetorical, poetical and sophistical arguments by the kind of premises they use; dialectical arguments rely on widely accepted opinions, whereas demonstrative arguments rely on certain premises.

### 5. Kant (1781)

In the *Critique of Pure Reason* the "Transcendental Dialectic" is the logic of illusion: the analysis of the illusions into which reason falls when it tries to extend its concepts beyond possible experience. It treats three topics: the paralogisms of rational psychology (the soul), the antinomies of cosmology (the world), and the ideal of pure reason in rational theology (God).

### 6. Fichte (1794)

In the *Wissenschaftslehre* (1794) Fichte derives the structure of consciousness from the I positing itself, positing a not-I in opposition, and reconciling the two. The triad of thesis, antithesis and synthesis is closely associated with Fichte's procedure, though Fichte did not use those exact terms as a formula.

### 7. Hegel (1807-1831)

For Hegel dialectic is the self-movement of concepts, in which each determination is found to contain its own opposite and passes over into a richer determination. The key term is sublation (*Aufhebung*), which in his own account means at once cancelling, preserving and lifting up. Hegel turned dialectic from a method of conversation into the logic of "Spirit" (*Geist*) and of history.

A note on terminology: the formula "thesis, antithesis, synthesis" is not Hegel's own and is rarely used by him. It was popularised in the nineteenth century, notably by Heinrich Moritz Chalybäus (1837). Hegel's own vocabulary includes "determinate negation" (central to the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, 1807) and the "negation of the negation", which appears in the *Science of Logic* (1812-16).

### 8. Marx and Engels

Marx adopted the Hegelian method and, in the afterword to the second German edition of *Capital* (1873), held that in Hegel it stood on its head and had to be turned the right way up: dialectic became a movement of material social relations and of class conflict rather than of ideas, applied in *Capital* (1867) to the analysis of the commodity and capital. Engels extended the idea to nature in *Dialectics of Nature* (written 1873-83, published 1925).

### 9. Twentieth-Century Developments

- **Lukács**: *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) made totality and the dialectical relation of subject and object central to Marxist method.
- **The Frankfurt School**: Adorno's *Negative Dialectics* (1966) rejects the claim that dialectic ends in a reconciling synthesis.

## Major Themes

- **Negation**: "every determination is a negation" (*omnis determinatio est negatio*), a formula associated with Spinoza's letter to Jarig Jelles and taken up by Hegel.
- **Totality**: the whole is conceived as more than the sum of its parts.
- **Historicity**: reality is understood as in motion rather than fixed.
- **Contradiction**: opposed elements within a thing or a society drive change.

## Dialectic in Psychotherapy: DBT

Marsha Linehan named Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), developed in the late 1980s and set out in her 1993 manual, partly on the basis of a dialectical worldview. In her usage dialectic means holding two apparently opposed poles together, above all unconditional acceptance and the push toward change, without resolving one at the expense of the other. She also draws on the notions of wholeness, polarity and continual change. This is a looser usage than the Platonic, Hegelian or Marxist senses of the term.

## Critique of Dialectic

- **Schopenhauer** was a harsh critic of Hegel, whom he regarded as an obscurantist; he also wrote a short posthumous work on "eristic dialectic" (the art of winning arguments), using the word in a different sense.
- **Kierkegaard** objected that the Hegelian system dissolves the existing individual into the movement of the whole.
- **Karl Popper**, in "What Is Dialectic?" (1940), argued that dialectical talk of contradiction is logically loose and can license any conclusion.
- **Poststructuralists** such as Derrida and Deleuze (for example in *Nietzsche and Philosophy*, 1962) criticised Hegelian dialectic for subordinating difference to identity.
- **Postcolonial critics** have charged Hegel's philosophy of history, and some Marxist stage theories, with Eurocentrism.

## Later Reception

Jürgen Habermas retains themes from Hegelian-Marxist critical theory but, in *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981), replaces the dialectic of history with an account of communicative rationality grounded in dialogue.
