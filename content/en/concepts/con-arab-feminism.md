# Contemporary Arab Feminism

A multi-voiced intellectual and activist current, extending from the 1920s to the present, which struggles for gender equality in Arab societies through varied approaches. These range from explicit secular critique of patriarchal and religious structures to a reformist reading of religious texts from within ("Islamic feminism").

## The first wave: early twentieth century

Organizational beginnings go back to **Huda Sha'arawi** in Egypt, who founded the **Egyptian Feminist Union** in 1923 and publicly removed her face veil at the Cairo railway station on her return from an international women's conference in Rome, a public symbol of demands for legal and educational equality. In Lebanon, **Nazira Zein al-Din** published *al-Sufur wa-l-hijab* (Unveiling and Veiling, 1928), which discussed the veil from a religious reformist perspective. This first wave articulated demands mainly concerning education, the right to work and the reform of personal status laws, within the context of national independence movements.

## The second wave: radical and structural critique

From the 1970s, **Nawal El Saadawi** (Egypt) emerged as the most prominent voice of radical Arab feminism. In her book *Woman and Sex* (1972) she addressed unspoken issues such as female genital mutilation and sexual repression. After her imprisonment in 1981 under President Sadat she founded the **Arab Women's Solidarity Association** (1982), an independent feminist organization that the Egyptian authorities ordered dissolved in 1991.

In parallel, **Fatima Mernissi** (Morocco), a sociologist, offered in *Beyond the Veil* (1975) *The Veil and the Male Elite* (1987) and *The Forgotten Queens of Islam* (1990; Arabic *al-Sultanat al-mansiyyat*) a sociological and historical-religious reading that questions the assumption that the subordination of women is necessarily rooted in the religious texts themselves. Her work is often cited as an influence on what later became known as **Islamic feminism**, the demand for equality through reinterpreting the texts from within the Islamic tradition, although she did not adopt the label herself.

## Contemporary diversity

Arab feminism today is not one homogeneous current. It is divided among secular feminists, reformist Islamic feminists and digital youth movements (such as campaigns against harassment and online bullying), and it intersects with questions of class, migration and political conflict in the region. Contemporary voices such as **Mona Eltahawy** have provoked wide debate by linking feminism to explicit critique of religious and political patriarchal authority at once, while other voices continue to call for gradual reform from within existing religious and legal frameworks.

## Impact

This movement contributed to uneven legal reforms across Arab states (notably the Tunisian Code of Personal Status of 1956 and the Moroccan family code of 2004), and broadened the public debate on domestic violence, harassment and the right to work. Considerable disparity remains between Arab states, however, in the actual legal reform achieved.
