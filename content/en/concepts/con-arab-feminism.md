# Contemporary Arab Feminism

Contemporary Arab feminism comprises a diverse intellectual, social, and political current that has developed since the early twentieth century. It advocates for women's rights and gender equality across Arab societies through various methodologies. These approaches range from explicit secular critiques of patriarchal structures and state legal codes to reformist re-readings of scriptural sources from within the Islamic tradition, often described as Islamic feminism.

## The First Wave: Early Twentieth Century

Institutional feminist activism emerged in Egypt around **Huda Sha'arawi**, who co-founded the **Egyptian Feminist Union** (*al-Ittihad al-Nisa'i al-Misri*) in 1923. On returning from the International Woman Suffrage Alliance congress in Rome that same year, Sha'arawi and Saiza Nabarawi removed their face veils at Cairo railway station, a gesture that became a public marker of demands for female education, political rights, and reform of personal-status law. In Lebanon, **Nazira Zein al-Din** published *al-Sufur wa-l-hijab* (Unveiling and Veiling, 1928), then *al-Fatat wa-l-shuyukh* (The Young Woman and the Shaykhs, 1929), a scriptural and legal argument against compulsory face-veiling and seclusion. This generation tied education, the vote, and marriage and divorce law to the wider claim of national self-determination.

## The Second Wave: Radical and Structural Critiques

During the 1970s, Egyptian physician and writer **Nawal El Saadawi** (1931–2021) became a prominent voice of radical Arab feminism. In *al-Mar'a wa-l-jins* (Woman and Sex, 1972) and *al-Wajh al-'ari li-l-mar'a al-'arabiyya* (1977; English *The Hidden Face of Eve*, 1980), she wrote about female genital cutting, domestic violence, and the sexual double standard. She treated those practices as bound up with patriarchy, class inequality, and authoritarian rule, and she argued that honor belongs to a person's intellect and conduct rather than to the body or to virginity. After her imprisonment in the mass arrests of September 1981, under Anwar al-Sadat, she founded the **Arab Women's Solidarity Association** in 1982. Egyptian authorities dissolved the association in 1991.

Moroccan sociologist **Fatima Mernissi** (1940–2015) combined fieldwork with historical and textual criticism. *Beyond the Veil* (1975) studied male-female relations in a modern Muslim society. *Le harem politique* (1987; English *The Veil and the Male Elite*, 1991) examined hadith reports used to restrict women, and *Sultanes oubliées* (1990; English *The Forgotten Queens of Islam*, 1993) recovered women who had held political authority in Islamic history. In the preface to the English edition of *The Veil and the Male Elite* she wrote: "If women's rights are a problem for some modern Muslim men, it is neither because of the Koran nor the Prophet, nor the Islamic tradition, but simply because those rights conflict with the interests of a male elite." Later Islamic feminist writing often returned to this distinction between the founding texts and the interests of their male interpreters.

## Plurality and Legal Impact

Contemporary Arab feminism is not one doctrine. It includes secular critics of family law, authors who reread the Qur'an and fiqh for more egalitarian rulings, among them the Egyptian scholar Omaima Abou-Bakr and the Moroccan writer Asma Lamrabet, and campaigns against harassment and domestic abuse. Feminist argument has also left a mark on statute law, notably Tunisia's Code of Personal Status (*Majallat al-ahwal al-shakhsiyya*, 1956) and Morocco's reformed Family Code (*al-Mudawwana*, 2004). Legal and social inequalities remain wide from one Arab jurisdiction to another, and citizenship, personal autonomy, and family law are still central political disputes.
