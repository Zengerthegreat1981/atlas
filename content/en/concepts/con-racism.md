# Racism: Ideology, Structure, and Practice

**Racism** is the system of ideas and practices that treats people as members of distinct "races," ranks those races in a hierarchy, and distributes power, wealth, and respect along racial lines. It operates at several levels at once: as prejudice in individual minds, as discrimination in everyday interaction, and as structure embedded in institutions — schools, labour markets, housing, policing, and law. Sociology insists on this multi-level view because racism persists even where few people hold openly racist beliefs: a society can produce racially unequal outcomes through rules and routines that no one designed with racist intent.

## Definition and Development

The modern concept of race was created in the age of European colonialism and slavery. From the sixteenth century onward, and especially in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, European writers classified humanity into races, placed Europeans at the top, and used the classification to justify conquest, enslavement, and dispossession. Science in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries gave racism a biological vocabulary — skull measurements and, later, appeals to blood groups and heredity — but the categories were social from the start: who counted as "white" or "Black" changed across time and place, and groups such as the Irish, Italians, and Jews were racialised differently in different periods.

W. E. B. Du Bois, in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), gave an early and influential sociological account of race as lived experience. Du Bois described "double consciousness": Black Americans saw themselves both through their own eyes and through the eyes of a white world that despised them, measuring their souls "by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity." His famous prediction — that "the problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color-line" — framed race not as a biological fact but as the central political division of modernity. Du Bois also insisted that race was a social and historical product, not a natural kind, an insight that sociology took decades to absorb fully.

## Key Contributions: Racial Formation and Structural Racism

Michael Omi and Howard Winant, in *Racial Formation in the United States* (1986), developed the concept of **racial formation**: race is a socio-historical process in which racial categories are created, inhabited, transformed, and destroyed. Their concept of **racial projects** captures how ideas about race are linked to the distribution of resources: a project such as slavery, segregation, or affirmative action simultaneously gives meaning to race and organises social structure around it. Omi and Winant showed that race cannot be reduced to class or to prejudice; it is an autonomous organising principle of social life.

Eduardo Bonilla-Silva, in "Rethinking Racism: Toward a Structural Interpretation" (1997), argued that sociology had been hampered by treating racism as a matter of individual prejudice. He proposed instead the concept of the **racialised social system**: a society in which economic, political, social, and ideological levels are partially structured by the placement of actors in racial categories. In such a system, racism is the ideology that rationalises racial domination, and racial inequality persists because it is built into institutions, not because individuals are bigoted. His later work on "colour-blind racism" showed how, in the post-civil-rights era, inequality is defended with the language of individualism and meritocracy rather than with open claims of racial superiority.

## Examples

Racism's forms are historically specific. In the United States, slavery, Jim Crow segregation, and redlining — the systematic denial of mortgages to Black neighbourhoods — created racial wealth gaps that persist generations after the policies were formally abolished. In South Africa, apartheid (1948–1994) made racial hierarchy the explicit basis of law. In Europe, antisemitism culminated in the Holocaust, while contemporary racism targets Muslim, Roma, and migrant communities. In the Arab world, racism has taken forms including discrimination against Black Arabs and against migrant workers, and the legacy of the trans-Saharan and Indian Ocean slave trades remains under-studied in both scholarship and public memory.

Institutional racism can operate without any individual act of hatred. A hiring process that rewards "cultural fit," a school system that tracks children by language, or a policing practice that concentrates stops in poor neighbourhoods can each reproduce racial inequality. This is why sociologists distinguish **prejudice** (an attitude), **discrimination** (an act), and **racism** (a system): the first two can exist without the third, but the third is what explains the persistence of inequality.

## Critique and Contemporary Questions

Debates continue about how racism relates to other systems of domination. Some Marxist sociologists argue that racism is ultimately a product of capitalism, used to divide workers; others, following Omi and Winant and Bonilla-Silva, insist that race has its own logic and cannot be reduced to class. Intersectional scholarship, building on Black feminist thought, shows that racism is experienced differently by women and men, and that race, gender, and class interact rather than simply adding up.

Contemporary research examines racism in new domains: algorithmic systems that reproduce racial bias in hiring, credit, and policing; the racial politics of migration and border control; and the global circulation of racist ideas through social media. The concept of racism remains contested — some argue it has been diluted by overuse, others that its expansion is necessary to capture how inequality actually works. What sociology has established is that race is real in its consequences even though it has no biological foundation, and that racism must be studied as a system, not only as a state of mind.

## Comparative Histories beyond the Atlantic World

Historians have traced racial or race-like hierarchies in many settings, while debating whether the term fits each. Frank Dikötter's *The Discourse of Race in Modern China* (1992) examined how older distinctions between civilised and barbarian were joined to imported biological ideas of race in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. In Japan, the Burakumin, descendants of outcast status groups, faced exclusion justified at times as inherited difference although they are not distinguishable by appearance; George De Vos and Hiroshi Wagatsuma compared this to caste and race. Bruce Hall's *A History of Race in Muslim West Africa* (2011) showed that ideas of race linked to genealogy and slavery shaped relations around the Niger Bend before and during colonial rule. In Rwanda, Mahmood Mamdani argued, colonial government turned the Hutu and Tutsi distinction into a racialised one by casting Tutsi as foreign settlers, with consequences that bear on the 1994 genocide. Such comparisons suggest that European colonial racism was highly influential but interacted with local hierarchies, and that comparison must specify what is inherited, ranked and enforced in each case.
