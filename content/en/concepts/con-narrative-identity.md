# Narrative Identity: The Self as a Story I Tell About Myself

Narrative identity is the idea that personal identity is constituted, at least in part, by the stories through which a person makes sense of their life, ordering events into a meaningful whole with characters, a plot and a temporal shape. The notion is associated above all with Paul Ricœur, who developed it in *Temps et récit* (*Time and Narrative*, 3 vols., 1983-85) and *Soi-même comme un autre* (*Oneself as Another*, 1990). Closely related views were put forward by Alasdair MacIntyre in *After Virtue* (1981), who argued that human life has a narrative unity, and by Daniel Dennett in "The Self as a Center of Narrative Gravity" (in Kessel, Cole and Johnson, eds., *Self and Consciousness*, 1992). In personality psychology, Dan McAdams (*The Stories We Live By*, 1993) uses "narrative identity" for the internalized, evolving life story a person constructs.

## The Philosophical Meaning

The central claim is that the self is not a fixed inner substance or a bundle of traits but is articulated in narrative. Ricœur distinguishes **sameness** (*idem*: identity as persistence of character and traits) from **selfhood** (*ipse*: the identity of someone who acts, speaks and is accountable). **Narrative identity** mediates between the two, because a story can hold together permanence and change: the character of a protagonist can change while the protagonist remains recognizably the same person. For MacIntyre, to be intelligible as an agent is to be a character in a narrative that links birth, life and death, and moral reasoning takes place within such narratives. Dennett's proposal is different in kind: the self is a "center of narrative gravity," an abstraction (like a physical center of gravity) generated by the stories a human organism tells and that others tell about it; it is a useful theoretical fiction and not a hidden entity. Thus Ricœur's account is hermeneutic and ethical, while Dennett's is a deflationary account of the self in the philosophy of mind.

## The Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

Narrative therapy, developed by Michael White and David Epston (*Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends*, 1990), holds that people's problems are often sustained by "problem-saturated" dominant stories about themselves; therapy invites clients to externalize the problem and to "re-author" their lives by identifying neglected, alternative events and meanings. Narrative Exposure Therapy (NET; Schauer, Neuner and Elbert, 2005) is a short-term, structured treatment for trauma in which the person constructs a chronological narrative of their life, including traumatic events, with the aim of integrating fragmented memories; it has been studied in trials with survivors of war and organized violence, but it is a distinct approach and not simply an application of Ricœur. This description is of approaches and not a recommendation for any individual's treatment.

## The Difference from Neighbouring Concepts

Narrative identity differs from **personal identity** in the metaphysical sense (Locke, Parfit), which asks what makes a person the same over time, whereas narrative identity asks how a person gives their life coherence and meaning. It also differs from **narrative theory** in literary studies (Genette, Barthes), which studies the structure of narrative texts, whereas narrative identity treats narrative as a form of self-understanding. Critics (for example Galen Strawson, "Against Narrativity," 2004) argue that not all people experience their lives as a story and that narrativity is neither necessary for nor always conducive to a good life.

## Connections and Context

Ricœur's narrative identity belongs to philosophical hermeneutics: interpretation of one's life, like interpretation of a text, proceeds through the hermeneutic circle between part and whole. A separate entry deals with Ricœur's formulation of the *idem/ipse* distinction; this entry emphasizes the psychological and therapeutic extension. Narrative therapy is a clinical tradition that draws on narrative ideas (notably from Michel Foucault and the anthropologist Barbara Myerhoff) rather than directly from Ricœur.
