# Statistical Significance

Statistical significance is a judgement that an observed pattern in data — a difference between groups, a correlation, an effect of an intervention — is unlikely to have arisen by chance alone under a specified null hypothesis. In practice it is expressed through a p-value: the probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed if the null hypothesis were true. When that probability falls below a conventional threshold, most often 0.05, the result is called statistically significant. The concept is central to quantitative sociology, where researchers generalise from samples to populations and must decide whether an apparent association, such as one between education and income, reflects a real social pattern or sampling noise.

## Origins and Development

The modern apparatus of significance testing emerged in the 1920s and 1930s from two partly incompatible traditions. Ronald Fisher, in *Statistical Methods for Research Workers* (1925), introduced the p-value as an informal measure of evidence against a null hypothesis and popularised the 0.05 level as a convenient convention, not a law of nature. Fisher treated the test as a flexible tool for the working scientist: a small p-value indicated that the data were surprising under the null, and warranted further investigation.

Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, in their 1933 paper on the most efficient tests of hypotheses, proposed a different framework. They replaced Fisher's single-hypothesis test with a decision procedure involving two hypotheses, two kinds of error — rejecting a true null hypothesis (Type I) and failing to reject a false one (Type II) — and the concept of statistical power, the probability of detecting a real effect. Their approach fixed the significance level in advance and treated the test as a rule for behaviour over repeated sampling rather than a measure of evidence in a single study. Most textbook presentations of "hypothesis testing" silently blend Fisher's evidential p-value with Neyman and Pearson's decision-theoretic framework, a hybrid that many statisticians and historians of statistics regard as an inconsistent mixture of two frameworks, yet which remains the standard practice in the social sciences.

Through the mid-twentieth century, significance testing became the gatekeeper of publishable quantitative research. Journals expected p-values below 0.05, and a generation of sociologists learned to treat that threshold as the boundary between a finding and a non-finding.

## Key Debates

The first major debate concerns what a p-value does not say. A statistically significant result does not show that an effect is large, important, or practically meaningful. Jacob Cohen argued forcefully in 1994 that the ritual of null-hypothesis significance testing confuses statistical significance with substantive significance: with a large enough sample, even a trivial difference becomes "significant", while a large and important effect in a small sample may fail to reach the threshold. Cohen's remedy was to report effect sizes and confidence intervals alongside, or instead of, p-values — a practice that has slowly become standard in sociology.

A second debate concerns the threshold itself. In 2018, a group of statisticians led by Daniel Benjamin proposed redefining statistical significance, lowering the conventional threshold from 0.05 to 0.005 for claims of new discoveries, on the grounds that the existing standard produces too many false positives. Others countered that moving the line merely relocates the problem, and that the real issue is the dichotomous thinking the threshold encourages — treating results as either "real" or "nothing" — rather than the precise value of the cut-off.

A third debate, sharpened by the replication crisis of the 2010s, concerns research practices that significance testing incentivises. John Ioannidis argued in 2005 that most published research findings are false, because small samples, small effects, flexible analysis choices and publication bias combine to flood the literature with significant results that do not replicate. Practices such as p-hacking — trying many analyses until one yields p < 0.05 — and HARKing — presenting a post-hoc hypothesis as if it had been predicted — are direct consequences of treating significance as the criterion of success. Preregistration of hypotheses and analysis plans, now increasingly required by journals, is one institutional response.

A fourth debate pits frequentist significance testing against Bayesian alternatives. Bayesians argue that researchers actually want the probability that a hypothesis is true given the data, which a p-value does not provide, and advocate Bayes factors and posterior probabilities. Critics reply that Bayesian methods depend on prior probabilities that are themselves contested, and that the practical gains are smaller than promised.

## Contemporary Relevance

In 2016 the American Statistical Association took the unusual step of issuing a formal statement on a specific matter of statistical practice, warning that p-values do not measure the probability that a hypothesis is true, do not measure the size or importance of an effect, and that scientific conclusions should not rest on a p-value threshold alone. The statement reflected wide concern among statisticians that p-values had been misused.

For sociology, the stakes are substantive. Quantitative claims about inequality, discrimination, mobility and the effects of policy all pass through significance tests on their way to publication and to public debate. A finding that a hiring audit shows racial discrimination, or that a welfare reform changed employment rates, gains authority when it is "statistically significant" — and that authority can mislead when the effect is tiny, the sample unrepresentative, or the analysis one of many tried. Contemporary best practice therefore treats statistical significance as one piece of evidence among others: effect sizes, confidence intervals, replication, and theoretical plausibility all bear on whether a pattern matters. The concept remains indispensable as a guard against being fooled by randomness, but its history shows that the guard itself needs watching.
