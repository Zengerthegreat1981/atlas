# Self-Actualization

A central concept in Maslow's psychology, describing the highest level of the hierarchy of human needs, at which the individual reaches his full potential and expresses his essential capacities in fields consistent with his nature.

## Definition

Maslow developed the concept in his 1943 paper "A Theory of Human Motivation" and in *Motivation and Personality* (1954), and elaborated it in *Toward a Psychology of Being* (1962). The term itself came from Kurt Goldstein. Maslow described self-actualization as:

- **Use of potentials** (Use of Potentials): turning "what I can be" into "what I actually am."
- **Fulfilling the individual mission** (Mission / Calling): every person has a unique "mission," which requires unique expression.
- **Inner harmony** (Inner Harmony): a decrease in conflict between levels of the personality.
- **Spontaneity and simplicity** (Spontaneity): the ability to express oneself naturally without defense.

## Characteristics of the self-actualized person

Through the study of "exemplary models" (Biographical Studies) of a small group of historical and contemporary figures (he named, among others, Abraham Lincoln, Thomas Jefferson, Jane Addams, William James, Albert Einstein, Eleanor Roosevelt, Albert Schweitzer and Spinoza), Maslow identified characteristics such as the following. The method was informal and the sample small and selected by Maslow's own judgement:
- A realistic perception of reality and acceptance of it.
- Acceptance of oneself and others as they are.
- Spontaneity, simplicity and naturalness.
- Focus on external problems (Problem-centered).
- Autonomy and privacy (Autonomy).
- Peak experiences (moments of intense wonder or unity).
- Deep human relationships, limited in number.
- A continuing freshness of appreciation of ordinary things.
- A preference for contributing to a cause deeper than the self.

## Its relation to other concepts

Self-actualization in Maslow intersects with the "actualizing tendency" (Actualizing Tendency) formulated by Carl Rogers in his book *On Becoming a Person* (1961): Rogers assumes that this tendency is an innate force continuously present in every living organism, whereas Maslow makes self-actualization the summit of a hierarchy that is reached only after the lower needs are satisfied. The specialized branch "Psychology of Self-Actualization and Human Motivation" gathers Maslow's later works on this concept specifically, including *Farther Reaches of Human Nature* (1971).

## Versus the lower motives

Maslow distinguished between:
- **Deficiency needs** (Deficiency Needs, D-Needs): biological and social (food, safety, belonging, esteem).
- **Growth needs** (Being Needs, B-Needs): cognitive, aesthetic and spiritual (self-actualization, knowledge, beauty, justice).

## Criticism

- **Lack of gender and cultural diversity**: the chosen models are mostly Western and male. The hierarchy has also been criticised for its weak empirical support and its individualist emphasis.
- **Difficulty of measurement**: instruments such as Shostrom's Personal Orientation Inventory (1964) exist, but their validity is debated, and the construct is hard to operationalise.
- **Fluctuating interpretation**: is it a final goal (a state) or a continuous process of growth? Maslow's own writing leans toward process in places and state in others, and the question remains open.
