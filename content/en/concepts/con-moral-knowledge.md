# Moral Knowledge

Moral knowledge is a branch of epistemology (the theory of knowledge) that investigates whether human beings can know which actions are right and which are wrong, and examines the source of this knowledge and the means of justifying it. Are moral values grasped by pure reason, as mathematical truths are; by innate intuition; by experience and induction; or can they not be known objectively at all, because they are not the kind of thing that can be known?

This question occupied Muslim theologians under the heading of *al-husn wa-l-qubh* (good and evil). The Mu'tazila held that reason alone, without depending on revelation, is capable of knowing with certainty the goodness and badness of actions, just as it knows the validity of logical propositions. They cited reason's immediate perception of the wrongness of injustice and the goodness of truthfulness even before revelation was sent down. The Ash'ari rejected this epistemic independence, holding that revealed scripture is the only source of moral knowledge, since reason alone has no way to perceive what pleases God and what displeases Him. Al-Ghazali, despite his Ash'ari affiliation, opened in *al-Mustasfa* a door to rational knowledge of the general interests (*maqasid*) on which legal reasoning (*ijtihad*) is built, thus combining two epistemic sources.

In Western philosophy, Plato in the dialogue *Meno* proposed that knowledge of virtue is a kind of recollection (*anamnesis*) of eternal truths that the soul apprehended before descending into the body. G. E. Moore developed the theory of "intuitionism," maintaining that goodness is a simple property that cannot be analyzed, grasped directly rather than inferentially, and that any attempt to reduce it to natural properties falls into what he called the "naturalistic fallacy." W. D. Ross offered a more moderate version that distinguishes between "prima facie duties," which are grasped intuitively, and the "actual duty," which is determined by weighing the circumstances. On the other side stood the non-realists and proponents of error theory, such as Mackie, who deny that there is moral knowledge in the true sense at all, since no object exists to be known outside our subjective attitudes.

Those who hold intuitionist knowledge face a difficult epistemological objection. How do we account for the "intuitive faculty" that is supposed to perceive moral truths? What is its physiological or metaphysical nature? And why do people's intuitions differ widely across cultures, if moral knowledge is genuinely objective? Intuitionists reply that disagreement in application does not negate the existence of general principles on which there is agreement (such as the wrongness of injustice), but reflects differences in circumstances and in the information available.

An example: the knowledge that "torturing an innocent child for amusement is wrong" appears to be immediate and certain knowledge that requires no complex inference. Intuitionists use it as a paradigm example of the existence of direct moral knowledge.

This debate is also linked to the question of the method of moral inquiry itself. Should the philosopher begin from abstract general principles and deduce particular judgments from them (a deductive method), or should he begin from clear intuitive judgments about particular cases and formulate general principles consistent with them (the method of "reflective equilibrium" formulated by John Rawls)? Many contemporary moral philosophers favor the second method as more realistic in confronting the complexity of actual moral cases, and more capable of dealing with conflicts among initial intuitions, compared with a strict deduction from a single higher principle that may collide with concrete cases whose certainty we find difficult to give up.
