# Popular Constitutionalism

**Popular constitutionalism** is a current in contemporary American constitutional theory holding that final authority to interpret the constitution does not belong to the Supreme Court alone, but should remain distributed among the people, their elected institutions and the courts together. This contrasts with the prevailing doctrine of **judicial supremacy**, which treats a court's ruling as the final word, binding on all other branches without subsequent political debate.

## The Historical Basis: The People as an Early Check on the Courts

The current, led by Larry Kramer in *The People Themselves* (2004), draws on historical research into the early American record, which shows that in the first decades after the founding the public actually exercised direct forms of oversight over constitutional interpretation, through mass protest, political pressure and other non-judicial responses to contested interpretations. This suggests that the courts' exclusive monopoly over final interpretation is a later historical development rather than an original founding premise of the American system.

## Social Movements as Constitutional Actors

Reva Siegel and Jack Balkin developed a complementary dimension of this current through the idea of constitutional meaning made outside the courts. They show how organized social movements (such as the women's rights and civil rights movements) reshape popular understanding of constitutional principles through decades of mobilization and public debate, before the judiciary formally adopts this changed understanding as precedent. Popular mobilization thus precedes judicial change rather than merely following it.

## Akhil Reed Amar's Textual-Structural Reading

Akhil Reed Amar offers a complementary path based on a structural reading of the constitutional text itself. He concludes that the continuing founding sovereignty of the people ("We the People") remains, in principle, a source that ranks above any specific institutional monopoly of interpretation. This opens the door, in theory, to direct popular mechanisms for reshaping constitutional understanding outside the strict formal procedure alone.

## The Principal Critique: The Risk to Minorities

The current faces a fundamental objection: that weakening judicial supremacy may expose minority rights to the danger of temporary political majorities in the absence of a relatively independent judicial guardian. Proponents of popular constitutionalism reply that history shows popular mobilization and judicial action interacted to protect rights, and that the courts alone were not the sole protector of rights in every documented case throughout the history of the republic.

## Limits of Transfer beyond the American Setting

The argument responds to a particular problem: a hard-to-amend constitution combined with a powerful court. Its force changes where those conditions differ. In systems built on parliamentary sovereignty, as classically in the United Kingdom, elected legislators already hold the last word, and the live question concerns the strength of rights review. Scholars such as Mark Tushnet and Stephen Gardbaum describe weak-form or Commonwealth arrangements, in which courts assess rights but legislatures may respond or override.

Many continental European states entrust review to a specialized constitutional court, created deliberately, often after authoritarian rule, to restrain majorities. Judges are commonly chosen through political bodies for limited terms, and amendment is frequently easier than in the United States, so democratic correction has other channels. Some constitutions also protect certain principles even against amendment, as Germany's does. Membership in the European Union and adherence to the European Convention on Human Rights place further interpretive authority in courts that a single national public cannot control. Finally, where institutions are fragile, appeals to the people's interpretive authority may be used by governing majorities to weaken independent courts, a risk that comparative scholarship on democratic backsliding emphasizes.
