# Socialization Process

## Definition

The socialization process is the sequence of stages, mechanisms, and encounters through which a person is inducted into the life of a society. While socialization names the phenomenon as a whole, the socialization process names its unfolding in time: the ordered steps by which a newborn, with no knowledge of language, roles, or rules, becomes a competent member of a group, and by which adults continue to enter new roles throughout life. It is a process of learning and internalisation, but also of interaction and adjustment, since each new situation demands that the individual read expectations, practise new behaviours, and correct mistakes until the new conduct becomes habitual.

## Stages and Sequence

The process is usually described in stages. Primary socialization begins in infancy, when the child acquires language, basic bodily habits, and emotional bonds, and learns to distinguish self from others. Secondary socialization follows as the child enters school and peer groups, encountering formal rules and expectations beyond the family. Adolescence and adulthood bring anticipatory socialization into future roles — a student rehearsing the conduct of a professional, an immigrant learning the norms of a new country — and, at moments of rupture, resocialization, in which old identities are dismantled and new ones built. Erik Erikson, in *Childhood and Society* (1950), mapped this trajectory as a series of psychosocial crises, each resolved more or less successfully, from trust in infancy to identity in adolescence and generativity in adult life.

Jean Piaget's studies of moral judgement, published in 1932, showed that the child's grasp of rules itself develops in stages: from treating rules as sacred commands handed down by authority, to understanding them as agreements that can be negotiated among equals. George Herbert Mead, in the lectures published as *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934), described the mechanism of this development: the child first plays at the roles of particular others, then plays games governed by the rules of all participants at once, and finally takes the attitude of the "generalized other," the community as a whole.

## Mechanisms

Sociologists identify several mechanisms through which the process operates. Imitation is the earliest: the child copies the gestures and speech of those around it. Identification goes further, as the child takes a significant other as a model and adopts that person's attitudes as its own. Internalisation is the deepest mechanism, in which external norms become part of the self and guide conduct without supervision. Reinforcement — praise, blame, reward, and sanction — shapes which behaviours persist and which are abandoned. Talcott Parsons and Robert Bales, in *Family, Socialization and Interaction Process* (1955), analysed how these mechanisms operate within the family, with distinct roles shaping the child's first experience of the social order.

The process can also break down or reverse. Erving Goffman, in *Asylums* (1961), described the mortification of the self in total institutions, where inmates are stripped of their previous identity before being rebuilt as institutional members. Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann, in *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966), observed that secondary socialization is more fragile than primary socialization precisely because it lacks the emotional intensity of childhood, and must be continually reinforced by the institutions concerned.

## Contemporary Relevance

Attention to the socialization process matters whenever societies try to change behaviour deliberately. Early childhood education, civic instruction, professional training, and the rehabilitation of offenders are all interventions in this process, and their success depends on understanding its stages and mechanisms rather than merely its outcomes. Digital media have added new stages and agents: children now pass through peer cultures formed online, and adults are resocialised by platforms whose norms are set by algorithms as much as by people. The concept also explains failure: when the stages are disrupted — by war, displacement, or institutional breakdown — individuals may be left without the taken-for-granted competence that social life requires, and rebuilding it is a slow, staged task. Refugee resettlement programmes illustrate this clearly: adults who were fully competent members of one society must pass through a new round of secondary socialization, learning a language, a labour market, and unfamiliar bureaucratic routines, while their children move through primary socialization in a culture their parents are still learning. The process is rarely smooth, and its frictions — misunderstandings, loss of status, generational conflict — are themselves sociologically revealing.
