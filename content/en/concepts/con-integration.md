# Integration: Holding a Society Together

**Integration** is the process by which the parts of a society — its individuals, groups, and institutions — are bound together into a coherent whole, so that people share norms, participate in common institutions, and feel themselves members of the same society. It is one of sociology's oldest questions: what holds society together? The answer has changed across the history of the discipline, from shared religion to the division of labour to shared citizenship, but the question has remained central, because every society faces the problem of how to turn a collection of individuals into a community.

## Definition and Development

Émile Durkheim gave the concept its classic formulation in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893). In societies with little division of labour, Durkheim argued, integration rests on mechanical solidarity: people are alike, share the same beliefs, and are bound by a strong collective conscience. In modern societies, integration rests on organic solidarity: people are different, depend on one another through the division of labour, and are bound by their interdependence. Durkheim's point was that modern society is not held together by sameness but by difference — by the fact that we need each other.

Durkheim also showed what happens when integration fails. In *Suicide* (1897), he argued that people who are weakly integrated into society — who lack ties to family, religion, or community — are more likely to take their own lives. Anomie, the breakdown of norms and integration, was for Durkheim a pathology of modern life, produced when change is too rapid for institutions to keep pace. Integration, in his account, is not only a sociological concept but a condition of human well-being.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Talcott Parsons, in *The Social System* (1951), made integration one of the four functional prerequisites of any social system. Every society, Parsons argued, must solve four problems: adaptation to the environment, goal attainment, integration, and the maintenance of patterns. Integration is the problem of coordinating the parts of the system, managing conflict, and maintaining solidarity. Parsons's account was criticized for assuming that societies are naturally harmonious, but it established integration as a problem that every theory of society must address.

David Lockwood, in "Social Integration and System Integration" (1964), made a distinction that reshaped the debate. Social integration refers to the orderly or conflictual relations between actors — whether people get along, cooperate, and share norms. System integration refers to the compatibility or contradiction between the parts of a social system — whether the economy, the polity, and the family fit together or pull apart. Lockwood's point was that a society can be socially integrated but systemically contradictory, or vice versa: people may get along while the system that sustains them is in crisis. His distinction made it possible to study conflict and order together, rather than treating them as opposites.

Jürgen Habermas, in *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981, English edition 1984), developed the distinction further. He argued that modern societies are integrated in two ways: through the lifeworld, the shared background of meanings reproduced through communication, and through the system, the economy and the state, coordinated by money and power. The problem of modernity, Habermas argued, is the colonization of the lifeworld by the system: when money and bureaucratic power invade areas of life — family, education, public debate — that should be governed by communication, integration is distorted and solidarity is damaged.

## Ethnographic Examples

Anthropologists have studied integration in the lives of migrants, who must find a place in a new society while maintaining ties to the old one. Integration, in this context, is not a one-way process of assimilation but a negotiation: migrants learn the language, find work, and participate in institutions, while also maintaining their own communities, languages, and practices. Research has shown that integration works best when host societies allow this dual belonging, and fails when they demand that migrants abandon their identity as the price of acceptance.

Integration is also studied in divided societies. In post-conflict settings, researchers ask how former enemies can live together: through shared institutions, through truth commissions, through everyday encounters in markets and schools. In multicultural cities, they ask how people of different religions and ethnicities share public space, and what makes some neighbourhoods cohesive and others fragmented. The answers consistently point to the importance of institutions — schools, workplaces, and civic associations — that bring people together across their differences.

## Contemporary Debates

Current scholarship debates whether integration is still the right word. Some argue that it implies a one-sided demand: that minorities must integrate into a majority that never has to change. They prefer terms such as inclusion, belonging, or conviviality, which emphasize mutual adaptation rather than the absorption of the outsider. Others argue that integration remains useful precisely because it names a real problem: societies do need shared norms and institutions, and the failure to build them produces fragmentation and conflict.

A further debate concerns the relationship between integration and diversity. Can a society be both integrated and diverse, or does integration require a shared culture? Research on multicultural societies suggests that integration does not require sameness: people can share institutions, laws, and a sense of citizenship while maintaining different religions, languages, and ways of life. What integration requires, on this view, is not cultural uniformity but a shared commitment to the institutions that make coexistence possible. Anthropology's contribution is to study integration as it is lived — in the daily work of getting along, in the institutions that make it possible, and in the conflicts that arise when it fails.
