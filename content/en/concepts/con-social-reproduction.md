# Social Reproduction

Social reproduction is the process by which a society maintains and renews itself over time: the daily and generational work of keeping people alive, raising children, caring for the sick and the elderly, educating new members, and transmitting the skills, values, and social positions that make the existing order continue. It asks a question that classical economics left in the shadows: workers do not simply appear at the factory gate each morning, ready to work. Someone fed them, clothed them, nursed them when they were ill, and raised them from childhood. Social reproduction names that hidden labour and the institutions that organise it — the household, the school, the health system, and the state — and treats them as central to how societies persist and how inequalities are passed on.

## Definition

The term is used in two closely related senses. In the Marxist sense, it refers to the reproduction of the conditions of production themselves. Marx argued in *Capital* (1867) that capitalism must reproduce not only commodities but also labour power, the capacity of workers to work, and the social relations that keep workers dependent on wages. This includes the daily renewal of the worker's strength through food, rest, and shelter, and the generational replacement of the workforce through childbirth and child-rearing. In the second, broader sense, developed by feminist theory, social reproduction covers all the activities involved in maintaining life on a daily basis and across generations. Barbara Laslett and Johanna Brenner's widely cited account (1989) treats it as the activities, attitudes, behaviours, emotions, responsibilities, and relationships directly involved in maintaining life on a daily basis and from one generation to the next. The two senses overlap but differ in emphasis: the Marxist version stresses the reproduction of the economic system, while the feminist version stresses the reproduction of human life and relationships, much of it unpaid and performed by women.

## Origins and Development

The concept's roots lie in Marx's account of reproduction, but it was developed into a sociological and anthropological concept in the twentieth century. Pierre Bourdieu and Jean-Claude Passeron's *Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture* (1970; English translation 1977) showed how schools reproduce class inequality by rewarding the cultural capital of privileged families while presenting that advantage as individual merit. Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis made a parallel argument for the American case in *Schooling in Capitalist America* (1976), arguing that schools socialise children into the discipline and hierarchy of the workplace. Feminist scholars in the 1970s, notably Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James, insisted that the unpaid domestic labour of housewives was productive labour for capital, since it reproduced the workforce at no cost to employers. Laslett and Brenner's synthesis in 1989 brought these strands together, defining social reproduction as a historically variable set of arrangements rather than a timeless function of the family. Since then the concept has been extended to cover the global care economy: migrant domestic workers, nannies, and nurses who sustain households in wealthy countries while their own families are cared for by others at home.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns the boundary between production and reproduction. If all labour that sustains life counts as reproduction, the concept risks becoming so broad that it explains everything and therefore nothing. Scholars such as Nancy Fraser have argued for keeping the distinction while recognising that capitalism depends on both: the economy of waged production rests on an economy of unpaid care, and the two are in tension, since the drive for profit undermines the very care work it depends on. Fraser's account of the "crisis of care" (2016) holds that neoliberal policies — cuts to public services, the casualisation of employment, and the commodification of care — have intensified that contradiction. A second debate concerns the role of the state. Public provision of childcare, healthcare, and pensions socialises some of the costs of reproduction, and the level of that provision varies enormously between welfare regimes, shaping gender equality and class inequality. A third debate concerns race and migration. The global organisation of care work is racialised: women from poorer countries perform domestic labour for wealthier households, creating what Arlie Hochschild called global care chains, in which care is extracted from the global South in the same way that raw materials once were.

## Contemporary Relevance

Social reproduction has become a central concept for understanding the crises of the twenty-first century. The COVID-19 pandemic made visible what the concept had long argued: when schools and nurseries closed, the economy depended on the unpaid care work that absorbed the shock, and that work fell overwhelmingly on women. Debates about ageing populations, falling birth rates, and the cost of childcare are debates about who will do the work of reproduction and who will pay for it. The concept also frames the politics of the welfare state, the campaign for the recognition and payment of domestic labour, and the struggles of care workers for wages and rights. In anthropology, it connects the study of kinship and the household to the study of global capitalism, showing how the most intimate relations — feeding a child, nursing a parent — are bound up with the largest structures of the economy. Its critical force lies in a simple reversal: what economics treats as a private, natural background to production, the care that makes all other work possible, is in fact the foundation on which society stands.
