# Gossip: Talk About Others and the Work It Does

**Gossip** is informal talk about people who are not present — about what they did, who they are seeing, what they said, and what it means. It is one of the most universal and most condemned forms of human communication: every society has it, and nearly every society deplores it. Yet anthropologists have long argued that gossip is not idle chatter but serious social work. It circulates information, enforces norms, builds and breaks reputations, and binds groups together. To gossip is to participate in the collective evaluation of conduct, and to be gossiped about is to be reminded that one's behaviour is watched and judged. Gossip is, in short, one of the main ways in which societies govern themselves without laws, courts, or police.

## Definition and Development

The classic anthropological defence of gossip was made by Max Gluckman in "Gossip and Scandal" (1963). Gluckman argued that gossip has two main functions. First, it maintains the unity, morals, and values of a group: by talking about who has violated the group's standards, members remind one another of what those standards are, and the threat of being gossiped about keeps people in line. Second, gossip draws the boundaries of the group: only those who share the group's history and values can gossip about its members in a way that makes sense, so gossiping together is a sign of belonging — and gossiping about outsiders, or being excluded from gossip, marks the edge of the group. Gossip is thus a privilege of membership, not a failure of it.

Robert Paine, in "What Is Gossip About? An Alternative Hypothesis" (1967), added a second dimension: gossip is also a matter of individual interest and information management. People gossip to gain advantage, to test alliances, to damage rivals, and to gather information about a changing social landscape. Gossip is a form of politics conducted by other means — a way of pursuing one's interests without open confrontation. Together, Gluckman and Paine established the two poles of gossip theory: gossip as social control and group maintenance, and gossip as strategic action by individuals.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Robin Dunbar, in *Grooming, Gossip, and the Evolution of Language* (1996), gave gossip an evolutionary frame. In primate groups, social bonds are maintained by grooming — time-consuming, one-to-one physical contact. As human groups grew larger, Dunbar argued, grooming could no longer do the job: language, and gossip in particular, took over as a form of "vocal grooming," allowing one person to maintain bonds with several others at once and to keep track of who was trustworthy, who had cheated, and who was allied with whom. Gossip, in this account, is not a by-product of language but one of the reasons language evolved: to manage the social world of a large group.

Niko Besnier, in *Gossip and the Everyday Production of Politics* (2009), studied gossip on Nukulaelae Atoll in Tuvalu, where he showed how gossip produces political life day by day. In a small island community, where everyone knows everyone else, gossip is the medium through which reputations are made and unmade, leaders are evaluated, and conflicts are fought without open confrontation. Besnier's work showed that gossip is not merely talk about politics but politics itself — the everyday process by which power is negotiated. John Haviland, in *Gossip, Reputation, and Knowledge in Zinacantan* (1977), made a similar point in highland Chiapas, Mexico: gossip is a system of knowledge, a way in which a community collectively knows and judges its members, and what is said matters because reputations have real consequences.

## Ethnographic Examples

Ethnography is full of gossip in action. Gluckman, drawing on Elizabeth Colson's study of the Makah of the north-west coast of North America and on other cases, treated gossip and scandal as a means by which groups assert their values and exclude outsiders. Among the Tzotzil-speaking people of Zinacantan, Haviland showed that gossip is shaped by the social relations of the community and bears directly on reputation. In Tuvalu, Besnier showed how gossip, in a small face-to-face community, shapes how people's conduct and standing are judged and thereby feeds into local politics.

Gossip also has its genres and its etiquette. In many societies there are recognised ways of starting gossip — a raised eyebrow, a "did you hear?", a story told as a joke — and recognised ways of denying it or passing it on without taking responsibility for it. Some gossip is praise, not blame: talk about someone's generosity or courage builds their reputation just as surely as scandal destroys it.

## Contemporary Debates

Contemporary anthropology debates gossip on several fronts. One concerns gender: gossip has often been stereotyped as women's talk, idle and malicious, while men's talk about others is dignified as politics or news. Feminist anthropologists have shown that this is a double standard: men gossip as much as women, and the dismissal of gossip as trivial has served to devalue women's social knowledge and political action. A second debate concerns the digital transformation of gossip: social media, messaging apps, and anonymous forums have made gossip faster, more public, and harder to control, raising new questions about reputation, privacy, and harm.

A third debate concerns gossip's moral ambiguity. Gossip can enforce good norms — honesty, generosity, fidelity — but it can also enforce bad ones, punishing those who defy unjust hierarchies and spreading rumours that ruin innocent people. Anthropologists resist the temptation to celebrate or condemn gossip in general: its value depends on what it is used for, by whom, and against whom.
