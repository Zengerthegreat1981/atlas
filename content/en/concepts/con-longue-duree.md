# Longue Durée

The longue durée is a central methodological concept in the historiography of the French Annales School, formulated by the historian Fernand Braudel in his landmark article "Histoire et sciences sociales: la longue durée," published in the journal *Annales* in 1958. It refers to the slowest and deepest level of historical time: the quasi-stable geographical, environmental and mental structures that change very slowly over centuries or even millennia, and that form the silent framework within which rapid political events take place, often without being fully perceived by their contemporaries.

## Content

Braudel, influenced by his personal experience of long contemplation during his captivity in a German prisoner-of-war camp during the Second World War (where he wrote the draft of his first work from memory), developed a hierarchical conception of three overlapping levels of historical time. He applied it in detail in his best-known work, *The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II* (1949):

- **Geographical time (*temps géographique*):** the longue durée itself, including quasi-permanent factors such as climate, terrain, patterns of settlement and historical trade routes, which move at a pace measured in centuries.
- **Social time (*temps social*) or "conjuncture" (*conjoncture*):** medium-term economic and social cycles (decades to half a century), such as price fluctuations and demographic growth.
- **Individual time (*temps individuel*):** the time of the rapid **event** (*événement*): a battle, the signing of a treaty, the death of a king. This is the level that traditionally dominated narrative political history, and which Braudel regarded as superficial "dust" if it is considered alone without being linked to the deeper structures.

Braudel argued that understanding real history requires considering these three levels together, giving special weight to the longue durée, which often explains why certain patterns of historical action recur despite changes in the actors and surface events.

## Impact and Criticism

The concept transformed modern historical writing by shifting the focus from political *histoire événementielle* (the history of events) to the history of long-term structures, and it deeply influenced the development of economic, environmental and demographic history, as well as fields such as historical geography and archaeology. Interest in the concept re-emerged strongly in the 2010s through the writings of historians of the "history manifesto," including David Armitage and Jo Guldi in *The History Manifesto* (2014), who called for a revival of long-range analysis against an academic specialisation excessively focused on very short periods.

The concept has been criticised from several directions. Some historians argue that an excessive focus on quasi-static structures may neglect the agency of individual historical actors and their capacity to bring about real and rapid change. Moreover, the boundaries between the three temporal levels (geographical, social and individual) are in practice less clear than the theoretical classification suggests, and Braudel himself acknowledged the difficulty of separating them completely in the practice of historical writing.
