# Madhhab as a Legal-School Institution

This concept treats the **madhhab** (school of law) not merely as a set of agreed juristic opinions, but as a **social institutional structure** with mechanisms of transmission, internal hierarchy and historical legitimacy acquired over centuries. This perspective complements the study of the substantive content of the legal schools with a study of how they persist and organize themselves socially.

## Internal Hierarchy of the School

A single school contains a clear hierarchy among the adherents: the **absolute mujtahid** (the founder of the school, capable of deriving both principles and branches directly from the texts), the **restricted mujtahid or mujtahid of the school** (capable of exercising ijtihad within the principles of his school's founder without departing from them), and finally the **muqallid** (the follower who adheres to the school's opinions without independent juristic capacity). Later Sunni writers such as Ibn al-Salah (d. 643/1245) and al-Nawawi (d. 676/1277) described finer ranks, including a mujtahid within the school who weighs between its opinions (*mujtahid al-fatwa* or *al-tarjih*). This hierarchy organizes the mechanism of producing legal opinions (fatwas) and rulings within the school across successive generations, and determines who holds the authority to add a new opinion and who is required to confine himself to transmitting what has been established.

## Why Four Sunni Schools Remained?

Early Islamic jurisprudential history saw a wider diversity of schools (such as the school of al-Awza'i (d. 157/774) in the Levant, the Jariri school of al-Tabari (d. 310/923), and the Zahiri school later represented by Ibn Hazm (d. 456/1064)), but long-term institutional survival among Sunnis was limited to four schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali). The Ja'fari, Zaydi and Ibadi schools also continued among other communities. Wael Hallaq describes how early regional schools gave way to "personal" schools identified with a leading master, and George Makdisi described the schools as "guilds of law". Modern scholars discuss the reasons for this selective survival, weighing several combined factors: the early and systematic written codification of the principles and branches of each surviving school; the existence of teaching circles and successive generations of followers who ensured the continuity of transmission; and the support of political authorities for a particular school at certain periods (such as the Ottoman state's official adoption of the Hanafi school), in contrast with other schools that did not enjoy comparable institutional continuity despite sometimes the strength of their juristic arguments.

## School Affiliation and Social Membership

In medieval Islamic societies with multiple schools (such as Egypt and the Levant), school affiliation sometimes determined the judge hearing an individual's case (in 663/1265 the Mamluk sultan Baybars appointed chief judges from each of the four Sunni schools in Cairo), and endowments (*awqaf*) were sometimes designated for teaching a particular school in madrasas, so that the school became an actual social institution with practical effects that go beyond the mere theoretical difference of juristic opinion.

## Criticism of Strict Adherence to a School

This institutional structure faced criticism from reformers such as Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728/1328) and al-Shawkani (d. 1250/1834), who objected to the transformation of school affiliation into a constraint preventing scholars themselves from exercising ijtihad directly when they preferred a piece of evidence that contradicted their school's position. This debate is directly connected to the discussion of "ijtihad versus taqlid" in modern Islamic reformist thought.
