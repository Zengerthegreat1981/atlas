# Partiality & Impartiality

## Meaning and Definition

In moral philosophy, the term "partiality" refers to a legitimate or illegitimate inclination in favor of particular persons, such as relatives, friends, and others with whom we have special ties. This stands in contrast to "impartiality," which requires that the interests of all people be weighed on one scale, with no preference for one over another. The central problem here is whether morality requires us to regard the interest of a stranger as equal to that of our own son, or whether special relationships carry moral weight in themselves.

## Roots in Arab-Islamic Thought

Muslim jurists and moral thinkers treated this duality within the framework of "rights" and their gradations. Jurisprudence established that kinship has a right to kindness and maintenance that takes precedence over the right of the stranger, as in the verse "those of blood relationship are closer to one another." This is a legitimate partiality, regulated by the Sharia and not arbitrary. At the same time, the Qur'an enjoins justice even toward adversaries: "Let not the hatred of a people prevent you from being just." Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' ʿUlum al-Din*, discussed the balance between the right of oneself, the right of one's kin, and the right of the Muslim community as a whole, holding that virtue lies in ordering priorities rather than in abolishing particularity. Likewise, Ibn Hazm, in *al-Akhlaq wa-l-Siyar*, distinguished between a natural love, which is not blameworthy, and unjust favoritism that breaks justice.

## Western Philosophical Positions

In modern philosophy, utilitarians such as Bentham and Mill adopted a strict impartialist position: according to the principle of utility, each individual must be counted as one and no more. Bernard Williams objected with the argument of the "one thought too many," arguing that a husband who rescues his wife because she is his wife does not need a general moral justification. Samuel Scheffler developed an intermediate view that recognizes agent-centred prerogatives, and later defended the value of special relationships, within limits imposed by general moral demands. Thomas Nagel, for his part, in *The View from Nowhere* (1986), discussed the need to reconcile the personal and impersonal standpoints in any reasonable moral theory.

## Illustrative Example

Suppose a physician is faced with a choice between saving his own injured son or a stranger in a similar condition, with limited resources. Most moral philosophers, in contrast to strict utilitarianism, accept his partiality toward his son in the personal context. Yet in his capacity as a physician in a hospital, they require him to adhere to a standard of impartial medical priority.

## Contemporary Applications

The tension between partiality and impartiality appears clearly in public policy. A government employee is required to be impartial in awarding contracts even if one of the applicants is a friend of his, while it is accepted that he may favor his family in his personal gifts. In *The Expanding Circle*, Peter Singer discussed the need for a gradual shift toward a wider circle of moral concern. He argued that moral concern has historically widened beyond kin and tribe, and that reason can push it further; critics reply that special ties retain independent moral weight.
