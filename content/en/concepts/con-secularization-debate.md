# Secularization Debates

Secularism in Arab thought is a continuing debate about the relationship between religion and the state, and between religion and knowledge. A public confrontation took place in Egypt in 1902–1903 between Muhammad Abduh and the Syrian Christian thinker Farah Antun. In his journal *al-Jami'a*, Antun argued for the separation of religious and civil authority. He collected the articles as *Ibn Rushd wa-falsafatuhu* (Ibn Rushd and His Philosophy), published at Alexandria in 1903. Abduh replied in *al-Manar* that Islam has no religious authority of the ecclesiastical kind, and that the ruler's office is civil. The reply is not a call for a European-style secular state.

## The Caliphate and Ali Abd al-Raziq

Mustafa Kemal abolished the Ottoman caliphate in March 1924, and the question grew heated: is the caliphate obligatory under the Sharia? The Azhari scholar **Ali Abd al-Raziq** (1888–1966) issued his book *al-Islam wa-usul al-hukm* (Islam and the Foundations of Government, 1925), arguing that the caliphate is not a religious foundation, and that the Prophet was a messenger, not a king. The Azhar's Council of Senior Scholars, sitting as a disciplinary body in August 1925, expelled him from the ranks of the ulama, and he was then removed from the Sharia judiciary. Rashid Rida and Muhammad al-Khidr Husayn, among others, replied. The book has remained a reference point in later debates. Albert Hourani, in *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age*, treated it as a central text of the Egyptian liberal trend.

## Islamists and Their Defenders

Hasan al-Banna founded the Muslim Brotherhood at Ismailiyya in 1928, in the climate that followed the abolition of the caliphate and growing argument over the place of religion in public life. Sayyid Qutb, in *Ma'alim fi al-tariq* (Milestones, 1964), set out a theory of *hakimiyya* (divine sovereignty), developing a theme already argued by Abu al-A'la Mawdudi. On the other side, liberal and leftist writers defended a secular order. Fouad Zakaria did so in *al-Haqiqa wa-l-wahm fi al-haraka al-islamiyya al-mu'asira* (Truth and Illusion in the Contemporary Islamic Movement, Cairo, 1986).

## Critical Positions

Muhammad 'Abid al-Jabri, in *al-Din wa-l-dawla wa-tatbiq al-shari'a* (Religion, the State and the Application of the Sharia, 1996), set aside the term "secularism" as a poor fit for a tradition without a church, and proposed instead democracy and rationality. Aziz al-Azmeh, in *al-Ilmaniyya min manzur mukhtalif* (Secularism from a Different Perspective, 1992), traced the several historical meanings of the term in Europe and in the Arab world. Talal Asad, in *Formations of the Secular* (2003), argued that the secular is a mode of rule that defines what counts as religion and as morality.

## Limits of Comparison with Europe

European secularism took shape in conflicts between churches and states. Muslim societies had no single ecclesiastical institution of that kind, so arguments about separation took a different form. The Sharia and modern codes also remained intertwined. Egypt drew its civil codes from French models while leaving personal status largely under the Sharia. Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri was the principal drafter of the Egyptian Civil Code issued in 1948, and he used French civil law, Islamic jurisprudence and other comparative material. What followed was a matter of degree.
