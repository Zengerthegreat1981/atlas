# Secularization Debates

Secularism in Arab thought was not an abstract import. It is a continuing debate about the relationship between religion and the state, and between religion and knowledge. The first great confrontation took place in Egypt at the beginning of the twentieth century, between Muhammad Abduh and the Syrian Christian thinker Farah Antun. Antun published *Ibn Rushd wa-falsafatuhu* (Ibn Rushd and His Philosophy, 1903) in Alexandria, calling for the separation of the religious and civil authorities. Abduh replied in the journal *al-Manar* that Islam has no religious authority in the ecclesiastical sense, and that the ruler is civil.

## The Caliphate and Ali Abd al-Raziq

Mustafa Kemal abolished the Ottoman caliphate in March 1924, and the question grew heated: is the caliphate obligatory under the Sharia? The Azhari scholar **Ali Abd al-Raziq** (1888–1966) issued his book *al-Islam wa-usul al-hukm* (Islam and the Foundations of Government, 1925), arguing that the caliphate is not a religious foundation, and that the Prophet was a messenger, not a king. The Azhar sheikhs convened a disciplinary council in August 1925, expelled him from the company of the scholars, and removed him from the Sharia judiciary. Rashid Rida and al-Khidr Husayn, among others, replied to him. The book remains a reference in every subsequent debate, and Albert Hourani regarded it as one of the most prominent fruits of the Egyptian liberal trend.

## Islamists and Their Defenders

Hasan al-Banna founded the Muslim Brotherhood in 1928 in the climate that followed the abolition of the caliphate and growing concern over the separation of religion and public life. Sayyid Qutb, in *Ma'alim fi-l-tariq* (Milestones, 1964), built the theory of *hakimiyya* (divine sovereignty). On the other side, liberal and leftist thinkers, such as Fouad Zakaria in *al-Haqiqa wa-l-wahm fi-l-haraka al-islamiyya al-mu'asira* (Truth and Illusion in the Contemporary Islamic Movement, 1986), called for secularism as a condition for progress.

## Critical Positions

Muhammad 'Abid al-Jabiri, in *al-Din wa-l-dawla wa-tatbiq al-shari'a* (Religion, the State and the Application of the Sharia, 1996), rejected the use of the term "secularism", since it does not fit Islam, which has no church, and proposed instead two slogans: democracy and rationality. Aziz al-Azmeh wrote *al-Ilmaniyya min manzur mukhtalif* (Secularisms from Various Viewpoints, 1992), tracing the multiple meanings of the term in European and Arab history. In the West, Talal Asad in *Formations of the Secular* (2003) argued that secularism is not neutrality but a mode of rule that redefines religion and morality.

## Limits of Comparison with Europe

European secularism arose from the struggle between the church and the state, whereas Muslim societies never had a unified ecclesiastical institution, so the form of separation differed. Moreover, the relationship between the Sharia and modern law remained intertwined. Egypt adopted the French civil code, while keeping personal status matters in the hands of the Sharia; and 'Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri drafted the Egyptian civil code in 1948, drawing on both. The matter is closer to degrees of separation than to rupture.
