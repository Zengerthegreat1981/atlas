# Memory and History

This concept examines the ambiguous relationship between **collective memory**, understood as a living and changing practice that a society or group carries about its past, and **history**, understood as a critical and methodical reconstruction of that past carried out by a knowledge institution (the university, the archive, the professional historian). The French sociologist Maurice Halbwachs laid the theoretical foundation for this distinction in his book *The Social Frameworks of Memory* (1925), arguing that individual memory itself is socially conditioned, and that what groups remember is built within present social frameworks that cannot be separated from the group's current needs.

## Nora and the "Realms of Memory"

The French historian Pierre Nora developed this distinction in his encyclopedic project *Les Lieux de Mémoire* ("Realms of Memory", three volumes, 1984–1992). He drew a clear line between memory, which he described as a "life" carried by living groups, constantly evolving, subject to forgetting and distortion, organically tied to the present and rooted in the sacred and in emotion, and history, which he described as "a problematic and incomplete reconstruction of what no longer exists," produced by critical institutions that seek distance and verification. For Nora, "realms of memory" (monuments, commemorations, symbols, archives) arise precisely when spontaneous living memory recedes and the need to deliberately "produce" memory through symbolic media takes its place. In other words, they emerge from the tension when history "besieges" memory, distorts it and freezes it.

## Memory as Subject and as Historical Tool

Since the 1980s, the field of "memory studies" has developed as an intersection of history, anthropology, sociology and literary studies. It addresses questions such as:

- **Memory and collective trauma**: how the Holocaust, colonialism and civil wars are narrated generation after generation, and how this affects present political identity.
- **Memory politics**: how states and political movements deploy narratives of particular pasts (monuments, school curricula, national holidays) to legitimize the present. This is sometimes called "official memory," as opposed to the "counter-memory" carried by marginalized groups.
- **Oral memory and oral history**, which gives the testimonies of ordinary individuals a source status equivalent to the archival document. It was developed by historians such as Alessandro Portelli.

## The Methodological Debate

The field contains a structural tension. On the one hand, collective memory offers the historian a living and rich source, with a subjective and emotional dimension that a dry document lacks. On the other hand, memory is subject to intense selectivity and is constantly reshaped according to the needs of the present, which makes it an unstable source for proving events. Many historians therefore tend to treat collective memory as a **subject of historical study** (how a group remembered an event, and why in that particular way) rather than as a **direct, reliable source** for establishing that the event itself occurred.

## Connection to Other Concepts in the Atlas

This concept intersects with historical relativism (the idea that every account of the past, memory included, is conditioned by the position of its narrator) and with historical consciousness (the distinction societies make between their "remembered past" and their critical knowledge of their own history). It also provides a necessary background for understanding debates on transitional justice and policies for acknowledging crimes of the past.
