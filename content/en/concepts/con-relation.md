# Relation and Encounter

The concept that determines what the instrument of therapy is in the first place: a technique applied by an expert, or an encounter between two people?

## Framing the Concept

The question: is the therapeutic relationship a **vessel** for the treatment, or **is it** the treatment? The answer of this school — despite disagreement among its currents — leans strongly toward the second.

## Core Disputes Within the Concept

The founding dispute: **Sartre against Buber** — is encounter possible? And a second, contemporary dispute: **is there a describable individual outside relation?** Ernesto Spinelli says no, and most of the school says yes in varying degrees.

## How It Appears in the Session

The first observation is not about what the client says but about what happens between the two: when one of them withdraws, when the rhythm changes, when the therapist feels boredom or pity or an urge to rescue. These are signals about the structure of the client's relationships in general.

## The Most Common Misunderstanding

Reducing encounter to warmth or empathy. In Buber it is a rare event, not a permanent mood, and it cannot be produced by will or performed.

## Example and Counter-Example

**Example:** A therapy session reaches a moment in which the therapist and client both feel that something "real" has happened — not a technique or professional empathy but an encounter in Buber's sense: a genuine, fleeting I-Thou, not the usual professional I-It. This moment cannot be planned, but the ground can be prepared for it through full presence and the absence of prior calculation.
**Counter-example:** A therapist tells a trainee, "Try to create an I-Thou encounter with every client in every session," as if it were a technique to be applied by will. This contradicts Buber directly: genuine encounter cannot be made by will or performed, and trying to "produce" it deliberately automatically turns it into a performance, that is, a disguised I-It relation.

## What Does Not Follow from This Concept

It **does not mean** that the therapeutic relationship must be an I-Thou encounter at every moment — most useful therapeutic work happens in a natural professional I-It relation (assessment, planning, follow-up), and deep encounter is a rare moment, not a permanent required state.
**Nor does it mean** that visible warmth or empathy equals existential encounter — a therapist who is outwardly warm and empathic can remain in a one-sided I-It relation, while a genuine encounter may occur in a temporary silence or a difficult confrontation that carries no superficial warmth at all.

## The Arab and Cultural Context

Buber's and Levinas's philosophy of encounter arose from deep religious and Jewish thinking about the other, but was recently formulated in universal philosophical terms largely secular in tone. In Arab contexts, close concepts already exist in the heritage — such as hospitality, the inviolability of the other, and the notion of *silat al-rahim* (maintaining kinship ties) in family relations — but they are not exactly identical to existential "encounter," which presupposes an individualist parity between two independent selves, whereas many traditional Arab relationships are built on a recognized hierarchy (elder and younger, father and son) that parity does not necessarily abolish but which is lived differently from what Buber assumes.
There is no single Arabic word that conveys the precise difference between Ich-Du and Ich-Es as Buber formulated it; the available translations ("I-You" versus "I-It") are literal renderings that do not carry the full philosophical charge of the German original, and this creates a real gap between the text and its non-specialist Arab reader, calling for additional explanation that translation alone does not suffice.

## Comparative Table of Formulations

| Thinker | Core Formulation & Key Textual Passages | Clinical Implications in the Room | Critical Divergence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Martin Buber** | *I and Thou* (1923): *"All real living is meeting"* (*"Alles wirkliche Leben ist Begegnung"*). The primary word *I-Thou* can only be spoken with the whole being, establishing the realm of the "between" (*das Zwischen*), where the other is encountered in mutuality rather than objectified as an *I-It*. | Prioritizes genuine presence over technical intervention. The therapist's primary task is to be fully available and undefended, creating conditions where real meeting can occur. | Directly counters Sartre's ontological conflict; rejects the view that relation is intrinsically an objectifying struggle. |
| **Jean-Paul Sartre** | *Being and Nothingness* (1943) & *No Exit* (1944): *"Hell is other people"* (*"L'enfer, c'est les autres"*). The gaze of the Other (*le regard*) objectifies my consciousness, transforming my freedom (*pour-soi*) into a fixed object (*en-soi*); relation is fundamentally an ontological conflict between subjectivities. | In therapy, helps clarify interpersonal dread, shame, and defensive objectification; cautions against romanticized notions of mutual transparent union. | Rejects Buber's faith in genuine I-Thou mutuality as an illusory evasion of fundamental conflict. |
| **James Bugental** | *The Art of the Psychotherapist* (1987): Describes presence as a quality of fully participating in the situation or relationship; an operationalized clinical stance characterized by mutual accessibility and authentic expressiveness. | Converts philosophical presence into concrete clinical practice: training therapists to notice mental withdrawal, defensiveness, and interruptions in moment-to-moment contact. | Pragmatic and clinical; operationalizes Buber's mystical-philosophical encounter into trainable psychotherapeutic craftsmanship. |
| **Irvin Yalom** | *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980) & *The Gift of Therapy* (2002): Emphasizes the therapeutic relationship itself as the healing agent, and the "here-and-now" relationship as a living relational microcosm of the client's interpersonal difficulties outside therapy. | Engages in calculated therapist transparency and immediate reflection on the unfolding client-therapist dynamic to break defensive interpersonal patterns. | More interactive and self-disclosing than classical analysis, though criticized when transparency risks becoming therapist display. |
| **Ernesto Spinelli** | *Practising Existential Psychotherapy: The Relational World* (2007): Asserts that no independent individual exists prior to relation; all psychological phenomena emerge within the shared relational matrix. | Rejects diagnostic or objective formulations of the client prior to or outside the unique therapeutic relationship; focuses strictly on what emerges between therapist and client. | The most radical relational stance, challenging individualist psychopathology and limiting cross-case generalization. |
| **Emmy van Deurzen** | *Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy in Practice* (2012): Relation constitutes the *Mitwelt* (social dimension), one of four existential dimensions of human reality (*Umwelt*, *Mitwelt*, *Eigenwelt*, *Überwelt*). | Explores the client's social life through core dialectics of connection and loneliness, acceptance and rejection, power and cooperation, without reducing human existence solely to relational contact. | Structurally broader and less romantic than American humanistic models, viewing relation as one dimension among others. |
