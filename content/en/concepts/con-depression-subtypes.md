# Depression Subtypes & SAD

**DSM-5-TR** describes the variety of clinical presentations of major depressive disorder using **specifiers**, which are added to the primary diagnosis to describe the predominant symptom pattern in a given episode, rather than defining each pattern as a separate disorder in its own right.

## Two Contrasting Specifiers: Melancholic and Atypical

The **melancholic specifier** (With Melancholic Features) describes an episode marked by either a loss of pleasure in all or almost all activities (anhedonia) or a lack of mood reactivity (the mood does not improve even temporarily in response to positive events), accompanied by at least three of the following: a distinct quality of depressed mood, early-morning awakening (two or more hours before the usual time), marked worsening of mood in the morning, psychomotor slowing or evident agitation, significant loss of appetite or weight, and excessive or inappropriate guilt. The **atypical specifier** (With Atypical Features) describes an almost opposite pattern: preserved **mood reactivity** (the mood actually improves in response to positive events), together with two or more of: increased appetite or weight gain, hypersomnia, a sensation of heaviness in the limbs ("leaden paralysis"), and a long-standing, pervasive sensitivity to interpersonal rejection. Because the atypical specifier requires preserved mood reactivity while the melancholic specifier requires either anhedonia or a lack of reactivity, the two patterns are in practice largely distinct, although the same person may show one in one episode and the other in a later episode.

## Depressive Disorder with a Seasonal Pattern

Commonly known as "seasonal affective disorder" or SAD, this is not a separate diagnosis in DSM-5-TR but a **seasonal pattern specifier** (With Seasonal Pattern) added to the diagnosis of major depressive disorder (or bipolar disorder). It requires that depressive episodes occur regularly at a particular time of year (usually autumn or winter) and that full or near-full remission occurs at another regular time (usually spring), for at least two consecutive years without nonseasonal episodes in between, and that seasonal episodes substantially outnumber nonseasonal episodes across the person's entire lifetime history. Clinically, people with this pattern tend to show symptoms closer to the atypical pattern (hypersomnia, increased appetite, and strong craving for carbohydrates) than to the melancholic pattern.

## History of the Distinctions

European descriptive psychiatry, especially Kraepelin's account of melancholia within manic-depressive illness, treated a severe, biologically flavored depression as different from milder or situational forms. Mid-twentieth-century debates over "endogenous" and "reactive" depression never settled on a clean biological boundary. DSM-III (1980) reintroduced melancholia as a specified form of major depression defined by symptoms rather than by a presumed cause. DSM-III-R (1987) added a seasonal pattern. Norman E. Rosenthal and colleagues at the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health described a winter depression with hypersomnia and carbohydrate craving in 1984 and reported early observations with bright-light treatment. A phase-shift account, associated with Alfred J. Lewy and others, proposed that the seasonal pattern is linked to delayed circadian timing; that account remains a hypothesis alongside other explanations.

## Prevalence

No single prevalence figure is stable. Estimates for a seasonal pattern change with latitude, with the instrument used, and with whether the count is of a clinical diagnosis or of winter mood change in the general population. Winter worsening is reported more often at higher latitudes, but many people with seasonal mood change do not meet criteria for a depressive disorder. Melancholic and atypical features likewise vary with setting: melancholic features are more often described in severely ill inpatient samples than in community surveys.

## Clinical Significance

Identifying the subtype can inform treatment planning, though the evidence is mixed. The melancholic specifier is traditionally associated with a relatively better response to electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) and tricyclic antidepressants, whereas **light therapy** (phototherapy) is an evidence-supported treatment studied specifically for the seasonal pattern, alongside standard treatments for depression. The same person may meet criteria for one specifier in one episode and a different specifier later. DSM-5-TR also provides other specifiers, including with anxious distress, with mixed features, with psychotic features, with catatonia, and with peripartum onset. The peripartum specifier covers onset during pregnancy or within four weeks after delivery; it is a timing description, not a claim that the episode has a single cause.
