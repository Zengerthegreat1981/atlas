# Double Consciousness (Du Bois)

A concept formulated by W. E. B. Du Bois to describe the psychological-social condition of the African American, who finds himself compelled always to look at himself through the eyes of a white society that looks at him with a mixture of contempt and pity.

## Content

Du Bois first used the concept in a 1897 essay ("Strivings of the Negro People"), and developed it in its most famous form in the first chapter of *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), where he wrote that the American world yields the Black individual no true self-consciousness but only lets him see himself through the revelation of the other world, and described "this sense of always looking at one's self through the eyes of others, of measuring one's soul by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity." The central idea: this "doubled consciousness" is not a passing individual psychological disorder but an existential structure imposed by a racist social structure that makes the Black individual an object in the eyes of others and a subject in his own experience at one and the same time, producing an inner conflict between two identities ("American" and "Black") that do not easily merge into one coherent identity.

## Impact and Later Development

The concept became one of the most influential frameworks in African American cultural studies, the social psychology of race, and later postcolonial studies, since it provides an analytical tool for understanding the experience of a double or multiple identity in any marginalized group living under the gaze of a different dominant society. The concept was later revived in discussions of critical race theory and postcolonial studies, although the contexts to which it was applied differ widely from its original American context.

## The Intellectual Context of the Concept's Formulation

Du Bois formulated this concept in an intellectual context shaped by his German philosophical reading during his studies at the University of Berlin (1892-1894), and scholars have often linked the idea to Hegel's account of a self formed through the other's recognition of it. At Harvard he had also studied under the psychologist and philosopher William James, and the phrase "double consciousness" had earlier currency in psychological writing on divided selves. But Du Bois turned this abstract philosophical framework into a concrete sociological analytical tool for understanding a historically specific lived experience: the experience of Black Americans in the society immediately after the abolition of slavery, where they were legally freed but remained in practice deprived of social recognition and full citizenship. Du Bois was the first African American to earn a doctorate at Harvard University (1895, in history), and he pioneered the systematic empirical study of Black American communities, as in his field study *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899), with an academic seriousness they had not previously received.

## The Concept's Relation to the Concept of the "Veil"

Du Bois links the concept of double consciousness to the image of the "Veil" that he uses metaphorically and repeatedly in his book, to describe the symbolic barrier that separates the Black world from the white world in America, which makes it hard for whites to see Blacks as complete human beings, while Black people themselves are compelled to look through this veil to understand how the dominant white world looks at them. These two metaphors (doubleness and the veil) intertwine to offer a composite diagnosis of a complex psychological-social experience that goes beyond mere superficial racial discrimination to a deep cognitive and existential structure that forms identity itself.
