# Endogamy

## Definition

Endogamy is the rule or practice of marrying within a defined group — a caste, a religion, an ethnic community, a class, an occupation, or a locality. Where exogamy requires marriage outside the group, endogamy requires or strongly prefers marriage inside it, and in doing so it draws and defends the group's boundary. The two rules are complementary, not contradictory: most societies combine them, prescribing marriage within one unit (the caste, the faith) and outside another (the clan, the lineage). Endogamy is thus less a single institution than a principle of social closure, applied at different scales and with different degrees of strictness.

## Origins and Development

Endogamy is ancient and nearly universal, but its classic analysis comes from the study of caste in India. Louis Dumont, in *Homo Hierarchicus* (1966), argued that the Indian caste system rested on endogamy: each caste married within itself, and it was this closure, combined with the religious ideology of purity and pollution, that maintained the hierarchy. Marriage outside the caste was not merely discouraged; it was polluting, threatening the status of both parties and their children. Endogamy, for Dumont, was not a preference but a religious necessity — the mechanism by which hierarchy reproduced itself generation after generation.

André Béteille's *Caste, Class, and Power* (1965), a village study in Tamil Nadu, added a sociological dimension by examining how caste, landholding and political power were related, and increasingly diverged, in the village of Sripuram. Sociological accounts of this kind direct attention to the material and political interests, as well as the religious ideas, bound up with marriage within the caste.

Claude Lévi-Strauss, in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949), argued that every marriage rule is simultaneously endogamous and exogamous: forbidding marriage with one category necessarily permits it with another. Robin Fox's *Kinship and Marriage* (1967) developed this into a widely used teaching synthesis, showing how endogamous and exogamous rules interlock to produce the marriage systems of particular societies.

## Forms and Ethnographic Cases

Endogamy takes several characteristic forms. Caste endogamy, the strictest, confines marriage to the caste, as in classical India. Religious endogamy confines it to the faith: traditional Judaism strongly favoured marriage within the community, and in the Jewish diaspora endogamy became a key means of preserving identity across centuries of dispersion. Ethnic endogamy confines marriage to the ethnic group; under apartheid in South Africa, and under anti-miscegenation laws in parts of the United States, it was enforced by the state itself. Occupational endogamy appears where a profession or calling is inherited, and royal endogamy — marriage within a dynasty, sometimes between close kin — has been used by monarchies from ancient Egypt to Habsburg Europe to keep the bloodline and the throne intact.

Each form reveals a different logic. Religious endogamy protects purity of belief; caste endogamy protects rank; ethnic endogamy protects the boundary against outsiders; royal endogamy protects succession. But all share a common function: they use marriage to decide who belongs.

## Key Debates

The central debate concerns what endogamy does. For Dumont, it reproduced hierarchy: endogamy was the social mechanism of a religious order. In more materialist accounts, it reproduced inequality of a more material kind, keeping property and power within the group. Jack Goody, in *The Development of the Family and Marriage in Europe* (1983), added a comparative argument: across much of Eurasia, he argued, strategies of close or in-group marriage — including marriage between cousins — were often ways of keeping property within the family across generations, whereas the medieval Church's prohibitions on kin marriage helped set Europe on a different course. The debate is not only about causes; it is about whether endogamy is fundamentally religious, economic, or political.

A second debate concerns choice and coercion. Endogamy can be experienced as identity and belonging — marrying someone who shares one's language, faith, and way of life — or as control, particularly over women. Feminist anthropologists have shown that endogamous rules often fall most heavily on women, whose marriages are policed as the guardians of the group's boundary, and whose transgressions are punished more severely than men's. The same rule can thus be both a resource and a constraint, depending on whose standpoint is taken.

A third debate concerns change. Across much of the world, endogamous boundaries are weakening: mixed marriages are more common, urban life brings groups into daily contact, and individual choice increasingly competes with group prescription. Yet endogamy also persists and even revives: diaspora communities often renew it as a defence of identity, and class endogamy — marrying within one's educational and economic stratum — remains strong in many societies that have abandoned caste or religious rules.

## Contemporary Relevance

Endogamy matters today because it illuminates how groups survive. In debates over multiculturalism, assimilation, and identity, the question of whom people marry is one of the most sensitive indicators of how boundaries are holding or dissolving. The genetic consequences of strict endogamy have also made it a subject of medical genetics and public health.

Exogamy explains how groups connect; endogamy explains how they endure. Together they describe the double movement of kinship — opening outward through alliance, closing inward through belonging — that structures social life.
