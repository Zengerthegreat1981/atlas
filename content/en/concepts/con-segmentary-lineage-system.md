# Segmentary Lineage System

A central concept developed by E. E. Evans-Pritchard in *The Nuer* (1940), which describes how patrilineal kinship systems organize politics, warfare and peace in the absence of a central state.

## Central Idea

In this system, society is made up of **lineage groups** that expand through reproduction over generations and gradually divide into smaller branches ("segments"), so that each branch shares a common ancestor that is closer to it than to other, more distant branches. The decisive organizing rule is this: when a dispute arises, the two closest branches ally against a third branch that is more distant. Yet those two branches may themselves fight each other when no third party is present. In other words, the degree of alliance and hostility is determined relatively by the distance of kinship from the opponent in each particular situation, rather than by fixed, permanent alliances.

## Political Function

This system replaces the central state in regulating conflict, distributing resources and inheriting property among the Nuer, a pastoral people of South Sudan. There is no permanent chief or central authority. Instead there is a dynamic balance among the competing and allied forces of the branches, depending on the context.

## Impact

The concept became an analytical model that influenced the study of stateless tribal societies around the world (especially in the Middle East and Africa). Later, however, it was reinterpreted by Marshall Sahlins (1961) and criticized, notably by Adam Kuper (1982) and from the situational perspective associated with the Manchester School, with critics charging that it exaggerated structural order and neglected actual conflict and history.

## The Arab-Islamic Heritage

Centuries before Evans-Pritchard, Ibn Khaldun analyzed related dynamics through the concept of *ʿaṣabiyya* in the *Muqaddimah* (1377). He described how kinship bonds among members of a tribe strengthen as their connection to a common ancestor becomes closer, how branches of closely related descent allied against more distant branches, and how strong tribal *ʿaṣabiyya* becomes the foundation for the emergence of states when multiple tribes unite behind a single leadership. He also described how this *ʿaṣabiyya* gradually weakens with urban settlement and luxury. This thesis intersects deeply with the logic of the shifting segmentary lineages in Evans-Pritchard's account, although the historical and methodological context of the two differs.

## Critiques

Later anthropologists argued that the model presents an idealized structural balance that is rarely achieved in practice. Sahlins, in a study of the Tiv and Nuer, interpreted segmentary organization as a means of predatory expansion, and Kuper's critical retrospect questioned how far lineage theory fit the ethnographic record. Anthropologists associated with the Manchester School, such as Max Gluckman, whose 1940 analysis of a social situation in Zululand was a forerunner of the "extended case method," called for tracing actual disputes involving named individuals, to show how people maneuver flexibly between multiple and sometimes contradictory rules rather than mechanically following an abstract kinship scheme.
