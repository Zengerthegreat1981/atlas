# Qualia: The "What-It-Is-Likeness" of Experience

Qualia are the qualitative, subjective properties of conscious experience: "how it seems" to see red, to taste coffee, or to feel pain. The term *qualia* (singular *quale*) is old in logic. Clarence Irving Lewis used it in *Mind and the World Order* (1929) to denote the qualities given in sensation. Around it three main arguments then crystallised: "What is it like to be a bat?" by Thomas Nagel (1974); Frank Jackson's Mary's room in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982); and what David Chalmers called "the hard problem" in his article "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (1995) and his book *The Conscious Mind* (1996). The phrase "what it is like" is Nagel's.

## Echoes of the Question in the Arabic Heritage

The idea has counterparts in Islamic philosophy without bearing the name. Ibn Sina posited the "floating man," who perceives his own self without any external sense; this is an inquiry into the first self-awareness, not into the "how" of sensory experience. In *Hikmat al-Ishraq* (1186), Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi made *al-ʿilm al-ḥuḍūrī* (knowledge by presence, the self's knowledge of itself without the mediation of an image) the foundation of knowledge. This is a form of direct, non-representational self-knowledge, which can be compared with, but is not identical to, the first-person character of experience that qualia concern (see Ziai, *Knowledge and Illumination*, 1990). Closer still is al-Ghazali's remark in *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* (*Deliverance from Error*) that the difference between one who knows the definition and causes of drunkenness and one who is actually drunk resembles the difference between knowing about mystical states and experiencing them; for him *dhawq* (tasting) is a knowledge that descriptions cannot convey. This is a thematic counterpart, not an influence, to Jackson's story of Mary: a colour scientist who has lived in a black-and-white room and knows all the physical facts about colour. When she sees red for the first time, does she learn something?

## The Philosophical Meaning

Qualia are the qualitative dimension that cannot be reduced to physical or behavioural information. Nagel concluded that objective explanation cannot tell us how experience appears from the standpoint of the organism itself. Joseph Levine (1983) expressed this as the "explanatory gap" between a brain description and the how of experience. Chalmers supported property dualism or similar hypotheses. Daniel Dennett, in "Quining Qualia" (1988), denied that anything with these properties exists, and regarded the conception as built on illusions about introspection. Jackson himself later rejected the conclusion of his argument (see "Mind and Illusion," 2003).

## The Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

Qualia are a topic in philosophy of mind and not a clinical construct. A loose parallel is sometimes drawn with the "felt sense" in Eugene Gendlin's focusing approach (*Focusing*, 1978), the vague bodily sense of a situation that precedes words, and with phenomenological work on lived experience in psychology; these are analogies and not applications of the qualia debate.

## Metacognition and Interoception

**Metacognition** is the monitoring and evaluation of one's own cognition, studied for example by asking how well confidence ratings distinguish correct from incorrect judgments (Fleming and Lau, 2014). It bears on qualia because evidence about experience arrives through reports, and these depend on self-monitoring. Higher-order theories, such as David Rosenthal's, go further and hold that a state is conscious when one is suitably aware of being in it. Opponents reply that experience may outrun what a subject can judge or report, and confidence measures alone do not decide between these views.

**Interoception** is the sensing of the body's internal condition. A. D. Craig (2002) proposed pathways, with an important role for the insular cortex, underlying the felt state of the body, and later theorists, Anil Seth among them, link emotional experience to the brain's predictions about bodily signals. Sarah Garfinkel and colleagues (2015) distinguished interoceptive accuracy (performance on an objective task), sensibility (self-reported attentiveness to the body) and awareness (how well confidence tracks accuracy), and showed that these can come apart. The distinction makes some questions about felt bodily states testable. It measures the relation between signals, performance and judgment, and leaves open why any of this is accompanied by experience.

## The Difference from Neighbouring Concepts

Qualia differ from physical properties: a light wave about 700 nanometres long is an objective property, whereas "redness" as lived is a subjective experience. They differ from Locke's "primary and secondary qualities," because they concern experience rather than the properties of objects; and they differ from intentionality, because a quality may exist without an intended object.

## Limits of the Concept

Reductive physicalists refuse to treat qualia as a phenomenon requiring a special explanation, and regard Mary's argument as a manipulation of the concept of "knowledge." Likewise, the experimental verification of others' experiences is difficult, because reports of them are verbal.
