# Society: The Web of Relations That Outlives Its Members

**Society** is the largest and most inclusive form of human association — the web of relationships, institutions, norms, and shared meanings within which people live their lives. It is more than a collection of individuals: it has a structure and a history that outlive any single person, and it shapes the people who live in it even as they remake it. Society is the central concept of sociology, and also its most contested, because every theory of society carries a view of what holds human beings together and what pulls them apart.

## Definition and Development

Ferdinand Tönnies, in *Community and Society* (1887; English edition 1957), drew the distinction that has framed the concept ever since. *Gemeinschaft*, community, is association based on natural will — kinship, neighbourhood, shared custom — where people belong by birth and feeling. *Gesellschaft*, society, is association based on rational will — contract, exchange, and the pursuit of self-interest — where people relate as strangers bound by agreement. Tönnies saw modernity as a movement from community to society, and he mourned what was lost: the warmth, the certainty, and the solidarity of the older form.

Émile Durkheim, in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893), gave a different account of the same transition. Traditional societies are held together by mechanical solidarity — likeness, shared belief, and collective ritual. Modern societies are held together by organic solidarity — interdependence through the division of labour, where people who are very different from one another depend on each other's specialized work. For Durkheim, modern society was not a loss of solidarity but a new kind of solidarity, based on difference rather than likeness.

## Key Contributions: Structure and Agency

Talcott Parsons, in *The Social System* (1951), treated society as a system of interlocking institutions — family, economy, polity, religion — each performing functions that keep the whole working. His account stressed integration: norms are internalized through socialization, and institutions fit together into a stable order. Parsons's society is a system that tends toward equilibrium, and his critics would later attack exactly that image of harmony.

Anthony Giddens, in *The Constitution of Society* (1984), offered an influential alternative. Society, for Giddens, is not a thing standing over individuals; it is produced and reproduced by what people do. His theory of structuration holds that structure — rules and resources — both constrains action and is the product of action: every time people follow a rule, they keep the rule alive, and every time they break or reinterpret it, they change it. Society is therefore neither a machine nor a mere aggregate of individuals but an ongoing process of structuration, stretched across time and space.

## Examples

Society is visible in its institutions. A court, a school, a market, and a hospital are not just buildings; they are organized practices that persist across generations, staffed by different people over time but recognizable as the same institution. A language is a social fact of this kind: no single person invented it, no single person can change it at will, yet it changes constantly through use. A national holiday, a census, a tax system — each is a piece of the web that makes a society a society rather than a crowd.

Societies also differ in their boundaries. A nation-state is the most familiar form, but societies have also been empires, city-states, tribal confederations, and now, arguably, a global society linked by trade, media, and migration. The question of where one society ends and another begins is itself a sociological question, and globalization has made it harder to answer.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

The concept of society has been criticized from several directions. Methodological individualists argue that only individuals are real, and that society is a convenient fiction. Postmodern critics argue that the idea of a bounded, coherent society no longer fits a world of flows, networks, and diasporas. Anthropologists have pointed out that the concept was built from the European experience of the nation-state and fits poorly the societies of the past and of other parts of the world.

Yet the concept endures because it names something real: the fact that human life is lived inside structures that precede us and will survive us. It now frames research on global society — climate change, pandemics, and digital platforms that connect people across borders — and on the fragmentation of national societies into groups that share less and less. The question Tönnies and Durkheim posed — what holds us together? — remains the question sociology exists to answer.

## Society and the Digital Public Sphere

Jürgen Habermas's *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (German 1962; English 1989) described an arena, historically restricted in membership, where private people debated common affairs and formed public opinion. Digital networks have reopened the question of where such an arena lies. Manuel Castells argued that a network society organises power and identity around flows of information, and Yochai Benkler stressed that cheap networked production allows many more people to publish and cooperate outside markets and states. Others are more cautious: Zizi Papacharissi (2002) noted that new spaces for talk do not by themselves create a public sphere, and Cass Sunstein warned that personalised filtering can sort audiences into like-minded groups. Research has since disputed how far such sorting occurs. Platform ownership and ranking systems also shape what becomes visible.

## Society in Post-Colonial Contexts

Colonial rule often drew borders and legal categories that did not match earlier social ties. Peter Ekeh (1975) argued that colonialism in Africa produced two publics with different moral expectations, one tied to primordial communities and one to the state. Mahmood Mamdani's *Citizen and Subject* (1996) described a divided rule of urban civil law and rural customary authority whose legacy outlasted independence. Partha Chatterjee, writing from India, argued that many people deal with the state less as rights-bearing members of civil society than as governed population groups negotiating for entitlements.
