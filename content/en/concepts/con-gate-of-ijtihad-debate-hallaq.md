# The "Closing of the Gate of Ijtihad" Debate

An academic debate central to contemporary studies of usul al-fiqh. **Wael Hallaq** addressed it in his 1984 article "Was the Gate of Ijtihad Closed?", around a question that is both historical and theoretical: **did independent jurisprudential ijtihad actually cease at a specific historical stage, or is this an orientalist assumption not supported by sufficient textual evidence?**

## The traditional orientalist thesis

A view prevailed in much Western scholarship, associated with figures such as Snouck Hurgronje and Joseph Schacht, and later adopted by some Muslim writers: that **"the gate of ijtihad" was closed** around the early fourth century AH (tenth century CE). By then the four Sunni schools of law (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali) had taken shape, and later jurisprudential development became limited to **taqlid** (following the opinions of earlier imams) rather than independent deduction from the original texts. This thesis was sometimes used to explain what was regarded as "rigidity" or "decline" in later Islamic jurisprudential thought.

## Hallaq's critique: no textual evidence of an official closure

Hallaq challenges this thesis by examining usul and juristic texts from later centuries, well beyond the fourth century AH. They show prominent jurists, among them Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728/1328), al-Suyuti (d. 911/1505) and al-Shawkani (d. 1250/1834), practicing ijtihad or asserting the continuing duty and possibility of it. According to Hallaq, there is no **documented decree or consensus** that officially closed the gate at any specific moment. He argues that the idea reflects a doctrinal discussion among some jurists about how demanding the qualifications for absolute ijtihad are, not a historical fact that independent legal reasoning ceased. He does not deny that most jurists after the formation of the schools worked within a madhhab (ijtihad within the school's framework, alongside taqlid).

## Methodological importance

The debate goes beyond its specific historical question to raise a broader methodological lesson in the comparative study of legal theory. Entrenched narratives about the "rigidity" of non-Western legal traditions, sometimes paired with a portrayal of Western law as perpetually evolving and vital, may reflect **preconceived assumptions more than textual evidence**. This is a methodological warning that applies to any approach to studying an "other" legal tradition from an external perspective.
