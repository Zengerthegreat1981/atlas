# Segregation

Segregation is the spatial and social separation of groups, most commonly along lines of race, ethnicity, religion, or class, enforced by law, custom, economic pressure, or violence. It shapes where people live, where their children go to school, whom they marry, and what opportunities are open to them. For anthropology and sociology, segregation is a key concept because it shows how inequality is organised in space and in everyday life — not only as an abstract structure, but as a lived experience of boundaries, distance, and exclusion.

## Origins and Development

The systematic study of segregation began in the United States, where W. E. B. Du Bois documented the separation of Black and white life in Philadelphia in *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899), one of the first empirical sociological studies. Du Bois showed that segregation was not a natural outcome of group preference but a product of discrimination in housing, employment, and public life. Gunnar Myrdal, in *An American Dilemma* (1944), extended this analysis, arguing that segregation contradicted America's own democratic ideals and was sustained by a vicious circle of prejudice and disadvantage.

In the mid-twentieth century, segregation was most visibly associated with the Jim Crow laws of the American South and with apartheid in South Africa, both systems of legally enforced racial separation. But social scientists also analysed segregation that no law required. Thomas Schelling, in "Dynamic Models of Segregation" (1971), showed through a simple model that even mild preferences for living among people like oneself could produce sharply segregated neighbourhoods — a finding that complicated the assumption that segregation must result from overt prejudice or deliberate policy. Segregation, in this view, can emerge from the accumulation of many small individual choices as well as from state action.

## Segregation in the American City

The most influential account of urban segregation is Douglas Massey and Nancy Denton's *American Apartheid* (1993). Massey and Denton argued that residential segregation is the linchpin of racial inequality in the United States: because schools, jobs, services, and political influence are organised by neighbourhood, concentrating Black Americans in poor, segregated areas multiplies every other disadvantage. They traced this concentration to deliberate policies — redlining by banks, restrictive covenants, public housing placement, and white flight — rather than to the choices of Black residents themselves. Their work reframed segregation as a structural mechanism, actively produced and maintained, and it shaped decades of research on neighbourhood effects.

William Julius Wilson, in *The Truly Disadvantaged* (1987), added a class dimension, showing how deindustrialisation and the departure of middle-class residents left poor Black neighbourhoods isolated from jobs and from the social networks through which work is found. Segregation, in Wilson's account, is not only about race but about the intersection of race and class, and about the loss of the institutions — churches, businesses, community organisations — that once buffered poor communities against hardship.

## Dimensions and Debates

Segregation is measured along several dimensions: evenness, the distribution of groups across neighbourhoods; exposure, the likelihood of contact between groups; concentration, clustering, and centralisation. Different dimensions can move in different directions — a city can become less evenly segregated while remaining highly unequal in exposure — so careful measurement matters for both research and policy.

A central debate concerns the causes of segregation and, therefore, the remedies. If segregation results mainly from discrimination, then anti-discrimination law and fair housing enforcement are the answer. If it results from income differences, then the answer is economic. If it results from preference, then it may be seen as a matter of choice. Most scholars now argue that all three operate together, and that preferences themselves are shaped by the history of discrimination and by the social meanings attached to neighbourhoods. Another debate concerns the consequences of desegregation: whether policies such as school busing, housing vouchers, and mixed-income development genuinely improve the lives of the disadvantaged, or whether they disperse communities and destroy the social ties that sustained them.

## Contemporary Relevance

Segregation remains a central concern in the twenty-first century, in forms both old and new. Residential segregation by race persists in American cities and in European cities, where immigrant communities are concentrated in particular districts. Schools are in many places more segregated now than they were a generation ago. Segregation also appears in new domains: in the digital world, where algorithms sort people into separate information environments; in gated communities and private developments; and in the segregation of the elderly, the disabled, and the poor in institutions and housing estates. Anthropologists document how people experience these boundaries in everyday life — how they navigate them, cross them, and sometimes resist them — and how segregation shapes identity, belonging, and the possibility of a shared public life.
