# Sampling: Selecting Cases to Represent a Population

**Sampling** is the process of selecting a subset of cases — people, households, organizations, or events — from a larger population in order to study the population as a whole without examining every member of it. A good sample allows a researcher to describe a population and test explanations about it from a manageable number of observations; a bad sample produces findings that describe only the people who happened to be studied. Sampling is therefore the foundation of survey research and of much quantitative sociology: every conclusion drawn from a survey depends on how the sample was chosen.

## Definition and Types

The central distinction is between **probability sampling**, in which every member of the population has a known, non-zero chance of selection, and **non-probability sampling**, in which selection depends on the researcher's judgement or on convenience. Only probability samples allow the researcher to calculate sampling error and to generalize statistically to the population; non-probability samples can still be useful for exploration, but their findings cannot be generalized in the same way.

The basic form of probability sampling is the **simple random sample**, in which each member has an equal chance of selection. A **stratified sample** first divides the population into groups — by age, region, or occupation — and then samples randomly within each group, guaranteeing that small but important groups are represented. A **cluster sample** selects whole groups, such as schools or neighbourhoods, and then studies everyone or a random selection within them; it is cheaper when the population is spread over a large area, though it is less precise. A **systematic sample** selects every *n*th member from a list, which is simple in practice but risky if the list has a hidden pattern. Non-probability forms include convenience samples, quota samples, and snowball samples, in which existing respondents recruit further ones — a method often used for hidden populations such as undocumented migrants or drug users.

## Key Contributions

**Leslie Kish** (1910–2000) transformed survey sampling from a craft into a science in *Survey Sampling* (1965). Working at the University of Michigan's Survey Research Center, Kish systematized the design of complex samples — stratification, clustering, and multistage selection — and showed how to calculate their precision. His concept of the design effect measures how much less precise a clustered sample is than a simple random sample of the same size, and his book remains the standard reference for survey designers.

**William G. Cochran** (1909–1980) provided the mathematical foundations in *Sampling Techniques* (first edition 1953; third edition 1977), covering estimation, variance, and the treatment of non-response. Cochran's work connected sampling theory to the practical problems of real surveys: how to handle populations that change, frames that are incomplete, and respondents who refuse to answer. Together, Kish and Cochran established the principle that a sample's value depends not on its size alone but on its design.

**Graham Kalton** clarified the practice of survey sampling in *Introduction to Survey Sampling* (1983), a concise guide to choosing between designs, weighting results, and coping with the imperfections of real sampling frames. Kalton's work is valued for showing how theoretical principles translate into decisions a researcher must actually make — what to do when no complete list of the population exists, or when some groups respond at much lower rates than others.

**Earl Babbie** (born 1938) brought sampling to generations of students in *The Practice of Social Research* (first edition 1975), presenting it as part of the logic of scientific inquiry rather than as a technical specialty. Babbie emphasized the link between sampling and validity: a study that samples poorly cannot support its conclusions, however sophisticated its statistics. His textbook made the distinction between probability and non-probability sampling, and the idea of representativeness, part of every sociologist's basic vocabulary.

## Examples

Sampling shapes the findings sociologists report. A national opinion poll that samples only landline telephones will over-represent older people and under-represent the young; a study of job satisfaction that samples only one company describes that company, not the labour market. Stratified sampling is used to ensure that small minorities appear in sufficient numbers for analysis, while cluster sampling makes it feasible to study schoolchildren across a whole country. In qualitative research, purposive sampling selects cases for their theoretical interest — a striking case, a deviant case, a typical case — rather than for statistical representativeness, a different logic with different aims.

Non-response is the persistent problem of sampling. When the people who refuse to answer differ systematically from those who do — the wealthy refusing income questions, the distrustful refusing political ones — even a perfectly designed sample becomes biased in practice. Modern surveys combat this with callbacks, incentives, and weighting, but the problem cannot be eliminated entirely.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics note that sampling assumes a stable, listable population, which fits poorly with mobile, transnational, and online lives. Random-digit dialling once solved the problem of reaching households; mobile phones, caller ID, and falling response rates have undermined it. Online panels and social-media data offer new sources, but they raise the same old question in a new form: whom do these cases represent? The concept of sampling remains indispensable, but its practice is being reinvented for a world in which populations are harder to define and to reach — and in which the claim to represent a population is itself a political act.
