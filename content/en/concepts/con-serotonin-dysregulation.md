# Serotonin Dysregulation

A term referring to the hypothesis of a dysfunction in the activity or concentration of the neurotransmitter **serotonin** (5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-HT) in the brain, and its supposed link to psychiatric disorders, most notably depression. Popularly known as the "chemical imbalance hypothesis," it has been one of the most common explanations of depression in medical and popular discourse since the 1960s, but it has come under fundamental critical review over the last two decades.

## Origins of the Hypothesis

The "monoamine hypothesis" of depression emerged in the 1960s, based on indirect clinical observations. Notably, reserpine, which depletes biogenic amines (including serotonin and noradrenaline), sometimes caused depressive symptoms in some patients. Meanwhile, drugs that increase the availability of these transmitters (such as tricyclic antidepressants and monoamine oxidase inhibitors) showed an antidepressant effect. From these observations the hypothesis was formulated that depression results from a deficit in serotonin or noradrenaline activity at synapses.

## Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs)

A family of drugs, the **selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors** (SSRIs), was built on this hypothesis. Fluoxetine (Prozac), approved in the United States in 1987, was among the first to be widely marketed. SSRIs later became the first-line treatment most widely prescribed for depression and anxiety worldwide. They work by inhibiting the serotonin reuptake transporter (SERT), thereby increasing serotonin's availability in the synapse. Their efficacy has been documented in many randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses (for example, Cipriani and colleagues, 2018, found all 21 antidepressants studied to be more effective than placebo in acute treatment, with small to moderate average effect sizes). How their clinical effect arises is not settled: the pharmacological action on SERT is rapid, while clinical improvement typically takes weeks, so downstream mechanisms such as neuroplasticity have been proposed without a decisive consensus.

## The Contemporary Critical Review

Joanna Moncrieff and colleagues published in 2022, in *Molecular Psychiatry*, a comprehensive umbrella review of dozens of previous studies across several fields (serotonin concentration and its metabolites, receptor sensitivity, serotonin transporter genes, and tryptophan depletion studies). They concluded that there is **no consistent evidence** that depression is associated with reduced serotonin activity or concentration compared with healthy people. The review received wide media coverage but was also met with methodological criticism from other researchers (for example, Jauhar and colleagues, 2023), who argued that the absence of decisive evidence for the hypothesis does not necessarily mean denying any role for serotonin in mood regulation, and that the clinical effectiveness of SSRIs is documented regardless of the accuracy of the theorized mechanism of their action.

## Current Status

The current scientific consensus tends to regard the "chemical imbalance hypothesis" as an oversimplification that contemporary understanding has moved beyond. This understanding views depression as a multifactorial condition involving genetic, environmental, and inflammatory factors, and neuroplasticity, not a simple chemical imbalance in a single transmitter. Nevertheless, serotonin remains an active research subject, and it still constitutes a clinically effective drug target, even while its precise causal mechanism is not fully understood.
