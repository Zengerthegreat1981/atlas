# Oral Tradition

Oral tradition is the transmission of knowledge, history, law, and literature by word of mouth, from one generation to the next, without the aid of writing. It includes epic poems and folk tales, genealogies and origin myths, proverbs and riddles, songs and praise poetry, and the memorised precedents on which customary law rests. For most of human history, and in many societies today, oral tradition is not a poor substitute for writing but the primary medium of culture — the way a society remembers who it is, what it knows, and what it values.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish oral tradition from orality in general. Orality is the condition of a society without writing; oral tradition is the deliberate, structured transmission of valued knowledge within it. A story told at bedtime is oral, but a genealogy recited at a chief's installation, an epic sung over several nights by a trained bard, or a legal precedent remembered by elders is oral tradition: it is formal, it is performed, and its accuracy matters to the community.

Oral traditions are not fixed texts. Each performance is a new creation, shaped by the audience, the occasion, and the skill of the performer. A singer of tales does not memorise a poem word for word; as Albert Lord showed in *The Singer of Tales* (1960), the singer learns a stock of formulas, themes, and story patterns and composes the epic afresh in performance. This is why the same epic can be different every time it is sung, and why oral tradition is at once remarkably stable — the story survives for centuries — and endlessly variable.

## Origins and Development

The study of oral tradition was transformed in the 1930s by Milman Parry and Albert Lord, who studied the epic singers of Yugoslavia to solve the riddle of Homer. Parry showed that the repeated phrases of the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* were the formulas of an oral composer, and Lord's *The Singer of Tales* (1960) demonstrated that oral epic was a living art, not a primitive stage of literature. The Homeric poems, on this view, were not written by a single author but composed in performance by generations of singers.

Jan Vansina's *Oral Tradition as History* (1985) gave oral tradition a new standing as a historical source. Working in Africa, Vansina showed that oral traditions — genealogies, king lists, foundation stories — could be used to reconstruct the past, provided the historian understood how they were transmitted, how they changed, and what their purpose was. A tradition that telescopes several kings into one, or that invents a founding ancestor, is not a failed history but a different kind of history, one that tells us what a society needed to remember.

Ruth Finnegan's *Oral Literature in Africa* (1970) widened the field further, documenting the extraordinary variety of African oral genres — from the praise poetry of the Zulu to the riddles of the Yoruba — and arguing against the idea that oral literature was simple or unsophisticated. Walter Ong, in *Orality and Literacy* (1982), argued that writing changes thought itself, fostering abstract, analytical reasoning, while oral cultures think in formulas, stories, and vivid images.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One major debate concerns reliability. Can oral tradition be trusted as history? Sceptics point to the telescoping of generations, the invention of ancestors, and the reshaping of the past to serve the present. Defenders reply that written history does the same, and that oral traditions carry information — about migrations, droughts, wars, and genealogies — that no document records. The question is not whether oral tradition is true or false, but what kind of truth it carries and how it should be read.

A second debate concerns the effect of writing. Jack Goody, in *The Domestication of the Savage Mind* (1977), argued that literacy transforms society, enabling lists, tables, bureaucracies, and critical thought. Critics replied that this overstates the case: oral societies can be highly critical and analytical, and literacy does not automatically produce democracy or science. The debate continues, but it has made anthropologists more careful about attributing to writing what may be due to schooling, the state, or the market.

A third dimension is performance. Oral tradition is not only a text but an event: the voice, the gesture, the music, the audience, the night. Recent work has emphasised the aesthetics of performance — the beauty of a well-turned proverb, the power of a praise singer's voice — and the politics of who is allowed to perform, who is allowed to listen, and whose version of the past is heard.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Oral tradition is not a relic. It survives in the storytelling of diaspora communities, in the hip-hop and spoken-word scenes of the world's cities, in the oral histories collected by museums and truth commissions, and in the testimony of elders in land claims and court cases, where oral tradition is now recognised as legal evidence in countries such as Canada and Australia. The recording of oral traditions — once an act of salvage by outside scholars — is increasingly carried out by the communities themselves, on phones and video, as an act of cultural survival.

The critique of the field has also been important. Early collectors often treated oral traditions as the property of a timeless folk, ignoring the individual genius of performers and the political uses of tradition. Contemporary anthropology treats oral tradition as a living, contested practice — a way of making the past present, and of arguing about what the past means.
