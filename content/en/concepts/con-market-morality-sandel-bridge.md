# Markets and Morality: What Money Can't Buy

Welfare economics commonly assesses the extension of market mechanisms into a new domain largely in terms of efficiency: does total surplus (including **consumer surplus**) increase, that is, does the good reach those who value it most, regardless of the nature of the good itself? The philosopher Michael Sandel, in his book *What Money Can't Buy: The Moral Limits of Markets* (2012), challenged the adequacy of this criterion alone. He proposed that some goods have a moral or social character that changes fundamentally when they are commodified, regardless of the efficiency of the resulting exchange.

## Two Arguments Against Commodification, Not One

Sandel distinguishes carefully between two types of objection to expanding markets, which are often confused in public debate. The first is the **Fairness Objection**, which holds that exchange under conditions of extreme poverty is not truly free exchange but a disguised form of coercion (such as selling a human organ under urgent need). Many economists themselves accept this argument without breaking from market logic. The second argument, more profound philosophically and more controversial, is the **Corruption Objection**: some goods and practices carry an inherent social or moral meaning that is damaged or corrupted as soon as the logic of price enters them, regardless of how free and voluntary the parties are. When a child is paid money to read a book, the number of books read may increase (an apparent efficiency), but the intrinsic motivation to read (love of knowledge for its own sake) may decline or be "crowded out" by an external monetary incentive. This is a corruption in the social meaning of the act itself, not in the fairness of the exchange.

## Sandel's Classic Examples

Sandel surveys many examples, including paying for places in line (which turns a shared allocation by queue into a paid privilege, as with scalped tickets to free public performances); hiring others to apologize on one's behalf (a practice he describes in China, which empties the apology of its meaning as an expression of remorse); and life insurance policies on strangers' lives, which turn death into an occasion for financial speculation. He also discusses cash incentives for children to read books or earn good grades. In each example, Sandel does not necessarily object to the economic "efficiency" of the arrangement; he objects to the market imposing a single conception (the value of a good is purely exchange value) on practices that carried a substantively different meaning (a common right, honest expression, social solidarity).

## The Bridge with Public Policy Economics

This framework offers a critical tool for evaluating common economic proposals, such as tradable pollution permits, paying students for grades, or regulated markets for human organs. It prompts policymakers to ask, before applying any market solution: what is the original social meaning of this domain, and would that meaning be harmed if it were subjected to the logic of price, regardless of potential efficiency gains? This does not mean rejecting the market absolutely, but deliberately defining its moral limits, rather than automatically extending price logic to every domain on the sole ground of efficiency.

## Critical Reading

Sandel faces criticism from economists who argue that the "corruption" argument is empirically vague, that it is difficult to measure when "crowding out of intrinsic motivation" actually occurs and when it does not, and that many sensitive moral markets (such as blood donation versus paid selling, studied by Richard Titmuss in *The Gift Relationship*, 1970) produce mixed results depending on context. Empirical work on the "crowding out" of intrinsic motivation by incentives (for example Gneezy and Rustichini's study of fines at day-care centres, 2000) supports the concern in some settings, but the evidence is not uniform. This makes the general moral judgment harder to apply than the case-by-case study.
