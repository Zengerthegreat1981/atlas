# Personal Status Law in Arab States

**Personal status law** is the common name in the Arab states for the legislation regulating marriage, divorce, descent, custody, maintenance, inheritance and bequest. It is often the only, or the main, branch of law for which the Arab states have continued to derive their rulings directly from classical Islamic jurisprudence, even after replacing the other branches of law (civil, commercial and criminal) with laws inspired by modern European models.

## Historical Origins

The codification of personal status in the Arab world began with individual, informal attempts, such as Muhammad Qadri Pasha's *al-Ahkam al-Shar'iyya fi al-Ahwal al-Shakhsiyya* in Egypt in the late nineteenth century, before moving to the stage of official, binding legislation with the issuance of the Egyptian Personal Status Law (Law No. 25 of 1920) and its supplement of 1929 (Law No. 25 of 1929), which is regarded as an early model that was later imitated in other Arab states with local differences in details. These laws generally followed a method based on selecting rulings from multiple juristic schools (rather than limiting themselves to one official school), according to what the legislator considered most suitable for the conditions of the age, a practice that jurists call "combining the schools" (*talfiq bayn al-madhahib*).

## Common Features Across Arab States

Despite detailed differences among the legislation of the various Arab states, these laws share a number of general features: they retain the general framework of the traditional jurisprudential inheritance rules (the distribution of the prescribed shares); they regulate the conditions of marriage, its impediments and its effects in the traditional jurisprudential pattern, with some modern procedural restrictions (such as the formal documentation of the marriage contract); and they regulate the right to divorce and judicial separation, generally including modern mechanisms such as judicial *khul'*, which some Arab states introduced (such as Egypt in 2000, by Law No. 1 of 2000) drawing on classical juristic discussions of separation in exchange for consideration.

## National Differences

The statutes diverge sharply on particular points. Tunisia's code of 1956 abolished polygamy and made divorce effective only through the courts. Syria (1953) and Iraq (1959) instead subjected polygamy to judicial permission, while many other states continue to allow it with fewer controls. Minimum marriage ages and the scope for judicial exceptions vary, as do the guardian's role in an adult woman's marriage and the rules on custody. Lebanon has no single code for all citizens: religious communities apply their own laws through their own courts. Kuwait legislated in 1984, the United Arab Emirates in 2005, Qatar in 2006, and Saudi Arabia in 2022.

## Amendments over the Decades

Egypt revised its laws of the 1920s by decree in 1979; after the Supreme Constitutional Court struck that decree down in 1985, Law No. 100 of that year re-enacted much of it, followed by the reform of 2000 and the creation of family courts in 2004. Morocco's code of 1957-1958 was amended in 1993 and replaced in 2004 by a Family Code that placed the family under the joint responsibility of both spouses and tightened conditions for polygamy. Algeria amended its 1984 Family Code in 2005. Jordan replaced its 1951 law in 1976, issued a new temporary law in 2010 and a permanent one in 2019. Syria made notable amendments in 1975 and 2019. Iraq amended its 1959 law several times, including in 2025 to permit recourse to sect-based rules.

## Tension Between Jurisprudential Authenticity and Social Reform

Personal status law constitutes a major arena of continuing tension between preserving traditional jurisprudential reference and responding to contemporary social reform demands concerning women's rights and equality. Many Arab states witness ongoing debates about amending provisions such as polygamy, the conditions of unilateral divorce by the husband, and child custody after divorce, amid attempts to offer contemporary interpretive readings that take the general objectives of the Sharia into account without a complete break with the traditional jurisprudential framework.
