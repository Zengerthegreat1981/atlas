# Supererogation

## Meaning and Definition

Supererogation is a good act that goes beyond what the agent is required to do. It is praiseworthy if performed, but its omission is not blamed. This differs from obligatory acts, whose omission is blamed and whose performance is rewarded. The difficulty of the concept lies in explaining how an act can be ethically good without being obligatory. Some rigorous consequentialist theories deny this possibility, obliging the agent always to perform the best possible act.

## The Jurisprudential Counterpart: Voluntary Acts

In Islamic jurisprudence this concept corresponds closely to the category of recommended acts (*mandub*, also called *mustahabb*, *sunna* or *nafl*/*nawafil* in various schools and contexts), one of the five legal rulings (obligatory, recommended, permissible, disliked, forbidden). Its performer is rewarded, and its omitter does not sin. Examples include voluntary prayers, charity beyond the obligatory zakat, and voluntary fasting. The category is distinct from obligations, which jurists themselves divided into individual obligation (*fard 'ayn*) and communal obligation (*fard al-kifaya*). The analogy is not exact, since the recommended act is graded by the Lawgiver's encouragement rather than by the agent's sacrifice. Ibn al-Qayyim, in *Madarij al-Salikin*, discusses excellence (*ihsan*), which the Prophet described as worshipping God as though you see Him, as the highest of the spiritual stations. It can be read as going beyond the minimum of obedience. Islam did not leave this category devoid of importance. It encouraged it strongly and promised great reward for it, while explicitly maintaining the essential distinction between it and the obligatory.

## Western Philosophy

J. O. Urmson brought the concept into modern analytic philosophy in his essay "Saints and Heroes" (1958). He argued that the traditional threefold classification of acts as obligatory, permissible or forbidden leaves no room for saints and heroes, and that theories which make the best available act a duty for everyone leave no legitimate space for voluntary good action. The concept raises a deep philosophical problem: if a given act is purely good, why is it not obligatory? David Heyd, in *Supererogation: Its Status in Ethical Theory* (1982), defended a systematic treatment of the notion, arguing that ethics needs a middle zone between binding obligation and neutral permissibility. Such a zone preserves for the agent the freedom of moral excellence without burdening him with endless demands.

Urmson's discussion is organized around two figures. The saint does what is beyond duty, at cost to himself, over a sustained course of life; his example of a saintly act is a doctor who volunteers to stay and treat victims of a plague when he could leave without blame. The hero acts at a decisive moment in the face of grave danger, as a soldier who throws himself on a grenade to save his comrades. Both exceed the ordinary limit of duty in different ways: the first through long steadfastness and the second through momentary boldness. Both are widely honored in religious and secular moral traditions alike.

## Illustrative Example

Donating a kidney to a stranger is commonly cited as a supererogatory act: it is praised in the highest terms, yet the failure of any other person to do so is generally not regarded as a moral failing that deserves blame. Some philosophers dispute such classifications.
