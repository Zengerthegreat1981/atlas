# Psychoanalytic Theory

**Psychoanalytic Theory** is the theoretical system founded by **Sigmund Freud** in Vienna beginning in the 1890s (with Josef Breuer's collaboration in *Studies on Hysteria*, 1895) to explain mental life. It holds that a large part of human motivation and behavior is governed by **unconscious** processes that the individual cannot reach directly, and that conflict among these internal forces is the origin of psychological symptoms.

## Core Foundations

### The Unconscious and Psychic Conflict

Freud proposed that the mind is divided into levels: **consciousness**, the **preconscious**, and the **unconscious**, which contains repressed desires, drives, and memories that affect behavior without the individual's awareness. Later Freud developed the **tripartite structural model**: the **Id** (the reservoir of instinctual drives), the **Ego** (the mediator between drives and reality), and the **Superego** (conscience and internalized moral norms). In this framework, psychological illness arises from conflict among these three structures, the intensity of which is managed through defense mechanisms such as repression, projection, and sublimation.

### Psychosexual Development and Transference

Freud presented a theory of psychosexual stages (oral, anal, phallic, latency, and genital), holding that later disorders reflect difficulties or fixations in these early stages. In therapy he focused on the phenomenon of **transference**, in which the patient projects their feelings and early relational patterns onto the therapist, as a window onto unconscious dynamics.

## Later Developments

Several major schools broke away from Freud or developed his theory. **Ego psychology**, with Anna Freud and Heinz Hartmann, focused on the adaptive functions of the ego. **Object relations theory**, with Melanie Klein, Donald Winnicott, and Otto Kernberg, shifted the focus from instinctual drives to early relationships with significant people (objects). **Self psychology**, with Heinz Kohut, focused on the development of the coherence of the self and needs for mirroring and validation. Carl Jung broke with Freud around 1913 and founded **analytical psychology**, with the concepts of the collective unconscious and archetypes.

## Impact

Psychoanalytic theory had an enormous influence beyond psychiatry, extending into literary criticism, the arts, anthropology, and philosophy in the twentieth century. It established the talking cure as a therapeutic model and supplied the basic vocabulary (the unconscious, repression, transference, defenses) that remains present in contemporary clinical practice even outside the strictly analytic tradition.

## Limits

The theory has faced broad methodological critique, beginning with Karl Popper, who regarded it as unfalsifiable and therefore outside the bounds of empirical science in the strict sense. Behavioral and cognitive currents saw in it retrospective explanations that are difficult to test experimentally. It has also faced feminist critique of some of its early concepts (such as penis envy), and critique from contemporary neuroscience regarding the biological validity of the structural model of the psyche. Nevertheless, some of its branches, such as object relations theory, have been more open to clinical and experimental testing than the classical Freudian model.
