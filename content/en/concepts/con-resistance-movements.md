# Resistance Movements

Resistance movements are organised collective efforts to oppose, undermine, or overthrow a dominant power — a colonial regime, an authoritarian state, an occupying army, or an unjust social order. Unlike the wider category of social movements, which may seek reform within a system, resistance movements are defined by their oppositional stance: they confront power that is experienced as domination, and they often operate under conditions of repression where open protest carries severe risk. Their repertoire ranges from armed struggle and mass civil disobedience to strikes, boycotts, and the quiet, everyday acts through which subordinated people refuse compliance.

## Origins and Development

The study of resistance has roots in several traditions. Antonio Gramsci, writing in his *Prison Notebooks* (1929–1935, published posthumously), argued that ruling groups maintain power not only through coercion but through hegemony — the shaping of common sense so that domination appears natural — and that resistance therefore requires a counter-hegemonic struggle over ideas as well as institutions. Frantz Fanon, in *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), analysed anti-colonial resistance as a process through which colonised peoples reclaim their humanity, arguing that decolonisation is necessarily a violent rupture, a claim that remains one of the most debated in the literature.

A decisive shift came with James C. Scott's *Weapons of the Weak* (1985), a study of Malaysian peasants that showed how people who cannot afford open rebellion resist anyway: through foot-dragging, sabotage, gossip, desertion, and other acts that are individually small but collectively consequential. Scott's *Domination and the Arts of Resistance* (1990) generalised the argument, distinguishing the "public transcript" of deference performed before power from the "hidden transcript" of criticism and defiance that circulates out of its sight. Resistance, on this view, is not confined to dramatic uprisings; it is a continuous feature of unequal social life. Gene Sharp's *The Politics of Nonviolent Action* (1973) added a strategic dimension, cataloguing nearly two hundred methods of nonviolent resistance and arguing that even dictators depend on the cooperation of those they rule — cooperation that can be withdrawn.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One central debate concerns definition: how broad should the concept be? Jocelyn Hollander and Rachel Einwohner (2004) noted that scholars use "resistance" to mean everything from armed revolution to wearing a forbidden hairstyle, and warned that if everything counts as resistance, the concept explains nothing. Defenders of the broad view reply that narrowing it to organised, public, and intentional opposition erases the agency of the most subordinated people, whose resistance must by necessity be covert. A second debate concerns the role of violence. Fanon's defence of anti-colonial violence and Sharp's case for nonviolent strategy represent opposing poles, while many movements have combined both, and historians continue to argue about which approach has been more effective in different conditions.

A third dimension is the relationship between resistance and power. Michel Foucault's argument that power and resistance are co-constitutive — that resistance arises within relations of power rather than outside them — challenged romantic images of a pure opposition standing apart from domination. Anthropologists have added that resistance movements are also shaped by the cultures they emerge from: they draw on religious idioms, kinship networks, and local histories, and their goals are not always the liberal freedoms assumed by outside observers. Some movements resist modernity itself, defending ways of life that modernisation threatens.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Resistance movements remain a central object of study in anthropology, political science, and history, from the anti-apartheid struggle and the civil rights movement to the Arab uprisings, the Hong Kong protests, and indigenous land defence movements. The digital age has transformed their organisation: encrypted messaging, social media, and diaspora networks allow movements to coordinate under repression, though the same technologies enable surveillance and infiltration. Scholars also study how states learn to anticipate and absorb resistance, and how movements themselves can reproduce hierarchies of gender or class even as they challenge domination from above.

Critics caution against romanticising resistance. Not all opposition to power is emancipatory: movements can be authoritarian, exclusionary, or violent toward other subordinated groups. And the focus on resistance can obscure the many people who accommodate domination, not out of false consciousness but because survival requires it. The concept's value lies in its insistence that domination is never complete — that even under the harshest regimes, people find ways to say no, and that those refusals, organised and sustained, are among the forces that change history.
