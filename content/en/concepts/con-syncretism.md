# Syncretism

Syncretism is the blending of different religious or cultural traditions into a new whole. When enslaved Africans in the Caribbean prayed to Catholic saints who doubled as Yoruba orishas, when Japanese villagers honoured both Shinto kami and Buddhist bodhisattvas at the same shrine, when Mexican Catholics celebrated the Day of the Dead with pre-Hispanic offerings of marigolds and food — in each case, elements from distinct traditions were combined, reinterpreted, and made into something that belonged to neither source alone. Syncretism is one of the oldest facts of religious life, yet the word itself has been controversial: for centuries it was used as an accusation, a charge that a religion had been corrupted by foreign admixture. Anthropologists have worked to turn it from a term of condemnation into an analytical tool for understanding how religions actually live, change, and survive contact with one another.

## Definition

Syncretism describes both a process and a result. As a process, it is the active combining of beliefs, practices, symbols, and institutions from different traditions. As a result, it is the new formation that emerges — a religion or ritual system that cannot be reduced to any one of its sources. Anthropologists distinguish several ways blending happens. Sometimes elements are simply added side by side, as when a community keeps two calendars of festivals. Sometimes one tradition provides the form and another the content, as when African deities were worshipped under the names and images of Catholic saints. Sometimes the blending is creative and deliberate, producing new theologies, new ritual languages, and new identities. Syncretism is also not the same as tolerance or pluralism: blended religions can be fiercely exclusive, and the mixing of traditions can be driven by domination, by survival under persecution, or by the creative agency of believers, as often as by open-mindedness.

## Origins and Development

The term syncretism comes from the Greek *synkretismos*, and was used by Plutarch for the temporary union of quarrelling Cretan communities against a common enemy. In early modern Europe it was revived, notably in Christian theological controversies, as a term for the improper mixing of doctrines. In anthropology, the concept was developed in the study of Afro-American religions. Melville Herskovits, in *The Myth of the Negro Past* (1941), argued against the claim that enslaved Africans had lost their culture, showing that African religious ideas survived in the Americas by blending with Catholicism and Protestantism — in Haitian Vodou, Cuban Santería, and Brazilian Candomblé. Roger Bastide's *Les Religions africaines au Brésil* (1960; translated as *The African Religions of Brazil*, 1978) showed how Yoruba, Fon, and Catholic elements were combined in Candomblé, not as a confused mixture but as a coherent system with its own logic, in which each orisha was paired with a saint and each ritual had its place. Robert F. Thompson's *Flash of the Spirit* (1983) traced African aesthetic and religious ideas across the Atlantic, showing how they were reworked in new contexts. Karen McCarthy Brown's *Mama Lola* (1991), a study of a Vodou priestess in Brooklyn, showed syncretism as a lived, everyday practice: a woman who prayed to Catholic saints, served the lwa, and consulted doctors, moving between traditions as the situation required. The most influential theoretical statement came in *Syncretism/Anti-Syncretism* (1994), edited by Charles Stewart and Rosalind Shaw, which argued that syncretism is inseparable from anti-syncretism — the efforts of religious authorities, reformers, and nationalists to police boundaries and denounce mixture as inauthentic. To call something syncretic, the editors argued, is always a political act.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns who gets to define mixture. Religious elites often denounce blending as corruption, while practitioners experience it as faithful continuity: the Vodou priestess who serves the Virgin Mary does not see herself as mixing two religions but as serving the spirits in the forms available to her. Anthropologists therefore ask whose categories are being used, and with what consequences. A second debate concerns power. Blending is not always a meeting of equals: it can be imposed by conquest, mission, or colonial rule, and the resulting combinations may encode domination — the African deity hidden behind the saint because open worship was forbidden. But blending can also be a form of resistance, a way of preserving forbidden traditions under a permissible surface. A third debate concerns whether syncretism is a useful analytical term at all. Some scholars argue that all religions are syncretic, since every tradition is the product of earlier blendings; others reply that the term remains useful because it draws attention to the politics of mixture — to who calls what syncretic, and why.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Syncretism remains central to understanding religion in a globalised world. Migrant communities blend the religions of home and host country; New Age and neo-pagan movements deliberately combine elements from many traditions; Pentecostal churches in Africa and Latin America incorporate healing practices, music, and spirit idioms from older religions. Critics of the concept warn that it can smuggle in the assumption that there were once pure, unmixed traditions that were later blended — an assumption history does not support. The enduring insight is that religions are not sealed systems but living practices, constantly made and remade through contact, and that the question is never whether blending happens, but how, under what conditions, and in whose interest.
