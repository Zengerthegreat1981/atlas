# Islamic Translation Methodology

The translation of Greek and Syriac works into Arabic in the Abbasid period was not an improvised undertaking. Established practices and conventions took shape, which are apparent from the testimonies of the translators themselves and from the remarks of littérateurs and historians. The clearest of these is the *Epistle of Hunayn ibn Ishaq to Ali ibn Yahya al-Munajjim*, in which he recounts what he translated from the books of Galen into Syriac and Arabic and how he worked on them.

## Two Methods Attributed to the Translators

Al-Safadi (d. 764 AH/1363 CE), in *al-Ghayth al-musajjam*, his commentary on the *Lamiyyat al-'Ajam*, reports a classification of translators. One approach takes each Greek word and sets an Arabic word against it; if it finds no equivalent, it keeps the Greek word. He attributes this to Yuhanna ibn al-Bitriq, Ibn Na'ima al-Himsi and others, and he finds it weak because Arabic syntax does not match Greek, and because figurative language does not pass word by word. The second approach takes the sentence as a whole, grasps its meaning, and expresses that meaning in Arabic. He attributes this to Hunayn ibn Ishaq and to a translator he calls al-Jawhari, and he prefers it. The report is late. The al-Jawhari of the passage is not securely identified, and he should not be confused with the lexicographer of that name. Actual practice was more varied than the two labels suggest.

## Hunayn's Work: Establishing the Text Before Translating

Hunayn's epistle reveals a method of comparing manuscripts. He says that he gathered several Greek copies of a book by Galen in order to compare them and correct the original before translating it, and that he reviewed an earlier Syriac translation before rendering the work into Arabic. He later joined with his son Ishaq and his nephew Hubaysh, and his school became a workshop in which some translated and others corrected. Gotthelf Bergsträsser published and edited this epistle in 1925. It is the principal surviving account, in a translator's own words, of how Galenic books were collated and rendered.

## Terminology

The translators had to create a scientific language. They relied on giving existing Arabic words technical senses, such as *'arad* (accident), and on loanwords that remained Arabized, such as *hayula* (matter, from Greek *hyle*). Where no equivalent existed, they kept a foreign term in Arabized form, as in *falsafa* (philosophy) and *ustuqus* (element). Terminology generally settled only after generations. The equivalents of *jawhar* (substance), *dhat* (essence) and *mahiyya* (quiddity), for example, differed among earlier translators and later philosophers.

## al-Jahiz's Position: The Roots of Skepticism

In *al-Hayawan*, al-Jahiz (d. 255 AH/869 CE) presented a critical view. A translator conveys what is in the original only if he equals its author in knowledge of the subject and in knowledge of the two languages, a condition that is rarely met. For this reason, he feared that books would be distorted by translation, and he held that poetry cannot be translated. The passage is an early Arabic argument for that limit. It is not a theory of translation in the later technical sense.

## Limits of the Method

These conventions should not be overstated as a codified method. They are closer to the experience of a workshop, of which only a few testimonies survive. Knowledge of most of what happened in the translation from Syriac is limited: many Syriac originals have been lost, so comparison is possible only with what has survived. Gutas holds that the choice of books to be translated was not arbitrary, but was determined by the practical and intellectual needs of the Abbasid court and elite, such as administration, medicine and astrology. This explanation remains open to debate.
