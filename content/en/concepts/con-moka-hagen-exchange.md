# Moka Exchange

A complex ceremonial economic system documented among the Hagen people of Mount Hagen in the Western Highlands Province of Papua New Guinea. The moka is a periodic exchange of pigs, shell valuables and decorative items of high value, used to establish **political and social alliances** and to redefine the status of individuals and groups.

## Ethnographic Definition

The moka is not buying or selling but a **periodic ceremonial exchange**:
- **High-quality pigs**, **shell valuables** and **decorative items** of carefully calculated value are exchanged
- The exchange takes place at **public festivities** witnessed by the whole tribe
- A moka gift **obliges the recipient to return a larger moka** after an interval, often of a year or more
- Failure to return = **loss of social standing** and the loss of the tribe's respect

## Difference from the Kula

Unlike the kula ring (which is a **continuous cycle** between islands):
- The moka is a **limited ceremonial event** with clearly defined time periods
- The moka is **more competitive**: men compete over who gives the largest moka
- The moka is a **bilateral chain of gifts and counter-gifts** between partners, rather than a ring circulating between island communities

## Social and Political Importance

The moka is **not purely a material economy** but:
1. **A means of establishing leadership**: the man who owns and gives the most moka (a *Bigman*) becomes a leader
2. **A means of peace and alliance**: the exchange of moka between tribes eases tensions and improves relations
3. **A means of demonstrating standing**: the moka is not a **personal possession** but a **symbol of social power**

## The Different Role of Women

Marilyn Strathern's work in Mount Hagen (notably *Women in Between*, 1972) and Andrew Strathern's studies of the moka show that:
- The production of pigs (which are given in the moka) depends heavily on **women's labor**: women raise and feed the pigs
- The moka **could not happen without women's labor**, yet moka transactions are conducted publicly by men, so women's contribution is easily overlooked
- Women are not simply passive: they influence which exchanges men can undertake, and the question of how much agency they have in moka is a recurring feminist debate

## Strathern's Critique of Western Individualism

Marilyn Strathern used the study of the moka in *The Gender of the Gift* (1988) to offer a broader theoretical critique of the Western concept of the autonomous "individual." In its place she drew on the idea of the **"dividual"** person (a term introduced by McKim Marriott in South Asian studies) to describe Melanesian personhood: a person constituted by multiple relationships with others (such as ancestors and relatives by marriage), with these relationships made visible and activated through exchanges such as moka.

## Broader Impact

The study of the moka, alongside the kula and the potlatch, has become one of the ethnographic examples frequently cited in discussions of Marcel Mauss's theory of the gift. It helped to widen the debate from purely reciprocal exchange to questions of gender, agency and personhood in contemporary anthropology.
