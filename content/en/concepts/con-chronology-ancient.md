# Ancient Chronology

Ancient chronology is the methodological discipline within historical and archaeological research that determines the temporal sequence and calendar dates of events, reigns, and material cultures in ancient civilizations. Because ancient societies lacked a continuous, universally recognized calendar era (such as the modern Gregorian calendar or the Islamic Hijri calendar), establishing a reliable timeline requires coordinating fragmentary textual evidence with astronomical records and physical dating sciences.

## The Central Problem: Relative versus Absolute Dating

Most ancient Near Eastern, Mediterranean, and East Asian civilizations dated events by regnal years—reckoning time as "Year X of King Y's reign." While this system establishes internal sequences, it creates significant challenges for historians:

- **Relative Chronology**: Orders events, strata, and dynasties sequentially (which ruler preceded or followed another) based on king lists, genealogies, and stratigraphic excavation, without tying them to an absolute calendar year.
- **Absolute Chronology**: Tethers a specific historical event or reign to a fixed astronomical or calendar date on the modern Julian or Gregorian scale.

When king lists contain scribal corruptions, omissions of rival claimants, or overlapping co-regencies counted twice, floating chronologies emerge that drift by decades or centuries unless anchored to an astronomical or scientific benchmark.

## Major Regional Frameworks and Anchor Points

Historians establish chronological anchors across the ancient world through distinct regional frameworks:

- **Mesopotamia**: Chronology in the first millennium BCE is securely anchored by the Neo-Assyrian eponym lists (*limmu*), which record a solar eclipse in the month of Simanu during the eponymy of Bur-Sagale, astronomically fixed to 15 June 763 BCE. For the second millennium BCE, chronological debate centers on the Venus Tablet of Ammisaduqa (an Old Babylonian astrological compilation), giving rise to competing High, Middle, and Low chronologies that place the fall of Babylon under the Hittite king Mursili I at 1651, 1595, or 1531 BCE, respectively.
- **Ancient Egypt**: The Egyptian civil calendar (365 days without a leap year) wandered relative to the solar year, producing the Sothic cycle of approximately 1,460 years, marked by the heliacal rising of Sirius (*Sothis*). Recorded Sothic dates (such as those in the Kahun and Ebers papyri), combined with lunar dates and monumental king lists (the Palermo Stone, the Turin Royal Canon, and Manetho's dynastic divisions), provide the framework for Old, Middle, and New Kingdom chronologies.
- **Cross-Civilizational Synchronisms**: Diplomatic archives such as the fourteenth-century BCE Amarna Letters record direct correspondence between Egyptian pharaohs (Amenhotep III, Akhenaten) and monarchs of Babylonia, Assyria, Mittani, and Hatti, allowing chronologies of disparate regions to be cross-calibrated.

## Scientific and Archaeological Methods

Modern historiography supplements textual synchronisms with physical dating methods:

- **Radiocarbon ($^{14}\text{C}$) Dating and Dendrochronology**: Tree-ring sequences (such as those from Anatolia and the Aegean) offer calendar-year calibration curves (IntCal), helping resolve long-standing debates such as the dating of the Thera (Santorini) volcanic eruption.
- **Archaeomagnetism and Stratigraphy**: Shifts in the Earth's magnetic field recorded in fired ceramics and architectural destruction layers can supply supplementary dating evidence for fired structures and destruction layers.
