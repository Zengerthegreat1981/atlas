# Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

## Definition

**Obsessive-compulsive disorder** (OCD) is a psychiatric disorder marked by recurrent **obsessions** and/or **compulsions** that cause marked distress and consume enough time (DSM-5 gives more than one hour per day as an example of time-consuming symptoms) to impair the person's functioning. **Obsessions** are recurrent, unwanted thoughts, urges, or images that intrude on the mind and provoke intense anxiety. **Compulsions** are repetitive behaviors or mental acts that the person feels driven to perform in response to an obsession or according to rigid rules, aiming to reduce anxiety or prevent an imagined catastrophe. This relief is usually temporary and partial.

## Classification and Prevalence

DSM-5 classifies the disorder within a separate category, "Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders," distinct from the anxiety disorders under which it was previously classified. Symptoms are commonly described in several dimensions: **contamination/cleaning**, **symmetry/ordering**, **forbidden thoughts** (aggressive, sexual, or religious), **doubt about harm/checking**, and **hoarding-related symptoms** (hoarding disorder is a separate diagnosis in DSM-5). Lifetime prevalence has been estimated at about 2.3% in the United States (National Comorbidity Survey Replication), with similar or somewhat lower figures elsewhere.

## Neurobiological Basis

Research points to a central role for a neural circuit linking the **orbitofrontal cortex**, the basal ganglia (especially the caudate nucleus), and the thalamus. There is also evidence of an imbalance in the **glutamate and GABA** balance within this circuit, although findings are not conclusive and no single mechanism is established.

## Treatment

- **Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT)**: specifically the technique of **exposure and response prevention** (ERP), in which the patient is gradually exposed to triggers of obsessions while actively refraining from the usual compulsive behavior, so that the feared outcome does not occur and anxiety typically lessens. ERP is the first-line treatment recommended by most clinical guidelines. In a randomized trial, exposure and ritual prevention, clomipramine, and their combination were each superior to placebo (Foa and colleagues, 2005).
- **Drug treatment**: **selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)** are used, with the evidence base showing benefit over placebo. The US Food and Drug Administration has approved five drugs for OCD in adults: sertraline, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, paroxetine, and clomipramine (a tricyclic antidepressant with a strong serotonergic effect).
- **Severe cases**: At a high level of severity (commonly rated with the clinician-administered Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale, Y-BOCS, 10 items, total 0-40), clinicians often consider combining medication and behavioral therapy.

## Legacy

Understanding the neural and behavioral basis of OCD has contributed to the development of treatment protocols (ERP in particular), which are among the most thoroughly documented psychological interventions for any psychiatric disorder. It also informed the DSM-5 classification, which created a separate chapter, "Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders," that includes body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding disorder, trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder), and excoriation (skin-picking) disorder.

## Limitations

A substantial proportion of patients do not achieve a full response to first-line treatment, whether drug or behavioral. For very severe, treatment-resistant cases, neuromodulation such as deep brain stimulation is used in a few specialized centers; this remains a developing research field.
