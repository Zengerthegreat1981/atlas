# Experimental Design in Psychology

An experiment is a study in which the researcher deliberately manipulates one or more variables and observes the effect on another, while trying to hold everything else constant or balanced. Its great strength is that, done well, it supports causal conclusions that observational and correlational research cannot. Much of the craft of experimental design consists of anticipating the alternative explanations a sceptic could offer and arranging the study so that they are ruled out.

## Core elements

The **independent variable** is what the experimenter manipulates; the **dependent variable** is the outcome measured. Conditions differ in the level of the independent variable, commonly a **treatment** group and a **control** group that receives no treatment, a placebo, or a standard alternative. Variables must be given **operational definitions**: concrete procedures for manipulating or measuring abstract concepts such as "stress" or "aggression". How well the operationalisation captures the concept is a question of construct validity.

## Randomisation and control

The key move is **random assignment**: participants are allocated to conditions by chance, so that, on average, pre-existing differences such as age, mood or motivation are balanced across groups. Random assignment is distinct from random sampling, which concerns how participants are drawn from a population and bears on generalisation. Other forms of control include holding procedures constant, standardising instructions, using **blinding** so that participants (single-blind) or both participants and experimenters (double-blind) do not know the condition, and using placebo conditions to separate a specific effect from expectation. Demand characteristics, cues that tell participants what the study is about, and experimenter expectancy effects, as in Robert Rosenthal's work, are classic threats that blinding addresses.

## Between-subjects and within-subjects designs

In a **between-subjects design**, different people experience different conditions. It avoids carry-over but needs more participants and relies on randomisation to equate groups. In a **within-subjects (repeated-measures) design**, each person experiences every condition, which removes individual differences from the comparison and increases statistical power, but invites order effects such as practice and fatigue. These are handled by **counterbalancing**, varying the order across participants. A **mixed design** combines both, and **factorial designs** cross two or more independent variables, allowing researchers to examine interactions, where the effect of one variable depends on the level of another.

## Confounds and threats to validity

A **confound** is a variable that varies together with the independent variable and offers a rival explanation for the result. Campbell and Stanley's classic treatment listed threats to internal validity including history, maturation, testing, instrumentation, regression to the mean, selection, and attrition. Regression to the mean, the tendency of extreme scores to move toward the average on remeasurement, is a notorious source of false conclusions when participants are chosen for extreme scores. External validity threats include unrepresentative samples and artificial laboratory settings. Researchers often face a trade-off: tight control raises internal validity but may reduce realism.

## Quasi-experiments and other designs

When random assignment is impossible or unethical, for example comparing existing classrooms or studying the effects of a natural disaster, researchers use **quasi-experimental designs** such as non-equivalent control groups, interrupted time series, and regression discontinuity. These can give useful evidence but require careful argument that groups were comparable. **Natural experiments** exploit events that approximate random assignment. **Single-case designs**, common in behaviour analysis, repeatedly measure one participant across baseline and intervention phases. Correlational studies, however sophisticated statistically, cannot remove the possibility of unmeasured third variables.

## Analysis and interpretation

Experimental data are typically analysed with tests such as t-tests and analysis of variance, increasingly accompanied by effect sizes and confidence intervals rather than significance values alone. Sample size should be planned in advance through power analysis, since underpowered studies both miss real effects and produce inflated estimates of the effects they do detect. Preregistration, specifying hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection, has become a recommended practice to curb flexible analysis.

## Limits and debates

Experiments are not a universal solution. Laboratory tasks with student participants may say little about behaviour in everyday life, and critics have long questioned the ecological validity of such work. Statistical significance is easily confused with practical importance. Ethical constraints prevent experimental tests of many important questions, such as the effects of severe trauma. Deception, sometimes used to avoid demand characteristics, raises its own ethical issues. The replication crisis showed that even properly randomised experiments can yield unreliable conclusions when sample sizes are small and analytic choices are flexible. Finally, an experiment shows that a manipulation changes an outcome under particular conditions; explaining why requires further work on mechanisms and theory.
