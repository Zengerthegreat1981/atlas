# Cognitive Dissonance

## Definition

Cognitive dissonance is a state of psychological discomfort that arises when a person holds two contradictory beliefs at the same time, or acts in a way that contradicts his beliefs or personal values. This feeling of inconsistency drives him to change one of the two conflicting elements, or to add a new element that reconciles them, in order to restore internal consistency.

## Historical Context

The American social psychologist Leon Festinger formulated the theory in his 1957 book *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance*. He and his colleagues Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter had earlier studied a religious group that had predicted the end of the world and, after the prophecy failed, explained the failure in ways that preserved their belief rather than abandoning it; this study, documented in *When Prophecy Fails* (1956), was an application of dissonance ideas to a real-world case.

## The Founding Study

In 1959 Festinger and his colleague James Carlsmith conducted a famous experiment in which they asked participants to perform a boring task and then to convince another person that it was enjoyable, in exchange for either one dollar or twenty dollars. The results showed that those who received only one dollar (weak external justification) changed their internal evaluation of the task and considered it more enjoyable than did those who received twenty dollars (sufficient external justification), in what later became known as the "Insufficient Justification Effect," a result contrary to the predictions of classical behavioral reinforcement theory.

## Main Ideas

Festinger assumes that the drive to reduce dissonance works as a basic psychological motive similar to physiological drives such as hunger, and he identifies three strategies for reducing it: changing one of the two dissonant beliefs, reducing the importance of one of them, or adding new justifying beliefs (Consonant Cognitions) that ease the contradiction without changing either of the original elements.

## Applications and Impact

The theory's influence went beyond social psychology to fields such as advertising and marketing (justifying purchase decisions after they are made, known as "post-purchase dissonance") and public health (justifying continued smoking despite knowing its harms). Motivational interviewing, used in addiction treatment, works with a related idea (developing discrepancy between a person's behavior and their values), although it draws on several theoretical sources rather than on dissonance theory alone. Contemporary research shows growing interest in studying the neural foundations of cognitive dissonance through functional magnetic resonance imaging, which has detected dissonance-related activity in brain regions concerned with conflict monitoring such as the anterior cingulate cortex, although this neural literature is still expanding and has not yet settled its complete precise mechanism.
