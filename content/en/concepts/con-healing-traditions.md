# Cultural Healing Traditions

## Definition

Cultural healing traditions are the practices through which communities address psychological and social distress outside of, or alongside, official psychiatry: recitation of Quranic verses (ruqyah) and reading, collective rituals, herbs, folk healers, sheikhs and saints, and spirit gatherings. They are not measured by symptom outcome alone; they restore the patient's place and meaning within their community.

## In the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The Arabic heritage combined natural medicine and spiritual healing without a sharp separation. Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya, in *Zad al-ma'ad* and *al-Tibb al-nabawi* (Prophetic Medicine), wrote that the Quran has a healing effect, cited the hadith account of ruqyah with the Opening chapter (al-Fatiha), and described combining medicine with supplication. (Penelope Johnstone translated *Prophetic Medicine* as *Medicine of the Prophet*, Islamic Texts Society, Cambridge, 1998.) Some historical accounts of medieval hospitals (bimaristans), discussed by Dols in *Majnun* (Clarendon Press, 1992), describe music, conversation, and regimen alongside humoral treatment. Physicians, including al-Razi and Ibn Sina, identified natural causes for melancholia and regarded it as a disturbance of the humors, and the relationship between physicians and ruqyah practitioners remained a mixture of coexistence and tension (see the entries on Ibn Sina and al-Razi). Alongside them, Sufism practiced sessions of dhikr and sama, which in popular memory were associated with calming the soul (see the entry on purification of the soul), and the zar ritual, often led by women (see the entry on zar).

## Anthropological and Psychological Study

Claude Lévi-Strauss studied "the efficacy of symbols" in the treatment of a Cuna shaman's difficult childbirth, arguing that the myth provides the patient with a language through which to organize their pain. Jerome Frank, in *Persuasion and Healing* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1961; third edition with Julia Frank, 1993), presented the "common factors" among all forms of healing: a trusted relationship, a place dedicated to healing, a theory that explains suffering, and a ritual that activates hope and a sense of capability (see the entry on the efficacy of ritual healing). Arthur Kleinman, in *Patients and Healers in the Context of Culture* (University of California Press, 1980), divided health systems into popular, professional, and folk sectors, and showed that patients move between them.

## Partnership and Risk

Some clinical and public-health programs have tried to work with traditional healers rather than only to compete with them. Al-Krenawi and Graham, in *International Social Work* in 1999, discussed the role of Quranic healers in mental health among Arabs and ways for professionals to collaborate with them. However, practices may cause harm: delayed access to medical treatment, beating and restraint under the pretext of expelling jinn, and mistreatment in "healing" settings (see the entry on mental health and human rights).

## What Is Not Well Known

Much of the evidence for the effectiveness of these traditions is descriptive or relies on patients' accounts, and controlled studies are few. It is difficult to separate the effect of the ritual from the effect of the group and of expectation, and the same problem exists in evaluating modern psychotherapy. Critics warn against "simplifying" the traditions by turning them into a general alternative treatment, and against commodifying them in health markets.
