# Tradition: The Transmission of the Past into the Present

**Tradition** is the transmission of beliefs, practices, customs, and institutions from one generation to the next, and the authority that the past is felt to carry in the present. To call something traditional is to say that it is done this way because it has always been done this way — and that this continuity is itself a reason to continue. Tradition is therefore not simply the past surviving into the present; it is a relationship to the past, a claim that the past authorizes the present. Anthropologists study tradition as a living process, constantly reinterpreted, contested, and remade, rather than as a fixed inheritance.

## Definition and Development

The classical sociological account of tradition was given by Max Weber, who in *Economy and Society* (written 1911–1920, published posthumously 1922, English edition 1968) distinguished traditional authority from charismatic and rational-legal authority. Traditional authority rests on the belief in the sanctity of age-old rules: the ruler rules because his ancestors ruled, the custom binds because it has always bound. Weber treated tradition as one of the fundamental legitimations of power, and as the opposite of the rational calculation that characterizes modern bureaucracy.

Edward Shils, in *Tradition* (1981), gave the concept its fullest treatment. Tradition, Shils argued, is anything that is handed down from the past to the present, and it is the substance of every society: language, beliefs, practices, institutions, and artefacts are all traditions in this sense. Societies are not divided into traditional and modern ones; every society, however modern, is made of traditions, and every tradition is subject to change. Shils's point was that tradition is not the enemy of reason or progress but the medium through which human beings live: we cannot start from scratch, and even our innovations build on what we have received.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, in *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), transformed the study of tradition by showing that many traditions are not ancient at all. The Highland kilt, the royal Christmas broadcast, the rituals of the British monarchy, the folk dances of new nations — all were invented in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, often quite recently, and then presented as if they were old. Hobsbawm's distinction was between genuine traditions, which have a real continuity with the past, and invented traditions, which claim such a continuity in order to legitimize new institutions. His work showed that tradition is a political resource: those who can define what is traditional can define what is legitimate. The contributors to the volume also showed that colonial rulers and new states alike drew on invented traditions — national ceremonies, costumes, and emblems — to build legitimacy, as Ranger's chapter on colonial Africa illustrates.

Anthony Giddens, in "Living in a Post-Traditional Society" (1994), argued that modern societies are becoming post-traditional: tradition no longer dictates how people live, and individuals must reflexively choose their identities, relationships, and beliefs. But Giddens insisted that this does not mean tradition disappears. Traditions persist, but they must now be justified: they survive because people choose them, not because they are unquestioned. In a post-traditional society, tradition is one option among others, and its authority depends on its ability to persuade.

## Ethnographic Examples

Anthropological fieldwork has shown how tradition works in practice. In many societies, tradition is invoked in disputes: to claim land, to settle a marriage, to justify a ritual, people appeal to what their ancestors did. But fieldwork also shows that what counts as traditional is itself contested. Elders and young people, men and women, different lineages and factions disagree about what the tradition requires, and each side presents its own version as the authentic one. Tradition is therefore a field of argument, not a settled fact.

Fieldwork has also shown how traditions are maintained and changed. Rituals are repeated, but each repetition is slightly different, and over time the differences accumulate into change. Oral histories are retold, and each retelling adapts the story to the present. Migrants carry traditions with them and remake them in new settings: a wedding, a funeral, or a festival in a diaspora community is both a continuation of the old tradition and a new creation, shaped by the conditions of exile.

## Contemporary Debates

Current scholarship debates the politics of tradition in an age of heritage and cultural revival. UNESCO's recognition of intangible cultural heritage has given traditions new value, but it has also raised questions about who owns a tradition and who decides what counts as authentic. Some argue that the language of tradition is used to resist globalization and to defend local identities; others argue that it is used to justify inequality, particularly the subordination of women and minorities, by presenting it as timeless custom. A further debate concerns the relationship between tradition and modernity: are they opposed, or are they two sides of the same process, with modernity itself producing new traditions? Anthropology's contribution is to study tradition as it is lived — not as a relic of the past but as a resource people use, argue over, and remake in the present.
