# Exposure and Response Prevention

A behavioral treatment approach (also known as "exposure and ritual prevention," ERP/EX-RP). It is a **first-line psychological treatment** recommended for **obsessive-compulsive disorder** (OCD) in major clinical guidelines, including those of the American Psychiatric Association and the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE).

## Basic Principle

The treatment rests on two interlocking components. **Exposure** is the gradual and systematic confrontation of the person with the thoughts, images, situations or objects that provoke their anxiety or obsessions (such as touching a "contaminated" surface in the case of contamination obsessions). **Response prevention** is the conscious refraining from performing the **compulsive act** (such as repeated washing) that the person has become accustomed to using to relieve the anxiety produced by exposure. The essential idea is that the compulsive act keeps the obsession alive through immediate negative reinforcement (temporary relief of anxiety). Preventing this act during exposure allows anxiety to rise and then fall on its own, through "habituation" or, according to modern models, "inhibitory learning," without the need for the ritual. The brain gradually learns that the frightening situation does not carry the danger that was expected.

## Historical Origins

The method traces back to the British psychologist **Victor Meyer**, who in 1966 reported improvement in a small series of inpatients with obsessive-compulsive rituals who were exposed to their triggers while being prevented from performing their rituals under supervision. This differed from the gradual, relaxation-based desensitization common in behavior therapy at the time. The method was developed in the 1970s by Stanley Rachman, Isaac Marks, and colleagues, and then, especially through the work of Edna Foa and colleagues in the 1980s, into organized systematic protocols, and was later incorporated into the broader framework of **cognitive behavioral therapy**.

## Therapeutic Structure

The program usually begins by building a **fear hierarchy** of triggers, from the least to the most anxiety-provoking. The patient then progresses through this hierarchy with the therapist's accompaniment, one exposure after another, with strict abstention from the accompanying compulsive behavior. Exposure is used either **imaginally** (to confront obsessive thoughts that are difficult to enact in reality) or **in vivo** (in real situations). It is often combined with **homework assignments** to generalize the skill beyond the therapy session.

## Effectiveness and Limits

Randomized controlled trials show substantial effectiveness for the method. In one large trial (Foa et al., 2005), exposure and ritual prevention, alone or combined with clomipramine, produced better outcomes than clomipramine alone or placebo. Guidelines describe combining it with **selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors** (SSRIs) in more severe cases. Many patients improve substantially, but not all do, and relapse can occur. Its main practical obstacle is that the initial anxiety that exposure requires leads some patients to withdraw early from treatment, which calls for clinical skill in pacing the gradient and in providing support. The treatment should be delivered by a trained clinician.
