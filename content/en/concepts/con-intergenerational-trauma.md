# Intergenerational Trauma Transmission

A concept in psychiatry and clinical psychology that describes how the psychological, behavioral, and biological effects of major collective traumas, such as genocide, enslavement, forced displacement, and war, can **be transmitted** from the generation of direct survivors to their children and grandchildren, even when the subsequent generation was not directly exposed to the original traumatic event.

## Origins: The Children of Holocaust Survivors

The concept of "intergenerational trauma" entered the psychiatric literature through early clinical observations in the 1960s of behavioral and psychological problems in the children of Holocaust survivors. Clinicians in Canada, such as Rakoff, Sigal, and Epstein (1966), and elsewhere reported that some children of survivors presented with anxiety, depression, and identity difficulties despite never having been personally exposed to the camps. Later work was more mixed. A meta-analysis (van IJzendoorn, Bakermans-Kranenburg, and Sagi-Schwartz, 2003) found evidence of secondary traumatization in children of survivors in clinical samples, but not in non-clinical community samples, and some studies suggest that offspring may be more vulnerable to stress when facing difficult life events. Findings for the third generation are less consistent.

## Psychological and Social Mechanisms

The transmission is partly explained by well-documented psychosocial mechanisms: parenting patterns shaped by over-protectiveness or emotional detachment in survivor parents; family silence surrounding the details of the trauma; and what Marianne Hirsch called "postmemory," the second generation's relationship to events they did not live through, mediated by stories, images, and family behavior; the implicit transfer of ways of dealing with threat and of viewing the world as necessarily dangerous; and the partial, unconscious identification of children with their parents' suffering.

## Emerging Epigenetic Evidence

Recent research, much of it associated with the work of **Rachel Yehuda**, has explored a potential biological pathway for transmission through **epigenetic** mechanisms (variable gene expression without alteration of the DNA sequence itself). Yehuda and colleagues (2016) reported that methylation of the *FKBP5* gene (linked to regulation of the hormonal stress axis) differed between Holocaust survivors and comparison groups, and differed in the opposite direction in their adult offspring. Earlier work from the same group found lower cortisol and altered HPA axis function in some offspring of survivors, particularly where the mother had post-traumatic stress disorder. These studies involved small samples (a few dozen participants), cannot rule out postnatal environmental explanations, and do not establish a mechanism of germline transmission in humans. They should be read as preliminary, not as established fact.

## Extension of the Concept

The application of the concept has expanded from the Holocaust context to other collective traumas whose intergenerational effects have been documented: descendants of survivors of the transatlantic enslavement of Africans; descendants of Indigenous peoples affected by settler colonialism, including forced residential schools in Canada (Bombay, Matheson, and Anisman, 2014) and the removal of children in Australia (the Stolen Generations); and children of survivors of later mass atrocities (such as in Rwanda and Cambodia), where patterns of anxiety, family disruption, and difficulties in emotional regulation have been reported across generations. The strength of the evidence varies between populations.

## Clinical Impact

Recognition of this concept has led to the development of therapeutic approaches that address the **family and collective dimension** of trauma rather than the affected individual alone. These include systemic family therapy and collective community healing programs aimed at survivors and their descendants together, rather than individual treatment isolated from the broader historical and family context.

## Limits

Scientific debate remains open about the relative weight of biological-epigenetic mechanisms versus psychosocial mechanisms (parenting, family narrative, environment) in explaining transmission. Some of the early epigenetic studies have faced methodological criticism concerning small sample sizes and the difficulty of replicating results, which calls for caution in generalizing the biological findings as conclusive.
