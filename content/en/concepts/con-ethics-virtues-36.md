# Justice Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Justice ethics is the set of conceptions that makes securing for every rights-holder his right, and placing everything in its proper position, a central measure of virtue and of upright conduct. In this sense justice is not merely an external law imposed on individuals. It is a firmly established disposition of the soul that moves its possessor to fairness even in the absence of supervision.

In the Islamic tradition, justice is among the greatest objectives of the Sharia. Al-Raghib al-Isfahani, in *al-Dhari'a ila Makarim al-Shari'a* (The Means to the Noble Qualities of the Sharia), discussed justice (*'adl*) as a foundation of the soundness of individual and social conditions. Al-Mawardi, in *Adab al-Dunya wa-l-Din*, listed pervasive justice among the foundations of a well-ordered society. Ibn Miskawayh, in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (The Refinement of Character), held, following Plato and Aristotle, that justice arises when the soul's faculties are in harmony under the rule of reason, so that wisdom, temperance and courage are balanced. Al-Farabi made justice the pillar on which the "virtuous city" stands, since social cooperation cannot be upright without a fair distribution of benefits and duties.

## The Western Historical Context

For Plato, justice is the harmony of the three powers of the soul (reason, spirit, and appetite), each performing its proper function. He projected this onto the level of the city in the *Republic*, where each class fulfills its appropriate role. Aristotle, in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, distinguished between "distributive justice," which distributes wealth and offices according to merit, and "corrective justice," which restores balance after an actual harm. In the modern era, John Rawls, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), developed a contractualist conception based on the "veil of ignorance," which guarantees principles of justice that favor no one.

## Objections and an Example

Distributive theories face the objection of the difficulty of defining the criterion of merit itself: is it effort, need, or actual contribution? An illustrative example is the distribution of inheritance in Islamic jurisprudence, in which a male heir in many cases receives twice the share of a female heir of the same degree (Qur'an 4:11). Jurists explain this by the difference in the financial burdens assigned to each of them. This shows that justice is not necessarily arithmetical equality, but a balance that takes account of context and responsibility.

## Justice as a Psychological Virtue

Philosophers of ethics distinguish between justice as an external social arrangement and justice as a firmly rooted disposition in its possessor, such that the individual inclines toward fairness even when no authority or law oversees him. This is the meaning brought out by Ibn Miskawayh, who makes justice the fruit of the soul's moderation rather than mere adherence to rules imposed from outside. The truly just person is one whose inner state is upright, and this is reflected in the outward character of his dealings, not one who acts fairly out of fear of punishment or in hope of reward. Hence the upbringing of the young in justice from childhood has been a central theme in both Islamic and philosophical ethical literature, on the principle that virtue is acquired through habituation and practice rather than through theoretical instruction alone.
