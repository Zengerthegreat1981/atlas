# GABA-Glutamate Balance in Psychiatric Disorders

## Definition

**Glutamate** is the most widespread **excitatory** neurotransmitter in the human brain, while **gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)** is its principal **inhibitory** counterpart. The concept of "GABA-glutamate balance" refers to the neurobiological hypothesis that a stable quantitative and functional relationship between these two transmitters is necessary for the normal regulation of neural excitation, and that **disruption of this balance**, whether through excess excitation or insufficient inhibition, is associated with a broad spectrum of psychiatric disorders.

## Key Features

- **Schizophrenia**: The NMDA receptor hypofunction hypothesis (NMDA being one of the glutamate receptors) on GABA-operating inhibitory neurons proposes an imbalance between excitation and inhibition that contributes to the positive, negative, and cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia.
- **Depression and mood disorders**: Several spectroscopy studies report reduced GABA and altered glutamate-related signals in specific cortical regions (for example the occipital and anterior cingulate cortex) in patients with major depression, though findings are not uniform. This supports a role for amino acid transmitters alongside the traditional monoamine hypothesis (serotonin and norepinephrine).
- **Anxiety disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder**: Some findings suggest altered glutamatergic and GABAergic function in circuits that process fear and regulate emotion (such as the amygdala, the prefrontal cortex, and, in obsessive-compulsive disorder, cortico-striato-thalamic loops), including generalized anxiety disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and obsessive-compulsive disorder. The evidence in these conditions is limited and mixed.
- **Therapeutic basis**: This hypothesis has formed the basis for developing drugs that act directly on the glutamate system, most notably **ketamine**, an NMDA receptor antagonist reported to produce rapid antidepressant effects (Berman et al., 2000), and its S-enantiomer esketamine, approved in a nasal spray formulation in the United States in 2019 for treatment-resistant depression under clinical supervision. Drugs that enhance GABA-A receptor function (such as benzodiazepines) have long been used for short-term anxiety treatment.

## Context

This hypothesis developed in parallel with advances in **magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS)**, which from the early 1990s made it possible to measure GABA and glutamate-related signals in the living human brain without surgical intervention. Human pharmacological studies of the NMDA antagonist ketamine (Krystal et al., 1994) also showed that blocking this receptor can produce schizophrenia-like symptoms in healthy volunteers.

## Impact

This framework helped shift the traditional "chemical imbalance" model in psychiatry, which historically focused on monoamines such as serotonin and dopamine, toward a more comprehensive model that accounts for **neural networks of excitation and inhibition** as a whole. It also opened new therapeutic avenues (such as ketamine) for treatment-resistant depression.

## Limits

The relationship between GABA/glutamate disruptions and psychiatric symptoms remains largely correlational rather than fully established as causal, and findings are sometimes inconsistent across studies depending on the brain region examined and the technique used. Much of the evidence comes from MRS, which is indirect: it measures metabolite concentrations in a tissue volume (often combining glutamate and glutamine, as "Glx"), not synaptic activity itself, and cannot easily separate neuronal from other pools. This calls for caution in interpreting it as a single comprehensive explanation of complex psychiatric disorders.
