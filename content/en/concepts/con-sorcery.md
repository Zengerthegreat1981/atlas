# Sorcery

Sorcery is the deliberate use of learned techniques — spells, charms, medicines, poisons and ritual acts — to harm, control or manipulate another person, usually in secret and for personal ends. In anthropology it is distinguished from the broader category of magic, which includes beneficial and protective techniques, and from witchcraft, which in the classic Azande usage is an innate, often unconscious power residing in the body of the witch. Sorcery is acquired rather than inborn: it must be learned, bought or inherited as knowledge, and it is performed with substances and words rather than by an inner capacity. It is also distinguished from healing magic by its intent and its social evaluation: sorcery is magic put to harmful use, and the sorcerer is a figure of fear and moral condemnation.

## Origins and Development

The classic anthropological account of sorcery comes from E. E. Evans-Pritchard's study of the Azande of north-central Africa, conducted in the then Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. The Azande distinguished sharply between witchcraft (*mangu*), an inherited substance in the body that could harm others without its owner's knowledge, and sorcery, the use of medicines and spells, which was a deliberate and learnable act. Sorcery could be detected, countered and punished, and Azande oracles were consulted to establish who was responsible for misfortune. Bronisław Malinowski, in the Trobriand Islands, found a similar pattern: sorcery was a recognised technique, practised by specialists who were paid for their services, and fear of sorcery regulated social behaviour, deterring theft, adultery and insult. Max Marwick, working among the Cewa of Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia), showed that sorcery accusations follow the fault lines of social tension: accusations cluster between matrilineal kin competing for land, office and prestige, and they flare up at moments of death, illness and misfortune, when people search for a human cause behind suffering.

Sorcery accusations are rarely random. John Middleton and Edward Winter's comparative study of East Africa showed that they typically target people in ambiguous or resented positions — the wealthy, the childless, the newcomer, the rival — and that they express conflicts that cannot be voiced openly. The accused sorcerer is often someone whose success or behaviour has aroused envy, and the accusation is a way of levelling that success or of explaining misfortune. In many societies the sorcerer is also a healer: the same knowledge that can harm can cure, and the specialist who knows how to send illness also knows how to remove it. This ambiguity makes the sorcerer a dangerous but indispensable figure, consulted in secret even by those who denounce sorcery in public.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns the relationship between sorcery and social change. Early anthropologists treated sorcery beliefs as traditional survivals destined to fade with education and modernisation. Peter Geschiere's study of Cameroon showed the opposite: sorcery accusations have intensified with modernity, as new forms of wealth, urban life and political competition generate new envies and new suspicions, and as sorcery is invoked to explain the sudden riches of politicians and businessmen. A second debate asks whether sorcery accusations are functional — releasing tension and enforcing norms — or destructive, tearing communities apart through suspicion, violence and witch-hunts. The evidence suggests both: accusations can resolve conflicts by identifying a culprit and prescribing a remedy, but they can also escalate into killings, especially of elderly women and other vulnerable people. A third debate concerns the anthropologist's stance: how to write about sorcery without either endorsing its reality or dismissing believers as irrational. Evans-Pritchard's solution was to treat Azande beliefs as a coherent rational system, intelligible on its own terms, and this remains an influential model for ethnographic writing on the subject.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Sorcery remains a pressing contemporary issue. In Papua New Guinea, sorcery-related violence, including the torture and killing of accused sorcerers, has prompted government inquiries and new legislation. In sub-Saharan Africa, accusations continue to shape elections, business and family life, and Pentecostal churches have made the identification and deliverance of sorcerers a central part of their ministry. Anthropologists now study sorcery not as a relic of the past but as a modern idiom through which people make sense of inequality, risk and misfortune — a language for talking about power when other languages fail. The study of sorcery also raises ethical questions for the ethnographer: how to report accusations without endangering the accused, and how to respect beliefs that can have lethal consequences. It remains one of anthropology's most sensitive and most socially consequential topics.
