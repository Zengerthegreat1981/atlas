# Women's Liberation in Arab Thought

The question of women entered modern Arab thought first through education. In Egypt, a school for women medical practitioners (*hakimat*), including midwifery, was opened at Abu Za'bal in 1832 under Clot Bey. Rifa'a al-Tahtawi, in *al-Murshid al-Amin li-l-Banat wa-l-Banin* (*The Faithful Guide for Girls and Boys*, 1872), wrote that girls must learn reading, writing and arithmetic, and that a nation advances through the education of its women as it does through the education of its men. Al-Tahtawi appealed to the examples of learned Muslim women to defend his call against the charge of imitating the West.

## Qasim Amin

Qasim Amin (1863–1908) stirred a storm with *Tahrir al-Mar'a* (*The Liberation of Women*, 1899) and then *al-Mar'a al-Jadida* (*The New Woman*, 1900). He called for girls' education, reform of the laws on polygamy and divorce, and the easing of face-veiling, arguing that an educated woman is better suited to raising children. He treated the face veil as a custom rather than a requirement of the Sharia, and he argued that polygamy as practiced does not meet the Quranic condition of justice and should be restricted. How far Muhammad Abduh contributed to the first book remains disputed. Muhammad Tal'at Harb replied to *Tahrir al-Mar'a* with *Tarbiyat al-Mar'a wa-l-Hijab* (*The Upbringing of Women and the Veil*, 1899). Muhammad Farid Wajdi answered *al-Mar'a al-Jadida* in *al-Mar'a al-Muslima* (*The Muslim Woman*, Cairo, 1901), defending prevailing practice, including the veil. Some opponents tied Amin's call to foreign influence.

## Women's Voices

Women were not only a subject of discussion. Hind Nawfal issued *al-Fatat* (*The Young Woman*) in Alexandria in 1892, generally regarded as the first Arabic women's magazine. Zaynab Fawwaz, author of the biographical dictionary *al-Durr al-Manthur fi Tabaqat Rabbat al-Khudur* (1894), collected essays in *al-Rasa'il al-Zaynabiyya* (*The Zaynab Letters*, c. 1905). Malak Hifni Nasif, known as Bahithat al-Badiya ("researcher of the desert"), collected her articles in *al-Nisa'iyyat* (*Feminine Matters*, Cairo: Matba'at al-Jarida, 1910), calling for education and reform while keeping the veil. Huda Sha'rawi founded the Egyptian Feminist Union in 1923 and, on returning from a women's congress in Rome, removed her face veil at Cairo railway station. In Lebanon, Nazira Zayn al-Din issued *al-Sufur wa-l-Hijab* (*Unveiling and the Veil*, Beirut, 1928), arguing from religious texts against the requirement of the face veil.

## Critical Reading

Leila Ahmed, in *Women and Gender in Islam* (1992), re-examined this history. She held that a "colonial feminism", as in Lord Cromer, who called for the liberation of Egyptian women while opposing their enfranchisement in his own country, used the veil as a symbol of the backwardness of the East, and that Qasim Amin was influenced by this discourse. Margot Badran, in *Feminists, Islam, and Nation* (1995), presented a more complex picture of Egyptian nationalist feminism that was engaged in revolution and politics. The volume *Remaking Women* (1998), edited by Lila Abu-Lughod, explores how feminism and modernity in the Middle East were entangled with colonial and state projects.

## Limits of the Movement

The first women's movement remained elitist and class-bound, and concentrated on education and personal status matters more than on work. In the second half of the twentieth century, Nawal El Saadawi offered a bolder critique of social structures and the body, and Fatima Mernissi read religious texts historically. The debate over family law in most Arab countries continues between jurists and civil legislation.
