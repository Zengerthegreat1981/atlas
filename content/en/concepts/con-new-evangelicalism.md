# New Evangelicalism

A term describing a movement within conservative American Protestantism that arose in the late 1940s as a reaction from within fundamentalism itself to its strict cultural isolationism. Its pioneers, including Harold Ockenga, Carl F. H. Henry and Billy Graham, sought to retain conservative evangelical doctrine (the authority of the Bible, the virgin birth, the bodily resurrection) while rejecting the separation from academic and public culture that characterized classical fundamentalism after the Scopes trial (1925).

## Historical Context

Fuller Theological Seminary was founded in Pasadena in 1947 as an academic platform for this trend. It attempted to produce evangelical theological scholarship that engages modern intellectual thought rather than boycotting it. In this context a generation of evangelical thinkers took shape, among them Carl F. H. Henry, whose *The Uneasy Conscience of Modern Fundamentalism* (1947) criticized fundamentalism's retreat from social engagement. Francis Schaeffer, working from L'Abri in Switzerland, is a related but distinct figure: an American Presbyterian who offered a rational defense of the Christian faith addressed to the doubts of modern Western culture, and who became highly influential among American evangelicals.

## From Theology to Politics

Hankins, in his biography of Schaeffer, shows how Schaeffer's influence helped move many evangelicals, who had begun with an intellectual-cultural project, toward direct political activism by the late 1970s (the abortion issue specifically), contributing to the emergence of what is later called the American "Religious Right." This account remains confined to the American Protestant context.

## The Dispute with Separatist Fundamentalism

New Evangelicalism faced sharp opposition from classical fundamentalist leaders, most notably Carl McIntire and Bob Jones, who regarded any dialogue with academic or ecumenical circles as a compromise of doctrinal purity and a slide toward theological liberalism. The dispute sharpened over Billy Graham's cooperation with a range of churches in his evangelistic campaigns (including mainline and Catholic churches), notably around his 1957 New York crusade, which the separatist fundamentalists rejected outright, regarding it as a betrayal of the fundamentalist principle of "separation from doctrinal error."

## Billy Graham's Preaching as an Example

Graham's evangelistic practice shows the movement's combination of conservative doctrine and broad engagement. His sermons, which drew national attention from the Los Angeles campaign of 1949, were plain and urgent: they connected current anxieties, personal and political, with sin, the death and resurrection of Christ and the need for conversion, repeatedly resting their authority on the Bible rather than on theological argument. Each meeting ended with an invitation to come forward, where trained counsellors met inquirers and referred them to local churches. This follow-up was the point of friction with separatists, because cooperating churches included mainline congregations whose theology they rejected. Graham also used modern organization and media, founding the Billy Graham Evangelistic Association in 1950 and broadcasting the *Hour of Decision* radio programme. In the 1950s he moved away from segregated seating at his crusades, and in 1957 he invited Martin Luther King Jr. to offer a prayer at the New York meetings. His preaching thus retained a revivalist call to individual decision while refusing the institutional isolation that fundamentalist critics demanded.

## The Role of Harold Ockenga

The coinage of the term "New Evangelicalism" is attributed to Harold John Ockenga, pastor of Park Street Church in Boston, first president of Fuller Theological Seminary and an early president of the National Association of Evangelicals (NAE, founded 1942); he used it in a 1947 address at Fuller. In the late 1940s he explicitly called for moving beyond the isolation of classical fundamentalism toward a positive "engagement" in American intellectual and social life, without surrendering the essence of evangelical doctrine. According to historians of the movement, this made him one of the movement's leading figures and spokesmen.
