# PTSD Diagnostic Criteria

The official set of criteria adopted by the American Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM) to determine whether the symptoms of a person exposed to a traumatic event meet the diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). The diagnosis first entered the manual in its third edition (DSM-III, 1980), and its criteria were fundamentally reformulated in the fifth edition (DSM-5, 2013).

## Structure of the Criteria in DSM-5

In addition to the criterion of exposure to a traumatic event (actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence, whether through direct exposure, witnessing, learning that a close relative was exposed, or repeated exposure to the aversive details in a professional capacity), the fifth manual requires the presence of four clusters of symptoms that have lasted for more than one month:

- **Criterion B (intrusion)**: at least one of five intrusion symptoms: recurrent intrusive memories, recurrent nightmares, flashbacks, intense psychological distress on exposure to reminders of the event, and marked physiological reactions to triggers associated with it.
- **Criterion C (avoidance)**: at least one of two symptoms: avoidance of memories, thoughts or feelings associated with the event, or avoidance of external reminders (places, people, conversations).
- **Criterion D (negative alterations in cognition and mood)**: at least two of seven: inability to remember important aspects of the event, persistent negative beliefs about oneself or the world, distorted blame of oneself, persistent negative emotional state, loss of interest in previously important activities, feeling detached from others, and persistent inability to experience positive emotions.
- **Criterion E (alterations in arousal and reactivity)**: at least two of six: irritability and anger outbursts, reckless or self-destructive behavior, hypervigilance, exaggerated startle response, difficulty concentrating, and sleep disturbance.

## Difference from DSM-IV

The fourth manual divided the symptoms of the disorder into only three groups (re-experiencing, avoidance/emotional numbing, and hyperarousal). The fifth manual separated the old "avoidance/numbing" group into two independent groups (avoidance, and negative alterations in cognition and mood). It also added a dissociative subtype (persistent depersonalization or derealization), a delayed-expression specifier, and a separate set of criteria for children aged six and younger. DSM-5-TR (2022) retained the structure with minor text revisions.

## ICD-11 Comparison

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 (in effect from 2022) uses a deliberately narrower structure. PTSD requires exposure to an extremely threatening or horrific event and three core symptom clusters: re-experiencing in the present (such as flashbacks or nightmares), avoidance of reminders, and a persistent sense of heightened current threat (hypervigilance or exaggerated startle), persisting for at least several weeks and causing significant impairment. ICD-11 also introduced **complex PTSD**, which requires the PTSD symptoms plus persistent disturbances in self-organization: affect dysregulation, a negative self-concept, and difficulties in relationships. DSM-5 has no separate complex PTSD diagnosis.

## Clinical Significance

These criteria provide a unified diagnostic language that allows comparison across studies and clinics. They are, however, also the subject of ongoing academic debate about the expansion of the definition of the "traumatic event" itself across editions, and about the numerical symptom threshold, which may exclude cases with genuine clinical distress that do not meet the exact required number of symptoms in each group (see the entry on subthreshold symptoms). The criteria are diagnostic descriptions only; this entry is educational and does not constitute clinical advice.

## Limits

The criteria are based primarily on Western clinical data (mainly American), and their applicability across cultures with different concepts of trauma and of expressing psychological distress remains a matter of ongoing research debate.
