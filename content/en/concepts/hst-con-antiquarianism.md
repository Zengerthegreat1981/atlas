# Antiquarianism and Archaeological Interest

Antiquarianism in classical antiquity denotes the systematic collection, cataloging, and analytical investigation of ancient material remains, inscriptions, sacred rituals, legal customs, topography, and linguistic etymologies. As famously conceptualized by modern historiographer Arnaldo Momigliano, classical culture drew a fundamental distinction between the **historian** (*historicus*), who composed dynamic, chronological narratives centered upon political decision-making and warfare, and the **antiquarian** (*antiquarius* or *philologos*), who arranged static past realities systematically by categories—such as public rites, ancestral laws, monuments, and sacred sanctuaries—to preserve cultural inheritance from decay and oblivion.

## Greek Precursors and Topographical Inquiries

Systematic antiquarian interest developed across several interrelated Greek intellectual traditions:
- **Local histories and foundation myths**: Classical cities produced local chroniclers and antiquarians (*horographoi*) who investigated local foundation legends (*ktiseis*), priesthood lineages, and civic laws.
- **Epigraphic collection**: In the Hellenistic era, scholars began treating stone inscriptions (*stele*) as primary evidentiary objects. Scholars such as Polemon of Ilium (second century BCE) traveled extensively across Greece reading and compiling public inscriptions, earning the contemporary epithet *stelokopas* ("stele-picker").
- **Pausanias and the Periegetic tradition**: In the second century CE, Pausanias composed his *Periegesis Hellados* (*Description of Greece*), a ten-book topographical and antiquarian survey of Roman Greece. Pausanias meticulously examined ancient temples, sacred groves, archaic wooden cult statues (*xoana*), inscribed bronze tripods, and ancestral tombs. Rather than merely recounting military conflicts, Pausanias documented local religious memory and preserved descriptions of material culture that modern archaeology has repeatedly corroborated.

## Roman Antiquarian Scholarship

Antiquarian research reached institutional maturity in the late Roman Republic and early Empire, driven by aristocratic anxiety over the disintegration of archaic customs during rapid imperial expansion:
- **Marcus Terentius Varro**: A leading Roman antiquarian, Varro (116–27 BCE) authored the monumental *Antiquitates Rerum Humanarum et Divinarum* (forty-one books), dividing Roman ancestral culture into systematic categories: men, places, times, and sacred things. Varro's rigorous etymological and institutional excavations (*De Lingua Latina*) influenced the Augustan-era interest in religious and ancestral revival.
- **Legal and administrative antiquarianism**: Roman scholars such as Verrius Flaccus (author of the encyclopedic lexicon *De Verborum Significatu*) and later Aulus Gellius (*Noctes Atticae*) compiled archaic legal formulas, sacrificial terms, and obsolete civic rituals, recognizing that language itself preserves strata of social history.
- **Biographical and monumental research**: Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus, who held imperial secretarial posts under Hadrian, utilized private imperial correspondence, decree archives, and physical ephemera to construct thematic, analytical biographies rather than linear chronological narratives.

## Methodological Significance and Legacy

While ancient narrative historians occasionally looked down on antiquarian collecting as pedantic or lacking rhetorical grandeur, antiquarianism established the empirical foundation of historical criticism:
- **Materiality versus rhetoric**: Unlike political historians, who often invented direct speeches to display rhetorical *decorum*, antiquarians scrutinized non-narrative artifacts—coins, stone decrees, and physical architectural foundations—that could not be easily manipulated by literary fashion.
- **Modern emergence of archaeology**: In early modern Europe, Renaissance humanists rediscovered Varronian and Pausanian methods to decipher Roman ruins. When eighteenth-century skepticism questioned the veracity of literary chronicles, antiquarian epigraphy and numismatics provided the verifiable bedrock that transformed historical study into modern archaeological and documentary science.
