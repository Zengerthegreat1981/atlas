# Whistleblowing

## Meaning and Definition

Whistleblowing is the act by which a member of an organization (whether a company or a government agency) discloses illegal or unethical practices that they know about from within the organization. The disclosure may be made to internal oversight bodies or to the public outside the organization. The whistleblower usually risks their job, reputation and personal safety. The act creates a moral tension between the duty of loyalty to the organization and the duty of honesty toward society and the law.

## The Position in Islamic Thought

This concept finds strong support in the principle of *nasiha* (sincere counsel), which the Prophet ﷺ made the essence of religion: "Religion is sincere counsel." When asked, "To whom?" he said, "To God, to His Book, to His Messenger, to the leaders of the Muslims and to their common folk" (narrated by Muslim). This hadith is taken to establish a general duty of honest advice, including to those in authority. Related is the duty to "enjoin good and forbid evil," which, according to many jurists, applies when one is able and no greater harm would result, including in the face of injustice and corruption by those who hold power. The Prophet ﷺ said, "The best jihad is a word of truth before an unjust ruler" (narrated by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi). Nevertheless, jurists restricted such duties by a weighing of benefits and harms, developed notably by al-Izz ibn Abd al-Salam in *Qawa'id al-Ahkam* and by al-Shatibi in *al-Muwafaqat*. A person should not disclose a wrongdoing if the harm resulting from disclosure would be greater than the harm of silence. Careful verification is also required to prevent baseless backbiting and slander.

## Treatment in Western Philosophy

The philosopher Sissela Bok addressed disclosure in the context of the ethics of secrecy and confidentiality, in *Secrets* (1982) and in her 1980 essay "Whistleblowing and Professional Responsibilities." She analyzed the conflict between loyalty to the organization and obligations to the wider public, and argued that dissent must be weighed carefully against the harm of breaching confidentiality. The business ethicist Richard De George proposed criteria for external whistleblowing (directed at the public rather than only internal management). Roughly: the harm at stake must be serious; the employee should first have reported it internally and exhausted internal channels; the employee must have documented evidence that would convince a reasonable observer; and there must be a reasonable chance that going public will prevent the harm. He argued that when the harm is serious, it has been reported, and internal channels have been exhausted, whistleblowing is morally permissible, and that when the further conditions of convincing evidence and a reasonable chance of success are also met, it is a duty. These criteria have been widely discussed and criticized as too demanding.

## Issues and Challenges

Many countries now enact laws to protect whistleblowers from employment retaliation. However, enforcement remains fraught with practical risks. Many whistleblowers face dismissal or legal prosecution even when their claims are accurate. A question also arises about distinguishing disclosure motivated by the public interest from disclosure motivated by personal revenge or financial gain.

## Illustrative Example

An employee at an industrial company discovers that the factory is dumping toxic waste into a nearby river in violation of the law. After failing to persuade management internally to stop the violation, the employee reports it to environmental authorities, risking their job in order to protect the health of the local population. This is a hypothetical case of the kind used to illustrate the debate.

## Further Primary Texts

Ralph Nader, Peter Petkas and Kate Blackwell edited *Whistle Blowing* (1972), a conference report that helped bring the term into public debate. Norman Bowie's *Business Ethics* (1982) proposed that justified whistleblowing requires proper moral motive, prior use of internal channels, compelling evidence, serious harm and a likelihood of success. Michael Davis's article Some Paradoxes of Whistleblowing (1996) criticized the standard harm-based account associated with De George and proposed instead a complicity theory, on which the duty arises from the employee's own involvement in the wrong. C. Fred Alford's *Whistleblowers: Broken Lives and Organizational Power* (2001) studied the retaliation that many experience.

Legal texts include the United States False Claims Act of 1863, whose qui tam provisions let private persons sue on the government's behalf and share in recoveries; the Whistleblower Protection Act of 1989 for federal employees; the United Kingdom's Public Interest Disclosure Act 1998; and the European Union's Directive 2019/1937, which requires member states to provide reporting channels and protection against retaliation for people reporting breaches of Union law.
