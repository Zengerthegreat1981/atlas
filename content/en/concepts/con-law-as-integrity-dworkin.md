# Law as Integrity

The mature theory that **Ronald Dworkin** set out in *Law's Empire* (1986), to answer the question: **what makes a particular legal interpretation the correct one** in a contested case, when no clear text settles it?

## Three Competing Conceptions of Law

Dworkin presents "law as integrity" as an alternative to two rival conceptions. The first is **conventionalism**, close to positivism, on which law is confined to what was explicitly agreed upon in the past. The second is **legal pragmatism**, on which the judge is free to make the best decision for the future without any obligation to remain consistent with the past. Dworkin rejects both in favor of a third position. Integrity, as a political ideal, asks that the state act on a coherent set of principles, and it has a legislative and an adjudicative aspect: legislators should keep the law principled, and judges should treat the law as principled.

## The Central Idea: Constructive Interpretation

Dworkin holds that interpreting law is closer to interpreting a literary work with many authors over time. His famous metaphor is the "chain novel," in which each judge writes a new chapter bound to be consistent with what came before. A judge must offer an interpretation that **fits** prior legal practice and, at the same time, **justifies** it in the best possible moral and political light. In Dworkin's account, fit works as a threshold: interpretations that do not fit the settled materials sufficiently are excluded, and among those that pass, the judge chooses the one that shows the practice in its best moral and political light.

## An Imaginary Methodological Tool: Judge Hercules

Dworkin uses a fictional figure, **Judge Hercules** (introduced in his 1975 essay "Hard Cases" and developed in *Taking Rights Seriously* and *Law's Empire*), a hypothetical judge with an exceptional capacity to absorb the full history of the legal system and its moral and political values, to show how ideal interpretation would look in practice. The common critique of this device is that it is an unrealistic standard that no human judge actually possesses. Dworkin himself partly conceded this, treating Hercules as an analytical model rather than a realistic description.

## The Roots of the Dispute with Legal Positivism: Rules and Principles

Before the "integrity" theory came a dispute between Dworkin and **H.L.A. Hart**, his predecessor in the Oxford chair of jurisprudence, over whether the positivist "rule of recognition" is adequate to explain judicial reasoning. In his early essay *The Model of Rules* (1967), and later in *Taking Rights Seriously* (1977), Dworkin argued that judges actually work with **principles**, not merely explicit rules. Such principles (for example, that no one may profit from their own wrongdoing, which Dworkin illustrates with *Riggs v. Palmer*, a New York case of 1889) are weighed according to their relative strength when they conflict. Rules, by contrast, apply in an all-or-nothing manner. This distinction is the foundation on which the theory of law as integrity was later built: the principles embedded in legal practice are what provide the judge with material for constructive interpretation when the decisive text is absent.

## A Comparative Context: Islamic Jurisprudence and Interpretive Consistency

One may loosely compare Dworkin's idea of fit with historical consistency to the principle of **observing the objectives of the Sharia** in al-Shatibi, though the comparison is an analytical analogy and not a claim of influence or equivalence. Both reject interpretation that is free of all constraint (as in legal pragmatism), and both require that a new ruling be consistent with the whole system of the existing order, juristic or legal, rather than decided in isolation from it. The essential difference is that Dworkin grounds his consistency in the accumulated history of human judicial practice, whereas the Muslim jurist grounds his in a transcendent textual reference (the objectives of the Lawgiver). Both nevertheless share the rejection of a rigid literal reading alone.

## Critique and Evaluation

Dworkin's theory is criticized for assuming that there is a **single right answer** (One Right Answer) for every legal question, even the hard ones. Positivists and realists alike question this assumption, arguing that many cases admit several reasonable solutions and that choosing among them depends on value preferences that no neutral interpretive method resolves. Moreover, the criteria of "fit" and "justification" may interact in ways that are hard to apply in practice, and the line between questions of fit and questions of moral justification is itself contested. Hart's posthumously published Postscript to *The Concept of Law* (1994) offers his reply to Dworkin.
