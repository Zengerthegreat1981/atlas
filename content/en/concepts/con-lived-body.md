# The Lived Body (Leib vs. Körper)

A founding concept in somatic phenomenology (Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Henry), which distinguishes between:

- Körper: the body as an objective physical thing (what the doctor sees, what is measured by science).
- Leib: the lived body, the body we live from within, the subject of our primary experience.

## Development

- Husserl in *Ideas II* (1952, published posthumously): the distinction between the "lived body" (Leib) and the "objective body" (Körper) was established. Husserl drafted its manuscripts between about 1912 and 1928, and showed in them that the body is the "center of orientation" from which we proceed to things, and that it is something we experience both from within and from without at once, as in the example of one hand touching the other.
- Merleau-Ponty in *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945): he made the "lived body" (Corps propre) the central "subject" of phenomenology, neither "pure consciousness" (Descartes) nor an "objective body" (science). For him perception is a bodily act, and the body is the "subject of perception".
- Henry (1965) in *Philosophy and Phenomenology of the Body*: he pushed to an extreme the "phenomenology of the inner body" against objectivism.
- Helmuth Plessner, in *Die Stufen des Organischen und der Mensch* (1928), had already formulated the doubleness: the human being "has" a body (Körper haben) and "is" a lived body (Leib sein) at once.

## The Body in the Islamic Philosophical Tradition

Islamic philosophy did not know the duality between Leib and Körper in this form, but Mulla Sadra Shirazi offered a conception close to its meaning: the body for him is not something foreign to the soul but a "descending level" among the levels of its existence, and the soul is "corporeal in origination, spiritual in subsistence"; so the distinction between the objective body and the lived body corresponds in him to a distinction between the apparent body and the body that the soul governs. This is a comparison in meaning, not a historical one, for Sadra's framework is metaphysical, not phenomenological.

## Influence

- It influenced feminist philosophy of the body: Iris Marion Young, in "Throwing Like a Girl" (1980), connected the lived body with the patterns of movement that culture imposes on women, and Irigaray wrote on sexual difference in embodiment.
- It influenced clinical psychology (Binswanger, Boss) and somatic psychology, and in medicine S. Kay Toombs in *The Meaning of Illness* (1992), where the patient's body turns from silence into an obstacle.
- Drew Leder showed in *The Absent Body* (1990) how the body disappears from consciousness when it works smoothly and appears as a burden in illness and pain.

## Limits

The lived body must be distinguished from the body schema (Body Schema), an unconscious motor system separate from lived experience itself, and from the traditional mind-body problem, whose duality the concept of the "lived body" originally seeks to overcome. Frantz Fanon, in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), criticized phenomenology's neglect of the role of race, adding to the body schema a "historical-racial schema" imposed by the gaze of the other.
