# Myth and Second-Order Signification in Barthes

An analytical mechanism formulated by Roland Barthes in *Mythologies* (1957) to explain how contemporary popular culture produces ideological meanings that appear "natural" and self-evident, despite being a specific cultural and historical construction.

## The Two Semantic Levels

Barthes distinguishes two overlapping systems. The first is ordinary language: a signifier and a signified yield a literal sign. His example in "Myth Today" is a *Paris-Match* cover showing a young Black soldier in French uniform saluting the flag. Myth is a second-order system that takes that whole sign and uses it as a new signifier for a further signified, in this case an image of French imperial harmony.

## The Naturalization of Ideology

The basic function of myth for Barthes is "transforming history into nature." Ideological meanings (which are tied to a specific social and historical context and are subject to change) are presented as eternal, natural, and self-evident truths, so that the constructed character of these meanings is forgotten and the ideological discourse is accepted without question. The semiological analysis in Barthes aims at "denaturalizing" these myths, that is, revealing the ideological layer hidden behind what appears to be a neutral, self-evident meaning.

## Analytical Examples

Barthes applied this analysis to a variety of popular cultural phenomena (professional wrestling, soap-powder and detergent advertising, and the launch of a new Citroën car), showing how these ordinary popular phenomena carry implicit ideological layers about social class, nationality, and gender that usually pass without conscious notice by the average consumer.

## Form, Fullness, and Distortion

In "Myth Today," the mythical signifier (the *form*) is described as both full and empty. It is full of the first-order meaning (the photograph of the soldier) and emptied enough for a new concept (imperial harmony) to occupy it. Barthes calls the relation *distortion*: the literal meaning is not cancelled. It stays in view and lends the myth an appearance of naturalness while carrying another ideological meaning. The later technical term "empty signifier" belongs to other theorists and is not Barthes's name for this form.

## Form, Concept and Signification

In the vocabulary of the essay "Myth Today," the signifier of the mythical system is called the *form*, the signified the *concept*, and the resulting sign the *signification*. The first-system sign (meaning) is thus reduced to a form that the concept can inhabit.

## Myth as Speech, Not Subject Matter

Barthes emphasizes that myth for him is not a specific content (anything can become a myth), but rather **a form of speech** (*une parole*), or a mode of communication: any signifying material (an image, a text, an advertisement, a sporting scene) can be taken over by this mythical mechanism if it is used to carry an unspoken ideological meaning that appears self-evident.

## Critique and Later Use

This analytical tool has had a wide influence on British cultural studies and on the analysis of advertising and media discourse, becoming a standard instrument for exposing ideological "naturalization" in contemporary popular discourse. The method has also been criticized for sometimes presupposing the "dominant ideology" it seeks to expose, rather than inferring it from the text itself.
