# Liberation and Democratization Movements

## Definition

**Liberation and democratization movements** in contemporary Arab thought form a broad intellectual and political current. It combines the demand to end political despotism with the building of representative systems of government grounded in constitutionalism, pluralism and individual rights. It has passed through several waves over more than a century and a half of modern Arab thought, from late Ottoman constitutional reformism to the uprisings of the "Arab Spring" in the second decade of the twenty-first century.

## Historical Context

The early intellectual roots of this current go back to the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when Arab and Ottoman reform movements sought to draw inspiration from European constitutionalism by using the state's own instruments to bring about gradual liberal reform. Examples include the Ottoman constitution of 1876 and the constitutional revolution of 1908 associated with the Young Turks, together with Egyptian reform and constitutional movements. Among Arab writers, Rifa'a al-Tahtawi introduced European political ideas, Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi argued for limiting rulers' power through consultative institutions, and 'Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi, in *Taba'i' al-Istibdad* (The Characteristics of Despotism), analysed despotism and its effects on society. These were followed by successive waves of Arab liberationist thought during the independence period, then by confrontation with post-independence authoritarian regimes during the Cold War. The most recent wave is the **Arab Spring** (2010–2011), which began in Tunisia and Egypt and spread to Libya, Syria, Yemen and Bahrain. It toppled entrenched regimes in several countries before facing complex and divergent outcomes: a partial democratic transition in Tunisia, civil wars in Syria, Libya and Yemen, and a return of authoritarianism in Egypt.

## Main Dimensions

- **The "democratic transition" approach (transitology)**: for a long period, until critiques such as Thomas Carothers's "The End of the Transition Paradigm" (2002), academic studies of Arab politics were dominated by an approach that treated political developments in the region as steps either "forward" or "backward" on a linear path toward an assumed liberal democratic model. This approach was later widely criticized as reductive and based on a ready-made Western model.
- **Religion and the state**: the relation of political Islam to democracy became one of the most contested questions in this current. Rashid Ghannouchi, in *al-Hurriyyat al-'amma fi al-dawla al-islamiyya* (Public Liberties in the Islamic State, 1993), argued that public liberties can sit inside an Islamic reference. Other writers have treated the coercive application of one legal school as incompatible with equal citizenship, or have separated the democratic claim from religious authority.
- **Civil society as a central actor**: literature after 2011 focused on the role of civil society organizations, youth movements and trade unions (such as the Tunisian General Labour Union, which received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2015 as part of the Tunisian National Dialogue Quartet). These were treated as essential actors in the success or failure of democratic transition processes, moving beyond the traditional focus on political and military elites alone.
- **Sharp divergence in the outcomes of the recent wave**: the results of the Arab Spring showed a sharp contrast between a relatively successful transition (Tunisia, although it experienced later regression after 2021), the collapse of the state into civil war (Syria, Libya, Yemen), and the reproduction of an authoritarian regime by new means (Egypt). This led contemporary Arab thought to undertake a deep critical review of the assumptions of "democratic inevitability" that prevailed in the early literature on the movement.

## After 2011

The uprisings produced Arab political writing that is not the transitology of the 1990s. Azmi Bishara's *Fi al-thawra wa-l-qabiliyya li-l-thawra* (On Revolution and Susceptibility to Revolution, 2012) defines susceptibility to revolution as the judgment that suffering is an injustice and that collective action against it is possible. He separates that moment from the longer task of a democratic order, which he treats as specific to each country, and he does not treat the fall of a ruler as that order. In Tunisia, the jurist Yadh Ben Achour chaired the 2011 Higher Authority for the Realization of the Objectives of the Revolution, of Political Reform, and of the Democratic Transition. In *La deuxième Fâtiha* (2011) he argued that a Qur'anic ethic can underwrite a universal account of human rights, freedom, and the rule of law. Arguments of this kind, and the dispute over Tunisia's reversal after July 2021, replaced the expectation of one regional path.
