# Sacred Sound and Sonic Theology

A central concept in the study of music and religion, reflecting the intersection between the sonic dimension and the theological and spiritual dimensions of religious practice.

## Definition

Sacred Sound and Sonic Theology refers to a way of understanding religious music not as ritual ornament but as **a theological expression parallel to speech and text**, in which music carries independent doctrinal and spiritual meanings.

## Guy Beck's Model: Sound as Divinity in Hinduism

In *Sonic Theology: Hinduism and Sacred Sound* (1993), Guy Beck presents a central argument: that the Hindu tradition, drawing on the idea of *nada brahman* (sound as ultimate reality) and on mantra traditions, goes further than regarding sound as merely a means of worshipping the deity. It regards the sacred syllable "Om" as a manifestation of divinity itself, not merely a symbol of it. Correctly pronounced sound, articulated with proper recitation, produces an objective spiritual effect regardless of the listener's understanding of its linguistic meaning. This contrasts with discursive (textual) theology, which makes the rational understanding of meaning a condition for spiritual effect.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Qur'anic recitation (tajwid) occupies a special status parallel to this interest in sonic theology. Scholars of Qur'anic readings developed a precise discipline for regulating the articulation points of letters and the rules of elongation (*madd*) and nasalization (*ghunna*); in the tradition's own understanding, careful vocal performance is not aesthetic ornament but part of preserving the text in the form in which it was revealed. A hadith reported in the canonical collections urges believers to beautify the Qur'an with their voices. Meanwhile, classical Arabic music theory (for example al-Kindi and al-Farabi) developed independent theories on the effect of melodic modes on the soul. The historical juristic debate on the permissibility of singing and instrumental music, however, has remained a contested question, separate from the consensually acknowledged merit of Qur'anic recitation.

## The Ethnomusicological Approach of Bruno Nettl

Bruno Nettl, a leading figure of modern ethnomusicology, offers a methodological framework for studying religious music across cultures without assuming the superiority of one model (such as the classical Western model) over others. He focuses on the function of music within its own specific ritual context, which allows very different religious sound patterns (Gregorian chant, Qur'anic recitation, Buddhist chanting) to be compared on equal methodological footing rather than through a predetermined value hierarchy.

## Sound as a Carrier of Collective Identity

Beyond the individual theological dimension, researchers note that recurring religious sound (the daily call to prayer, church bells, temple chanting) also creates a collective sense of shared time and place among members of the same religious community. Hearing this sound at a specific time becomes a sensory indicator of the listener's membership in a broader community sharing the same temporal rhythm of religious life, regardless of its direct doctrinal content.
