# Communities of Practice in Sociolinguistics

## Definition

A community of practice is a concept that Penelope Eckert and Sally McConnell-Ginet borrowed from social learning theory (Jean Lave, Étienne Wenger) and introduced into sociolinguistics in their landmark article "Think Practically and Look Locally" (1992). It describes a group of individuals who gather around a shared endeavour and, through repeated interaction, develop common practices (ways of speaking, values, beliefs and relations of power) that form a collective identity.

## An Alternative to Abstract Social Categories

The concept emerged as a critique of the classical variationist sociolinguistics associated with William Labov, which links linguistic variables directly to abstract demographic categories (class, gender, age) without explaining the mechanism of that link. Eckert and McConnell-Ginet argued that this abstraction may obscure or distort how the social meaning of variables actually takes shape on the ground. They proposed studying language through concrete social practices within specific groups, rather than through broad statistical categories.

## Eckert's Study at Belten High

Eckert applied this framework in her extended ethnographic study of adolescents in an American suburb (*Jocks and Burnouts*, 1989; and later *Linguistic Variation as Social Practice*, 2000). She showed that two socially distinct groups within the same high school, the "Jocks," oriented toward the institution of the school, and the "Burnouts," closer to street culture outside it, developed distinct phonetic patterns (such as their differing participation in the Northern Cities Vowel Shift). These patterns cannot be explained by social class alone, but by daily practice and local belonging within the group.

## The Local Construction of Social Meaning

The framework assumes that a linguistic variable does not carry a fixed social meaning in advance (for example, that one form is "prestigious" and another "vernacular" across all contexts). Instead, the variable acquires its meaning from its repeated use within the practices of a particular group. The same variable may become a sign of rebellion in one group and a sign of institutional commitment in another. This allows the explanation of fine-grained variation that broad demographic categories (such as "women" or "the working class") cannot account for.

## Impact on Language and Gender Studies

The concept reshaped language and gender studies in particular. It shifted the question from "How do men and women speak differently?" to "How are gender identities built through local linguistic practices within specific groups?" It became a methodological foundation for a third wave of variationist sociolinguistics, one that focuses on stylistic meaning rather than on the abstract statistical correlation between a variable and a category.
