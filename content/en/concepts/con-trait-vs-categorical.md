# Trait vs Categorical Models

A central classification debate in the diagnosis of personality disorders, contrasting two approaches. The first is the **categorical model**, which treats each personality disorder as a separate category that a person either has or does not have, according to a particular threshold of criteria. The second is the **dimensional or trait-based model**, which views personality traits as continuous spectrums on which all people are distributed to varying degrees, with no sharp dividing line between "normal" and "disordered."

## The Traditional Categorical Model

Since DSM-III (1980), the diagnostic manual has used a categorical model for personality disorders (eleven in DSM-III; ten in DSM-IV, DSM-5 and DSM-5-TR) grouped into three "clusters" (A, B, and C). A patient is diagnosed with one of them if they meet a certain number of **polythetic criteria** from a longer list. This model has drawn repeated methodological criticism: substantial overlap between diagnoses (comorbidity), heterogeneity within a single diagnosis (two patients with the same diagnosis may share only a few of the criteria), and arbitrary thresholds between the clinical state and normal personality traits.

## The Dimensional Model and the Big Five

The criticism rests on a broad body of research in personality psychology, in particular the **Big Five** or **Five-Factor Model**: openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism. Many studies have shown that personality disorders can be described more accurately as extremes on these continuous dimensions, rather than as separate categories.

## The Alternative Model in DSM-5 (AMPD)

In response to this debate, the DSM-5 personality disorders work group developed the **Alternative Model for Personality Disorders** (AMPD). It is a hybrid model that combines the dimensional and the categorical. It assesses personality functioning in the self and in interpersonal relations (Criterion A) through levels of severity, and describes five broad domains of pathological traits (Criterion B), each with more specific trait facets (25 in all): **negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism**. However, in 2012 the American Psychiatric Association Board of Trustees decided to keep the traditional categorical model in Section II (the official basis for clinical diagnosis and insurance), while the alternative model was placed in **Section III** as an "emerging measure and model" that needs further research. DSM-5-TR (2022) retained this arrangement.

## ICD-11

The World Health Organization's ICD-11 (in effect from 2022) took a more thoroughly dimensional route: it replaced the separate personality disorder categories with a single diagnosis of personality disorder graded as mild, moderate or severe, with optional trait domain qualifiers (negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, anankastia) and a borderline pattern specifier.

## Arguments on Each Side

Proponents of the dimensional model argue that it is more scientifically valid, less arbitrary, and better reflects clinical reality. However, it is more complex to apply in everyday practice, and it is harder to teach and to document quickly in medical and insurance records. Proponents of the categorical model maintain that categories, despite their flaws, provide a practical shared clinical language among physicians, and are needed for documentation, insurance reimbursement, and comparative epidemiological research over time.

## Broader Impact

This debate intersects with similar disputes in other diagnostic areas (such as the research proposal RDoC), and reflects a deeper tension in contemporary psychiatry. On one side is the need for a practical classification that can be used quickly in clinical settings. On the other is the need for a model that is more scientifically precise and reflects the actual complexity of individual differences in personality.
