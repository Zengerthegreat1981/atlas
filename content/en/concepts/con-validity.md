# Validity: Whether a Measure Measures What It Claims to Measure

**Validity** is the quality of a measurement or a research conclusion: the extent to which it actually captures what it claims to capture. A test of intelligence is valid if it measures intelligence and not merely test-taking skill; a study of a policy's effect is valid if its conclusion about cause and effect is sound. Validity is not a property a measure either has or lacks once and for all; it is a matter of degree, of evidence, and of the use to which the measure is put. It is the central standard by which social science judges its own instruments.

## Definition and Development

The modern concept of validity was established by Lee J. Cronbach and Paul E. Meehl in "Construct Validity in Psychological Tests" (1955). They argued that when a test is interpreted as measuring an attribute that cannot be directly observed — intelligence, anxiety, authoritarianism — its validation requires a **nomological network**: a web of theoretical propositions linking the construct to observable outcomes. A measure of anxiety is valid, on this account, to the extent that it behaves as the theory of anxiety says it should — correlating with the things anxiety should correlate with and not with the things it should not. Validation became a scientific enterprise in its own right, not a single statistic but a programme of research.

Samuel Messick, in "Validity" (1989), in the third edition of *Educational Measurement*, unified the concept. He argued that the older division into content, criterion, and construct validity was misleading: all validity is construct validity, because every use of a test involves an interpretation, and every interpretation is a claim about a construct. Messick also added a dimension his predecessors had neglected: the **consequences** of testing. A test can be statistically sound and still invalid in use if its consequences — who is selected, who is excluded, what is taught to the test — undermine its purpose. Validity, for Messick, is a judgement that combines evidence with values.

## Key Contributions: Internal, External, and Statistical Validity

Donald Campbell and Julian Stanley, in *Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs for Research* (1963, issued as a book in 1966), distinguished the kinds of validity that apply to research designs rather than tests. **Internal validity** asks whether the study's conclusion about cause and effect is correct: did the treatment really cause the change, or could something else explain it? **External validity** asks whether the conclusion generalizes: to other people, places, and times. A laboratory experiment may have high internal validity and low external validity; a field study may have the reverse. The researcher's job is to manage the trade-off.

Thomas Cook and Donald Campbell, in *Quasi-Experimentation* (1979), extended the framework. They added **statistical conclusion validity** — whether the statistical analysis supports the claimed relationship — and **construct validity of causes and effects**: whether the treatment and the outcome were actually what the researcher claims they were. Their work made clear that validity is not one question but a family of questions, each of which can fail independently. A study can measure the right thing, in the wrong people, with the wrong statistics — and each failure is a different kind of invalidity.

## Examples

Validity is at stake in everyday measurement. A school exam is valid as a measure of learning only if it tests what was taught and not, say, the student's familiarity with the exam format. A survey question about political attitudes is valid only if respondents understand it as the researcher intended. A hiring test is valid only if it predicts job performance rather than social background. In each case, the question is the same: does this instrument measure what we think it measures, for the purpose we are using it for?

The consequences of invalid measurement can be severe. An intelligence test used to sort children into tracks is invalid if its scores reflect language background rather than ability — and the children sorted on that basis pay the price. A risk assessment used in criminal justice is invalid if it predicts arrest rather than reoffending, because arrest itself reflects policing patterns. Validity is therefore not an academic nicety; it is a matter of fairness.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Messick's unified view of validity has been widely adopted, but it raises a difficult question: if validity depends on the consequences of a test's use, then validity is partly a moral and political judgement, not a purely technical one. Some researchers welcome this; others worry that it makes validity unmanageable, since the consequences of a test can never be fully known in advance. Campbell's framework, meanwhile, has been criticized for treating validity as a property of designs rather than of the inferences researchers draw from them.

Validity remains central to social research. It now frames debates about algorithmic measurement — whether a credit score, a hiring algorithm, or a facial-recognition system measures what it claims to, and for whom it fails; about cross-cultural research, where a measure valid in one society may be meaningless in another; and about the replication crisis, in which findings that seemed valid have failed to reproduce. The questions Cronbach and Meehl posed in 1955 — what does this measure mean, and what evidence would show it? — are still the questions that decide whether social science can be trusted.
