# Grundnorm (Basic Norm)

The **basic norm** (in German: *Grundnorm*) is a central concept in **Hans Kelsen**'s **pure theory of law**. It answers a question that threatens any hierarchical account of the legal system: if every rule draws its validity from a higher rule, from where does the **highest rule at the top** (the first constitution) draw its own validity?

## Kelsen's Solution: A Methodological Assumption, Not an Enacted Rule

Kelsen answers that the validity of the first constitution is not derived from a higher rule actually enacted (since none exists), but from a **methodologically presupposed rule**: "one ought to act in accordance with the first constitution". This rule is not an enacted legal rule but a **transcendental-logical presupposition** (borrowing a term from Kant), which any jurist or judge presupposes if they wish to treat a legal system as a binding system rather than mere facts of naked force.

## Effectiveness as a Condition, Not a Source

Kelsen requires that the legal system as a whole be **effective in a general sense** (that is, generally obeyed) for its basic norm to be presupposed. But for him effectiveness is a condition for the possibility of the presupposition, not the source of its logical validity. This distinction has provoked continuing debate as to whether it in fact conceals a social criterion (who holds actual power) behind a purely logical façade.

## A Famous Judicial Use: The Doctrine of Revolution

The concept was actually invoked in judicial decisions after constitutional coups (for example *State v. Dosso* in Pakistan (1958), *Uganda v. Commissioner of Prisons, ex parte Matovu* (1966), and *Madzimbamuto v. Lardner-Burke* in Southern Rhodesia (1968)), where courts treated a new regime that had become effective as having changed the "basic norm". Pakistan's Supreme Court later repudiated *Dosso* in *Asma Jilani v. Government of the Punjab* (1972). The use of the Grundnorm in such cases was criticized as converting an analytical tool into a justification for the fait accompli, and Kelsen's theory itself does not say that judges ought to accept an effective usurper.

## Relation to Hart's Rule of Recognition

H. L. A. Hart's rule of recognition in *The Concept of Law* (1961) plays a comparable role of identifying the ultimate criteria of validity in a legal system. The two differ sharply. For Hart the rule of recognition is not presupposed but exists as a social practice of officials who accept it from the internal point of view, so its existence is a question of fact. For Kelsen the Grundnorm is a presupposition of juristic thought that makes a normative reading of the legal order possible. Later in his career Kelsen described the Grundnorm as a fiction in the sense of Hans Vaihinger's "as if" philosophy.

## A Comparison with the Source of Obligation in Islamic Usul

Usul al-fiqh offers a different solution to the problem of the source of obligation. The validity of rulings, according to the majority of usuliyyun, is derived not from a methodological presupposition transcending any content, but from a specific objective source: **revelation** (the Book and the Sunna), from which the legitimacy of every subsidiary ruling is derived through a chain of derivation proofs (consensus, analogy, juristic preference). In other words, where Kelsen stopped at a "presupposition" with no epistemic content except its logical function, the usuliyyun established an objective epistemic source that dispenses with the formal presupposition. Yet a similar problem arises when the usuliyyun are asked: what is the proof for the authority of the Qur'an itself as a source? Here the theologians resorted to rational proofs (such as its inimitability, *i'jaz*) outside the derivational circle itself, in an intellectual movement structurally parallel to Kelsen's need for a foundation standing outside the legal chain to justify the first link in it.

## The Impact on the Concept of Usurpation and Domination

The concept of the Grundnorm has also been used to explain how a system arising from a revolution or coup can be considered a "valid" legal system once its effectiveness is firmly established. This corresponds to an old jurisprudential debate about the legitimacy of the "dominator" (*al-mutaghallib*) who seizes power by force: whereas the early jurists required a valid contract for the imamate, later jurists (such as al-Mawardi, in his treatment of governorship by seizure (*imarat al-istila'*), and Ibn Jama'a (d. 733/1333) more explicitly) permitted recognition of the legitimacy of the de facto situation in order to preserve the unity of the community. This approaches the logic of accepting the "new basic norm" in Kelsen without any moral justification for the seizure itself.
