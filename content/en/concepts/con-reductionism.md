# Reductionism

Reductionism is the philosophical and methodological view that complex phenomena (such as consciousness, behaviour, and social systems) can be explained by analysing them into simpler components, or by deriving the theories that describe them from the laws of a more basic science. The term covers several distinct theses that are often run together, and much of the debate about it turns on which thesis is meant.

Historical antecedents go back to ancient atomism, but the modern mechanistic programme is usually traced to the seventeenth century. **René Descartes** treated animal bodies (and, in principle, the human body) as machines whose operations could be explained by matter in motion, while holding that the rational mind was a separate substance. In the twentieth century the **Vienna Circle** and the Unity of Science movement (notably Otto Neurath's project of an encyclopedia of unified science) sought a common scientific language and, in some versions, the reduction of the special sciences to physics.

## Distinctions and Theoretical Definition

- **Ontological reductionism**: the claim that complex entities are nothing over and above their material constituents and the relations among them (for example, the claim that mental states are nothing but brain states).
- **Methodological reductionism**: the research strategy of isolating variables and studying parts in order to understand the whole. It has been very productive in chemistry and molecular biology and is compatible with rejecting the ontological thesis.
- **Theory reduction (epistemological reductionism)**: the aspiration to derive the laws of one theory from those of a more basic one. Ernest Nagel's *The Structure of Science* (1961) gave the classic model, in which the laws of the reduced theory are deduced from those of the reducing theory together with bridge principles linking their vocabularies. Oppenheim and Putnam's "Unity of Science as a Working Hypothesis" (1958) proposed a layered picture running from elementary particles up through cells and organisms to societies.
- **Biological reductionism in psychiatry**: the tendency to explain psychological disorders chiefly through neurochemical or genetic mechanisms, often popularised as a "chemical imbalance" account, while setting aside psychological, social, and cultural factors.

## Critiques and Alternatives

Philosophers have argued that strict theory reduction rarely succeeds in practice: the vocabularies of higher-level sciences may not map neatly onto lower-level ones, and the same higher-level state may be realised by different physical states (the multiple-realisability argument associated with Hilary Putnam and Jerry Fodor). Physicist Philip W. Anderson's essay "More Is Different" (*Science*, 1972) argued that new properties appear at each level of complexity that are not usefully explained by the laws of the level below. This is the family of ideas discussed under **emergence**, which is the main conceptual alternative to reductionism: wholes may have properties and causal patterns that are not captured by describing their parts alone, though emergentists differ on whether such properties are merely epistemic or genuinely ontological.

In psychiatry, George Engel's paper "The Need for a New Medical Model: A Challenge for Biomedicine" (*Science*, 1977) criticised the biomedical model as reductionist and proposed the **biopsychosocial model**, which holds that biological, psychological, and social levels each contribute to illness and require explanation. Psychoanalytic and humanistic traditions raised related objections, and Gestalt psychology's emphasis on organised wholes is another historical counterweight. Reductive neuroscience, for its part, has produced real advances in psychopharmacology and in understanding brain function, and the point of the critique is not to deny these but to resist the claim that one level of explanation is sufficient.

## Distinction from Neighbouring Concepts

Reductionism differs from **scientific analysis**. Analysis is a methodological tool that breaks a system into elements while acknowledging their interactions; reductionism, in its stronger forms, is a doctrinal position that denies the independent explanatory standing of higher levels. Explanatory pluralism, which treats explanations at different levels as complementary, is a common middle position.
