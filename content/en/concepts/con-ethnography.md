# Ethnography

## Definition

Ethnography is the study of a social world through sustained, first-hand participation in it. The ethnographer spends extended time — months, often years — living alongside the people studied, observing their daily routines, taking part in their activities, listening to their conversations, and recording what happens in detailed fieldnotes. The aim is not to test a hypothesis from outside but to understand a way of life from the inside: how its members see the world, what counts as sensible or shameful, how order is produced in ordinary interaction, and how large forces such as class, race, and the state are experienced in everyday life. Ethnography is both a method, the practice of fieldwork, and a product, the written account — the monograph or article — that results from it.

The method rests on participant observation, a deliberate balancing act. The ethnographer participates enough to gain access and insight, yet observes with enough detachment to notice what insiders take for granted. This produces a distinctive kind of knowledge: of process rather than outcome, of meaning rather than frequency, of the gap between what people say they do and what they actually do. A survey can count the unemployed in a neighbourhood; an ethnography can show how unemployment is lived, how it reshapes family life, and how people improvise strategies for getting by.

## Origins and Development

Ethnography emerged in anthropology in the early twentieth century. Bronisław Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922), based on fieldwork among the Trobriand Islanders, established the model: long-term residence, learning the local language, and the study of a society as a functioning whole. Malinowski insisted that the ethnographer's goal was "to grasp the native's point of view", and his methods — the tent pitched in the village, the daily diary, the collection of cases rather than mere rules — became the discipline's standard.

Sociology adopted ethnography through the Chicago school. William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943), a study of an Italian-American slum in Boston, showed that a neighbourhood dismissed as disorganised had its own intricate social order — its own hierarchies, obligations, and codes of loyalty. Erving Goffman's *Asylums* (1961), based on fieldwork in a mental hospital, revealed the "total institution" and the ingenious ways inmates resisted it. In Britain, the community studies of the 1950s and the shop-floor ethnographies of the 1960s and 1970s extended the method to working-class life, trade unions, and the factory.

The 1980s brought a reflexive turn. *Writing Culture* (1986), edited by James Clifford and George Marcus, argued that ethnographies are not transparent windows onto other worlds but authored texts, shaped by the ethnographer's position, by colonial histories, and by the literary conventions of the genre. This critique did not kill ethnography; it made ethnographers more self-conscious about power, representation, and their own presence in the field, and it opened the method to new subjects — elites, institutions, online communities, and the ethnographer's own society.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns objectivity and subjectivity. Critics argue that ethnography depends too heavily on a single researcher's impressions and cannot be replicated. Ethnographers reply that their method has its own disciplines: prolonged engagement, triangulation across sources, the recording of disconfirming evidence, and the presentation of enough raw material for readers to judge the interpretation. Generalisation, they argue, works differently in ethnography than in survey research: not by sampling a population but by identifying mechanisms and processes that can be recognised elsewhere.

A second debate concerns the ethics of fieldwork. The ethnographer's presence changes the field; relationships formed in the field are real relationships, not merely instruments; and publication can expose people who trusted the researcher. Questions of informed consent, anonymity, and the duty to protect informants are therefore acute, and they become more complex in research on illegal or stigmatised activity, where the ethnographer may witness wrongdoing. Contemporary ethnography takes these questions seriously, treating ethics as a continuous practice rather than a form signed at the start.

A third dimension is the scope of the method. Classic ethnography studied a single bounded community; contemporary ethnography is often multi-sited, following people, goods, or ideas across borders, and increasingly digital, studying communities that exist partly or wholly online. Institutional ethnography studies organisations — hospitals, schools, courts — from the standpoint of those who navigate them, while autoethnography uses the researcher's own experience as data. The method's flexibility is one reason for its durability.

## Contemporary Relevance

Ethnography remains one of sociology's most distinctive contributions. It is the method best suited to studying what cannot be captured by a questionnaire: how policies are implemented on the ground, how inequality is experienced day to day, how new technologies are actually used rather than how their designers imagined them. In recent decades, ethnographies of the gig economy, of immigration enforcement, of financial trading floors, and of online gaming communities have shown how the method adapts to new social forms. At the same time, ethnography has influenced neighbouring fields — design, public health, and management — where "going into the field" is now a standard way of understanding users and clients. Its enduring value lies in a simple insight: to understand social life, it is not enough to count it or to theorise about it; sometimes you have to be there, watching, listening, and taking notes.
