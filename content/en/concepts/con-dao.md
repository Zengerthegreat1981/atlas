# Dao (道): The Cosmic Principle

*Dao* (道, formerly romanised *Tao*) is the founding concept of philosophical Daoism. It is usually translated "the Way" or "the path", but its meaning goes beyond that. In the *Daodejing* (also *Tao Te Ching*, attributed to Laozi) the Dao is the source and pattern of the cosmos, the way things proceed when no one interferes. It is not a law in the positivist sense, nor a personal god, nor matter in the physical sense.

## Texts and Dating

Tradition ascribes the *Daodejing* to Laozi, an older contemporary of Confucius in the sixth century BCE. Modern scholarship generally dates the text, in its received form, to the fourth or third century BCE and doubts that it was the work of a single author. The oldest surviving manuscripts are the bamboo slips from Guodian (c. 300 BCE, containing excerpts) and the silk manuscripts from Mawangdui (buried 168 BCE). The *Zhuangzi*, named after Zhuang Zhou (fourth century BCE), is the other foundational Daoist text.

## What Distinguishes It

- **Not fully describable**: "The Dao that can be told is not the constant Dao" (*Daodejing* 1). Any definition fixes what is meant to be open. Language can only point, and the Dao is something that the pointing finger cannot capture.
- **Prior to things**: Chapter 25 speaks of something formless and complete that existed before heaven and earth, of which the author says he does not know the name and styles it "Dao". The Dao is not a product of the cosmos but what makes the cosmos possible.
- **Immanent in everything**: "The myriad things carry yin on their backs and embrace yang" (chapter 42). In the *Zhuangzi* (chapter 22) the Dao is said to be present even in the lowliest things.
- **Working through non-interference**: the Dao "does nothing, yet nothing is left undone" (chapter 37), which grounds the ideal of *wuwei*, effortless action in accord with the way things are.

## Difference from Similar Concepts

- The Greek *logos* is usually understood as a rational order accessible to reason, whereas the Dao is an unspeakable pattern of transformation that is followed rather than formulated as laws. Comparison is nonetheless complicated by the varied senses of *logos*.
- Brahman in Advaita Vedanta is the one reality underlying appearances, whereas the Dao is better understood as a pattern of ceaseless transformation. Both are said to exceed ordinary description.
- It differs from the God of the Abrahamic religions in that there is no creating will or purpose.
- It is not literal nothingness (無 *wu*): being and non-being arise together (chapter 2, chapter 40), so the Dao is the source in which both appear.

## The Dao in Other Traditions

The word *dao* was not exclusive to Daoists. In Confucian texts the Dao is the proper way of human conduct and rule: Confucius says in the *Analects* (4.8) that a person who hears the Way in the morning may die content in the evening. For Confucians the Dao is chiefly a moral and political path, whereas the Daoists, in the *Daodejing* and *Zhuangzi*, apply the term to the spontaneous course of nature. The two uses differ and should not be conflated; the *Liji* (Record of Rites) belongs to the Confucian usage.

## Its Place in the Later Tradition

In the second century CE, religious Daoism (such as the Celestial Masters movement, founded in 142 CE) venerated Laozi as a deity, and the Dao acquired cosmological and ritual dimensions. In the third century CE the "Learning of the Mysterious" (*Xuanxue*) of thinkers such as Wang Bi (226-249) gave the *Daodejing* a metaphysical reading in which non-being (*wu*) is the root of all things. In the twentieth century Martin Heidegger took an interest in the Dao: he worked with Paul Shih-yi Hsiao in 1946 on a partial translation of the *Daodejing*, and in *Unterwegs zur Sprache* (1959) he reflects on "way" (*Weg*) and on the Chinese word Tao.
