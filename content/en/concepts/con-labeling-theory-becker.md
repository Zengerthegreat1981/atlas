# Labeling Theory and Moral Entrepreneurs in Becker

## Definition

An analytical framework formulated by the American sociologist Howard Becker that shifts the question of the sociology of deviance from the characteristics of the "deviant" individual to the process of social classification, and the power that produces the label of "deviance" in the first place.

## Historical Context

Becker's theory arose within a symbolic interactionism rooted in the Chicago School tradition, and as a response to the approaches then dominant in the sociology of deviance and crime, which searched for individual causes (psychological, biological or social-structural such as poverty) to explain why some individuals become "deviant." Becker held that this very question wrongly assumes that deviance is an objective quality inherent in the act. Instead, one should study the social process through which particular acts are classified as "deviant" in the first place, and who holds the power of this classification.

## Main Ideas and Method

In *Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance* (1963), Becker argued that "deviance" is not a quality objectively inherent in a particular act but the product of an interactive social process: "Social groups create deviance by making the rules whose infraction constitutes deviance, and by applying those rules to particular people and labeling them as outsiders." From this starting point he formulated the concept of "Moral Entrepreneurs": individuals or groups with authority or social influence who work actively to impose new rules (rule creators) or to apply existing rules strictly to others (rule enforcers), thereby attaching the label "deviant" to those who violate them. Becker also studied the "deviant career" through his field study of marijuana users: how the individual gradually learns, through continuous interaction with a deviant subgroup that teaches him the meaning, the technique and the moral justification, to enter a stable "deviant" social role. This is a process of social learning, not a prior individual predisposition.

## Impact and Critique

Labeling theory became a central framework in the sociology of deviance. It shifted the research focus from the supposed individual psychological or biological characteristics of the "deviant" to the process of social classification and the power behind it: who holds the power to formulate the rules, and who holds the power to apply them selectively to particular groups and not others. The theory was later developed by Edwin Lemert in his distinction between "primary" and "secondary" deviance, and it deeply influenced the critique of class and racial discrimination in the contemporary criminal justice system (who is classified as a "criminal" depends on who applies the law to whom). Some critics raised objections to labeling theory regarding its tendency to portray the "deviant" as a purely passive victim of social classification, sometimes ignoring his individual responsibility or his own motives. Objections were also raised to its difficulty in explaining the nature of the first act that set off the labeling process in the first place, before any social classification. Nevertheless, this theory remains among the most influential frameworks in the history of the sociology of deviance and crime.
