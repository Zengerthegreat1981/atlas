# Voting Systems

## Definition

Voting systems are the rules by which individual votes are translated into collective decisions: who may vote, how votes are cast and counted, and how they are converted into seats, offices, or outcomes. The simplest system is plurality, or first-past-the-post, in which the candidate with the most votes wins; others include proportional representation, in which seats are allocated in proportion to votes; ranked-choice and preferential systems, in which voters order candidates; and two-round systems, in which a second ballot is held if no candidate wins an outright majority. Anthropologists and political scientists study voting systems not as neutral technical devices but as institutions that shape who wins, who participates, and what kind of politics results.

## Origins and Development

The modern study of voting systems begins with Maurice Duverger's *Political Parties* (1951). Duverger observed a striking regularity, now known as Duverger's law: plurality systems in single-member districts tend to produce two-party competition, while proportional representation tends to produce multiparty systems. The mechanism is both mechanical — small parties win few seats under plurality rules — and psychological: voters abandon parties they expect to lose, so as not to waste their vote. Duverger's insight was that electoral rules are not neutral containers for politics; they actively shape the party system itself.

Douglas Rae, in *The Political Consequences of Electoral Laws* (1967), made the argument comparative and systematic. Studying twenty Western democracies, he showed that electoral laws affect not only the number of parties but also the proportionality of results, the competitiveness of elections, and the stability of governments. Proportional systems, he found, produce fairer representation of votes but more fragmented parliaments and coalition governments; plurality systems produce clearer majorities but at the cost of excluding smaller parties. Arend Lijphart's *Democracies* (1984) developed this into a typology of democratic models: majoritarian democracies, exemplified by Britain, concentrate power in a single party, while consensus democracies, exemplified by the Netherlands and Switzerland, disperse it through proportional representation, coalitions, and power-sharing. Lijphart argued that consensus systems were better at representing diverse societies — a claim with obvious anthropological resonance.

Rein Taagepera and Matthew Shugart, in *Seats and Votes* (1989), supplied the mathematical foundations, showing how district magnitude — the number of seats per constituency — is among the most powerful determinants of proportionality. A country that elects one member per district will behave very differently from one that elects ten, even if both call themselves democracies.

## Key Debates

The first debate concerns fairness versus governability. Proportional systems represent voters accurately but can produce unstable coalitions and give small parties disproportionate leverage. Plurality systems produce stable single-party governments but can leave large minorities unrepresented — a party may win half the votes and a fraction of the seats. There is no system that maximises both values; every choice is a trade-off, and different societies weight the trade-off differently.

A second debate concerns inclusion. Voting systems determine whose votes count. Rules about registration, districting, and ballot access can include or exclude whole populations, and anthropologists have documented how electoral design has been used both to empower and to marginalise. Gerrymandering — drawing district boundaries to favour one party — is the most notorious example, but subtler mechanisms abound: thresholds that bar small parties, or the choice between voting in person, by post, or electronically, each of which favours different groups.

A third debate concerns the anthropology of voting itself. Political scientists model voters as rational choosers; anthropologists observe what voting means to people. In many societies, voting is less an expression of individual preference than a collective act — a family, a clan, or a village voting together — and the secret ballot, designed to free the individual, can sit uneasily with social obligations. Ethnographies of elections have shown how votes are bought, how candidates perform generosity, and how elections become rituals of community as much as mechanisms of choice. The meaning of a vote, like the meaning of any social act, depends on its context.

## Contemporary Relevance

Voting systems are under active debate almost everywhere. New Zealand adopted proportional representation in 1993; Britain debated it in a 2011 referendum and rejected it; several American cities and states have adopted ranked-choice voting; and debates over electronic voting, voter identification, and access to the ballot are politically charged in many democracies. Beyond the state, voting systems shape the governance of international organisations, trade unions, and Indigenous councils, where questions of representation are often more contested than in national politics.

For anthropology, voting systems are a reminder that democracy is not a single thing. The rules that translate votes into power are cultural artefacts — chosen, contested, and changed — and they determine, in part, whose voice is heard. To study them is to study how societies decide, collectively, what counts as fair.
