# Islamic Theological Debates

Kalam arose from political and then doctrinal questions. Who is the believer? Does a person create his own acts? Does God have attributes additional to His essence? Is the Quran created? W. Montgomery Watt, in *The Formative Period of Islamic Thought*, argues that these questions emerged early, in the late first century AH, and that answers to them took shape among the disputing sects before the doctrinal schools became settled.

## Faith and the Judgment on the Grave Sinner

After the first Discord, the heresiographers report that the Kharijites declared the grave sinner an unbeliever, that the Murji'a deferred judgment on him to God, and that al-Hasan al-Basri called him a hypocrite. In the same traditional account, Wasil ibn Ata (d. 131 AH/748 CE) withdrew from al-Hasan's circle with the doctrine of "the intermediate position between the two positions," and this withdrawal is presented as the beginning of Mu'tazilism. Modern studies treat the classroom scene as a later story. Al-Shahrastani (d. 548 AH/1153 CE), in *al-Milal wa-al-nihal*, arranges the sects under these labels.

## Divine Decree and Attributes

Ma'bad al-Juhani and Ghaylan al-Dimashqi, in the Umayyad period, denied that God decrees human acts of disobedience. They were answered by advocates of compulsion (*jabr*), among them Jahm ibn Safwan (d. 128 AH/746 CE), who also refused eternal attributes distinct from the essence. Classical heresiography then lists five Mu'tazili principles: divine unity, justice, the promise and the threat, the intermediate position, and commanding right and forbidding wrong. That list is a school platform; it should not all be read back onto Wasil. The People of Hadith opposed the Mu'tazila by affirming the attributes without interpretive reduction (*ta'wil*).

## The Mihna and al-Ash'ari's Turn

In 218 AH/833 CE al-Ma'mun declared the createdness of the Quran a doctrine of the state and began the inquisition (*mihna*), which continued under his two successors. Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 241 AH/855 CE) held that the Quran is the uncreated speech of God. Al-Mutawakkil ended the mihna around 234 AH/848–849 CE. Around 300 AH/912 CE, Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 324 AH/935–936 CE) left Mu'tazilism. He affirmed the attributes without likeness to creatures and taught *kasb* (acquisition) in human acts. *Maqalat al-Islamiyyin* surveys the doctrines of the sects. His own arguments are set out in *al-Luma'* and in *al-Ibana*; the received text of *al-Ibana* is closer to traditionist language than *al-Luma'* is. In Samarqand, al-Maturidi (d. 333 AH/944 CE) taught a doctrine close to al-Ash'ari's on the attributes and on acts, while allowing reason to establish that one must acknowledge God before revelation arrives.

## After the Disputes

Al-Ghazali gave Aristotelian logic a recognized place in legal theory and in theology. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 606 AH/1210 CE) then recast kalam as a philosophical theology in debate with Ibn Sina. The Hanbali scholar Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728 AH/1328 CE) criticized that development, in particular the claim that rational proof overrides scripture. The argument did not stop with him. The teaching manuals of al-Taftazani (d. 792 AH/1390 CE) and al-Sayyid al-Sharif al-Jurjani (d. 816 AH/1413 CE) carried this philosophical form of kalam into the next century. Other schools remained active alongside the Sunni argument: the Ibadiyya, the Zaydiyya and the Imamiyya.

## Limits of the Classical Account

The heresiographical books present an orderly picture that goes back to the hadith of the "division of the community" (*iftiraq al-umma*). That is, each sect has a beginning and a founder. Michael Cook, in *Early Muslim Dogma*, argues that the sources for the earliest doctrinal positions are few and late, so that tracing those positions historically is fraught with doubt. Josef van Ess, in his encyclopedic study of the second and third centuries AH, redrew the map of persons and opinions from the surviving texts rather than from the later classification of sects. Moreover, the binary image of "reason versus transmission" oversimplifies reality: the Hanafis, the Malikis and the Shafi'is held varied positions between the two.
