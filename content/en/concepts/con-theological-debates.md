# Islamic Theological Debates

Kalam arose from political and then doctrinal questions. Who is the believer? Does a person create his own acts? Does God have attributes additional to His essence? Is the Quran created? W. Montgomery Watt, in *The Formative Period of Islamic Thought*, argues that these questions emerged early, in the late first century AH, and that answers to them took shape among the disputing sects before the doctrinal schools became settled.

## Faith and the Judgment on the Grave Sinner

After the first Discord, the Kharijites declared the grave sinner an unbeliever, the Murji'a deferred judgment on him to God, and al-Hasan al-Basri said that he was a hypocrite. In this context, according to the traditional account, Wasil ibn 'Ata (d. 131 AH/748 CE) withdrew from the circle of al-Hasan, holding the doctrine of "the intermediate position between two positions," and this was the beginning of Mu'tazilism. Al-Shahrastani (d. 548 AH/1153 CE), in *al-Milal wa-al-Nihal*, presents these sects in their categories.

## Divine Decree and Attributes

Ma'bad al-Juhani and Ghaylan al-Dimashqi denied divine decree (*qadar*) in the late Umayyad period. They were answered by the Jabriyya, among them Jahm ibn Safwan (d. 128 AH/746 CE), who also denied the attributes. The Mu'tazila then built their system on five principles: divine unity, justice, the promise and the threat, the intermediate position between two positions, and commanding the right and forbidding the wrong. The People of Hadith opposed them by affirming the attributes without interpretation (*ta'wil*).

## The Mihna and al-Ash'ari's Turn

In 218 AH/833 CE al-Ma'mun declared the createdness of the Quran a doctrine of the state and tested the scholars. Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 241 AH/855 CE) held firm to the view that the Quran is the uncreated speech of God, until al-Mutawakkil lifted the mihna around 234 AH/848 CE. Decades later, around 300 AH/912 CE, Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 324 AH/935 CE) turned away from Mu'tazilism and set out a middle way that affirms the attributes without anthropomorphism and holds the doctrine of *kasb* (acquisition) in human acts. He stated this in *Maqalat al-Islamiyyin* and *al-Ibana*. In Samarqand, al-Maturidi (d. 333 AH/944 CE) established a close doctrine that gives reason a greater place.

## After the Disputes

Aristotelian logic entered kalam through al-Ghazali and al-Razi, and late kalam was transformed into a religious philosophy. The Hanbali school, through Ibn Taymiyya, criticized this development. On the margins, other schools remained alive: the Ibadiyya, the Zaydiyya and the Imamiyya.

## Limits of the Classical Account

The heresiographical books present an orderly picture that goes back to the hadith of the "division of the community" (*iftiraq al-umma*). That is, each sect has a beginning and a founder. Michael Cook, in *Early Muslim Dogma*, argues that the sources for the earliest doctrinal positions are few and late, so that tracing those positions historically is fraught with doubt. Josef van Ess, in his encyclopedic study of the second and third centuries AH, redrew the map of persons and opinions from the surviving texts rather than from the later classification of sects. Moreover, the binary image of "reason versus transmission" oversimplifies reality: the Hanafis, the Malikis and the Shafi'is held varied positions between the two.
