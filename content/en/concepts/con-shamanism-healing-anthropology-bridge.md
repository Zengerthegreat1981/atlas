# Shamanism and Healing in Anthropology

## From Siberia to a General Term

The word *shaman* comes from the Tungusic languages of Siberia. Anthropologists came to use it to describe healers and mediators who enter altered states of consciousness to communicate with spirits or other worlds for the purposes of healing and divination. Mircea Eliade, in *Shamanism and the Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* (French original 1951; English translation 1964), formulated the broadest definition: shamanism is a "technique of ecstasy" in which the shaman journeys with his spirit to the sky or the underworld. Many researchers criticized this definition because it exaggerates the unification of divergent phenomena. I. M. Lewis, in *Ecstatic Religion* (1971), opposed it, linking shamanism to the phenomenon of spirit possession and offering a social theory: the phenomenon of possession by spirits is used by women and the disadvantaged as a means of claiming attention and status when other channels are unavailable. This explanation remains controversial.

## The Symbolic Efficacy of Healing

In "The Effectiveness of Symbols" (1949), Claude Lévi-Strauss analyzed a healing chant among the Cuna Indians of Panama that helps a woman through a difficult childbirth. He argued that the shaman provides the patient with a symbolic language that organizes her chaotic pain into a coherent myth, so that her physiological condition changes, and he compared this with psychoanalysis. In "The Sorcerer and His Magic" (1949), he added that healing succeeds through the convergence of the patient's trust, the healer's trust, and the consensus of the community. James L. Dow extended this in "Universal Aspects of Symbolic Healing" (*American Anthropologist*, 1986), proposing that symbolic healing has a universal structure: a shared cultural environment, a persuasive healer, and vocabulary familiar to the patient. As for Michael Harner, in *The Way of the Shaman* (1980), he transferred the practice to Western settings in the form of "core shamanism," and he was criticized for selecting elements from scattered cultures and emptying them of their context.

## Arab and Islamic Traditions: Jinn, Zar, and Ruqya

In the Arab world there is no shaman in the Siberian sense, but there are comparable systems: belief in possession by jinn, Islamic *ruqya* (healing recitation of the Qur'an), *zar* rituals in Egypt, Sudan, and the Gulf, and the Moroccan Hamadsha brotherhood. Vincent Crapanzano, in *The Hamadsha* (1973), studied a Moroccan Sufi group that heals through dance and possession by a female jinn named 'Aisha Qandisha, and highlighted the intersection between folk medicine and Sufism. Janice Boddy, in *Wombs and Alien Spirits* (1989), studied *zar* rituals among women in northern Sudan as a symbolic language about femininity and society. In classical texts, Ibn al-Qayyim, in *Zad al-Ma'ad*, examined what is called the Prophetic medicine, including *ruqya* and the treatment of epilepsy; and Ibn Taymiyya distinguished permissible *ruqya* from seeking help from jinn, a doctrinal distinction that defines what is acceptable among practices.

## Limits of the Connection

The claim that "shamanism is a system of knowledge and healing" faces reservations. The concept of the shaman derives from European accounts of Siberian peoples and is difficult to transfer, without modification, to the cultures of the Americas, Africa, and the Arab world. Michael Taussig, in *Shamanism, Colonialism, and the Wild Man* (1987), showed how the image of the shaman is also shaped by the imagination of the colonizer. Moreover, theories of symbolic efficacy do not explain all healing: different illnesses entail different effects, and these theories may not be treated as a substitute for medicine. Finally, folk healing should not be reduced to psychological catharsis, since practitioners understand it in a fundamentally different way.
