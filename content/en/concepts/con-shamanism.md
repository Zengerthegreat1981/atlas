# Shamanism: The Spirit Mediator and the Anthropology of Healing

**Shamanism** is a religious complex centred on a specialist — the shaman — who enters altered states of consciousness, often described as soul flight or trance, to communicate with spirits on behalf of a community. The shaman heals the sick, divines the causes of misfortune, guides the souls of the dead, and negotiates with the spirits who control game, weather, and fertility. The word itself comes from the Tungus *saman*, a term recorded among Siberian peoples, but anthropologists have used it comparatively for similar practitioners across Siberia, Central and North Asia, the Americas, and beyond. Shamanism is not a single religion with a doctrine and a church; it is a set of techniques, relationships, and cosmologies built around direct, experiential contact with a spirit world.

## Definition and Development

The comparative study of shamanism was established by Mircea Eliade, whose *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* (1951, English edition 1964) defined the shaman as a master of ecstasy — one who can leave the body at will and journey to the sky or the underworld. For Eliade, shamanism was an archaic, even primordial, form of religion, rooted in the religious experience of early humanity. His synthesis drew together Siberian, Central Asian, and American materials into a single pattern: the shaman's call through illness or vision, initiation by spirits, mastery of drumming and song, and the healing séance in which the shaman recovers a lost soul or extracts an intruding spirit.

Later anthropologists questioned Eliade's universalism. The term "shaman" had been stretched so far, they argued, that it obscured real differences: the Siberian shaman, the Amazonian ayahuasca healer, the Korean *mudang*, and the Nepalese *jhankri* work within different cosmologies, social roles, and political histories. Some scholars proposed restricting "shaman" to its Siberian and Central Asian core, while others kept it as a comparative label for spirit mediators who use trance. What survived the critique was Eliade's central insight: in many societies, religious authority rests not on scripture or office but on a person's demonstrated ability to enter the spirit world and return with knowledge and power.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Claude Lévi-Strauss, in "The Sorcerer and His Magic" (1949), offered a strikingly social account of shamanic healing. The shaman's cure works, he argued, not through pharmacology but through a shared symbolic language: the shaman, the patient, and the audience all believe in the same cosmology of spirits and bodily forces, so the shaman's performance can reorganise the patient's experience of illness into a meaningful narrative. Healing is thus a collective act of meaning-making, and its efficacy depends on a social consensus about what spirits are and what they can do. Lévi-Strauss's essay became an influential text in the anthropology of healing and in discussions of symbolic efficacy.

Roberte Hamayon and others working in Siberia and Mongolia showed that shamanism is also a matter of alliance and exchange. The shaman does not command spirits so much as bargain with them: offerings are made, promises kept, and debts honoured, in a relationship modelled on human sociality. Caroline Humphrey and Urgunge Onon, in *Shamans and Elders* (1996), a study of the Daur Mongols of northeastern China, examined how shamanic knowledge, authority, and experience are bound up with elders, kinship, and the state. Work of this kind shows that shamanism is not a survival from the archaic past but a living practice that adapts to state power, market economies, and new forms of suffering.

## Ethnographic Examples

Among the Evenk and Yakut peoples of Siberia, the shaman's séance is a public, theatrical event: drumming, singing, and mimetic dance carry the shaman into trance, while the audience watches, questions, and judges the performance. The shaman's costume — hung with iron ornaments, mirrors, and ribbons — is itself a map of the cosmos, and the drum is described as the shaman's horse, carrying the rider between worlds. Among the Shipibo of the Peruvian Amazon, healing involves the ingestion of plant medicines and the singing of *icaros*, songs said to be learned from the plants themselves; intricate geometric designs (*kene*), painted and embroidered mainly by women on cloth and pottery, have been associated with these visions and songs.

In Korea, the *mudang*, most often a woman, is called to her vocation through a spirit sickness that only initiation can cure; her rituals, the *kut*, bless households, appease restless ancestors, and send the dead on their way. In each case, the shaman's authority is earned and continually tested: a shaman who fails to heal loses clients, and a shaman who abuses power may be accused of sorcery.

## Contemporary Debates

Contemporary anthropology debates shamanism on several fronts. One concerns the global spread of "neo-shamanism" — workshops, retreats, and ayahuasca tourism in which urban Westerners seek shamanic experience outside its original social context. Some see this as a creative renewal of spiritual practice; others see it as appropriation that strips shamanism of its communal obligations and endangers both practitioners and the plants and peoples involved. A second debate concerns the relationship between shamanism and biomedicine: in many places, patients consult shamans and clinics in parallel, and anthropologists study how the two systems of healing coexist, compete, and sometimes collaborate.

A third debate returns to the question of definition. Should "shamanism" name a Siberian religion, a cross-cultural type of religious specialist, or a technique of ecstasy found in many religions? Most anthropologists now treat it as a family of practices rather than a single essence, united by the figure of the mediator who journeys between worlds for others. What remains distinctive is the shaman's claim: that healing and knowledge come not from texts or institutions but from direct, embodied encounter with powers beyond the human.
