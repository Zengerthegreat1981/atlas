# Inequality

Inequality is the unequal distribution of valued resources, opportunities, and rewards in a society. Those resources include income and wealth, but also education, health, status, and power. Inequality is not the same as difference: people differ in height, talent, and taste, but inequality refers to differences that are socially patterned, systematic, and consequential, shaping life chances from birth. Where a person is born, to whom, and with what resources, profoundly affects how long they will live, how much they will earn, and how much say they will have in public life. Sociology studies inequality not as a natural fact but as a product of social arrangements that can be explained and, in principle, changed.

## Definition

Sociologists distinguish several dimensions of inequality. Economic inequality concerns the distribution of income and wealth: who owns what, who earns what, and how those shares change over time. Social inequality concerns status and prestige: the respect accorded to different occupations, groups, and ways of life. Political inequality concerns power: who can influence decisions and who must live with decisions made by others. These dimensions are related but not identical. A wealthy person may lack political influence, and a respected professional may earn modestly, but in most societies the dimensions reinforce one another, so that advantage in one sphere brings advantage in others.

Inequality can also be distinguished from poverty. Poverty is an absolute or relative lack of resources; inequality is relational, describing the gap between groups. A society can be poor but equal, or rich but highly unequal.

## Origins and Development

Classical sociology was founded on the study of inequality. Karl Marx, in *Capital* (1867), argued that inequality arises from the ownership of the means of production: capitalists, who own factories and land, extract surplus value from workers, who own only their labour power. For Marx, inequality was not an accident but the engine of capitalism, and it would deepen until the system was transformed. Max Weber, in *Economy and Society* (1922), broadened the analysis, arguing that inequality is organised along three dimensions, class, status, and party, which correspond to economic, social, and political power. A person can be advantaged in one dimension and disadvantaged in another, and the interplay between them shapes the structure of inequality.

Émile Durkheim, in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893), argued that inequality could be functional if it reflected genuine differences in talent and contribution, but pathological when it resulted from inherited privilege. Amartya Sen, in *Inequality Reexamined* (1992), reframed the question by asking "inequality of what?": income and wealth are only proxies for people's capability to live a life they have reason to value. Thomas Piketty, in *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (French 2013; English 2014), documented the long-run rise of wealth inequality, arguing that when the return on capital exceeds economic growth, wealth concentrates in fewer hands.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the causes of inequality. Some explanations emphasise individual differences in talent, effort, and choice; others emphasise structural factors, such as the organisation of labour markets, tax policy, discrimination, and the inheritance of wealth. Sociological research generally finds that structural factors explain more of the variation: two people with similar talents can have very different outcomes depending on their family background, neighbourhood, and schooling. This does not deny individual agency, but it situates agency within constraints.

A second debate concerns the consequences of inequality. Richard Wilkinson and Kate Pickett, in *The Spirit Level* (2009), argued that more unequal societies have worse health, higher crime, lower trust, and poorer educational outcomes, even for the rich, because inequality corrodes social relations. Critics question whether the correlation is causal, and whether inequality itself or the poverty and insecurity that accompany it does the damage.

A third dimension is intersectionality: inequality is organised not only by class but also by gender, race, ethnicity, and disability, and these axes interact. A Black woman, for example, may face disadvantages that are not simply the sum of racism and sexism, but a distinctive form of inequality produced by their intersection.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Inequality has returned to the centre of public debate. The richest one per cent now own a large and growing share of global wealth, while wages for many workers have stagnated. The COVID-19 pandemic exposed and deepened inequalities, as low-paid workers faced greater exposure to the virus and less protection from its economic effects. Climate change is also an inequality issue: those who contributed least to emissions are often those most exposed to its harms.

Critics argue that the concept is too broad, encompassing everything from income gaps to status hierarchies, and that it can obscure the specific mechanisms that produce each form. Others argue that an exclusive focus on inequality can neglect absolute deprivation: in poor societies, raising everyone's living standards may matter more than narrowing gaps. The sociological response is that the level and form of inequality are social choices, shaped by institutions, policies, and struggles.
