# The "Disease of Language" in Max Müller

A central thesis formulated by Max Müller to explain the origin of religious myths from within his comparative philological method. He first advanced it in the essay "Comparative Mythology" (1856) and developed it in *Lectures on the Science of Language* (first series delivered 1861, second series 1863 and published 1864) and in later writings on myth and religion.

## The Central Idea

Müller argues that early humans initially used names and descriptive attributes for tangible natural phenomena (the sun "pursues" the dawn, the storm "wrestles" with the sky). These are natural poetic metaphors, at a linguistic stage still closely tied to their sensory referent. But as generations passed and the metaphorical origin of these names was forgotten, descriptive attributes gradually turned into the names of independent persons and beings. "The dawn that the sun pursues" thus becomes a myth about a goddess pursued by a god (Müller read the story of Daphne and Apollo in this way). Müller summarised the process in the formula that ancient names (*nomina*) became divine beings (*numina*). Müller called this process the "disease of language". He did not mean an accidental malfunction. He meant an inevitable structural result of the nature of language itself, when collective memory loses its connection with the original meaning of words. In this sense, myth is not a lie or a primitive superstition, but an archaeological trace of a language that has forgotten itself.

## From This Thesis to the "Solar Myth"

Since many divine names in the ancient Indo-European languages (according to Müller's philological analysis of their Sanskrit, Greek and Latin roots) go back, in his view, to descriptive attributes of solar and celestial phenomena, he concluded that most of the great Indo-European myths (including some Greek myths about the gods) originally arose from expressions of the movement of the sun, the dawn and the storm. This reading was later known as the school of "Solar Mythology", and became the hallmark of Müller's method for explaining comparative religion. It was subsequently subjected to sharp anthropological criticism, above all from Andrew Lang (`thk-andrew-lang`) and, in different ways, Edward Tylor and James Frazer. They preferred explanations based on the beliefs and customs of living peoples, and on survivals of earlier customs, over philological conclusions drawn from reconstructed linguistic roots. Müller's etymologies also came under attack on linguistic grounds, and the solar-mythology programme had largely lost its standing by the early twentieth century.
