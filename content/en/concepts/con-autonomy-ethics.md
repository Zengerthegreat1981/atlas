# Autonomy in Ethics

Ethical autonomy is the capacity of a rational agent to legislate for themselves rules of conduct grounded in their own reason, rather than submitting to an external authority, a passing desire, or an inherited custom. The autonomous agent is one who can examine their motives and desires, and endorse those that accord with a principle they themselves accept as a universal standard. In this way, they become the author of their moral law rather than a mere executor of it.

In the Islamic moral tradition, the concept of autonomy was not formulated in its modern form, but the idea of the responsibility of the rational, accountable agent is strongly present. The Mu'tazila, in their doctrine of the goodness and badness of things known by reason (*al-husn wa al-qubh al-'aqliyyan*), held that reason has an inherent capacity to perceive moral value before revelation arrives, so that a person is responsible by the judgment of their reason and not by the command alone. The Ash'ari school opposed them, holding that goodness and badness are known only through revelation, thereby locating the source of obligation outside the self. In *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, Ibn Miskawayh weighed the disciplining of the soul through habit against its guidance through reason. Miskawayh's aim in moral cultivation can be read as seeking that virtuous action to proceed from a convinced disposition rather than from external compulsion, which approximates the idea of autonomy in that true virtue springs from within the soul.

In modern Western philosophy, Kant formulated the concept in its most precise form: the autonomous will is one that legislates the universal moral law for itself, in contrast to "heteronomy" (dependence), in which the rule is derived from a source outside the will, such as pleasure, utility, or a bare divine command. For Kant, autonomy is connected to dignity: the rational being deserves respect because it is capable of self-legislation. Later thinkers followed, such as John Rawls, who made autonomy a condition for formulating the principles of justice behind a "veil of ignorance," and Harry Frankfurt, who distinguished between first-order desires and second-order desires, by which an agent endorses their desires and thereby truly owns them.

The concept faces several objections. Feminist thinkers and communitarians (such as Michael Sandel) argue that the Kantian image of the autonomous self is excessively abstract, since it ignores that the human being is a social being whose identity is formed through relationships and affiliations. Accordingly, an alternative, "relational autonomy" (Relational Autonomy), has been developed, which holds that independence does not preclude mutual dependence. The question also arises of the limits of autonomy in cases of addiction or mental illness, in which the agent's capacity for reflection is weakened.

*Illustrative example:* A patient who refuses a life-saving treatment, after fully understanding the consequences of their decision, exercises moral autonomy even if they go against the doctors' opinion, because their decision proceeds from free rational deliberation rather than from ignorance or coercion. The distinction between autonomy as a capacity (Capacity) and as actual exercise (Exercise) appears in the medical and legal context, where the validity of "informed consent" requires that the agent be aware of the alternatives and risks, and not merely enjoy formal freedom of movement.

Contemporary debates in political philosophy and economics branch out from ethical autonomy. Among them is the question of whether capitalist systems expand individual autonomy or restrict it through the mechanisms of advertising and consumption, and the question of digital autonomy in an age of algorithms that predict and steer users' desires. Autonomy is also linked to the concept of "responsibility," since an agent is held morally accountable only to the extent that they acted autonomously. This raises subtle questions about coercion, psychological manipulation, and behavioral addiction, in which the degree of actual autonomy declines without the capacity for rational discrimination being entirely lost.
