# Chiefdom

A chiefdom is a form of political organisation in which a number of villages or communities are united under a single chief, whose authority rests on kinship, rank, and the redistribution of goods rather than on a standing army or a bureaucracy. Chiefdoms are larger and more centralised than the bands and tribes studied by early anthropologists, but less centralised than states. The concept was central to the anthropological theory of political evolution in the twentieth century, and it remains a useful tool for understanding societies in which power is personal, inherited, and bound up with ritual.

## Definition

The classic definition comes from Elman Service's scheme of political evolution, set out in *Primitive Social Organization* (1962). Service distinguished four levels: band, tribe, chiefdom, and state. In a chiefdom, leadership is a permanent office rather than a matter of personal influence; the chief holds a ranked position, usually inherited, and is supported by a hierarchy of lesser chiefs. The chief collects tribute or surplus from the population and redistributes it in feasts, ceremonies, and public works, and this redistribution is the material basis of chiefly legitimacy.

Chiefs are typically distinguished from their subjects by rank, by sacred associations, and by control of prestige goods — fine cloth, shell valuables, metalwork — that circulate through their hands. Unlike a king, however, a chief generally lacks a monopoly of force and a specialised administration. Authority depends on consent, generosity, and the ability to mobilise labour for collective projects such as building mounds, terraces, or irrigation works.

## Origins and Development

Chiefdoms appear in the archaeological record in many parts of the world: the Mississippian societies of North America, with their great mound centres such as Cahokia; the polities of the Pacific, including Hawaii and Tonga; the chiefdoms of West and Central Africa; and the early societies of Mesoamerica and the Andes. They typically emerge where agriculture produces a reliable surplus and where population growth, competition for land, and trade create a need for coordination beyond the village.

Anthropologists have long debated how chiefdoms arise. Robert Carneiro's circumscription theory (1970) argued that chiefdoms and states develop where population pressure is combined with geographical limits, so that defeated groups cannot flee and must submit. Timothy Earle, in *How Chiefs Come to Power* (1997), emphasised the control of staple goods and prestige goods: chiefs build power by controlling the sources of wealth, the routes of exchange, and the ideology that legitimates their position. Hawaiian chiefs, for example, controlled irrigation systems and the distribution of prestige items, and their authority was reinforced by the belief that they were descended from the gods.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The most persistent debate about chiefdoms concerns the evolutionary scheme itself. Critics argue that Service's four stages imply a ladder of progress, with states at the top, and that real societies do not fit neatly into one category. Some societies move between centralised and decentralised forms; some combine chiefly authority with councils, age grades, or other institutions that check the chief's power. Morton Fried argued that the very idea of "chiefdom" obscured the diversity of political forms, and that rank and stratification could take many shapes.

A second debate concerns the nature of chiefly power. Is a chief a redistributor who serves the community, or an exploiter who extracts surplus for personal gain? Ethnographic evidence supports both views. Some chiefs are expected to be generous to the point of impoverishment, and a chief who hoards wealth loses legitimacy. Others use their position to accumulate wives, land, and retainers. The tension between the chief as servant and the chief as master is a recurring theme in the politics of chiefdoms.

A third dimension is the role of ritual and sacred authority. In many chiefdoms the chief is not only a political leader but a religious figure, responsible for the fertility of the land, the success of the harvest, and the favour of the ancestors. This sacred dimension makes chiefly authority hard to challenge, but it also makes the chief vulnerable: a failed harvest or a natural disaster can be read as evidence that the chief has lost the favour of the gods.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Chiefdoms are not only a feature of the past. In many parts of Africa and the Pacific, chiefs continue to exercise authority alongside modern states, allocating land, settling disputes, and representing their communities. Post-colonial governments have alternately suppressed, co-opted, and recognised chiefs, and the relationship between chiefly authority and democratic institutions remains contested. Anthropologists studying these arrangements warn against treating chiefs as timeless traditional figures: colonial administrations often created or strengthened chiefs as instruments of indirect rule, and today's chiefs navigate complex relationships with states, markets, and NGOs.

The concept of the chiefdom has also been criticised for its colonial baggage. Early anthropologists studied chiefdoms in societies that were being incorporated into empires, and their descriptions sometimes served administrative purposes. Contemporary scholarship therefore treats "chiefdom" as a heuristic — a way of describing a pattern of centralised, kinship-based authority — rather than as a natural stage in human development. Used carefully, it remains a valuable concept for comparing how power is organised in societies without states.
