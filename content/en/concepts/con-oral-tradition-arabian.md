# Pre-Islamic Arabian Oral Tradition

The pre-Islamic Arabian oral tradition was the primary cultural, mnemonic, and literary apparatus through which communal memory, customary norms, tribal genealogies (*ansab*), and poetic odes (*qasa'id*) were preserved and transmitted across the Arabian Peninsula prior to the codification of writing in early Islam. Functioning within a nomadic and semi-urban socio-political fabric defined by lineage and seasonal migration, the spoken word served as the authoritative repository of historical consciousness, earning the classical designation of Arabic poetry as the "register of the Arabs" (*diwan al-'Arab*).

## Mechanics of Transmission: From Reciter to Anthologist

The transmission of pre-Islamic poetry operated through an apprenticeship system across distinct historical phases:
- **The Poet and the Transmitter**: Composition was performative and oral. Major pre-Islamic bards (*shu'ara'*, sing. *sha'ir*) were shadowed by reciters (*ruwat*, sing. *rawi*)—often younger poets who memorized a master's corpus, accompanied him at tribal gatherings, and declaimed verses at markets such as 'Ukaz. Through this apprenticeship, tropes, rhythmic patterns, and traditional motifs were learned. The later science of meters (*'arud*), associated with al-Khalil ibn Ahmad (d. c. 175 AH / 791 CE), classified patterns that the oral practice had already used.
- **The Philological Era of Codification (*Tadwin*)**: In the second/eighth century, scholars in Basra and Kufa recorded oral lore in order to stabilize Arabic grammar and to gloss the Qur'an. Anthologists organized what they collected. Hammad al-Rawiya (d. c. 155 AH / 772 CE) is traditionally credited with gathering the *Mu'allaqat*, the celebrated set of long odes, conventionally seven. Al-Mufaddal al-Dabbi (d. c. 168 AH / 784–785 CE) produced *al-Mufaddaliyyat*, and al-Asma'i (d. 216 AH / 831 CE) compiled *al-Asma'iyyat*. The attribution of the *Mu'allaqat* collection to Hammad is a later report, and classical critics already doubted his reliability.
- **Narrative Lore and Tribal History**: Intertwined with poetic verse were the *Ayyam al-'Arab* ("Battle Days of the Arabs"), a genre of narrative oral prose detailing tribal feuds, alliances, chivalric exploits, and raids. These prose accounts provided the historical and situational context necessary for understanding the accompanying panegyric (*madh*), elegy (*ritha'*), and satire (*hija'*).

## The Authenticity Debate: Classical and Modern Critiques

The reliability of oral transmission has generated sustained critical scrutiny in both classical Islamic philology and modern Western scholarship:
- **Classical Doubts on Forgery (*Intihal*)**: Classical critics recognized early on that oral preservation was vulnerable to interpolation, partisan bias, and fabrication. In *Tabaqat fuhul al-shu'ara'*, the Basran philologist Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi (d. 231 AH / 845 CE) proposed criteria to distinguish genuine pre-Islamic verse from verses invented by rival tribes seeking ancestral glory or forged by transmitters like Hammad al-Rawiya and Khalaf al-Ahmar (d. c. 180 AH / 796 CE).
- **The Skeptical Turn in Modern Scholarship**: In 1925, D. S. Margoliouth published "The Origins of Arabic Poetry," arguing that much of the surviving corpus was an Islamic-era creation shaped by religious and grammatical needs. In the same years, Taha Husayn published *Fi al-shi'r al-jahili* (1926). Invoking Cartesian doubt, he argued that a large part of so-called pre-Islamic verse had been composed later, in the service of tribal, political, and religious disputes. After the book was withdrawn under pressure, he restated a moderated form of the argument in *Fi al-adab al-jahili* (Cairo: Matba'at al-I'timad, 1927). Neither settled the question.
- **Oral-Formulaic Re-evaluations**: Later scholars moved past a simple choice between wholesale authenticity and wholesale fraud. James T. Monroe, in "Oral Composition in Pre-Islamic Poetry" (1972), and Michael Zwettler, in *The Oral Tradition of Classical Arabic Poetry* (1978), applied the Milman Parry and Albert Lord theory of oral-formulaic composition to early Arabic verse. They argued that formulaic diction and stock phrases belong to a performed tradition, and that some variation among recensions can come from performance rather than from deliberate forgery alone. Their work did not certify every ascribed line, and Zwettler also stressed the passage of the tradition into writing.

## Cultural and Epistemological Impact

Despite philological variances, pre-Islamic oral poetry established the normative aesthetic, metric, and lexical standard for Arabic literature (*adab*). Early Arabic grammarians (such as Sibawayh) treated pre-Islamic verse as indispensable evidence (*shawahid*) for grammatical codification, while exegetes (*mufassirun*) utilized pre-Islamic poetic usage to elucidate rare Qur'anic vocabulary (*gharib al-Qur'an*). The oral tradition thus provided the linguistic bedrock upon which classical Islamic civilization articulated its theological, legal, and literary texts.
