# Pre-Islamic Arabian Oral Tradition

## Definition

**The Arabian oral tradition** is the cultural system through which knowledge and literature were preserved and transmitted in the Arabian Peninsula before and at the dawn of Islam. Its most prominent elements are **Jahiliyyah poetry**, the reports of **the battle days of the Arabs** (accounts of tribal wars), and genealogies, in a society in which writing was not a widely used means of recording.

## Mechanism of Transmission

Jahiliyyah poetry was transmitted through three successive layers: **the poet**, who composed the ode and memorised it; then **the reciters** (tribal reciters, *ruwat*), who memorised and passed it on from generation to generation, often out of tribal loyalty; and then **the collectors of poetry** in the Islamic period (from the eighth century CE onward), who recorded this oral inheritance in written works. The most famous of these reciters was **Hammad al-Rawiya** (d. c. 772 CE), to whom, according to tradition, the compilation of the **Mu'allaqat** is attributed. The Mu'allaqat are the seven long odes regarded as the peak of Jahiliyyah poetry. According to a later report they were named because they were "hung" (*'ullaqat*) on the curtains of the Ka'ba, although this report is historically doubtful.

## Main Features

- **Battle days of the Arabs (*ayyam al-'Arab*)**: a spoken record of tribal wars and raids, preserved in odes of pride, lamentation and satire. It later became a historical source for historians of Islam, although one marked by much literary distortion.
- **Al-Mufaddaliyyat**: a poetry collection compiled by al-Mufaddal al-Dabbi (d. c. 786 CE), comparable in importance to the Mu'allaqat, which includes odes by less well-known poets.
- **Problem of documentation**: early Arab critics themselves recognised fabrication and forgery in poetry attributed to the Jahiliyyah. Hammad al-Rawiya and Khalaf al-Ahmar in particular were accused of composing verses and falsely attributing them to earlier poets.
- **Modern doubt**: Taha Husayn, in his book *Fi al-Shi'r al-Jahili* (*On Pre-Islamic Poetry*, 1926), raised radical doubts about the authenticity of a large part of this inheritance. He argued that it was in fact composed in early Islam for religious and linguistic purposes. This thesis aroused wide controversy and remains a subject of academic debate.

## Influence

Despite the problems of its documentation, this oral inheritance was the **primary linguistic reference** for the scholars of the Arabic language and grammar in establishing the rules of classical Arabic (*fusha*) and rhetoric. It was also used as evidence in the interpretation of the Qur'an and the hadith. Jahiliyyah poetry thus became a religious and linguistic instrument of knowledge as much as a literary legacy.

## Limitations

The time gap between the original recitation of a Jahiliyyah ode and its actual recording may reach two centuries, a gap sufficient to admit broad linguistic and stylistic changes. This makes "Jahiliyyah poetry" as we have it a historically composite text, not a direct and pure testimony to that era.
