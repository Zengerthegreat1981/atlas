# Exposure Therapy

## Definition

Exposure therapy is a cognitive-behavioral technique in which the patient confronts, in an organized, gradual, and safe manner, the stimuli, memories, and situations they fear or avoid. The aim is for fear to weaken and for the patient to learn that the catastrophic outcomes they expect do not occur. It is one of the best-supported psychological treatments for phobias, panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Its techniques have separate entries of their own, and this entry presents the general idea, its history, and its limits.

## Historical Roots

In 1924 Mary Cover Jones reported in *Pedagogical Seminary* the case of a young boy, Peter, whose fear of a rabbit was reduced by gradually bringing the rabbit closer while he ate a food he liked (an early form of counterconditioning). In South Africa and later in the United States, Joseph Wolpe founded the method of "systematic desensitization" based on "reciprocal inhibition," described in *Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition* (Stanford University Press, 1958). In this method, a graded imagining of the stimulus is paired with relaxation. The decisive shift came with Victor Meyer, who published in 1966, in *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, the treatment of obsessive rituals through exposure and response prevention (see the entry on Exposure and Response Prevention). Flooding, in which patients are exposed to the most feared stimulus at length without graded steps, was developed in the same period (for example, in Stampfl and Levis's implosive therapy and in the work of Isaac Marks and colleagues).

## Theories of Mechanism

In 1986, Foa and Kozak presented the "emotional processing theory" in *Psychological Bulletin*. According to it, treatment requires activating the "fear structure" and introducing corrective information. Habituation within sessions and between sessions is considered an indicator of improvement (see the entry on Habituation through Exposure). The critique of this theory came from extinction research. Mark Bouton and others showed, in animal learning work, that extinction is a new memory that competes with the original fear memory and does not erase it, so fear can return with time, context, or reminder. On that basis, Craske and colleagues presented in *Behaviour Research and Therapy* in 2014 the "inhibitory learning" model. It holds that the aim of a session is to violate the patient's threat expectations rather than to reduce anxiety. It recommends varying stimuli and contexts and dropping safety behaviors.

## Evidence and Applications

In 1991 Foa and colleagues published in the *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* a trial of "prolonged exposure" for female rape survivors, compared with stress inoculation training, supportive counseling, and a wait-list. This protocol became a standard in the treatment of post-traumatic stress disorder. In 1995 Rothbaum and colleagues reported in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* that virtual-reality graded exposure was effective for fear of heights. Exposure can be carried out through imagination, in real life, or through bodily sensations (such as dizziness in panic). It proceeds according to a hierarchical ladder that the patient constructs (see the entry on Exposure Hierarchy).

## Practical Reservations

Some therapists hesitate to undertake exposure for fear of worsening symptoms, and early withdrawal often occurs if the logic of the treatment has not been explained to the patient and the patient has not been prepared for it. Informed consent is required, and the patient's privacy, sense of dignity, and family context must be respected. This matters especially in trauma linked to sexual violence or war, where recalling a memory before an unfamiliar therapist can carry an additional social cost.

## Limits

Exposure is not suitable for every patient. Those with severe dissociation or active substance use may need preparation first. Response varies, and some of those treated return to their symptoms. There is debate over whether combining it with benzodiazepines weakens the learning of safety (see the entry on Anxiolytics & Benzodiazepines), and the relative effectiveness of virtual-reality versus in vivo exposure continues to be studied.

*This entry is educational and is not clinical advice; exposure therapy should be delivered by a trained clinician.*
