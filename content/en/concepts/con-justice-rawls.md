# Justice as Fairness

## Meaning and Definition

Justice as fairness is the conception of a just social system formulated by John Rawls. Its institutions distribute rights, duties and wealth in a manner that every reasonable and fair person would accept if they did not know their particular position in society. The distribution is not based on what serves one's known personal interest.

## The Islamic Approach to Justice

In Islam, justice is a central value that precedes any later philosophical theory. It is commanded absolutely in the verse "Indeed, God commands justice and good conduct." It is even made a condition in dealings with enemies: "And do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness." Classical jurists addressed questions of distribution chiefly through the obligations of alms (*zakat*) and maintenance (*nafaqat*), and through considerations of public interest (*maslaha*). They did not require strict arithmetic equality, but a justice that takes need and sufficiency into account. This resembles, in some respects, the concern with the position of the most needy in Rawls's logic, although the sources differ. For Rawls, justice is the product of a hypothetical rational agreement. For the jurists, it is a fixed divine command that reason can understand but that is not built on purely human contract.

## Philosophical Context: Rawls's Theory

In *A Theory of Justice* (1971), Rawls proposed a thought experiment: the "original position" behind a "veil of ignorance." Here, the parties choosing the principles of justice do not know their social position, talents or religion in advance. According to Rawls, they would tend to choose two principles. The first gives priority to equal basic liberties for all. The second permits economic inequality only if it benefits the most disadvantaged groups (the "difference principle"), and only when it is joined with fair equality of opportunity.

Robert Nozick raised a radical objection in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974). He argued that Rawls's theory is "patterned," imposing a particular distribution regardless of how wealth was acquired. For Nozick, true justice is procedural. It concerns the legitimacy of acquisition and exchange, not the final outcome. Communitarians, such as Michael Sandel, also criticized the "unencumbered self" which, in his reading, the veil of ignorance presupposes (*Liberalism and the Limits of Justice*, 1982). They argued that human identity cannot be separated from its actual communal affiliations.

## Applied Example

The veil of ignorance is used in practice to assess social security policies. Designing a fair health system requires imagining that we might be born poor or sick. We would then design a system that protects this group, rather than a system that serves only the healthy and wealthy.

## The Priority of Liberty

An important feature of Rawls's theory is his insistence on the "priority" of the principle of basic liberties over the difference principle. A basic liberty (such as freedom of belief or expression) may not be sacrificed in exchange for any economic gain, however large. This ordering reflects his conviction that the political dignity of the individual cannot be bartered for material prosperity. Loosely, one might compare this with the ordering of the five essentials in the *maqasid* tradition, where preserving religion and life ranks above preserving property, though the rationales differ.
