# Psychotic Recovery

A concept referring to the possibility of living a satisfying and meaningful life after developing a psychotic disorder such as schizophrenia. The scientific literature distinguishes two levels. **Clinical recovery** is the disappearance or marked reduction of symptoms together with restored functioning. **Personal recovery** is an individual process of rebuilding identity, meaning, and hope, despite the persistence of some symptoms or the limitations the illness imposes.

## Origins of the Recovery Model

The rehabilitation psychologist William Anthony, director of the Boston Center for Psychiatric Rehabilitation, developed one of the most influential definitions in 1993. He described recovery as "a deeply personal, unique process of changing one's attitudes, values, feelings, goals, skills, and/or roles" and as "a way of living a satisfying, hopeful, and contributing life even with limitations caused by illness." This definition shifted the focus from "healing symptoms" alone to building a meaningful life. It became the foundation of the **Recovery Movement**, which was later adopted by mental health policies in the United States, the United Kingdom, and Australia.

## Empirical Evidence

Long-term longitudinal studies challenged the previously prevailing assumption that schizophrenia inevitably follows a deteriorating course (Kraepelinian pessimism). The most notable of these are Manfred Bleuler's studies in Zurich, the Vermont Longitudinal Study led by Courtenay Harding (Harding and colleagues, 1987), and the WHO international studies (IPSS/DOSMeD). These studies showed that a considerable proportion of patients reach good levels of functional recovery, or a substantial reduction of symptoms, over the long term; the Vermont study reported that roughly half to two-thirds of participants had significantly improved or recovered. Estimates vary widely with the definition of recovery and the length of follow-up: a meta-analysis by Jääskeläinen and colleagues (2013) found that a median of about 14% of people with schizophrenia met stricter criteria for sustained clinical and social recovery, while studies using broader criteria report higher figures. There is no unified quantitative consensus.

## Elements of Personal Recovery (CHIME Framework)

A systematic review led by Mike Slade and Mary Leamy and colleagues (Leamy et al., 2011) developed a unified framework known as **CHIME**. It comprises five elements that recur in recovery narratives: **Connectedness**, **Hope and Optimism**, **Identity**, **Meaning in Life**, and **Empowerment**. This framework has become a widely used reference in service design and in research on personal recovery.

## Applications and Criticism

The recovery model influenced the redesign of psychiatric services toward user-oriented approaches (recovery-oriented services). Examples include self-management crisis plans (WRAP), peer support workers, and early intervention in psychosis. On the other hand, researchers and activists have criticized the risk of "politicizing" the concept. Health systems may adopt recovery rhetoric as a pretext for reducing funding and long-term services, on the assumption that "everyone can recover on their own," which conflicts with the reality that a proportion of those affected need continuous support.
