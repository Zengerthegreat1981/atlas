# Structural Violence

A concept formulated by the political scientist Johan Galtung in 1969 and developed anthropologically by Paul Farmer to analyze global health and inequality.

## Central Idea

The concept describes the harm inflicted on people by unjust social, economic, and political structures (poverty, institutional racism, economic exploitation, exclusion from health care), with no direct individual agent who can be held personally responsible. This differs from direct violence, such as beating or killing, which has an identifiable perpetrator. Here the harm is "built into" the very structure of society, such as the unequal distribution of resources and of access to health care between the poor and the rich.

## Anthropological Application

Paul Farmer applied this concept to the global distribution of infectious diseases (tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS). He showed that those who fall ill and die at higher rates do so not for purely biological reasons, but because their poverty and structural exclusion deny them the prevention and treatment available to others in other countries or social classes.

## Impact

The concept has become a basic analytical tool in critical medical anthropology and in health-related human rights. It links the analysis of individual cases of illness to the unequal global economic and political structures that determine who gets sick and who is cured.

## The Origin of the Concept in Galtung

In his foundational article (1969), Johan Galtung distinguished **direct violence** (a specific perpetrator, a specific victim, and a clear act such as beating or war) from **structural violence** (built into the distribution of resources and power within the social system itself, without a direct individual agent). In a later article (1990) he added **cultural violence**: the symbolic, religious, and ideological systems that lend legitimacy to the first two types and normalize them socially. Paul Farmer drew on this idea in his work in Haiti, Peru, and Rwanda, where he combined medical practice with ethnographic and historical analysis through the organization Partners In Health.

## Case Study: Tuberculosis in Haiti

In his field research, Farmer documented how the rate of tuberculosis infection and resistance to treatment in Haiti is linked to a long history of colonialism, political instability, and foreign aid and trade restrictions, as well as to present poverty. He showed that the "choices" of poor patients (such as not completing treatment), which may appear individual on the surface, are in fact shaped by economic and political structures that lie largely beyond their personal control.

## Relation to the Broader Intellectual Tradition

The concept of structural violence intersects with the notion of "social justice" in moral and religious thought generally. It calls for holding the system, and not only individuals, accountable for poverty and chronic illness. This corresponds to repeated historical calls across multiple intellectual traditions to hold unjust social structures (such as the distribution of land and wealth) accountable, rather than being satisfied with transient individual charity toward their victims.
