# Ethics of Care

## Meaning and Definition

The ethics of care is a normative ethical theory that makes personal relationships, and the mutual responsibility of care between individuals who differ in dependency (such as a mother and her child, or a nurse and her patient), the basis of moral thinking. This replaces the general abstract principles of justice and rights, which presuppose independent, equal agents. The ethics of care criticizes the traditional frameworks (Kantian and utilitarian) for neglecting the dimension of dependency, vulnerability, and the emotional context in which actual moral decisions are made.

## Anticipations in Islamic Thought

Although the term is Western in origin, the Islamic heritage holds a rich conception of the ethics of care. It appears in the detailed specification of mutual rights within family and social relationships based on difference rather than abstract equality. The right of parents over a child does not rest on an equal contract but on the duty of filial piety (*birr*), which arises from the relationship of care and upbringing. The Qur'an links the worship of God with kindness to parents in several verses. Jurists specified the maintenance of relatives (such as parents, wife, and children) on the basis of kinship and need, not on the basis of an abstract contractual calculation. Al-Ghazali, in *Revival of the Religious Sciences*, devoted a book to the etiquette of companionship and spoke of the right of kinship, which requires continuing ties regardless of whether the other party deserves them. This approaches the idea of care not conditioned on fair exchange.

## Western Roots and Contemporary Debate

The ethics of care took shape as a feminist theory with Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* (1982). She objected to Lawrence Kohlberg's model of stages of moral development, arguing that it was built on male samples and valued the abstract "ethics of justice" above the relational "ethics of care," which she found to be more common, though not exclusive, among the women she interviewed. Nel Noddings then developed this current philosophically in *Caring* (1984). She treated the relationship between the "one-caring" and the "cared-for" as the basic unit of moral analysis, rather than the isolated individual bearer of rights. Virginia Held, in *The Ethics of Care* (2006), extended the theory to politics and social institutions, arguing that traditional moral theory has undervalued the practices of care.

## Objections and Examples

Critics argue that excessive focus on private care may justify neglecting distant strangers and the needy, and may entrench unfair traditional gender roles. Some care theorists, such as Held, reply that care can inform institutions and justice rather than replace them, while Noddings herself holds that our obligations are strongest toward those with whom we stand in actual caring relations. An example is a nurse who watches over an elderly patient at night. She does so not out of the calculation of an abstract duty, but out of the care relationship that has developed between them, going beyond her formal minimum duties in response to his actual need.

## Tronto's Political Account of Care

Joan Tronto's *Moral Boundaries* (1993) argues that care has been confined by boundaries separating morality from politics and public from private life, which keep it marginal and leave it largely to women and other less powerful groups. With Berenice Fisher she offered a broad definition of caring as an activity of the human species covering all that is done to sustain, carry on and mend the shared world so that people can live in it as well as they can. Tronto distinguishes four phases, caring about, taking care of, care-giving, and care-receiving, matched with the qualities of attentiveness, responsibility, competence, and responsiveness. Political questions then arise about who is assigned which phase: powerful people may take charge of care while leaving the hands-on work to others, and may enjoy what she calls "privileged irresponsibility." In *Caring Democracy* (2013) she adds a further phase, "caring with," and argues that allocating caring responsibilities fairly is a central task of democratic politics. Gilligan's own framing is also worth recalling: she identifies the different voice by its theme, not by gender.
