# Philosophy of Arabic Grammar

## Definition

**The philosophy of Arabic grammar** is an intellectual field that examines the logical and epistemological foundations of Arabic grammar, and its relationship to **logic** as a universal tool of thought, as opposed to grammar as a structure specific to a particular language. This inquiry is classically associated with the famous debate between the **grammarians** and the **logicians** in Baghdad in the fourth century AH (tenth century CE).

## Historical context

After the Abbasid translation movement that rendered Aristotelian logic into Arabic, a tension arose between the philologists, who regarded **grammar** as sufficient to regulate the soundness of thought within the Arabic language, and the logicians, who saw in **Greek logic** a universal rational instrument that transcends the specificity of any language. This tension was embodied in the **famous debate** held in Baghdad in 326 AH / 937 CE, in the presence of the vizier Ibn al-Furat, between the logician **Abu Bishr Matta ibn Yunus** and the grammarian **Abu Sa'id al-Sirafi**. Matta ibn Yunus affirmed that grammar is merely a local tool for regulating Arabic speech, while logic is a universal criterion of correct thought. Al-Sirafi responded that Arabic grammar is self-sufficient to regulate the soundness of meaning, and that the "logic" of the Greeks reflects the conventions of the Greek language and cannot claim universality over Arabic. The debate is transmitted in Abu Hayyan al-Tawhidi's *al-Imta' wa-l-mu'anasa*.

## Key dimensions

- **al-Farabi's intermediate position**: the philosopher **Abu Nasr al-Farabi**, although himself a logician, took a more balanced position in this debate. He distinguished between the function of grammar (regulating the words of a particular language) and the function of logic (regulating the rules of correct thought regardless of language). He regarded the two fields as complementary rather than contradictory, and held that the disagreement between them was partly a disagreement over the limits of each science's competence, rather than over the absolute superiority of one over the other.
- **Sibawayh and the founding of systematic grammar**: **Sibawayh** (d. c. 180 AH / 796 CE, by traditional reckoning) established in his book *al-Kitab* the first complete systematic Arabic grammar, treating linguistic phenomena with an almost logical inductive method. This made his work a foundational reference on which both sides later drew in the debate with the logicians.
- **The problem of universal logic versus linguistic specificity**: the core of the philosophical debate remains one question: are there correct rules of thought independent of any particular language (as the defenders of logic claimed), or does each language carry its own logical structure, sufficient to regulate the soundness of its speakers' thinking (as the grammarians claimed)? This question remains present in contemporary philosophy of language in different forms.
- **Later development: Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani and nazm theory**: **Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani** (d. 471 AH) later developed the theory of *nazm* (syntactic arrangement), expounded in *Dala'il al-i'jaz*, which linked meaning to the grammatical structure of the sentence itself. Al-Jurjani argued that eloquence lies not in isolated words but in the way words are combined according to the "meanings of grammar" (*ma'ani al-nahw*), so that grammatical relations such as predication, determination and word order carry the sentence's meaning. It is an indirect development of the idea that Arabic grammar is not merely a set of formal rules but a system of meaning, although al-Jurjani did not frame his theory as a reply to the logicians.
