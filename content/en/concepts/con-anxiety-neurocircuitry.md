# Anxiety Neurocircuitry

A framework in neuropsychology that describes the anatomical and functional basis of the normal **fear response** and how its dysfunction turns into **pathological anxiety disorders**. It is based on three central brain structures: the **amygdala**, the **medial prefrontal cortex**, and the **hippocampus**.

## Components of the Circuit

The **amygdala**, specifically its basolateral and central nuclei, is the main center for **fear acquisition** (conditioned fear) and for generating physiological and behavioral responses to threat (rapid heartbeat, freezing, hypervigilance). The **ventromedial prefrontal cortex** (vmPFC) exerts "top-down" control over the amygdala and is primarily responsible for **fear extinction**, that is, learning that a stimulus that once signaled danger no longer does. The **hippocampus** contributes context: it identifies the conditions and places in which fear may be generalized or inhibited, and stores the memory of the extinction context.

## Dysfunction in Anxiety Disorders

Functional neuroimaging studies of anxiety-related disorders (notably posttraumatic stress disorder, social anxiety disorder, and specific phobia, as in the meta-analysis by Etkin and Wager, 2007) show a recurring pattern: **overactivity of the amygdala** (and insula) in response to threat stimuli, with reduced activity in the **ventromedial prefrontal cortex**, which is supposed to restrain this activity; in that meta-analysis the prefrontal reduction appeared only in posttraumatic stress disorder, and findings for generalized anxiety disorder and panic disorder are less consistent. This imbalance in inhibitory control partly explains two central features of pathological anxiety: **generalization of fear** to stimuli that do not actually warrant it, and **resistance of learned responses to extinction**, meaning greater difficulty "forgetting" or inhibiting a fear the person learned earlier, even after the actual source of danger has disappeared.

## Therapeutic Significance

This neural framework provides a biological basis for understanding how **exposure therapy** works. Repeated, systematic exposure to the feared stimulus without an actual catastrophic outcome is viewed as direct training of the extinction circuit based on the prefrontal cortex–amygdala pathway. This explains why exposure is among the most successful therapeutic interventions for anxiety disorders, even though it was originally developed from purely behavioral observations that preceded the discovery of these circuits. The model also guides the development of adjunct drug treatments, such as D-cycloserine, a partial agonist at the NMDA receptor, which has been studied experimentally as a way of enhancing learning during exposure sessions, with mixed results.

## Limits

Most of the evidence for this model comes from animal studies (especially rodents) of Pavlovian threat conditioning. LeDoux has argued that these circuits are better described as systems for detecting and responding to threat, and that equating their activity with the conscious feeling of fear goes beyond what the animal experiments show (LeDoux, 2014). Translation to the cognitive and social complexity of human anxiety disorders (such as social anxiety, or chronic generalized anxiety not linked to a specific stimulus) remains a subject of debate and methodological caution. The Etkin and Wager meta-analysis also found reduced prefrontal activity in the posttraumatic stress disorder studies but not in those of social anxiety disorder or specific phobia.
