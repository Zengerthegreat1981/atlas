# Neurodiversity Framework

A conceptual framework that views neurodevelopmental differences (such as autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, learning disabilities, and developmental coordination disorder) as **natural variation** within human diversity, in the way that biodiversity is natural variation. They are not considered disease states that must necessarily be "treated" or "corrected." The framework distinguishes these differences themselves from the suffering or disability that may result from their interaction with an unsuitable environment.

## Origins and Terminology

The term "neurodiversity" emerged in the 1990s within online communities of autistic people and their allies. It is often attributed to the Australian sociologist **Judy Singer**, who developed the idea in a 1998 sociology honours thesis at the University of Technology Sydney and published a chapter using the term in 1999. The journalist Harvey Blume helped circulate the word in *The Atlantic* in 1998. A 2024 paper by Botha and colleagues argues that this single-origin story is misleading. Archival material shows "neurological diversity" already in use in autistic online discussions by the mid-1990s, and the authors treat the concept as a collective product of neurodivergent communities. The most prominent of them is **Jim Sinclair**, who wrote the foundational essay "Don't Mourn for Us" in 1993, before the term itself existed. Autistic online communities in the mid-1990s were an important early setting for the idea.

## Core Principles

- **Difference, not deficit**: neurodevelopmental differences are natural variations in how the human brain works, not deviations from a single "normal" standard.
- **The social model of disability**: much of the "disability" associated with these conditions arises from the failure of social and institutional environments (workplaces, schools) to accommodate this variation, rather than necessarily from the neurological condition itself.
- **Rejection of the single therapeutic narrative**: critique of a medical discourse that treats autism and similar conditions as targets exclusively for "treatment" or "normalization," and a call instead for support and environmental adaptation.

## Reception and Debate

The framework has been widely adopted within the rights activism of autistic people, and has gradually entered medical and psychological literature, although the medical category "neurodevelopmental disorders" in DSM-5 and ICD-11 is a separate, clinical grouping that does not itself adopt the neurodiversity position. On the other hand, some physicians and parents criticize it on the grounds that it may marginalize the needs of the most affected cases (such as autism with severe intellectual disability or an inability to communicate verbally). For these cases, the need for intensive medical and rehabilitative support is real regardless of the conceptual framework used.

## Limits

- A tension remains between adopting the neurodiversity framework as a celebration of difference, and the actual need of some individuals for intensive medical and rehabilitative support. Proponents of the framework try to address this by distinguishing between "difference" and "suffering resulting from lack of support."
- The traditional attribution of the term to Judy Singer alone has been challenged in recent scholarship, which favors a collective account of its origins.
