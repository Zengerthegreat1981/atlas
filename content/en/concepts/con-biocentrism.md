# Biocentrism

## Meaning and Definition

Biocentrism is a position in environmental ethics holding that every living organism, not humans alone nor conscious animals alone, possesses inherent moral worth (inherent worth) deserving respect simply by virtue of being a living being that seeks to preserve its existence and flourishing. This holds regardless of its capacity to feel or think. Biocentrism thus goes beyond both anthropocentrism, which confines moral value to humans, and zoocentrism, which extends it only to conscious animals. It holds that plants, insects, and bacteria also have "a good of their own" that must be taken into account.

## Historical Context

The American philosopher Paul Taylor is a leading figure in formulating this position systematically, in his book *Respect for Nature* (1986). He built his theory on the idea that every living organism is a "teleological center of life" that pursues its own biological ends, and that this alone suffices to grant it an independent moral standing separate from its usefulness to humans. An earlier ancestor is Albert Schweitzer's "reverence for life." Biocentrism is distinct from the "land ethic" formulated by Aldo Leopold in *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), which holds that an action is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community; that view is usually classed as ecocentric, as is the "deep ecology" movement associated with the Norwegian philosopher Arne Naess.

## Principal Positions

Taylor distinguishes between respect for nature as a fundamental moral attitude and the practical rules that regulate conflicts between human interests and the interests of other living beings. He proposes priority principles such as self-defense, proportionality and minimum wrong for cases where harm to living things cannot be avoided. There are also weaker versions of biocentrism, which say only that living beings have a value that merits weight in moral decision-making, without equating it with human value in cases of conflict. These positions contrast with broader theories such as ecocentrism, which grants value to ecosystems as whole units rather than to individual living things alone.

## Objections and Criticism

Biocentrism is objected to on the grounds that it plunges practical ethics into unbearable complexity. It is practically impossible to treat every bacterium and every plant as holders of rights roughly equal to those of an animal or a human. Critics argue that the concept of "a good of its own" on which Taylor relies is a functional biological concept rather than necessarily a moral one. In their view, the move from "this organism has a biological end" to "this organism has a moral right" is an unjustified logical leap.

## Illustrative Example

When a forest is uprooted to build a road, biocentrism does not stop at asking about the effect on the animals that live there. It regards the uprooting of every tree as the removal of a living individual with its own interest, which deserves independent moral consideration, not merely a resource measured by its usefulness to humans.
