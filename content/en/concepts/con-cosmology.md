# Cosmology

Cosmology, in anthropology, is the study of how a culture imagines the origin, structure, and order of the universe, and the place of human beings within it. It is the "worldview" in its deepest sense: the framework of creation stories, layered worlds, and relations between humans, ancestors, spirits, and gods within which religious beliefs, moral values, and social practices take their meaning. Anthropological cosmology should not be confused with physical cosmology, the natural science of the universe's actual structure. The anthropologist asks not what the universe is, but how a particular people conceive it, narrate it, and live inside that conception.

## Definition

A cosmology typically includes an account of origins — a creation myth or story of how things came to be as they are — and a map of the cosmos: the sky, the earth, and the underworld, or multiple layered worlds connected by a central axis such as a world tree or a sacred mountain. It also includes the beings who inhabit those worlds and the relations between them: gods, ancestors, spirits, animals, and humans, each with their own powers and obligations. Crucially, cosmology is not a separate department of thought, kept apart from everyday life. The ordering of the cosmos is usually also an ordering of society: the ranks of gods mirror the ranks of people, the layout of a village may reproduce the layout of the universe, and the calendar of festivals may follow the movements of the stars or the return of ancestral spirits. When a creation story places a particular social rank or gender in the "origin of things" itself, it gives that arrangement a legitimacy that goes beyond mere social agreement, rooting it in a supposedly given cosmic order.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of cosmology grew out of the study of myth and religion, but it took its modern shape when scholars began to treat cosmologies as coherent systems of thought rather than collections of picturesque beliefs. Mircea Eliade argued that many traditional cosmologies are organised around the idea of a sacred centre and a cyclical return to origins: rituals repeat the acts of creation and renew the world. Claude Lévi-Strauss, in his Mythologiques series, treated myths as a kind of intellectual work, showing how stories from the Americas transformed one another according to a hidden logic of oppositions and mediations. In Australia, ethnographers documented Aboriginal cosmologies organised around the Dreaming, or Dreamtime — an ancestral past that is not separate from the present but continues to shape the landscape and the lives of those who walk it. In the Americas, ethnographers described layered worlds linked by a central axis, and in Africa, cosmologies that bind the world of the living to the world of the ancestors in a single continuous social fabric.

## Key Debates

The most influential recent development in the comparative study of cosmology comes from Philippe Descola, whose book Beyond Nature and Culture, published in French in 2005 and in English in 2013, proposed a typology of cosmologies based on how each culture imagines the relationship between humans and non-humans — animals, plants, and things. Descola distinguished animism, in which non-human beings are credited with an interior life like that of humans; totemism; analogism; and the naturalism of the modern West, which draws a sharp line between nature and culture. Eduardo Viveiros de Castro's work on Amerindian perspectivism, published in 1998, pushed the argument further: in some Amazonian cosmologies, animals see themselves as human and see humans as animals, so that what counts as a person depends on the point of view. These approaches raise a methodological question that remains unsettled: how far can Western analytic categories such as "religion" or "myth" be applied to cosmologies that do not recognise a separation between those domains and reality itself? The risk is of projecting a Western division between the natural and the supernatural onto systems that never made that division.

## Contemporary Relevance

The comparative study of cosmology has implications well beyond the anthropology of religion. It exposes a modern Western assumption — the strict separation between objective nature and human culture — as a culturally specific construction rather than a universal standard, one not shared by many other cosmological systems. That insight has become politically significant in debates over Indigenous land rights, where courts and governments are asked to recognise sacred landscapes, ancestral beings, and relations with non-human entities as real features of the world rather than as beliefs about it. It also informs environmental anthropology: cosmologies that treat rivers, forests, and animals as persons or kin offer different grounds for conservation than models that treat nature as a resource to be managed. And in a world where scientific cosmology and religious cosmologies coexist and sometimes collide, the anthropological study of cosmology provides a way of understanding how people hold together different accounts of the universe — scientific, religious, and everyday — without necessarily treating them as competitors for the same truth.
