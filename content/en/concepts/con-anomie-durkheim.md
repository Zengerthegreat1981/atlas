# Anomie in Durkheim

A concept formulated by Émile Durkheim to describe a state of breakdown or weakening of the social norms that regulate desire and behavior, when the structure of society fails to provide clear moral regulation for individuals.

## The Content

Durkheim gave the term a prominent place in *The Division of Labor in Society* (1893), where it describes a state of "abnormal division of labor." It arises when economic specialization develops faster than the regulating moral rules can keep pace, so that economic relations are left without sufficient normative regulation. He developed the concept in greater detail in *Suicide* (1897), where he distinguished "anomic suicide" (*suicide anomique*) as one of four types. It occurs when the balance between the individual's desires and the social means available to satisfy them suddenly collapses, not only in economic crises (depression) but also in periods of sudden prosperity (when ambitions expand faster than the social structure can meet them or restrain them with clear norms). The central idea: human desire, unlike animal needs, which are biologically limited, has no internal natural limits. Society itself needs to impose external normative limits ("moral regulation"), otherwise the individual slides into a state of infinite, unsatisfiable desire, a source of deep psychological suffering.

## Impact and Later Development

Robert Merton revived the term in 1938 and radically reformulated it in a different American context. Instead of a "general breakdown of norms," Merton defined anomie as a specific structural gap between declared cultural "goals" (material success) and legitimate "means" that are unequally available by class. This interpretation is closer to an analysis of class inequality than to Durkheim's original diagnosis of a comprehensive societal normative breakdown. The distinction between the two formulations (the original Durkheimian and the later Mertonian) is necessary to avoid a common conflation of them in the secondary literature.

## Anomie and the Four Types of Suicide

In his book *Suicide* (1897), Durkheim classified the kinds of suicide into four types linked to the degree of social integration and regulation. **Egoistic suicide** (*égoïste*) results from weak social integration (as he observed in the relatively higher suicide rates among Protestants compared with Catholics, since Catholic communal cohesion provides greater social protection). **Altruistic suicide** (*altruiste*) results from excessive social integration that dissolves the individual into the group. **Anomic suicide** results from weak normative regulation. **Fatalistic suicide** (*fataliste*) results from excessive regulation that stifles the individual (as in the case of slaves). Durkheim used this comparative statistical classification as evidence that a phenomenon that seems purely individual, such as suicide, in fact obeys social laws that can be studied scientifically and objectively. By this he meant to establish sociology as a method independent of individual psychology.
