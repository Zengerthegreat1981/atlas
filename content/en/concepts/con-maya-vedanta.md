# Māyā (Philosophical Illusion / Cosmic Apparition)

A pivotal concept in [Vedanta](sch-vedanta), with different meanings depending on the school, and one of the pillars of the [major Indian philosophical traditions](ctx-hindu-philosophical-traditions). The Sanskrit word means "magic," "power" or "illusion" (already in the Vedic hymns it denotes a wondrous creative power, and the Śvetāśvatara Upanishad calls prakṛti māyā and the Lord the māyin, the wielder of māyā), but in later Indian philosophy it describes the relation between "absolute reality" (Brahman) and the "apparent world" (Jagat). The difference in its interpretation among Shankara, Ramanuja and Madhva is the essence of the [debate between Advaita and Dvaita Vedanta](dbt-advaita-vs-dvaita) over whether the world is real or not.

## Multiple Meanings

- **[Shankara](thk-shankara) (Advaita)**: māyā (closely tied to avidyā, ignorance) accounts for the appearance of the many in the one non-dual Brahman. The world is not "real" in the absolute sense, but it is not simply "nothing" either. Later Advaitins described it as "anirvacanīya," that which cannot be classified as either being or non-being. Māyā is also spoken of as the power of Brahman through which the One appears as many.
- **Ramanuja (Viśiṣṭādvaita)**: he rejects the Advaitin account of māyā as an indeterminable ignorance. For him the world is real, being the body of Brahman (God), and māyā refers to God's wondrous creative power and to the prakṛti that He uses. Māyā in this sense is not an illusion.
- **Madhva (Dvaita)**: the world and its differences are real and eternal, and matter (prakṛti) is real. All of it depends on God (Vishnu), the sole independent reality. Madhva sharply criticizes the Advaita doctrine that the world is illusory.
- **Kashmir Shaivism**: māyā is a power of Shiva that limits and veils, producing the experience of difference. The world is not unreal but a real manifestation of Shiva (Śiva-consciousness).

## The Anthropological Dimension

- In the human being, māyā (as avidyā) is the ignorance that makes the individual think himself separate from Brahman. In Advaita, liberation comes through jñāna (knowledge), while in Ramanuja's tradition devotion (bhakti) and surrender (prapatti) are central.
- Māyā is not a "sin" or "transgression" as in Christianity, but "ignorance" (Avidyā) that can be removed by knowledge.

## Its Place in Comparative Philosophy

- **The dream argument in Descartes**: the Advaita analogies of dream and of the rope mistaken for a snake are loosely comparable to Descartes's dream argument in the first Meditation. But Descartes doubts in order to reach a certain foundation for knowledge, whereas Advaita uses such analogies to point toward liberating knowledge of Brahman, and the two projects differ in aim and metaphysics.
- **Kant's appearance and thing-in-itself**: the contrast between the apparent world and ultimate reality is sometimes compared with Kant's distinction between phenomena and noumena, though Advaita holds that ultimate reality can be known in liberating realization.
- **Illusion in Freud**: in *The Future of an Illusion* (1927), Freud defines an illusion as a belief driven by wish-fulfilment. This is a psychological notion, whereas māyā is cosmological, so the comparison is only a loose one.

## Limit

Māyā is not a "nihilistic" concept as some readers of Western philosophy understand it. It is originally a "principle of manifestation," and each Vedanta school explains it differently. Confusing "Shankara's māyā" with "Madhva's māyā" leads to a fundamental misunderstanding of Indian philosophy.
