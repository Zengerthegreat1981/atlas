# Māyā (Philosophical Illusion / Cosmic Apparition)

A pivotal concept in [Vedanta](sch-vedanta), with different meanings depending on the school, and one of the central pillars of classical Indian metaphysics. The Sanskrit term *māyā* originally derived from the root *mā* (to measure, form, or display) and denoted creative, wondrous, or magical power in the Vedic hymns. In the *Śvetāśvatara Upaniṣad*, *prakṛti* (primal nature) is identified as *māyā* and the supreme Lord (*Maheśvara*) as the *māyin* (wielder of māyā). In classical and post-classical Indian philosophy, the term articulates the ontological and epistemological relation between the unconditioned, absolute reality (*Brahman*) and the empirical, multifaceted universe (*Jagat*). Divergence over its interpretation constitutes the core of the [debate between Advaita and Dvaita Vedanta](dbt-advaita-vs-dvaita).

## Systematic School Interpretations

- **[Shankara](thk-shankara) (Advaita Vedanta)**: Māyā is intimately connected with *avidyā* (primal ignorance). Because Brahman is non-dual, changeless consciousness, the appearance of multiplicity, change, and individual selves (*jīvas*) cannot possess absolute reality (*pāramārthika sattā*). Yet the empirical world is not an utter void or non-entity (*asat* like a hare's horn); it possesses empirical, practical reality (*vyāvahārika sattā*) until metaphysical liberation occurs. Advaitins classically define māyā as **anirvacanīya**—inexpressible or indeterminable either as real (*sat*) or as unreal (*asat*). Māyā has two primary functions: the power to veil (*āvaraṇa-śakti*), concealing the non-dual reality of Brahman, and the power to project (*vikṣepa-śakti*), superimposing (*adhyāsa*) the manifold world onto Brahman, as a rope is mistaken for a snake in dim light.
- **Vācaspati Miśra and the Sub-School Debates**: Post-Shankara Advaita divided sharply over the locus (*āśraya*) and object (*viṣaya*) of *avidyā*. In his commentary *Bhāmatī*, Vācaspati Miśra (9th–10th century CE) argued that the individual soul (*jīva*) is the locus of ignorance, while Brahman is its object. Vācaspati distinguished between psychological ignorance rooted in individual memory impressions (*saṃskāras*) and objective cosmic illusion, thereby safeguarding the absolute purity and unconditioned perfection of Brahman from being the subject of ignorance. In contrast, the rival Vivaraṇa school (founded by Prakāśātman) asserted that Brahman must be both the locus and the object of *avidyā*, treating māyā as an objective, positive ontological power (*bhāvarūpa*).
- **Ramanuja (Viśiṣṭādvaita / Qualified Non-Dualism)**: Ramanuja rejects the Advaitin doctrine of an indeterminable, illusory māyā. In his *Śrī-bhāṣya*, he formulates seven major objections (*saptavidha-anupapatti*) against Advaitin *avidyā*. For Ramanuja, the physical cosmos is utterly real, constituting the divine body (*śarīra*) of Brahman (Vishnu). Māyā signifies God's wondrous, real creative potency (*vicitra-sṛṣṭi-kāriṇī śakti*) and the primordial material matrix (*prakṛti*) utilized in cosmic manifestation, not cognitive deception.
- **Madhva (Dvaita / Dualism)**: Madhva maintains strict realism and five fundamental, eternal dualities (*pañca-bheda*). The world and matter are completely real. Madhva interprets māyā not as illusion, but as the inscrutable will and gracious power of Vishnu, the sole independent reality (*svatantra*), upon whom all dependent beings (*paratantra*) rely.
- **Kashmir Shaivism (Pratyabhijñā)**: Reinterprets māyā not as an error to be extinguished, but as the dynamic self-veiling power (*śakti*) of Shiva. Shiva freely limits His infinite consciousness to manifest finite subjects and objects as an aesthetic display of divine freedom (*svātantrya*).

## The Anthropological and Epistemological Dimension

In human existence, māyā manifests as *avidyā*—the cognitive misidentification of the true, witnessing Self (*Ātman*) with the body, mind, and ego. In Advaita, release (*mokṣa*) is attained exclusively through intuitive metaphysical knowledge (*jñāna*), dissolving superimposition. In Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita, liberation is achieved through devotion (*bhakti*) and complete surrender (*prapatti*) to divine grace. Unlike Western theological conceptions of original sin, ignorance in Vedanta is not moral corruption but epistemic misperception, remediable through philosophical discernment and contemplation.

## Modern Arabic-Language and Islamic Comparative Scholarship

Modern Arabic-language philosophical scholarship engaged the concept of *māyā* primarily through comparative mysticism and the history of Eastern philosophy:
- **Abu al-Ala Afifi and Comparative Mysticism**: In his landmark work *The Mystical Philosophy of Muhyid Din Ibnul Arabi* (1939) and his editorial commentaries on the *Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam*, Abu al-Ala Afifi examined the striking structural parallels between Advaitin *māyā* and Ibn 'Arabi's Akbarian doctrine of the cosmos as creative imagination (*khayāl*) and divine self-manifestation (*tajallī*). Afifi demonstrated that both traditions articulate a non-dual metaphysics wherein the empirical cosmos is neither absolute being (*wujūd muṭlaq*) nor absolute non-existence (*'adam maḥḍ*), but an ontologically dependent, intermediate manifestation.
- **Abd al-Rahman Badawi and Eastern Philosophy**: In his studies on Eastern philosophies (*al-Falsafa al-Sharqiyya*), Abd al-Rahman Badawi analyzed Shankara's metaphysics, defending *māyā* against European charges of crude nihilism by comparing it to modern epistemological distinctions between phenomenon and reality.

## Comparative Philosophy

- **Descartes's Dream Argument**: Advaita analogies comparing waking life to dreams or mistaking a rope for a snake resemble Descartes's radical doubt in the *First Meditation*. However, Descartes uses doubt methodologically to establish an indubitable rational foundation for the sciences, whereas Advaita deploys dream analogies pedagogically to redirect the seeker toward liberating, non-conceptual realization of Brahman.
- **Kant's Phenomena and Noumena**: The contrast between empirical appearance and ultimate reality is often compared to Kant's division between appearances and the thing-in-itself (*Ding an sich*). Nonetheless, while Kant considered noumena unknowable by theoretical reason, Vedanta maintains that ultimate reality (*Brahman*) is directly realized through contemplative discernment.
