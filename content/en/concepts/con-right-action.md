# Right Action

## Meaning and Definition

"Right action" is a central concept in normative ethics. It refers to the question of what makes an act right, independently of the goodness of its agent: what makes a particular act morally right at this moment? Is it its conformity with a universal rule, as in deontological ethics? Is it its consequences, as in utilitarianism? Or is it its performance by a virtuous agent, as in virtue ethics? The concept is structurally tied to the Kantian deontological school, which makes an act right by virtue of its conformity with a rule that can be willed as a universal law, regardless of its consequences.

## The Islamic Usuli Approach

The science of legal theory (*usul al-fiqh*) addressed this question through the theory of the legal ruling (*hukm shar'i*) and its division into obligatory, recommended, permissible, disliked, and forbidden acts. For the majority of jurists, the criterion of the rightness of an act is its conformity with the revealed text (the Qur'an and the Sunna) or with sound analogy (*qiyas*) based on a legal cause ('illa). This criterion blends the deontological character of following a rule with the teleological consideration of achieving benefit. Al-Shatibi built his theory of the objectives of the Shariah (*maqasid*) on the premise that all legal rulings aim to realize the benefit of people in this world and the next. On this view, the right act is not right merely because of its form, but because it secures the preservation of the five essentials.

## The Western Philosophical Context

Kant distinguished between the act "in conformity with duty" and the act "from duty." He held that the morally right act is one that complies with the categorical imperative: act according to a maxim that you can will as a universal law. Utilitarians such as John Stuart Mill, by contrast, hold that the right act is the one that maximizes the balance of pleasure over pain for the greatest number of those affected, regardless of the rule followed. W. D. Ross offered a reconciling solution through the concept of multiple "prima facie duties" (such as fidelity, non-maleficence, beneficence and justice, among others). These are weighed against one another in each situation to determine the final "actual duty." This approach is close to the jurists' method of weighing benefits and harms.

## Illustrative Example

The dilemma of lying to save the life of an innocent person shows the difference. The strict Kantian rejects lying absolutely. The utilitarian justifies it for its great benefit. The jurist weighs the duty of truthfulness against the objective of preserving life, and prefers the preservation of life when the two conflict.

## The Relation Between Right Action and the Virtuous Agent

This discussion distinguishes between the question "what is the right act?" and the question "who is the virtuous agent?" A person may perform the right act by chance without inner virtue, and a virtuous person may sometimes fail to hit what is right despite the soundness of his intention. Advocates of virtue ethics hold that this very distinction is misleading, because an act's rightness cannot be known except by reference to the judgment of the wise agent in context. Deontologists and utilitarians insist, by contrast, that the criterion of the act is independent of the qualities of its agent.
