# Moral Agency

## Definition

Moral agency is a being's capacity to be a moral agent, that is, to be addressed with commands and prohibitions and to be held accountable for its actions. It is distinguished from the mere "moral patient", who deserves moral care without being itself required to act morally (such as an infant or an animal). Agency usually rests on three conditions: the capacity to understand (grasping the meaning of an act and its consequences), the capacity to choose (freedom of will), and the capacity to regulate behavior in accordance with moral consideration.

## Roots in Islamic Thought

Scholars of legal theory (*usul al-fiqh*) examined the conditions of *taklif* (legal accountability), which in essence correspond to the conditions of moral agency. They required reason, puberty and soundness of perception as conditions for the divine address to be directed at a human being. The saying "the pen is lifted from three: from the sleeper until he wakes, from the child until he reaches puberty, and from the insane until he regains reason" provides a precise delineation of the limits of responsible agency. The Mu'tazila elaborated this question through the doctrine that human beings originate their own voluntary acts (often discussed under *khalq al-af'al*, "the creation of acts"). Early Mu'tazili thinkers such as Abu al-Hudhayl al-Allaf are generally reported to have held that the human being is the true agent of their voluntary acts, which grounds the attribution of an act to the person and their accountability for it. The Ash'aris countered this position with the theory of "acquisition" (*kasb*), which makes the human being the acquirer of an act rather than its creator, in an attempt to reconcile absolute divine power with the need to affirm human agency sufficient for responsibility. Ibn Rushd, in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* (*Tahafut al-Tahafut*), also discussed the question of choice, arguing that human agency operates within a general causal order without this negating the meaning of choice.

## In Western Philosophy

Immanuel Kant distinguished between the rational being who possesses a "will" capable of self-legislation in accordance with pure reason, and other beings subject to purely natural laws. He thereby made moral agency depend on practical rationality rather than mere consciousness. Contemporary philosophy has raised the question of the limits of agency when applied to borderline cases: Does a person with dementia possess full moral agency? Can moral agency (not merely causal agency) be attributed to autonomous artificial intelligence systems? Luciano Floridi and J. W. Sanders, for example, have argued that artificial systems can be moral agents in a minimal, functional sense (they can be sources of morally qualifiable action and accountable for it) while arguing that this does not amount to the full moral responsibility attributed to human beings; this view is contested.

## Contemporary Importance

The concept of moral agency is invoked in criminal law to determine criminal capacity, in psychiatry to assess the responsibility of patients, and in the ethics of artificial intelligence to determine who bears responsibility when an autonomous automated system causes harm: is it the programmer, the manufacturing company, or the system itself? An illustrative example: a child who breaks a window while playing is not treated in the same way as an adult who does the same thing deliberately, because the former lacks the full conditions of moral agency.
