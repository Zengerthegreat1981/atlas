# Social Movements

Social movements are sustained, organised collective efforts to bring about — or to resist — social change. They occupy the space between everyday discontent and formal politics: looser than political parties, more durable than a single protest, they mobilise people around a shared grievance or vision and press their claims on states, institutions, or publics. Labour movements, civil rights movements, feminist movements, environmental movements, and nationalist movements are among the most consequential forms, and their histories are inseparable from the making of the modern world.

## Origins and Development

The concept took shape as scholars tried to explain why collective protest arises in some times and places and not others. Early crowd psychology, notably Gustave Le Bon's *The Crowd* (1895), treated collective action as irrational contagion, a view that dominated for decades. The field was transformed in the 1960s and 1970s by resource mobilisation theory, which treated movements as rational actors: grievances are widespread, its proponents argued, so what matters is the organisation, money, networks, and leadership that turn discontent into action. Charles Tilly's *From Mobilization to Revolution* (1978) added a historical dimension, showing that movements' repertoires of action — the strike, the demonstration, the petition — change with the political regime they confront.

Doug McAdam's study of the American civil rights movement, *Political Process and the Development of Black Insurgency* (1982), synthesised these insights into political process theory: movements succeed when political opportunities open, when organisations exist to seize them, and when participants develop a shared sense of injustice and agency. Sidney Tarrow's *Power in Movement* (1994) extended the analysis comparatively, while Alberto Melucci's *Nomads of the Present* (1989) drew attention to the "new social movements" — feminist, environmental, and peace movements — that organise around identity and culture rather than material interests alone, and that often prefer decentralised networks to hierarchical organisations.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One long-standing debate concerns the role of grievances versus resources and opportunities. Critics of resource mobilisation argued that it neglected why people care enough to act, and the study of framing — how movements define problems, assign blame, and propose solutions — supplied part of the answer. David Snow and his colleagues (1986) analysed "frame alignment", the ways in which movements link their interpretations to the beliefs and experiences of potential supporters, and later work stressed that frames mobilise more effectively when they resonate with existing cultural themes. A second dimension is the distinction between instrumental movements, which seek concrete policy changes, and expressive or identity-based movements, which seek recognition and cultural transformation; many movements pursue both at once.

A third dimension is the life cycle of movements: emergence, growth, institutionalisation, and decline. Some movements become political parties or non-governmental organisations; others are repressed, fragment, or are absorbed into the mainstream. Scholars debate whether institutionalisation is a success — the movement's goals entering policy — or a defeat, as its radical edge is blunted. The question of leadership and democracy within movements is equally contested: movements that claim to speak for the people often reproduce the hierarchies they oppose, and the tension between charismatic leadership and participatory organisation is a recurring theme.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Social movements remain a central subject in sociology, anthropology, and political science. Recent decades have seen the rise of transnational movements — against climate change, for global justice, for indigenous rights — that organise across borders, and of digitally networked movements, from the Arab uprisings to the global climate strikes, whose speed and scale depend on platforms that also expose them to surveillance and misinformation. The study of movements has also broadened to include conservative and reactionary movements, a reminder that collective mobilisation is a form, not a politics, and can serve exclusion as readily as emancipation.

Critics note that the field has often been biased toward visible, Western, progressive movements, neglecting quieter forms of collective action and movements in the Global South. Others argue that the classic theories, built on studies of twentieth-century movements, struggle with leaderless, digitally mediated mobilisations. The concept endures, however, because it names a durable feature of social life: that ordinary people, acting together and over time, can change the terms on which societies are governed — and that understanding how they do so is essential to understanding history itself.
