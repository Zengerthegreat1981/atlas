# Hukm Taklifi and Hukm Wad'i

Islamic usul al-fiqh classifies the **shar'i ruling** into two main categories that are functionally distinct. The first is the **normative ruling** (hukm taklifi), which addresses the obligated person directly by requesting an act, an omission, or a choice. The second is the **declaratory ruling** (hukm wad'i), which does not address the obligated person with a direct act, but establishes a matter that has a legal effect. It thereby organizes the **structure** of the transactions and events in which the obligation itself takes place.

## The normative ruling: the five categories

Most usulists divide the normative ruling into five categories. The **obligatory** (wajib) is an act whose doer is rewarded and whose omitter is punished. The **recommended** (mandub) is an act whose doer is rewarded and whose omitter is not punished. The **permissible** (mubah) is an act whose doing or omitting carries neither reward nor punishment. The **reprehensible** (makruh) is an act whose omitter is rewarded and whose doer is not punished. The **prohibited** (haram) is an act whose doer is punished and whose omitter is rewarded.

This five-part classification is the direct framework by which the obligated individual is addressed in his daily conduct. It roughly corresponds to the function of Hart's "primary rules", which directly govern conduct, although the usulists did not think in those terms.

The Hanafi school divides the categories more finely. It distinguishes the **fard** (established by decisive proof) from the **wajib** (established by probable proof), whereas the other schools generally treat the two terms as synonyms (with exceptions in some Hanbali and Shafi'i opinions on particular matters). The Hanafis also distinguish between a prohibition of strong force (*makruh tahriman*) and a mild disapproval (*makruh tanzihan*).

## The declaratory ruling: cause, condition and impediment

The declaratory ruling includes concepts such as the following. A **cause** (sabab) is an apparent, regulated attribute on which the Lawgiver makes the establishment of another ruling dependent; the entry of the prayer time, for example, is the cause of the obligation to pray. A **condition** (shart) is that on which the existence of a ruling depends without being directly influential in it; purity, for example, is a condition for the validity of prayer. An **impediment** (mani') is an attribute that prevents the ruling from following even when its cause exists; menstruation, for example, prevents the obligation of prayer despite the entry of its time.

The declaratory ruling also includes concepts such as **validity, nullity, invalidity and enforceability**, which determine whether a particular act meets the conditions for its legal effect to follow. These concepts do not directly command the obligated person to act. They organize the **structural framework** within which his acts are valid or void. In this respect they loosely resemble Hart's "secondary rules", and especially his power-conferring rules (such as those governing contracts and wills), although the source and method differ fundamentally. They are not equivalent to Hart's rule of recognition, which identifies the criteria of validity for the legal system as a whole.

## The relation between the two categories

The two rulings are not practically separated; they complement each other. The declaratory ruling, such as the consideration of a contract as a cause for the establishment of ownership under its conditions, is what prepares the ground on which the normative ruling falls, such as the obligation to fulfill the contract. Neither is reduced to the other or dispensed with. This precise distinction gives the jurist an analytical tool capable of handling complex matters, such as the validity of a formally defective contract while some of its effects remain, or the nullity of a transaction for lack of a condition despite the presence of its cause.

## Impact and critique

Some comparative legal theorists argue that this usulist distinction between the normative and declaratory ruling roughly parallels the modern distinction between direct imperative rules and structural regulatory rules in positivist legal philosophy. The explicit formulation of the two categories became standard in usul works of the later classical period. The usulists did not formulate this distinction in modern terms, nor for purposes of philosophical comparison; they derived it internally from the needs of analyzing the Islamic textual sources themselves. This analytical convergence remains an interpretive observation by modern scholars, not a claim of complete methodological identity between two historically distinct frameworks that differ radically in their sources and purposes.

## Original Ruling and Concession; Void and Defective Contracts

Usulists also distinguish *'azima*, the ruling prescribed generally in the first instance, from *rukhsa*, a concession that departs from it for a recognized excuse while the original evidence remains. Standard examples are the traveller's shortening of prayer and postponement of the Ramadan fast, and eating carrion to escape death. The force of a concession varies: eating carrion in true necessity is regarded by most as obligatory, while someone coerced to utter unbelief may do so with a heart secure in faith, yet steadfastness is considered more meritorious. A concession applies only within the limits of its excuse.

In contracts, most schools treat *batil* and *fasid* as equivalent names for nullity, although they separate them in some particular matters. The Hanafis distinguish them systematically in property transactions. A *batil* contract is defective in its essence (*asl*), as when a party lacks all capacity or carrion is sold, and produces no legal transfer. A *fasid* contract is sound in essence but defective in an attached quality (*wasf*), as with a usurious stipulation or an unknown term of payment. It should be rescinded, but if the buyer takes possession with the seller's permission, a defective ownership passes; removing the defect in time may cure the contract.
