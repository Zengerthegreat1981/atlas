# Patriarchy: A System of Male Domination

**Patriarchy** is a system of social structures and practices in which men dominate, oppress, and exploit women. The term, from the Greek for rule by the father, originally described societies governed by male heads of households, but feminist theory expanded it to name a system that operates across the family, the workplace, the state, religion, and culture. Patriarchy is not a description of individual men behaving badly; it is an account of how institutions are organized so that men as a group hold more power, resources, and authority than women as a group, and how that arrangement is maintained, legitimized, and resisted.

## Definition and Development

The concept was developed in second-wave feminism in the late 1960s and 1970s. Kate Millett, in *Sexual Politics* (1970), argued that patriarchy is a political system, not a natural order. Through the family, education, and literature, she showed, women are taught to accept their subordination as natural, while men are taught to expect dominance. Millett's analysis of literary works by D. H. Lawrence, Henry Miller, and Norman Mailer demonstrated how culture reproduces patriarchal attitudes, presenting male dominance as erotic and inevitable.

Shulamith Firestone, in *The Dialectic of Sex* (1970), rooted patriarchy in biology and reproduction. Women, Firestone argued, are oppressed because of their role in reproduction — pregnancy, childbirth, and childcare — and liberation requires the technological transformation of reproduction itself. Her argument was controversial, but it established a central debate in feminist theory: is patriarchy rooted in biology, in the economy, in culture, or in all of these together?

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Gerda Lerner, in *The Creation of Patriarchy* (1986), gave the concept a historical foundation. Drawing on the history of the ancient Near East, Lerner showed that patriarchy was created, not natural: she argued that it took shape over roughly two and a half thousand years as the archaic states of the ancient Near East formed, bound up with the control of women's sexuality and reproductive capacity and with the institution of slavery. Because it was created historically, Lerner argued, it can be ended historically. Her work answered the claim that male dominance is universal and timeless by showing its specific origins in particular societies.

Sylvia Walby, in *Theorizing Patriarchy* (1990), gave the most systematic sociological account. Walby defined patriarchy as a system of six structures: household production, paid work, the state, male violence, sexuality, and cultural institutions. She distinguished private patriarchy, in which an individual man — a husband or father — controls women within the household, from public patriarchy, in which women are subordinated collectively in public life, segregated into low-paid work and excluded from power. As women entered paid employment, Walby argued, patriarchy did not disappear; it changed form, moving from the private to the public sphere.

Intersectional scholarship has deepened the concept further. Kimberlé Crenshaw, in "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (1989), showed that patriarchy is experienced differently by women of different races and classes: Black women face a patriarchy that is inseparable from racism, and a feminism that speaks only of gender misses their experience. This insight has made patriarchy a more complex concept, attentive to how gender intersects with race, class, and colonialism.

## Patriarchy beyond the West

Scholars outside Europe and North America have both used and questioned the concept. Deniz Kandiyoti, in "Bargaining with Patriarchy" (1988), described a "classic patriarchy" found across much of the Middle East, South and East Asia, and North Africa, in which women gain security and standing in later life through sons and through strategies worked out within male-dominated households; she called these strategies "patriarchal bargains." The Nigerian sociologist Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí, in *The Invention of Women* (1997), argued that social hierarchy among the Yoruba was organised around seniority rather than gender, and that gender categories were imposed through colonial rule, challenging the assumption that patriarchy is a universal framework. These contributions reinforce the point that patriarchal arrangements vary by society and history.

## Examples

Patriarchy is visible in the division of labour. Across the world, women do most of the unpaid domestic and care work, while men dominate the highest-paid professions and the most powerful political offices. It is visible in the law: in many societies, women only recently gained the right to own property, to divorce, or to vote, and in some they still lack these rights. It is visible in violence: domestic violence, sexual harassment, and the control of women's bodies are not isolated incidents but patterns that maintain male power.

It is also visible in culture. The expectation that women should be beautiful, modest, and caring, and that men should be strong, decisive, and independent, shapes how children are raised, how work is valued, and how people understand themselves. These expectations are not imposed by force alone; they are learned, internalized, and often defended by women themselves — which is why feminist theory has paid so much attention to consciousness and to the ways women resist, reinterpret, and challenge patriarchal norms.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics have argued that patriarchy is too broad a term, lumping together very different arrangements under a single label, and that it can imply a false universality, as if all women everywhere were oppressed in the same way. Others have argued that it underplays the agency of women and the variation among men, treating men as a single dominant group. These criticisms have led scholars to speak of patriarchies in the plural, and to study how patriarchal structures vary across societies and change over time.

The concept remains central to sociology and anthropology. Contemporary research applies it to the gender pay gap, to the politics of reproduction, to violence against women, and to the persistence of male dominance in politics and business despite formal equality. It is also applied to new domains: online harassment, the algorithmic reproduction of gender stereotypes, and the global care economy, in which women from poorer countries care for the children and elderly of richer ones. Patriarchy remains the name for a system that formal equality has not ended — and for the struggle to end it.
