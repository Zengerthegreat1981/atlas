# Authority

Authority is power that is accepted as legitimate: the recognized right to issue commands, make decisions or define what counts as true, which others obey or defer to because they believe it is justified and not merely because they are forced to. The central sociological problem is how such acceptance arises and what sustains it.

## Weber's Types of Legitimate Domination

Max Weber's *Economy and Society* (posthumous, 1922) is the foundation of the modern discussion. Weber defined power as the chance to carry out one's will even against resistance, and domination (*Herrschaft*) as the probability that a command will be obeyed by a given group of people. Authority is the form of domination that rests on a belief in its legitimacy. He distinguished three pure types of legitimate authority according to the ground of that belief:

- **Legal-rational authority** rests on belief in the legality of enacted rules and the right of those holding office under them to issue commands. Its typical administrative form is bureaucracy.
- **Traditional authority** rests on belief in the sanctity of long-standing customs and of those who exercise authority under them, as in patriarchal and patrimonial rule.
- **Charismatic authority** rests on devotion to the exceptional sanctity, heroism or exemplary character of a person and the order he or she reveals.

Weber presented these as ideal types: real regimes usually mix them, and charisma tends to be routinized into traditional or legal forms after the founder's death.

## Power, Authority and Legitimacy

Later work separated authority from other forms of influence. Hannah Arendt, in her essay "What Is Authority?" (1961), argued that authority excludes both coercion by force and persuasion by argument. Robert Dahl's "The Concept of Power" (1957) treated power as the ability to get another actor to do what they would not otherwise do, and Steven Lukes's *Power: A Radical View* (1974) added agenda-setting and the shaping of preferences as further dimensions. Authority also operates outside politics. Paul Starr's *The Social Transformation of American Medicine* (1982) analyzed the "cultural authority" of physicians, the capacity to have one's definitions of reality accepted as valid, and its link to professional organization and state support.

## Obedience Research

Stanley Milgram's experiments at Yale, first reported in 1963 and developed in *Obedience to Authority* (1974), examined how far ordinary volunteers would follow an experimenter's instruction to administer what they believed were increasingly severe electric shocks to another person. In the best-known condition about two thirds of participants continued to the maximum level. Milgram interpreted the results as showing the power of situational authority. The work has been criticized on ethical grounds, for its artificial setting, and for its interpretation: later researchers, notably Alexander Haslam and Stephen Reicher, argue that participants acted as engaged followers who identified with the scientific project, not simply as obedient agents.

## Critiques and Limits

Weber's typology has been criticized for treating legitimacy as a matter of subjective belief that is hard to measure, and for underplaying how elites manufacture belief. Critical theorists such as Jürgen Habermas have asked whether legitimacy rests on genuine consent or on managed acceptance. The typology was also built mostly from European and a few comparative cases, and scholars have asked how well it fits political orders elsewhere. Feminist and postcolonial writers have shown how authority is distributed unequally by gender and race, so that who is heard as an authority is itself a product of inequality.
