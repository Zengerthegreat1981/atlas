# Cross-Cultural Morality

## Meaning and Definition

This concept addresses a twofold question: are there fixed moral standards that transcend differences among cultures, or is morality wholly relative, formed entirely within each culture in isolation from others? It gives rise to a methodological question: how can one judge a practice in another culture (such as female genital cutting or polygamy) without falling into cultural arrogance or into a helpless surrender to uncritical acceptance?

## The Islamic Position

Islamic thought offers a middle position that combines the stability of fundamental principles with the variability of their details. The great values, such as justice, honesty, and the prohibition of wrongdoing, are held as constant by both the Ash'aris and the Mu'tazilites, although they differed on the source of their recognition (divine law or reason). The forms of applying these values, however, vary with differing customs, within the jurisprudential maxim "custom is authoritative" (*al-'ada muhakkama*), which permits taking into account the custom of each land so long as it does not contradict a definitive text. Al-Shatibi, in *al-Muwafaqat*, discussed the idea of the "universal objectives" (preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny, and property) as a universal standard by which the legitimacy of local customs is measured. Whatever undermines these objectives is rejected, however deeply rooted it may be culturally.

## The Anthropological and Philosophical Context

Sociology and anthropology in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries advanced a strong moral relativism through William Graham Sumner (*Folkways*, 1906) and Ruth Benedict (*Patterns of Culture*, 1934), who held that "right" is simply what the group has become accustomed to, so that no standard outside the culture can judge it. This view, however, collided after the Second World War with the problem of condemning Nazism: if morality is purely relative, by what right do we condemn the genocide that its own culture regarded as legitimate? This led philosophers such as James Rachels (in *The Elements of Moral Philosophy*) to distinguish between differences in practices (which are real and wide-ranging) and differences in underlying values (which are much narrower). For example, Herodotus's contrast between peoples who eat their dead and Greeks who cremate them suggests different practices that may express a shared value: respect for the dead. Martha Nussbaum defended a "context-sensitive" universalism that recognizes a minimal universal set of human dignities (the capabilities approach), while leaving a wide margin for cultural diversity in the details.

## Applied Example

This tension is clearly visible in the debate over women's rights worldwide. While some practices, such as genital mutilation, are condemned by broad international consensus as a violation of a universal human dignity, questions of dress and marriage remain contested between those who see them as a legitimate cultural expression and those who see them as oppression violating a universal standard of equality.

## The Methodological Question

The hardest methodological question remains: who has the right to decide what is a "universal principle" and what is a "local variant"? One practical criterion, in the spirit of the Golden Rule, is reciprocity: would the members of a culture accept a practice if they were in the position of those who bear its burdens? A flat rejection suggests that the practice may express domination rather than a mutually shared value, though reasonable people disagree about how far this test can settle hard cases.
