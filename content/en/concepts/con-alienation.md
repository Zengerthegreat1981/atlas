# Alienation

Alienation is the condition in which people are estranged from something that should belong to them: the products and process of their labour, other people, the society they live in, or their own sense of self. The concept entered social theory through German philosophy and was developed most fully by Karl Marx; it was later reworked by sociologists into a set of psychological and organisational variables that can be studied empirically. It is distinct from anomie, which concerns a lack of social regulation rather than a loss of control over one's own life and work.

## Origins in Philosophy

The German terms *Entfremdung* (estrangement) and *Entäusserung* (externalisation) appear in G. W. F. Hegel's account of spirit objectifying itself in the world. Ludwig Feuerbach's *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) argued that religion is a form of human self-alienation, in which people project their own capacities onto a divine being. Marx took over this vocabulary but located the source of alienation in the organisation of labour rather than in thought or religion.

## Marx's Account

In the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, written in 1844 and first published in 1932, Marx identified four dimensions of alienated labour under capitalism. Workers are alienated from the product of their labour, which belongs to the capitalist and confronts them as an alien power; from the activity of labour itself, which is experienced as forced and external; from their species-being, the creative, social capacities he saw as characteristic of humans; and from other people, who appear as competitors or as owners. In the later *Capital* (1867) the vocabulary of alienation recedes in favour of exploitation, surplus value, and commodity fetishism, and scholars disagree about whether this marks a break or a continuation of the earlier themes.

## Later Sociological Formulations

Max Weber's analysis of rationalisation and bureaucracy, with its image of an iron cage, and Georg Simmel's *The Philosophy of Money* (1900), with its account of the growing gap between individual and objective culture, offered non-Marxist versions of the theme. Georg Lukacs's *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) developed the related concept of reification, the process by which social relations appear as thing-like and natural. Erich Fromm's *The Sane Society* (1955) gave a humanist psychological reading, and Herbert Marcuse's *One-Dimensional Man* (1964) argued that consumer society integrates and pacifies individuals.

## Empirical Research

The concept was made measurable by Melvin Seeman's "On the Meaning of Alienation" (1959), which distinguished five meanings: powerlessness, meaninglessness, normlessness, isolation, and self-estrangement. Robert Blauner's *Alienation and Freedom* (1964) compared workers in printing, textiles, automobile assembly, and chemical processing, and concluded that alienation was highest in assembly-line work and lower where workers had more control, with technology and industry shaping the experience of work. Harry Braverman's *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974) revived the argument that management strips skill from work, and Michael Burawoy's *Manufacturing Consent* (1979) showed from fieldwork in a Chicago factory how workers could be drawn into shop-floor games that secured their consent. Arlie Hochschild's *The Managed Heart* (1983) extended the idea to emotional labour, arguing that some service workers may become estranged from their own feelings. More recently Hartmut Rosa has described alienation as a relationship to the world that is mute and unresponsive, in *Resonance* (2016).

## Critiques and Debates

Louis Althusser argued that the humanist concept of alienation belongs to the early, pre-scientific Marx, and that it should be replaced by structural analysis. Others object that the concept presupposes a fixed human essence against which estrangement is measured, which is difficult to defend. Survey measures such as Seeman's capture feelings but not the structural conditions that produce them, and different studies use quite different indicators, which makes comparison difficult. Defenders reply that the concept remains useful for connecting work conditions, social inequality, and subjective wellbeing.
