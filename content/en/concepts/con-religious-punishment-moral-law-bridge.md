# Religious Punishment and Moral Law

## Punishment between Sharia and Purpose

In the Islamic tradition, punishment is not understood as pure retaliation, but as part of a system of rulings that protects what the jurists called the five necessities: religion, life, reason, progeny, and property. This classification was articulated by Abu Hamid al-Ghazali in *al-Mustasfa* and then expanded by al-Shatibi in *al-Muwafaqat*. Jurisprudential manuals divide offences into fixed penalties (*hudud*, such as theft, armed robbery, and adultery), retaliation (*qisas*) for offences against life and bodily harm, and discretionary penalties (*taʿzir*) left to the judge's discretion. Al-Mawardi, in *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya*, gathered what relates to the office of *hisba* (market supervision), grievances, and penalties. It is notable that the jurists surrounded the *hadd* with strict evidentiary conditions: penalties are averted by doubt, four witnesses are required for adultery, and the penalty for armed robbery is dropped by repentance before the offender is apprehended, as in Quran 5:34. Thus worldly punishment, expiation, and repentance sit side by side in this domain, and the moral and the legal are not sharply separated.

## Theoretical Questions: Religion as a Source of Moral Law

When this domain is read through the lens of sociology, Émile Durkheim's analysis in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) stands out: penal law in homogeneous societies expresses the collective conscience, and punishment is a collective emotional reaction to the violation of the sacred before it is a calculation of deterrence. Nietzsche, in *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887), traces the notion of guilt (*Schuld*) to the debtor-creditor relation, and sees religious punishment as the transformation of external pain into a tormenting internal conscience. Against them, Paul Ricœur, in *The Symbolism of Evil* (1960), offers a phenomenological reading of defilement, sin, and guilt as layers of symbolic experience that precede legislation. H. L. A. Hart, in *Punishment and Responsibility* (1968), adds a key distinction between justifying a system of punishment and distributing it, a distinction useful for examining any religious system of retribution.

## Points of Convergence and Divergence

The two domains converge in that both link act to retribution and presuppose a responsible, accountable agent. But religion adds an otherworldly dimension, so that worldly punishment becomes, for many jurists, an expiation that discharges the claim in the afterlife, a point on which they differ. Modern legal systems also separate crime from sin: positive law does not punish what causes no harm to others, whereas religious law sometimes punishes what also touches the right of God. On the other hand, Wael Hallaq, in *Sharia: Theory, Practice, Transformations* (2009), shows that the historical application of the *hudud* was rare compared with its common image, and that the weight of jurisprudence fell on procedural controls and on *taʿzir* and settlements.

## Limits of the Approach and Its Critique

Comparing religious punishment with moral law faces two dangers. The first is projecting concepts of modern law (such as the state and imprisonment) onto systems that preceded them: imprisonment is a penalty of little presence in classical jurisprudence compared with flogging, blood money, and fines. The second is generalising a single model across all religions, although each tradition has its own logic: the Indian conception of *karma*, the Christian doctrine of original sin, and the Islamic concept of the *hudud* differ in where retribution is located, in its duration, and in its susceptibility to expiation. Moreover, the theories of Durkheim and Nietzsche have strong explanatory force but are reductive, in that they read belief through its social function or its buried psychology, and they neglect the self-understanding of believers who see the divine law as divine justice.
