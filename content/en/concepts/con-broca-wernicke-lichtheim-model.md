# Broca-Wernicke-Lichtheim Model

## Definition

The Broca-Wernicke-Lichtheim model is the classical anatomical-functional model of language processing in the brain. It took shape gradually between 1861 and 1885 from three successive contributions. The first was Paul Broca's 1861 description linking loss of expressive speech with damage to the left inferior frontal gyrus (the area later named after him), in his famous patient "Tan." The second was Carl Wernicke's 1874 description of a different pattern of aphasia that affects comprehension rather than production, associated with damage to the posterior superior temporal gyrus. The third was Ludwig Lichtheim's 1885 formulation of a unifying connectionist model that joined the contributions of Broca and Wernicke in a single connectional diagram.

## Schematic Structure

The model posits two main language centres linked by a neural bundle (the arcuate fasciculus): a centre for the motor images of words (Broca's area) and a centre for the auditory images of words (Wernicke's area). It also posits a third, conceptual centre that represents meaning and connects to both. From the layout of these centres and the connections between them, seven expected patterns of aphasia were derived according to the site of the lesion: cortical motor aphasia (Broca), cortical sensory aphasia (Wernicke), conduction aphasia (interruption of the pathway between the two centres), subcortical motor and sensory forms (pure word dumbness and pure word deafness), and transcortical motor and sensory aphasias. The transcortical forms arise when a language centre is cut off from the conceptual centre without the centre itself being damaged.

## Historical and Classificatory Value

This model was an early systematic attempt to link linguistic function to specific brain locations and to explain the diversity of aphasic symptoms through a single connectional logic. Earlier writers, including Jean-Baptiste Bouillaud, had already argued for a frontal seat of speech. It founded a tradition that survives in clinical practice under the name "the classical classification of aphasia." Its descriptive terminology (Broca's aphasia, Wernicke's aphasia, conduction aphasia) is still used in everyday clinical communication, despite modern theories having moved beyond its details.

## Limits of the Model

Twentieth-century studies, especially Norman Geschwind's research in the 1960s (including his 1965 work on disconnection syndromes), showed that many aphasia patients do not match the model's predictions precisely, since the lesion site is not always associated with the expected clinical pattern. Later lesion-mapping work, including that of Nina Dronkers, indicated that lesion size and extension into subcortical white matter contribute substantially alongside cortical location. These gaps led to alternative models, the most prominent being Hickok and Poeppel's dual-stream model. It replaces the anatomical classification with a functional division into a ventral stream for understanding meaning and a dorsal stream for translating sound into articulation.

## Continuing Influence

Later work has revised or replaced many of its anatomical claims, but the syndrome names and the practice of relating symptom patterns to lesion sites remain part of clinical teaching in speech-language pathology and behavioural neurology.
