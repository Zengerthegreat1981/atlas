# Source Authority and Credibility

**The basic question**: How do we judge the **reliability of a source**? Was the witness present? Does he have a motive to lie? This question is not a marginal technical detail but the very core of historical work. Every historical conclusion is ultimately built on an assessment, explicit or implicit, of how far one source can be trusted over another when accounts conflict.

## Traditional Criteria for Assessment

Traditions of source criticism have developed a set of closely related criteria despite their differing contexts:

- **Temporal and spatial proximity to the event**: An eyewitness is more reliable than a transmitter relaying from another transmitter, and a contemporary of the event is more reliable than a historian writing centuries later, with variation according to the witness's own capacity for observation and understanding.
- **Absence of motive to distort**: A source that praises its political opponent or admits the error of its own side is more reliable than a source that glorifies its patron or vilifies its enemy, because testimony against one's own interest (testimony against interest) is less prone to fabrication.
- **Internal and external consistency**: Do the details of the account hang together internally? And do they agree with other independent sources (inscriptions, archival documents, testimonies from hostile parties who have no prior agreement to coordinate their accounts)?

## The Islamic Model of *al-Jarh wa-al-Ta'dil*

From the second century AH onwards, Muslim hadith scholars developed a highly specialised system for assessing the credibility of the transmitters themselves rather than only the text, known as **the science of *al-jarh wa-al-ta'dil*** (criticism and validation of transmitters). The biography of each transmitter in the chain of transmission (*isnad*) is examined: is he truthful and retentive in his memory, or is he known for concealment (*tadlis*), forgetfulness, or even deliberate lying? The ranks of transmitters (trustworthy, truthful, weak, abandoned) are classified in specialised reference works such as al-Mizzi's *Tahdhib al-Kamal*, so that the credibility of the text becomes a mathematical function of the credibility of each link in its chain of transmission. This methodology was unique worldwide in its early precision, compared with the absence of any parallel system of comparable organisation in the Greek or Roman historiography of the same period.

## Credibility Is Not an Absolute Attribute but Context-Relative

The most important methodological lesson is that credibility is not a fixed property of a source in itself, but a relative judgement that changes with the question being asked. A source politically biased and entirely unreliable in its account of the causes of a war may be an excellent and fully reliable source for details of everyday life or prices, where it has no interest in falsifying them. Modern historians therefore reject the notion of a "good source" or a "bad source" in absolute terms, and replace it with a more precise question: reliable in what precisely, and for what purpose?

## A Modern Tension: Criticism of Absolute Objectivity

Since the late twentieth century, postmodern historians (influenced by **Hayden White**) have questioned the possibility of separating "factual credibility" from the narrative structure that the historian imposes on his material. Even the most detailed, reliable source is read through an interpretive framework chosen by the historian himself. This criticism did not invalidate the need to assess credibility in practice, but it reminded us that judging the "reliability" of a source is an interpretive act, not a neutral extraction of a ready-made truth.
