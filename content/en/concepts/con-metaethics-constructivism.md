# Metaethical Constructivism

Metaethical constructivism is the view that moral truths are neither objective facts existing in the world independently of rational agents, nor merely a subjective expression of emotions. Rather, they are the product of a constructive process carried out by practical reason according to specific procedures of deliberation, choice, and rational agreement. On this view, moral rightness is what results from the correct procedure, not what matches a truth that precedes it.

## Definition

Constructivism occupies a middle position between moral realism, which assumes the existence of objective moral values independent of any human procedure (for example, that injustice is wrong regardless of what any mind thinks about it), and non-realism, which denies that there are moral truths at all. The constructivist accepts that there are correct and incorrect answers in ethics, but holds that the standard of rightness is the product of a constructive rational procedure, such as agreement behind a "veil of ignorance" or testing through the categorical imperative, rather than a correspondence to an independent moral reality that precedes reason.

## Historical Context

Immanuel Kant is the most remote philosophical origin of constructivism. He held that moral laws are not discovered in the order of the cosmos, but are legislated by practical reason for itself, through the test of universalizability in the categorical imperative. In the twentieth century, John Rawls, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), developed a political-moral constructivism in which the principles of justice are those that rational contractors would agree upon behind a "veil of ignorance" that hides their social positions from them. Justice is thus a procedural construction, not the discovery of a prior truth. Christine Korsgaard developed this idea in a more deeply Kantian direction, arguing that the source of moral obligation is the identity of the rational agent itself, and the necessity that the agent endorse principles that it can endorse as a coherent rational agent.

In Islamic kalam thought, a partial similarity can be observed in the position of the Mu'tazilites, who linked "good" and "evil" to reason's perception of real benefits and harms. Yet they remained closer to rational realism than to procedural constructivism, since they held that reason discovers the goodness or badness of an act rather than producing them through a deliberative procedure. The Ash'arites, by contrast, linked good and evil to pure divine command, a position closer to divine voluntarism (a divine command view), on which the divine will determines moral value rather than reason discovering a prior moral truth; it is sometimes compared to constructivism only in the loose sense that value is not independent of an authoritative will.

## Contemporary Significance

Constructivism is used today as a framework for justifying principles of justice and political rights in pluralistic societies that do not agree on a shared moral metaphysics. It allows practical agreement on fair procedures without the need to settle the metaphysical debate over the nature of values. It is objected to on the ground that it makes morality hostage to the chosen procedure: if the procedure is flawed or its conditions change, the moral truths themselves change with it. This seems incompatible with our intuition that some acts (such as torturing the innocent) are wrong under every possible procedure.
