# Rhetoric and Persuasion in Political History

In classical antiquity, historiography and rhetoric were inextricably intertwined. Far from being regarded as a detached archival science, historical writing was conceived as a high literary art (*ars rhetorica*) aimed at public persuasion, moral pedagogy, and political analysis. Ancient historians received their advanced education in rhetorical schools; in turn, their narratives analyzed the persuasive power of speech (*peitho* in Greek; *eloquentia* in Latin) as the decisive catalyst of political decisions in civic assemblies, senatorial debates, and battlefield harangues.

## Classical Greek Historiography: Rhetoric as Historical Engine

In classical democratic Athens, persuasive eloquence was the principal instrument of political authority. Thucydides embedded political oratory directly into the causal architecture of his *History of the Peloponnesian War*. Paired speeches—such as the Mytilenean debate between Cleon and Diodotus, or the contest between Nicias and Alcibiades over the Sicilian expedition—were not decorative interludes, but analytical dissections of how rhetorical arguments, appeals to collective emotion (*pathos*), and demagogic manipulation altered imperial policy and produced military catastrophe. Thucydides explicitly stated his method in composing speeches (1.22): adhering as closely as possible to the general sense (*xympasa gnome*) of what was actually said while composing what the situation demanded (*ta deonta*).

In the fourth century BCE, the Athenian orator and educator Isocrates transformed historical prose. Isocrates promoted history as an elevated, stylistically polished medium designed to provide ethical exempla and panegyric celebration of civic virtue. Historians trained in or influenced by Isocrates' academy, notably Ephorus of Cyme and Theopompus of Chios, introduced elaborate periodic sentence structures, moralizing digressions, and vivid emotional descriptions (*enargeia*) into mainstream historiographical practice.

## Hellenistic Dramatization versus Polybian Pragmatism

During the Hellenistic period, the tension between rhetorical entertainment and factual accuracy became a central methodological debate. The school of "tragic history," represented by writers such as Duris of Samos and Phylarchus, prioritized emotional theatricality, deploying sensational rhetoric to evoke pity (*eleos*) and terror (*phobos*) in the reader.

Polybius (*Histories*, Book 12) mounted a fierce philosophical attack against this dramatic historiography. He argued that the purpose of tragedy is to bewilder and charm an audience for immediate pleasure (*terpsis*), whereas the sole objective of serious history is to instruct and benefit statesmen through truth (*aletheia*). While Polybius rejected sensationalist drama, he firmly preserved composed speeches as essential analytical devices to illuminate the secret motives, political options, and strategic thinking of historical figures.

## Roman Historiography: Oratory, Invective, and Imperial Decline

In Roman intellectual culture, Cicero famously described historical writing in *De Legibus* (1.5) as *opus oratorium maxime* ("above all an oratorical task"). Roman historians utilized rhetorical eloquence to evaluate character and diagnose political health. Sallust adapted the austere, antithetical style of Thucydides and Cato the Elder to expose factional hypocrisy in *Bellum Catilinae* and *Bellum Iugurthinum*, crafting memorable rhetorical confrontations such as the senatorial debate between Julius Caesar and Cato the Younger.

Under the Principate, Tacitus analyzed the relationship between political freedom and rhetorical vigor. In his *Dialogus de Oratoribus*, Tacitus (chiefly through the speaker Maternus) presents the view that great republican oratory flourished amidst the turbulent political liberty of the Late Republic; with the pacification of the state under imperial autocracy, genuine civic eloquence inevitably atrophied, degenerating into superficial schoolroom declamation and sycophantic court adulation (*adulatio*).
