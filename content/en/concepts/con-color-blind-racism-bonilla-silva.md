# Color-Blind Racism in Bonilla-Silva

A founding concept formulated by Eduardo Bonilla-Silva to describe a newer, more covert form of racism that replaced the explicit racial prejudice of the segregation era, allowing individuals with apparently good intentions to support an existing system of racial inequality without feeling that they are racist bigots.

## The Central Idea

Bonilla-Silva identified four main "self-evident" discursive frames, apparently racially neutral, that together form "color-blind racism": "abstract liberalism", justifying opposition to race-conscious affirmative policies in the language of individual freedom of choice and abstract merit without acknowledging the existing historical structural inequality; "naturalization", explaining phenomena such as racial residential segregation as the spontaneous "natural" result of people's casual preference for similarity ("people tend to live near those who resemble them") rather than the result of actual structural discrimination; "cultural racism", attributing racial inequality to supposed "cultural" characteristics of a particular racial group (such as an alleged weak value placed on education or hard work) instead of to social structure and institutional discrimination; and "minimization of racism", claiming that racism has become a historical problem of the past that does not deserve serious contemporary attention, ignoring the continuing statistically documented evidence of actual discrimination. These four frames work together as an integrated system that allows actual structural racial inequality to continue without the need for an explicit racist justification that is socially rejected today.

## Empirical Methodology

In building this framework, Bonilla-Silva drew on two data sets: the 1997 Survey of Social Attitudes of College Students, a questionnaire of several hundred undergraduates at three universities, and the 1998 Detroit Area Study, whose respondents (including Black and white residents) answered questions on race, with a subset interviewed in depth. He analyzed the way white Americans talk about race issues when asked directly. He found that they rarely use explicitly racist language, relying instead on those four "self-evident frames" in an almost unconscious way and repeatedly across the different interviews, in a hesitant and halting style that reflects, in his view, a tension between a desire to appear non-racist and an implicit defense of an existing system of racial privilege.

## Illustration of the Frames

In the interview material, abstract liberalism appears when respondents endorse equal opportunity in principle while rejecting affirmative action or school integration measures as unfair to individuals. Naturalization appears in explanations of segregated neighborhoods or friendships as people "naturally" preferring their own kind. Cultural racism appears in explanations of group disparities by reference to family or work values, and minimization in claims that discrimination is no longer a major obstacle. Bonilla-Silva also describes recurring "styles" of talk, such as hedging and disclaimers, which accompany the frames.

## Critiques

Critics have asked how far discourse in an interview reflects underlying beliefs and behavior, and have noted that the samples (college students and Detroit-area residents of one period) limit generalization. Others argue that treating common positions on policy, such as opposition to affirmative action, as evidence of racism risks classifying principled disagreement as bias.

## Influence

This concept made a central founding contribution to contemporary sociological theory of race and racism. It offered a deeply influential analytical framework for understanding the persistence of racial inequality in the post-civil-rights era that officially pretends to complete racial neutrality. It became one of the most cited and taught frameworks in contemporary sociology of race, deeply influencing later research on racial discourse, implicit bias and public policies concerning racial justice.
