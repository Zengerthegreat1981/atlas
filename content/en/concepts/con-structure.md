# Structure: The Patterned Relations That Shape Social Life

**Structure** is the relatively stable pattern of relations between the parts of a society — its institutions, roles, classes, and groups — that shapes what people can do, what they expect of one another, and what outcomes their actions produce. Where an individual act is a single event, structure is the enduring arrangement that makes acts intelligible: the family, the economy, the state, and the class system are structures in this sense. Structure is one of sociology's most contested concepts, because it raises the central question of the discipline: how much of human life is determined by forces beyond individual choice, and how much is made by people acting together?

## Definition and Development

The concept entered sociology from several directions. Émile Durkheim treated social structure as the system of social facts — norms, institutions, and collective representations — that constrain individuals from outside. Karl Marx treated it as the relations of production: the way a society organizes labour, property, and the distribution of wealth determines its politics, law, and ideas. Both saw structure as real and powerful, though they disagreed about what it consisted of and how it could be changed.

Structural anthropology gave the term a different meaning. Claude Lévi-Strauss, in *Structural Anthropology* (1958, English edition 1963), argued that the apparent diversity of myths, kinship systems, and rituals conceals a small number of underlying structures — patterns of opposition and transformation, such as nature and culture, raw and cooked — that are universal to the human mind. For Lévi-Strauss, structure was not the observable institution but the unconscious logic that generates it. His work made structure a matter of relations rather than things: a term has meaning only through its opposition to other terms.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Pierre Bourdieu, in *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1977, French original 1972), offered a way out of the opposition between structure and agency. Social structure, for Bourdieu, exists as objective relations — the distribution of economic, cultural, and social capital — and as embodied dispositions, the habitus, the durable ways of perceiving and acting that people acquire through their position in the social world. Structure is thus both outside and inside people: it constrains action, but it is also reproduced by action, so that the social order is continually remade in everyday practice.

Anthony Giddens, in *The Constitution of Society* (1984), pursued a related line of argument in structuration theory. Structure, Giddens argued, is not a thing but a set of rules and resources that people draw on when they act, and that their action in turn reproduces. He called this the duality of structure: structures are both the medium and the outcome of social action. People are knowledgeable agents, not puppets of structure, but their knowledge is always bounded, and the unintended consequences of their actions can reinforce the very structures they seek to change.

William H. Sewell Jr., in "A Theory of Structure: Duality, Agency, and Transformation" (1992), refined Giddens's account. Sewell argued that structures are composed of schemas — cultural rules, available to actors in many forms — and resources, both human and material, that can be mobilized. His key addition was the multiplicity of structures: societies are made of many overlapping structures, and it is precisely this multiplicity that gives people agency, since they can transpose a schema from one structure to another and mobilize resources in new ways. Change, on this account, can arise from within structures: from the transposition of schemas, the unpredictable accumulation of resources, and the intersection of different structures.

## Examples

Structure is visible in the persistence of inequality. Two children born in the same city but into different classes face different schools, different networks, different expectations, and different life chances — not because of any difference in talent, but because the structure of class, education, and housing distributes resources unevenly. It is visible in gender: the structure of the labour market, the family, and the state together make it harder for women to reach the top of professions, even when no individual intends discrimination.

It is also visible in language and kinship. In many societies, the structure of kinship determines who may marry whom, who inherits, and who owes whom support; individuals negotiate within these rules, but the rules themselves outlast any single person. And it is visible in organizations: a bureaucracy's structure — its hierarchy, its division of labour, its rules — shapes the behaviour of everyone who works in it, however creative they may be.

## Structure and Agency in Digital Societies

Digital technologies have reopened the question of how structure and agency meet. Lawrence Lessig, in *Code and Other Laws of Cyberspace* (1999), argued that software architecture regulates conduct much as law, markets, and social norms do: what a system permits or blocks is built into its design. Wanda Orlikowski, extending Giddens, showed in studies of organizational computing that the structures associated with a technology are enacted in recurrent use, so that the same system can be taken up in different ways by different groups. José van Dijck, Thomas Poell, and Martijn de Waal, in *The Platform Society* (2018), traced how large platforms organize news, transport, health, and education through the collection of data, algorithmic selection, and commercial terms of service. Agency persists in these settings, as users learn how ranking systems seem to behave, adjust what they post, evade moderation, or organize collectively. It is unevenly distributed, however: platform owners can rewrite the rules unilaterally and observe behaviour at a scale users cannot. The theoretical dispute is whether software is simply another rule and resource in Giddens's sense, or a distinct kind of structure because it can execute rules automatically, without anyone having to interpret them.

## Structure in Non-Western Social Theory

Reflection on social structure did not begin in, and is not confined to, European and North American sociology. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddima* (completed in its first form in 1377), explained the rise and decline of dynasties through the relation between nomadic and settled ways of life and through *asabiyya*, group solidarity, which enables conquest and weakens under urban luxury. The Chinese sociologist Fei Xiaotong, in *From the Soil* (1947), contrasted a Western organizational mode of bounded groups with what he called the differential mode of association in rural China: concentric circles of relationships centred on each person, whose obligations weaken with social distance. In India, M. N. Srinivas analysed caste as a hierarchy that permitted some movement, describing through the concept of Sanskritization how lower-ranked groups sought higher standing by adopting the customs of higher ones. Syed Farid Alatas and others have argued that such ideas should be treated as sources of general theory, not merely as data to be explained by Western concepts. They also caution that no region has a single indigenous account of structure, and that each of these works needs critical assessment in its own historical setting.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics have argued that structure is too vague a term, used to explain everything and therefore nothing. Some have argued that it reifies society, treating an abstraction as if it were a real thing with causal powers of its own. Others have argued that structural accounts underplay human agency, portraying people as bearers of structures rather than makers of history. The structuration and practice traditions answered by insisting that structure exists only in and through action, and that it is always open to transformation.

The concept remains indispensable. Contemporary research applies it to digital platforms, asking how the structure of algorithms, data ownership, and platform governance shapes what users can see and say; to global supply chains, asking how the structure of production distributes risk and reward across continents; and to climate change, asking how the structure of energy systems and consumption makes individual choices insufficient. Structure remains sociology's way of naming the patterned constraints and possibilities within which people live their lives — and which their lives, in turn, remake.
