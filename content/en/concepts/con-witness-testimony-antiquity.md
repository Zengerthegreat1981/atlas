# Eyewitness Testimony in Ancient Historiography: Method and Problems

**Eyewitness testimony** was the foundation of all ancient history. Before organised written archives existed, history was confined to **direct questioning**: who saw the event? What did they say?

But oral testimony is a complicated problem. Memory fades, truth becomes mixed with fiction, and the witness may lie or be emotionally biased. How did the Greek and Roman historians distinguish between truth and fiction?

## Greek Theories of Testimony

### Herodotus: Acceptance with Reservation

He accepted oral testimony on one condition: that the **witness was present in the place and at the time**. When Herodotus says "I saw the pyramids myself", he raises the reliability of the account. But he also accepts testimony from priests and merchants who "saw".

The problem: he did not ask **how you remembered** or **whether you changed the story**. He accepted oral testimony as a basis without systematic critique of the mechanisms of memory.

### Thucydides: The Decisive Rejection of Myth

He rejected fabulous testimony entirely: if a witness said "I saw a goddess at war", he rejected it outright. But he accepted logical testimony from soldiers and commanders.

The problem: who judges? Thucydides, by his personal judgement, and this is an implicit bias.

### Polybius: Critique of the Narrator

He proposed a theory: assess the witness himself. Is he an **eyewitness in fact**, or a transmitter from a third party? Does he have an **interest in embellishment**? Was he a **witness of combat**, or an observer at a distance?

This is the first systematic attempt to assess the witness through internal criticism.

## Basic Problems

### 1. Memory and Distortion

An oral testimony given years after the event is exposed to forgetting and confusion. A recent study argues that memory distorts events under emotional pressure.

### 2. Cultural Bias

A Greek witness describing a war with the Persians may exaggerate the role of the Greeks. An Egyptian witness may tell a different story of the same event.

### 3. Fiction Disguised as Memory

A witness says "I remember that..." but in fact **imagines** or reformulates what he heard from others.

## How Ancient Historians Dealt with It

**The partial solution:** comparing multiple accounts. If one witness says one thing and another something different, the historian records both. Livy did this; Josephus did too.

**The remaining problem:** even multiple accounts may all be biased in one direction.

## Influence on Modern Practice

Contemporary historians return to this debate:
- **Modern oral history** applies systematic critique to oral testimony.
- **Social memory studies** examine how collective memory distorts an event.
- **Microhistory** uses rare testimonies and analyses them in depth.
