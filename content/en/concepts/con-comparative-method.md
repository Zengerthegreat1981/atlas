# Comparative Method

## Definition

The comparative method is the systematic comparison of cases, societies, institutions, or historical episodes in order to identify similarities and differences, test explanations, and build general theory. It is one of sociology's oldest and most fundamental methods, because comparison is the closest the discipline comes to the experiment: since sociologists cannot manipulate whole societies in a laboratory, they exploit the natural variation between them, asking why one country developed a welfare state and another did not, or why a revolution succeeded in one setting and failed in another. The comparative method is therefore not a single technique but a logic of inquiry that can be applied to quantitative data, to historical archives, and to ethnographic fieldwork. Its power lies in forcing the researcher to specify what is being compared, on what dimensions, and why the comparison should be informative.

## Origins and Development

Comparison has been central to sociology since its founding. Émile Durkheim's *Rules of Sociological Method* argued that sociology's experimental method is the comparative method, and he used comparison to show that suicide rates vary systematically with levels of social integration and regulation, a pattern no single case could reveal. John Stuart Mill's *System of Logic* supplied the formal logic: the method of agreement identifies a common cause among cases that share an outcome, the method of difference isolates the cause by comparing cases that differ in only one respect, and the method of concomitant variation traces how changes in one variable accompany changes in another. In the mid-twentieth century, comparative politics developed these ideas into a distinct methodology. Arend Lijphart's 1971 article on the comparative method argued that comparison is especially valuable when the number of cases is too small for statistics but too large for a single case study, and he identified strategies for increasing the number of cases or reducing the number of variables. Theda Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* demonstrated the method's power, comparing the French, Russian, and Chinese revolutions to show that revolutions result from the conjunction of state breakdown and peasant revolt, not from the intentions of revolutionaries alone. Charles Ragin's *The Comparative Method* introduced qualitative comparative analysis, a formal technique using Boolean algebra to identify the combinations of conditions that produce an outcome.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns the small-N problem: with few cases and many possible explanations, how can comparison distinguish the true cause from coincidental associations? Lijphart's strategies include selecting comparable cases that differ in only a few respects, focusing on a single region, or combining comparison with within-case analysis. A second debate opposes variable-oriented and case-oriented approaches. Variable-oriented research seeks general relationships between variables across many cases, while case-oriented research seeks to understand each case as a configuration of conditions, accepting that the same outcome can result from different combinations of causes, a pattern Ragin described as multiple conjunctural causation (often also termed equifinality). A third debate concerns the unit of comparison: should sociologists compare nations, regions, cities, organisations, or historical periods, and are nations the natural unit, or an artefact of methodological nationalism that obscures connections between cases? A fourth issue is selection bias: comparing only cases where an outcome occurred, such as only successful revolutions, makes it impossible to learn what distinguishes them from failures. A fifth concerns the tension between comparison and context: comparison requires cases to be commensurable, yet the categories used to compare them, such as class or the state, may mean different things in different settings, and forcing them into common categories can distort what is being studied.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The comparative method remains central to sociology, political science, and anthropology, and it has been transformed by new data and new tools. Large cross-national surveys, such as the World Values Survey and the European Social Survey, make it possible to compare attitudes and behaviours across dozens of countries, while qualitative comparative analysis has been extended to fuzzy sets and applied to topics from welfare states to democratic breakdown. Historical comparison continues to shape debates about the origins of capitalism, the causes of inequality, and the conditions of democracy. At the same time, the method faces criticism. Postcolonial scholars argue that comparison has often placed Europe as the implicit standard against which other societies are measured, treating difference as deviation. Others argue that in a globalised world, cases are not independent of one another, since trade, empire, and migration connect them, so that comparing nations as separate units can mistake the effects of those connections for national characteristics. The response has been a more reflexive comparativism: one that chooses cases deliberately, specifies scope conditions, combines cross-case comparison with within-case process tracing, and remains alert to the categories through which comparison is made. Understood in this way, comparison remains the discipline's core device for turning description into explanation.
