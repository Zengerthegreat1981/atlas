# Therapy (Existential Therapy)

Existential therapy is not a technique for symptom relief but a philosophical and relational approach to human suffering grounded in the conviction that many psychological problems arise from avoiding or denying fundamental existential realities: freedom, mortality, isolation, and meaninglessness.

## The Existential Frame

Unlike psychoanalysis (which seeks unconscious conflicts) or cognitive-behavioral therapy (which targets distorted thinking), existential therapy takes as its starting point the confrontation with existential givens. A person may be depressed not because of a chemical imbalance or automatic negative thoughts, but because they are avoiding the reality of their freedom and the responsibility it entails. Another may be anxious not because of a past trauma, but because they are beginning to sense the groundlessness of their existence and the finitude of their time.

This does not mean existential therapists deny biology or cognition; rather, they contextualize these within the existential frame. A person with depression may have a neurobiological component and also be confronting, consciously or unconsciously, the fact that their life has been lived inauthentically. The therapy addresses both.

## The Therapeutic Relationship

The existential therapist's presence is not that of an expert who diagnoses and prescribes, but of a witness and fellow traveler. The therapist takes the client's experience seriously not as pathology to be fixed but as a window into how that person is living and choosing. The therapeutic dialogue is collaborative and genuine; the therapist is not hidden behind professional neutrality but is present as a real person in relation to another real person.

This relational presence is healing not because it provides insight (though it may) but because it offers what many people have rarely experienced: to be fully seen and accepted by another human being without condition or performance. This encounter in the between—what Buber called the I-Thou meeting—is itself therapeutic.

## Goals of Existential Therapy

The goals of existential therapy are not symptom elimination or happiness. They are:

1. **Increased awareness** of the existential realities one has been avoiding (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness).
2. **Authenticity** in living: alignment between one's choices and one's values.
3. **Responsibility**: the acceptance that one's life is authored by oneself, not determined by circumstances or others.
4. **Meaning-making**: the capacity to create coherence and purpose even within constraints and limitations.

## Common Therapeutic Moves

Existential therapists frequently:
- **Highlight choices**: Gently pointing out that what the client describes as inevitable or imposed is actually a choice (even the choice to remain as is).
- **Explore death anxiety**: Rather than reassuring the client that they will live a long life, exploring what the awareness of mortality is trying to teach them.
- **Examine avoidance**: How is the client avoiding freedom, responsibility, authenticity, or confrontation with mortality? What function does this avoidance serve?
- **Validate existential guilt**: The guilt of not living one's own life, of conforming rather than choosing, is not a neurosis but an appropriate response to inauthenticity.

## Existential Therapy and Other Approaches

Existential therapy is not opposed to other therapies but can complement them. A person may need cognitive-behavioral intervention for social anxiety while also exploring what underlying existential anxiety (about visibility, judgment, freedom in the social sphere) that symptom is expressing. Someone may benefit from psychodynamic insight into family patterns while also grappling with the existential weight of having internalized those patterns as their own.
