# Economic Calculation Problem

The **economic calculation problem** is a thesis formulated by **Ludwig von Mises** in his pivotal article "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth" (1920). It holds that an economy that abolishes private ownership of the means of production necessarily lacks real prices for those means, which makes it impossible to calculate rationally which combination of means of production is more efficient than another. Comprehensive central planning thus becomes, in Mises's argument, incapable of rational economic calculation. Earlier doubts about socialist pricing had been raised by Max Weber and by N. G. Pierson, and Enrico Barone had argued in 1908 that a planned economy would have to solve the same equations as a market.

## The root of the problem: absence of real markets for factors of production

The core of the argument rests on the observation that prices in a competitive market arise from a real exchange between owners of alternatives who negotiate the terms of exchange on the basis of their own subjective assessment of the relative scarcity of what they own. When private ownership of the means of production (land, factories, machinery) is abolished, and the state becomes their sole owner, this competitive negotiating process disappears. No real price remains for these means that reflects their actual relative scarcity compared with possible alternative uses.

## The impossibility of rational comparison between alternatives

Without these prices, Mises argues, the central planner lacks any objective criterion for comparing alternative methods of production that use different combinations of the same scarce resources. How is he to decide to build a railway on one route rather than another, or to use steel rather than aluminium in a product, without knowing the real relative value of each resource compared with its possible alternative uses? His decision remains arbitrary or based on partial technical estimates that do not reflect the full economic cost of each option.

## The socialist calculation debate

This thesis opened the **socialist calculation debate** that extended through the 1920s and 1930s, when socialist economists, most notably Oskar Lange (building on Fred M. Taylor) and Abba Lerner, responded that a central planner could simulate the market arithmetically through hypothetical accounting prices (shadow prices), adjusted empirically by trial and error in response to actual surpluses or shortages of quantities. Hayek, who had edited the English translation of Mises's article in *Collectivist Economic Planning* (1935), later extended his critique of this response by linking it to the broader problem of dispersed knowledge, rather than arithmetic alone.

## Distinction from Hayek's knowledge problem

This concept is related to the knowledge problem later developed by Hayek, but it precedes it chronologically and differs from it in focus. Mises concentrated on the absence of real prices for means of production arising specifically from the absence of private ownership, while Hayek broadened the analysis to the nature of dispersed knowledge itself, partly independently of the system of ownership. This broadening made Hayek's argument of wider scope than Mises's original formulation, which was narrower in its focus on ownership.

## Later empirical assessment

This argument was invoked again in analysing the difficulties of centrally planned economies in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe, particularly in pricing intermediate capital goods and addressing the chronic shortage of consumer goods. Some economists saw these difficulties as a belated empirical confirmation of Mises's theoretical warnings from the 1920s themselves. Economic historians, however, are cautious about reducing the causes of these economies' failures to a single theoretical factor without considering other political and administrative factors (such as incentives and corruption) operating alongside it, and the extent to which the historical record confirms the argument remains debated.
