# Medical Advancement

Arab medicine was built on the translation of Galen, Hippocrates, and Dioscorides. **Hunayn ibn Ishaq** (d. 260 AH / 873 CE) was the principal translator of Galen into Arabic and Syriac. In his letter to 'Ali ibn Yahya ibn al-Munajjim he lists the Galenic books he knew and those he had translated. That labour did much to fix the Arabic medical vocabulary. Medicine did not remain mere transmission. Between the third and seventh centuries AH it produced its own encyclopaedias, clinical observations, and hospitals.

## Al-Razi: Experience and Criticism

**Abu Bakr al-Razi** (d. 313 AH/925 CE) wrote *al-Hawi* (The Comprehensive Book), a clinical encyclopaedia that gathers the texts of the ancients together with his own observations. In his treatise *al-Judari wa-l-hasba* (On Smallpox and Measles) he distinguished between the two diseases through precise clinical description. He wrote *al-Shukuk 'ala Jalinus* (Doubts about Galen), in which he criticized the great authority of ancient medicine wherever his own experience contradicted Galen's statement, which was an example of the physician's independence. He was head of a bimaristan in Rayy and then in Baghdad.

## The Canon and Surgery

'Ali ibn al-'Abbas al-Majusi wrote *Kamil al-sina'a* (The Complete Art of Medicine, also called *al-Maliki*), around 980 CE. Ibn Sina wrote *al-Qanun fi-l-tibb* (The Canon of Medicine) around 1025 CE, a logical organization of the whole of medicine into five books. It remained a reference in Europe until the seventeenth century, after Gerard of Cremona translated it. In al-Andalus Abu al-Qasim al-Zahrawi (d. c. 404 AH/1013 CE) wrote *al-Tasrif li-man 'ajiza 'an al-ta'lif* (The Method for One Unable to Compose), which contained a treatise on surgery with illustrations of its instruments. Ibn Zuhr wrote *al-Taysir* (The Facilitation), and Ibn Rushd wrote *al-Kulliyat* (Generalities).

## Ibn al-Nafis and the Lesser Circulation

'Ala' al-Din Ibn al-Nafis (d. 687 AH/1288 CE), in *Sharh tashrih al-Qanun* (Commentary on the Anatomy of the Canon), stated that blood passes from the right ventricle to the lungs and then to the left, and does not pass through the septum between them, as Galen and Ibn Sina had said. His text was discovered in 1924 by Muhyi al-Din al-Tatawi, and Max Meyerhof discussed it in his article in the journal *Isis* in 1935.

## The Bimaristan

Hospitals were established in Baghdad from the time of Harun al-Rashid. The Buyid 'Adud al-Dawla founded the 'Adudi bimaristan in Baghdad in 372 AH/982 CE; Nur al-Din Zangi founded the Nuri bimaristan in Damascus around 549 AH/1154 CE; and Sultan al-Mansur Qalawun completed his bimaristan in Cairo in 683 AH/1284 CE. These institutions included wards, clinical teaching and pharmacy. In 931 CE the Caliph al-Muqtadir ordered that physicians be examined before being licensed. The broadest source on the biographies of physicians is Ibn Abi Usaybi'a's *'Uyun al-anba' fi tabaqat al-atibba'* (Sources of Information on the Classes of Physicians), who died in 668 AH/1270 CE.

## Limits of Achievement

The theoretical framework remained the four humours of Galen. Anatomy rarely departed from reliance on texts, owing to the scarcity of human dissection. Ibn al-Nafis's distinction does not mean that he changed the school: his text remained little known until it was discovered in the twentieth century, and whether Europeans knew of it before the modern rediscovery is debated. Surgical intervention also remained limited, as many physicians considered it of lower rank than internal medicine.
