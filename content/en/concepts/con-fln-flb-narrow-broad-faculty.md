# The Faculty of Language: Narrow and Broad Senses

A distinction formulated by Hauser, Chomsky, and Fitch (2002) to break down the question of "the origin of language" into components amenable to comparative evolutionary research. The "Faculty of Language in the Broad Sense" (FLB) includes all the cognitive and sensorimotor systems involved in language (auditory perception, memory, intentionality), most of which are evolutionarily shared with other species. The "Faculty of Language in the Narrow Sense" (FLN), according to their hypothesis, is the abstract computational component unique to humans, which they hypothesize may comprise only recursion together with its mapping to the sensorimotor and conceptual-intentional interfaces. The proposal, and particularly the claim that recursion alone is specific to FLN, remains the subject of ongoing scientific debate.

## The Motivation for the Distinction

The aim of this distinction is to avoid a broad question ("how did language evolve?") in favor of more precise empirical questions: which components of language are evolutionarily shared with other species (and thus subject to the methods of comparative biology), and which of them, if any, are evolutionarily unique to humans specifically?

## The Main Critique

Steven Pinker and Ray Jackendoff (2005) objected to the thesis that FLN is limited to recursion alone, arguing that many other elements of language (such as speech perception, phonology, word learning, and morphology) also appear to be specialized for language and cannot be reduced to recursion alone, and that the evidence for treating the faculty as largely shared with other species is weaker than HCF suggested. Fitch, Hauser, and Chomsky (2005) replied to these objections.

## Examples of Components of Each Faculty

Typical examples of FLB components include auditory perception of sound frequencies (shared with many species), the working memory needed to process long sentences, and the ability to read the intentions of a speaker (theory of mind). Recursion (the capacity to embed a structure within a structure of the same type without theoretical limit, such as embedding a clause within a clause) is the main candidate that Hauser, Chomsky, and Fitch proposed as the only component unique to humans within FLN, based on the absence of conclusive evidence for a similar recursive capacity in other species, a point that later animal-cognition studies have contested.

## Connection to the Debate on Linguistic Innateness

The distinction bears on how much of language is claimed to be innate and specific to humans. The 2002 paper offers recursion, mapped to the two interfaces, as a candidate for that narrow residue. It does not establish that nothing else is language-specific. Usage-based accounts argue that grammatical structure, including recursive combination, can be learned from use together with general cognitive capacities, so that a dedicated FLN operation is not required. That disagreement is unresolved.
