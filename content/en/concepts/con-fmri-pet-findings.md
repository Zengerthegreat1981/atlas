# fMRI & PET Scan Findings

## Definition

Functional neuroimaging, whose most important techniques are functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and positron emission tomography (PET), makes it possible to observe brain activity and its receptors in mental illness without surgery. fMRI measures changes in blood oxygenation (the BOLD signal) as an indirect indicator of neural activity. PET uses radioactive isotopes that trace glucose, transmitters, and receptors, such as dopamine D2 receptors.

## Technical Origins

Seiji Ogawa and colleagues described blood-oxygenation-level-dependent (BOLD) contrast in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* in 1990, which led to human fMRI in the early 1990s, a technique that involves no ionizing radiation and offers better spatial resolution than PET. PET was developed in the 1970s by Michael Phelps, Edward Hoffman, and others. A study by Wong and colleagues in *Science* in 1986 reported elevated D2 receptor binding by PET in patients with schizophrenia who had not received medication, a finding that was not consistently replicated. PET studies from the late 1980s (Farde and colleagues) went on to measure how much of the D2 receptor a given antipsychotic occupies (see the entry on Antipsychotic Mechanisms).

## Key Findings

In depression, Helen Mayberg described in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 1999 the pattern of reciprocal activity between limbic and prefrontal cortical regions. She then reported in *Neuron* in 2005, in an open-label series of six patients, that deep brain stimulation of the subgenual cingulate region was followed by improvement in treatment-resistant depression; later controlled trials gave more mixed results (see the entry on Treatment-Resistant Disorders). In anxiety, Etkin and Wager reported in a 2007 meta-analysis in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* greater amygdala and insula activation in post-traumatic stress disorder, social anxiety disorder, and specific phobia than in healthy controls. Hariri and colleagues reported in *Science* in 2002 that variation in the serotonin transporter gene was associated with the amygdala's response to threatening faces, an example of "imaging genetics" (later replications have been mixed). In schizophrenia, studies have reported altered activation of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and elevated presynaptic dopamine synthesis capacity in the striatum (see the entry on Dopamine and Psychosis). In *JAMA Psychiatry* in 2015, Goodkind and colleagues reported in a meta-analysis of structural studies that gray matter loss in the dorsal anterior cingulate and anterior insula is shared across several diagnoses, supporting a transdiagnostic view.

## Criticism and Methodological Problems

The field has faced strong criticism. In *Perspectives on Psychological Science* in 2009, Vul and colleagues published an analysis of "puzzlingly high correlations" between brain activity and measures of personality and emotion, pointing to misleading statistical selection. In *PNAS* in 2016, Eklund and colleagues showed that some widely used cluster-based analysis methods produced inflated false-positive rates, which cast doubt on some earlier findings. In *Nature* in 2022, Marek and colleagues showed that correlations between brain structure or function and psychological traits are very small and require thousands of participants to be reliable, while many studies have relied on samples of a few dozen.

## Clinical Use and Its Limits

To date, there are no imaging tests approved for diagnosing a psychiatric disorder in routine practice. Imaging's role is limited to ruling out organic causes. Activity varies between individuals, and medications, smoking, sleep, and movement all affect the signal. A mental illness cannot be reduced to colored images. A statistical result at the level of groups does not automatically become a criterion at the level of the individual. The tendency of brain images to lend exaggerated authority to claims in media and in courtrooms is also criticized.
