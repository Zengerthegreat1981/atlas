# Protest: Collective Dissent and the Claim to Be Heard

**Protest** is the public expression of dissent by people who challenge a policy, a ruler, an institution, or an entire social order, usually through collective action that disrupts normal life: marches, strikes, boycotts, occupations, petitions, and riots. Protest is distinct from private complaint because it is performed in public, addressed to an audience, and intended to force a response. Anthropologists study protest as a social form with its own rituals, symbols, and organization, and as a window onto the grievances, hopes, and moral claims of people who feel excluded from the decisions that govern their lives.

## Definition and Development

A major shift in the modern study of protest came when scholars stopped treating crowds as irrational and began treating protesters as rational actors making claims. Charles Tilly, in *From Mobilization to Revolution* (1978), gave the field its classic framework. Protest, Tilly argued, is a form of collective action in which groups mobilize resources — people, money, networks, symbols — to advance shared interests against opponents. He distinguished between the repertoire of contention available in a given society — the limited set of protest forms people know how to perform — and the political opportunities that shape when protest is likely to succeed. A march is not a natural human reflex but a learned performance, passed down through generations of struggle.

Tilly's work showed that protest is historically patterned. In eighteenth-century Europe, the dominant forms were food riots, tax rebellions, and attacks on local officials. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, the repertoire shifted toward strikes, demonstrations, and electoral campaigns — forms suited to national states and capitalist economies. The form of protest, in other words, tells us as much about a society as the content of its demands.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Doug McAdam, in *Political Process and the Development of Black Insurgency, 1930–1970* (1982), explained how protest emerges from the interaction of political opportunities, organizational strength, and shared consciousness. Studying the American civil rights movement, McAdam showed that the movement grew not when grievances were greatest but when the political system opened — through court decisions, divisions among elites, and the emergence of black churches, colleges, and NAACP chapters as mobilizing structures. Grievance alone, he argued, is never enough: protest requires organization and a sense that change is possible.

Sidney Tarrow, in *Power in Movement* (1994), developed the concept of the cycle of protest. Protest movements rise, spread to new groups, and then decline, either because they win concessions, because they are repressed, or because their energy is absorbed into institutions. Tarrow emphasized the modular quality of protest forms: a tactic invented in one struggle — the sit-in, the boycott, the hunger strike — can be borrowed by entirely different movements in other places and times.

James Jasper, in *The Art of Moral Protest* (1997), restored emotion and morality to the study of protest. Protesters, Jasper argued, are not only calculating interests; they are moved by moral shocks — events that violate their sense of what is right — and by the pleasures of solidarity, the thrill of collective action, and the dignity of standing up. His account helps explain why people protest even when they expect to lose, and why protest cultures persist across generations.

## Ethnographic Examples

Anthropological fieldwork has shown how protest is woven into everyday life. In Latin America, anthropologists have documented how indigenous communities combine roadblocks and marches with rituals and communal assemblies, so that protest is also an affirmation of identity. In the Middle East, researchers have studied how Friday prayers, funerals, and market gatherings become occasions for protest under authoritarian rule, when open demonstration is impossible. In South Asia, they have shown how protests against dams, mines, and land seizures are framed through idioms of kinship, sacred landscape, and moral economy — the shared sense that rulers have violated their obligations to the people.

Protest also takes quiet forms. James Scott's work on peasant societies showed that where open protest is dangerous, people resist through foot-dragging, sabotage, gossip, and theft — what he called the weapons of the weak. Anthropologists have learned to recognize protest not only in the street but in the refusal to cooperate, the song that mocks the powerful, and the rumour that undermines a ruler's legitimacy.

## Contemporary Debates

Current scholarship debates the effects of digital media on protest. Social media can mobilize large crowds quickly, as in the Arab uprisings of 2011, but scholars ask whether such mobilization produces durable organizations or merely moments of spectacle. Others debate whether protest is becoming more transnational, as movements against climate change, austerity, and inequality share slogans, tactics, and symbols across borders. A further debate concerns the relationship between protest and violence: when does disruption become violence, who defines the boundary, and how do states use the label of violence to delegitimize dissent? Anthropology's contribution is to study protest from the inside — through the eyes of the protesters themselves — showing how it is experienced as moral action, as risk, as solidarity, and as hope.
