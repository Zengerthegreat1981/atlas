# Axis Mundi and Sacred Space in Eliade's Phenomenology

A central concept in Mircea Eliade's method for the comparative study of religion. He developed it in *Das Heilige und das Profane* (1957; English *The Sacred and the Profane*, 1959) and in earlier works such as *Patterns in Comparative Religion* (1949): when the sacred (hierophany) "manifests" in a place, it transforms that place into an "axis mundi" (center of the world), a symbolic point of connection between the three cosmic levels (heaven, earth and the underworld), which becomes a fixed reference point amid the surrounding "homogeneous space."

## Comparative Examples in Eliade

Eliade cites recurring forms of this pattern across distant cultures: the sacred mountain (Sinai, Olympus, the Hindu Meru), the cosmic pillar or tree (the Scandinavian Yggdrasil, the tree of life in several traditions), and the temple or the Kaaba as the "navel of the world" (*umbilicus mundi*). In all these cases the sacred place is a "center" around which the lived space of the religious community is organized, in contrast to the "ordinary" homogeneous space that has no fixed center.

## Checking an Example Against Its Source: The Achilpa Pole

One of Eliade's best-known illustrations in *The Sacred and the Profane* concerns the Achilpa, an Arrernte (Aranda) group of central Australia. In his telling, a sacred pole carried on their wanderings served as a cosmic axis linking them with the sky, and when it broke the people lost their orientation and lay down to die. His account depends on the reports of the ethnographers Baldwin Spencer and F. J. Gillen. In *To Take Place*, Jonathan Z. Smith went back to those reports and argued that they do not bear this weight: the ancestral narratives are concerned with journeys and the marks those journeys left on the landscape, and the idea of a cosmic center joining earth to heaven is largely supplied by the interpreter. Sam Gill, in *Storytracking* (1998), traced the chain of texts further and concluded that Eliade's version departs substantially from what his sources recorded, joining and reshaping separate elements. These are checks of published records rather than new fieldwork, and a failure in one case does not by itself refute the others. It does show that each of Eliade's comparative examples needs to be tested against its own sources.

## Its Relation to Pilgrimage

This concept links directly to Victor Turner's theory of pilgrimage. The movement of the pilgrim toward the sacred site can be read as a movement toward that "axis mundi": an exit from the everyday homogeneous space toward a point of connection with the transcendent, in parallel with the pilgrim's social exit from his usual structure toward the "communitas" that Turner described. The two frameworks, the temporal-social in Turner and the spatial in Eliade, complement each other in describing the comparative religious experience of pilgrimage, though each scholar developed them independently from his own methodological angle.

## The Arab-Islamic Heritage

The Kaaba in Mecca frequently appears in comparative literature as an example of Eliade's "axis mundi." In the Islamic religious conception, it is "the first house established for people" (Surah Al-Imran, 96). It is also a point of orientation toward which Muslims from all parts of the earth gather five times a day in prayer, and it becomes a symbolic center where the lines of the *qibla* coming from all directions "converge." This functionally resembles the idea of the "navel of the world" (*umbilicus mundi*) that Eliade uses to describe central sacred places in other traditions.  The comparison is a structural one, drawn by Eliade's readers, and does not imply historical borrowing between traditions.

## Jonathan Z. Smith's Critique

A prominent later methodological objection to Eliade's concept is Jonathan Z. Smith's critique. In *To Take Place* (1987), Smith argues that holiness is not a quality inherent in the place itself that waits to "manifest" in it (as the notion of hierophany suggests), but a relationship that the ritual act creates between people and place. A place becomes sacred because the community treats it ritually as sacred, not because a transcendent power chose it in advance. This reverses the order of causality in Eliade: the ritual makes the center, not the center calls forth the ritual.
