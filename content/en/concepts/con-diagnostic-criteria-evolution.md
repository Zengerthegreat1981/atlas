# Evolution of Diagnostic Criteria

This concept traces the major systematic shifts in the **classification of mental disorders** across the editions of the **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders** (DSM) published by the American Psychiatric Association. It is the most influential historical path in the emergence of modern psychiatric diagnostic systems worldwide, developing in parallel with the **International Classification of Diseases** (ICD) published by the World Health Organization.

## From Psychoanalysis to Descriptive Criteria

**DSM-I** (1952) and **DSM-II** (1968) adopted loose formulations strongly influenced by **psychoanalytic theory**. They described disorders in terms such as "reaction," which reflected implicit psychodynamic causal assumptions more than an objective description of the observable symptoms. As a result, **inter-rater reliability** (the consistency of diagnosis between clinicians) was very weak: two different physicians could give the same patient entirely different diagnoses.

## The DSM-III Revolution: Operational Criteria

**DSM-III** (1980), led by the psychiatrist **Robert Spitzer**, marked a radical shift, often described as the "Neo-Kraepelinian revolution" after Emil Kraepelin, the pioneer of descriptive, empirical classification in German psychiatry at the end of the nineteenth century. It drew on the Feighner criteria (1972) and the Research Diagnostic Criteria developed by Spitzer, Endicott, and Robins (1978). It replaced theoretical causal description with **explicit operational criteria**: specific lists of symptoms, clear numerical thresholds (such as requiring five of nine criteria), and minimum time durations for diagnosis. These were designed primarily to increase **reliability** among different clinicians, even at the cost of full resolution of **validity**, meaning the extent to which a diagnostic category corresponds to a real, distinct biological entity.

## Gradual Expansion

Subsequent editions increased the number of disorders: DSM-III-R (1987), DSM-IV (1994), and DSM-IV-TR (2000) added new diagnoses one after another; by commonly cited counts the number of diagnostic categories rose from roughly 100 in DSM-I to nearly 300 in DSM-IV. This expansion sparked ongoing debate about the risk of **diagnostic inflation**, the excessive widening of the definition of mental illness, and the transformation of the natural diversity of human experience into disease categories. **DSM-5** (2013) and **DSM-5-TR** (2022, a text revision rather than a fully new edition) introduced further changes, including partial attempts to add dimensions for measuring symptom severity alongside purely categorical classes.

## The ICD Parallel

The World Health Organization's *International Classification of Diseases* first included a chapter on mental disorders in its sixth revision (1948). ICD-10 appeared in 1992, and ICD-11 was approved in 2019 and came into effect in 2022.

## The Developmental Alternative: RDoC

As a critical response to the near-exclusive reliance of DSM on observable symptoms without necessary links to underlying biological mechanisms, the **National Institute of Mental Health** (NIMH) began the **Research Domain Criteria** (RDoC) project in 2009 and presented it in a 2010 article. It is not a clinical replacement for DSM but a research framework that proposes studying mental disorders through **functional dimensions that cut across diagnoses** (such as emotion regulation or working memory), measured at multiple levels (genes, cells, neural circuits, and behavior), rather than keeping to the boundaries of traditional diagnostic categories as the primary unit of analysis.
