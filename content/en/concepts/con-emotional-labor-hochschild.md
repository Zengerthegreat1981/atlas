# Emotional Labor, Surface and Deep Acting (Hochschild)

A concept formulated by Arlie Russell Hochschild to describe the systematic effort that a worker in service jobs expends to manage her visible and inner emotions according to imposed institutional or cultural standards.

## Content

In *The Managed Heart* (1983), Hochschild defined "emotional labor" as the management of feeling to produce a publicly observable facial and bodily display that is socially or institutionally desired, in exchange for a wage (in contrast to "emotion work" in the private, unpaid context, such as managing feelings within the family). She drew mainly on a study of flight attendants at a major airline, with a comparison group of bill collectors, whose work required the opposite display of toughness. The flight attendants were formally trained to smile constantly and to display warmth regardless of their actual mood, and were evaluated on how well they complied with these "feeling rules." She distinguished between two modes of performing this labor: "surface acting," in which the worker manufactures the required outward expression (a smile, a friendly tone) without her actual inner feeling changing, so that the gap between what is felt and what is displayed remains conscious and perceptible to the worker herself; and "deep acting," in which the worker actually tries to change her inner feeling so that it matches what the job requires (for example, calling up happy memories in order to smile sincerely at a customer), so that the performance becomes more outwardly "authentic" but requires deeper inner psychological effort.

## Impact

Hochschild warned that long, repeated practice of emotional labor may lead to "emotive dissonance" and to estrangement from one's own feelings, a risk she linked especially to jobs where the display is tightly controlled by the employer. Later empirical research on emotional labor and burnout has found mixed results, with surface acting more consistently associated with strain than deep acting. This framework became a basic analytical tool in studies of work and service, and Hochschild observed that women are more likely than men to hold jobs that demand emotional labor (for example flight attending, nursing and other care and service work), so that the burden is unevenly distributed by gender and is rarely recognized or paid for as skilled work. The concept intersects in substance with Erving Goffman's analysis of impression management, though Hochschild focused more on the worker's inner psychological cost and not only on the outward performance before an audience.

## Extensions

Eva Illouz, in *Cold Intimacies* (2007), extended the line of argument from workplaces to the wider interpenetration of emotional and economic logics in contemporary capitalism, including intimate relationships.
