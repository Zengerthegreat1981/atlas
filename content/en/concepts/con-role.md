# Role: Expected Behaviour and Social Position

A **role** is the set of expected behaviours, rights, and obligations attached to a social position, or status, that a person occupies. Whereas a status names a position in a social structure — teacher, mother, citizen, patient — a role is what a person is expected to do in that position. Role is therefore the dynamic, behavioural side of status: it connects the abstract structure of society to the concrete conduct of everyday life, and it is one of the central concepts through which sociology explains how social order is reproduced without constant coercion.

## Definition and Development

The modern sociological use of the concept was established by the anthropologist Ralph Linton, who in *The Study of Man* (1936) distinguished status from role: a status is a position in a social system, while a role is the dynamic aspect of that status, the behaviour expected of its occupant. Linton's distinction gave sociology a precise vocabulary for analysing how individuals move between positions and how societies assign duties and privileges.

George Herbert Mead had already prepared the ground in *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934), published posthumously from his lectures, by showing that the self emerges through taking the role of the other. Children learn to see themselves from the standpoint of others — first particular others, then the "generalized other" of the whole community — and this role-taking is what makes coordinated social action possible. Mead's account made role a process of the self rather than a mere script imposed from outside.

Talcott Parsons carried the concept into structural-functional theory, treating roles as the points where personality, culture, and social structure meet. In Parsons's framework, roles are institutionalised expectations: a doctor is expected to be technically competent and emotionally neutral, a judge impartial, a parent caring. When people enact their roles adequately, institutions function smoothly.

## Key Contributions: Role Sets, Role Conflict, and Performance

Robert K. Merton refined the concept (in "The Role-Set," 1957, and in the revised edition of *Social Theory and Social Structure*, 1957) by showing that a single status carries not one role but a **role set** — an array of role relationships with different partners. A teacher relates differently to pupils, parents, colleagues, and headteachers, and each relationship carries its own expectations. Role theorists also analysed **role conflict**, which arises when the expectations of two statuses clash (for example, a manager who must discipline a friend), and **role strain**, the difficulty of meeting the demands of a role or set of roles, a concept developed by William J. Goode (1960). These ideas made role theory sensitive to the contradictions of social life rather than picturing society as a smoothly oiled machine.

Erving Goffman, in *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959), gave the concept its most influential elaboration. Goffman treated social life as theatre: people perform roles before audiences, managing impressions through dress, speech, gesture, and the control of front-stage and back-stage regions. A waiter behaves differently in the dining room from in the kitchen; a doctor performs confidence at the bedside while expressing doubt in the staff room. For Goffman, roles are not simply followed but actively performed, negotiated, and sometimes subverted — role distance, the way people signal that they are not fully identified with a role, is itself a social skill.

## Examples

Role theory illuminates many ordinary situations. A new mother in many societies faces the expectations of the maternal role — constant availability, selfless care — while also occupying the role of employee, with its demand for punctuality and productivity; the resulting role conflict is a structural feature of her situation, not a personal failing. In medicine, the "sick role" described by Parsons grants the ill person exemption from normal duties in exchange for seeking competent help and wanting to recover — an exchange that explains why illness is treated as a social, not merely biological, condition. In organisations, formal job descriptions rarely exhaust the roles people actually play: the colleague who mediates disputes or the manager who acts as a confidant performs informal roles that keep the institution working.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics have argued that classical role theory overstates consensus and understates power. Feminist sociologists showed that roles such as "housewife" or "secretary" naturalised gendered inequality, presenting as neutral expectations what were in fact relations of domination. Marxist and conflict theorists objected that role theory describes how order is maintained while saying too little about who benefits from that order and how roles are imposed. Later interactionist work answered part of this critique by emphasising that roles are made and remade in interaction rather than simply received.

The concept remains indispensable. Contemporary research applies it to remote work, where the roles of employee and parent collide in the same room; to online life, where people manage multiple performed selves across platforms; and to migration, where people must learn entirely new role repertoires. Role remains the bridge concept between structure and action, and between the person and society.
