# Inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained, general rise in the price level over time, meaning a decline in the purchasing power of a unit of currency. It is distinguished from the rise in the price of a single good, since inflation measures the general movement of a broad basket of prices rather than the movement of one relative price.

## Monetary and structural explanations

**Milton Friedman** led the monetary explanation. In *The Counter-Revolution in Monetary Theory* (1970), restating a formulation he had used in lectures in the 1960s, he wrote that inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon, in the sense that it is produced by a faster rise in the quantity of money than in output. On this view, excess money growth raises the general price level. Other explanations stand alongside this: demand-pull inflation, where aggregate demand exceeds maximum productive capacity; cost-push inflation, where rising input prices (wages or energy) are passed on to the consumer; and structural inflation linked to supply bottlenecks or market monopolies.

## Measurement and the expectations loop

Inflation is usually measured by a consumer price index, and it interacts with individuals' own expectations. If workers and producers expect prices to keep rising, they demand higher wages and raise their prices in advance, so the expectations are self-fulfilled and a wage-price loop emerges that is difficult to break without a firm monetary policy or an external shock. The hyperinflation of the **Weimar Republic in Germany (1923)** is the classic example of hyperinflation, in which a large fiscal deficit financed directly by printing money fed expectations of further price rises (the dynamics analysed by Phillip Cagan in 1956).

## Contemporary Arab examples

Several Arab economies have experienced sharp inflationary episodes linked to the collapse of the exchange rate and the financing of budget deficits through monetary issuance. Lebanon after 2019, where the Lebanese pound lost most of its value, and Egypt after the devaluations and floats of the Egyptian pound (notably in 2016 and 2022–2024) are examples in which inflation was compounded by importing food and energy with a depreciating currency. The causes of such episodes are complex, combining monetary, fiscal and exchange-rate factors, and should not be reduced to a single monetary explanation.

## Instruments of control and their limits

Central banks usually rely on raising the interest rate to curb demand and slow credit growth, or on explicitly targeting inflation as a declared goal of monetary policy. These instruments face a dilemma, however, when inflation is imported through the exchange rate or results from an external supply shock (such as the oil price shock of 1973): the policymaker then faces a painful trade-off between curbing inflation and deepening recession. This is the heart of the debate between rules and discretion in monetary policy.

## The exchange-rate channel and imported inflation

In small open economies that depend on imports, a fall in the exchange rate passes directly to domestic prices through the higher cost of imported goods in local currency. This is known as imported inflation. The intensity of this channel increases as the share of imports in the household consumption basket rises, as is the case in many Arab economies that import food and energy.
