# Experiment: Controlled Testing in Social Research

An **experiment** is a research design in which the investigator deliberately manipulates one factor, the independent variable, while holding other conditions as constant as possible, in order to observe its effect on an outcome, the dependent variable. By comparing a treatment group with a control group that does not receive the intervention, and ideally by assigning participants at random, the experiment aims to isolate cause from mere association. It is the design that most directly embodies the logic of causal inference, and it has shaped the standards of evidence across the social sciences even where experiments themselves are rarely possible.

## Definition and Development

The experimental method entered the social sciences from the natural sciences and from psychology, where laboratory control was already established by the early twentieth century. Sociology adopted it more slowly, because social life resists being brought into a laboratory and because manipulating people's circumstances raises ethical questions that do not arise with chemicals. The decisive codification came with Donald Campbell and Julian Stanley's *Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs for Research* (1963), which classified designs by their vulnerability to threats to internal validity — history, maturation, selection, and the like — and gave generations of researchers a shared language for judging whether a study could support a causal claim.

A central distinction is between the **laboratory experiment**, conducted under controlled conditions, and the **field experiment**, conducted in a natural setting. Laboratory experiments maximise internal validity: the researcher controls the environment and can be confident that the manipulation caused the outcome. Field experiments sacrifice some control for realism, testing whether an effect survives contact with ordinary life. A third form, the **natural experiment**, exploits a naturally occurring division — a policy applied in one city but not another — to approximate experimental logic without any manipulation at all.

## Key Contributions: Classic Experiments and What They Showed

Three classic experiments define the method's reputation. Stanley Milgram's obedience studies, first reported in 1963 in "Behavioral Study of Obedience," showed that ordinary volunteers, instructed by an experimenter in a laboratory at Yale University, would administer what they believed were dangerous electric shocks to a stranger. The finding — that a majority of participants in the baseline condition obeyed to the highest voltage — demonstrated the power of situational authority over individual disposition, and it remains one of social psychology's most cited results.

The Stanford prison experiment, conducted by Philip Zimbardo and colleagues in 1971, assigned student volunteers at random to the roles of prisoner or guard in a simulated prison. The study had to be stopped after six days because guards became abusive and prisoners distressed. Whatever the later debates about its interpretation, it became a lasting demonstration of how rapidly roles and institutional settings can reshape behaviour.

In sociology proper, the most influential experiments are field experiments. Devah Pager's audit study, published as "The Mark of a Criminal Record" in 2003, sent matched pairs of young men — identical except that one had a criminal record — to apply for the same entry-level jobs in Milwaukee. The experiment showed that a criminal record sharply reduced callbacks, and that the penalty was far larger for Black applicants than for white ones, providing causal evidence of discrimination that survey data alone could not establish.

## Ethics, Validity, and Critique

Experiments in social research face two persistent tensions. The first is ethical. Milgram's and Zimbardo's studies deceived participants and caused them distress, and they helped produce the modern system of ethics review, informed consent, and debriefing. Field experiments that submit fake job applications consume employers' time and may deny real applicants opportunities, raising questions that researchers continue to debate.

The second tension is between internal and external validity. A laboratory result may be impeccable yet artificial: people behave differently when they know they are being studied, and an effect found with undergraduates in one country may not travel. The Hawthorne effect — changes in behaviour caused by being observed — is a standing warning. Sociologists therefore treat experiments as one tool among others, to be combined with surveys, ethnography, and historical comparison rather than treated as the only route to knowledge.

Field experiments involving vulnerable groups raise sharper concerns still, since participants often cannot consent or be debriefed; review boards typically weigh the risk to participants against the value of the knowledge and may waive consent only under strict conditions.

Replication has become a further concern. A large collaborative attempt to replicate 100 published psychology studies (Open Science Collaboration, 2015) reproduced a minority of effects at the original strength, which encouraged practices such as pre-registering hypotheses and analysis plans, sharing data, and running larger samples.

## Examples and Contemporary Uses

Today experiments are used to test the effects of classroom interventions, to measure discrimination in housing and hiring, to evaluate welfare and policing policies through randomised trials, and to study cooperation in economic games. Online platforms have made large-scale survey experiments cheap, allowing researchers to test how wording, framing, and information change attitudes. The experiment's core logic — compare like with like, change one thing, watch what happens — remains the clearest template for causal reasoning in the social sciences.
