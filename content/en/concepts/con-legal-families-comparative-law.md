# Legal Families

**Legal families** are a central classificatory concept in comparative law, used to group the world's legal systems into major clusters that share historical roots, intellectual methods and similar legal sources, as an analytical tool for organizing the study of the immense global legal diversity rather than studying each legal system in complete isolation from the others.

## René David's Classical Classification

**René David**, in his book *Les grands systèmes de droit contemporains* (1964), offered the most influential and widespread classification: the Romano-Germanic family (the European continental civil law), the common law family, the socialist family, and finally a group of other religious and traditional systems that include Islamic law, Hindu law and customary systems. David's criterion combines a technical dimension (modes of legal thinking and sources of law) with an ideological dimension (the underlying political and philosophical values), so the classification does not stop at surface similarity in terminology.

## Zweigert and Kötz's Classification: Legal Circles

**Konrad Zweigert** and Hein Kötz classified legal systems into "legal families" (*Rechtskreise*) according to their "style", which they defined by factors such as historical development, characteristic mode of legal thinking, distinctive legal institutions, the kind of legal sources recognized, and ideology. Their groupings are the Romanistic, Germanic, Anglo-American (common law), Nordic, socialist (treated in editions before the third), Far Eastern, Islamic and Hindu families. The separate Nordic and Germanic families are examples of the finer distinctions they drew. An earlier comparative classification had been proposed by Arminjon, Nolde and Wolff in their *Traité de droit comparé* (1950-1951).

## Islamic Law and Mixed Systems: A Challenge to Classification

The classification of Arab and Islamic legal systems poses a persistent methodological problem: many Arab states adopt civil codes derived from French or Egyptian models (the Egyptian code of 1948 was drafted by al-Sanhuri), yet at the same time retain Islamic Sharia rulings in personal status matters and sometimes in other branches, which makes it difficult to classify them within a single pure "family". **H. Patrick Glenn**, in *Legal Traditions of the World* (2000), proposed the concept of "legal tradition" in place of "family" or "system", which allows internal plurality without forcing each system into one pure family. Mixed jurisdictions such as Scotland, Louisiana and South Africa raise a similar difficulty.

## Contemporary Critique of the Family Classification

The concept of legal families has been criticized by comparatists such as Rodolfo Sacco and H. Patrick Glenn, who argue that it sometimes oversimplifies real internal diversity within each family (the French and German systems, although both belonging to the Romano-Germanic family, differ fundamentally in the structure of administrative law and in the style of legislative drafting), and that it may marginalize non-Western systems by placing them in a single peripheral "other" category despite their immense internal diversity. Nevertheless, the concept remains an essential teaching and analytical tool that cannot be dispensed with in teaching comparative law.

## Classifying Arab, Islamic and Mixed Systems

Arab systems are usually grouped by the routes through which modern legislation arrived. Egypt's civil code of 1948 served as a model for codes in states such as Syria and Libya, while Iraq's code, also associated with al-Sanhuri, draws more heavily on the Majalla. Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia and Lebanon show strong French influence. Sudan and some Gulf states passed through periods of common law influence, and Saudi Arabia long relied largely on uncodified jurisprudence, predominantly Hanbali, alongside regulations, before enacting a Civil Transactions Law in 2023. Jordan and the United Arab Emirates adopted civil codes that draw substantially on Islamic jurisprudence. A single Islamic family therefore joins systems whose courts and codes differ considerably.

Mixed-system scholarship takes another route. Vernon Valentine Palmer, in *Mixed Jurisdictions Worldwide* (2001), described a third legal family in a narrow sense: systems combining civil law, especially in private law, with common law, especially in public law, under a recognized dual heritage. Esin Örücü has argued that all systems are mixtures in differing degrees, better placed by their overlapping ingredients than in closed boxes.
