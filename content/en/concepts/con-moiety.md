# Moiety

A moiety is one of two complementary halves into which a society is divided, membership in each half being determined by descent. The word comes from the Latin *medietas*, meaning a half, and in anthropology it denotes a dual organisation: the whole community is split into two exogamous groups, so that a person must marry someone from the opposite moiety, and the two halves cooperate — and compete — in ritual, politics and everyday life. Moieties are the simplest form of dual organisation, and they are distinct from the phratry, a larger grouping of several clans, and from the clan itself, which is a single descent group. Where a society has moieties, every person belongs to one and only one of them, and the division shapes almost every social relationship.

## Origins and Development

Moieties were first described in detail among the Aboriginal peoples of Australia, where Lorimer Fison and Alfred Howitt documented the division of communities into two named halves, such as the *Kamilaroi* moieties, each with its own totems, marriage rules and ceremonial duties. Lewis Henry Morgan's work on the Iroquois also described dual divisions, and the concept quickly became central to the comparative study of kinship. Moieties are typically exogamous: marriage within one's own moiety is forbidden, often treated as incest, so that marriage always creates a bond between the two halves. This rule means that every family contains members of both moieties, since children belong to one parent's moiety while their other parent belongs to the opposite one, and it binds the society together through a dense web of cross-cutting ties.

The two moieties usually stand in a relationship of complementary opposition. One may be associated with the sun and the other with the moon, one with land and the other with water, one with one set of animal totems and the other with another. In ritual life the moieties often take turns: one half performs a ceremony while the other watches, assists or provides the audience, and at funerals the opposite moiety typically buries the dead, since a person cannot be buried by their own kin. Among some Australian groups, the moieties compete in games and contests; among the Tlingit and Haida of the North American Northwest Coast, the two moieties — Raven and Eagle, or Raven and Wolf — organise potlatches, crest ownership and the validation of rank. Claude Lévi-Strauss argued that dual organisation is an elementary form of reciprocity: the division into two halves institutionalises exchange, and the obligation to marry out makes every marriage an act of alliance between the halves.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns the origin of moieties. Early theorists, including Morgan, treated dual organisation as an early stage of social evolution, a primitive form that would develop into more complex clan systems. Later scholars rejected the evolutionary reading, noting that moieties occur in societies of very different complexity and that they can coexist with elaborate hierarchies, as among the Northwest Coast peoples. A second debate asks what holds the two halves together: is dual organisation primarily about marriage regulation, about ritual cooperation, or about political balance? Ethnography suggests all three, but their relative weight varies. A third debate concerns asymmetry: the two moieties are formally equal halves, yet in practice one may be senior, more prestigious, or associated with the chiefly line, and the tension between formal equality and practical hierarchy is a recurring theme in moiety systems.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Moieties remain socially alive. In Aboriginal Australia, moiety membership still governs who may marry whom, who speaks for country, and who has the right to perform particular ceremonies, and it is recognised in land-rights claims and in negotiations with the state. In the Pacific Northwest, moiety crests and names remain central to Indigenous identity and to the revival of the potlatch after its colonial prohibition. The concept has also travelled beyond kinship: anthropologists use "moiety" and "dual organisation" to analyse political factions, sports rivalries and the symbolic division of communities into complementary halves. The study of moieties also carries a cautionary lesson: early accounts often treated them as rigid, timeless structures, while contemporary ethnography shows them to be flexible, negotiated and historically changing — a reminder that kinship categories are lived and remade, not merely inherited.
