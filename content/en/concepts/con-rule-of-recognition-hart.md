# Rule of Recognition

A central concept in Hart's ***The Concept of Law***, providing a **social criterion**, not a presupposed logical one as in Kelsen's theory, for determining **what makes a particular rule a valid legal rule** within a given system.

## Definition: A Social Practice, Not a Written Text

The rule of recognition is not necessarily a written text (although it is often partly embodied in a written constitution), but an **actual social practice** shared by the officials of the system (especially judges) concerning the criteria by which law is accepted as distinct from other things (such as "what Parliament enacts is law" in the British system). Hart expresses this practice through the term **internal point of view**: officials do not merely observe a regularity of behavior, but use the rule of recognition as a critical standard for evaluating their own conduct and that of others.

## The Difference from Kelsen's Grundnorm

The concept is frequently compared with Kelsen's **basic norm** (*Grundnorm*), but the difference is fundamental: for Kelsen the Grundnorm is a **logically presupposed rule** (a presupposition), whereas for Hart the rule of recognition is an **observable social fact**, an actual practice of officials that can be described sociologically rather than merely presupposed logically. This difference is often taken to make Hart's theory more empirical in orientation than Kelsen's.

## Single or Multiple Criteria?

In complex legal systems, the rule of recognition may not consist of one simple criterion but of a **graduated set of criteria**, such as the ranking of sources of law in a written constitution (the constitutional text first, then legislation, then judicial precedents). Hart points out that this rule itself **is not subject to the validity test** imposed on other rules: as the highest rule, it is accepted or rejected as a matter of fact, not as a valid or invalid rule in the legal sense.

## An Applied Example

In the British system, the rule of recognition has historically taken the form of the principle of **parliamentary sovereignty**: what Parliament enacts is valid law. This criterion derives not from a higher constitutional text (since there is no codified written constitution), but from a firmly established practice shared by judges and officials, which embodies precisely the nature of Hart's social rule of recognition.

## Raz and Coleman's Extension

Joseph Raz and Jules Coleman later developed this concept in two distinct directions known as "exclusive positivism" and "inclusive positivism." Raz holds that the rule of recognition must rely on purely social sources (the Sources Thesis) without reference to moral criteria, whereas Coleman holds that there is no barrier to the rule of recognition itself including moral criteria as a condition of legal validity (such as requiring that legislation conform to "justice" in some constitutions), so long as this requirement ultimately rests on a social consensus among officials.
