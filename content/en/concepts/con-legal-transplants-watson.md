# Legal Transplants

A central concept in **comparative law**, formulated by the legal historian **Alan Watson** in his 1974 book *Legal Transplants: An Approach to Comparative Law*. It refers to **the transfer of a legal rule or institution** from one legal system to another that is socially and culturally different, with the rule remaining relatively effective and functional in its new context.

## The Watsonian thesis: transplanting is the rule, not the exception

Contrary to views that tie law closely to the character of a particular society, such as Montesquieu's claim that laws must suit the people for whom they are made and Savigny's idea of the "spirit of the people" (*Volksgeist*), Watson argues that legal transplantation is the **most fertile source** of legal development throughout history. He bases this on the study of several historical cases, such as the reception of Roman law in European societies that differed greatly from one another. He concludes that the community of jurists and judges tends to borrow rules from sources of intellectual prestige for internal professional reasons, more than in response to direct local social necessities.

## The dispute with Pierre Legrand: is transplanting possible at all?

The comparatist **Pierre Legrand** rejects the idea of legal transplants from its foundations. He relies on an interpretive position holding that the meaning of a legal rule cannot be separated from its local cultural, linguistic and historical fabric. When a rule is "transferred", what is transferred is nothing but an empty verbal formulation, since its meaning is rebuilt entirely within the new context. This makes it, in reality, a **different rule**, not the same rule in new clothing.

## The legal irritant: a later theoretical synthesis

Gunther Teubner, in "Legal Irritants: Good Faith in British Law or How Unifying Law Ends Up in New Divergences" (*Modern Law Review*, 1998), offers an intermediate concept that goes beyond the sharp binary between the positions of Watson and Legrand. The transferred rule works as a **legal irritant**, which provokes the receiving system. That system then rebuilds the rule's meaning from within, in ways that may deviate radically from its origin. This partly reconciles Watson's historical realism (rules are indeed borrowed) with Legrand's interpretive sensitivity (meaning changes substantially upon borrowing).

## Earlier and further critiques

Otto Kahn-Freund, in "On Uses and Misuses of Comparative Law" (*Modern Law Review*, 1974), had already argued that rules tied to a society's political and social structure are hard to transfer, while rules in less politically charged areas travel more easily.

## A further critique: the role of non-jurist actors

Alongside Legrand's interpretive critique, other historians have noted that Watson's focus on the jurist elite that transfers rules neglects broader political and economic factors. Colonial interests or pressures from international donor institutions, for example, may also explain why one society accepts an imported rule while another rejects it, even though both were exposed to the same potential source of borrowing.

## Impact and application

The concept is used today as a basic analytical tool for understanding cross-border harmonization projects (such as a unified European law), legal reform programs in developing countries that import Western legislative models, and questions about whether intellectual property and international trade laws can be harmonized across legal systems that differ radically in their historical and cultural formation.
