# Global Care Chains and the Second Shift in Hochschild

Two interrelated concepts formulated by Arlie Russell Hochschild to analyze the unequal gendered division of domestic and care work, first within a single family, and then across national and global class boundaries.

## Content

In *The Second Shift* (1989, with Anne Machung), Hochschild documented how women working for pay, after returning from the "first shift" (paid work outside the home), perform a far larger share of housework and childcare (the "second shift") than their male partners, even when both partners work full-time for pay: a cumulative burden culturally unrecognized and not counted economically as actual "work". She later developed this analysis to a global level, in her essay "Global Care Chains and Emotional Surplus Value" (2000), through the concept of "global care chains" (Global Care Chains). Women migrate from poor countries (the Philippines, Indonesia, Latin America) to rich countries to work in the care of the children or the elderly of wealthy families there, while other individuals in their home countries (relatives, or other, lower-paid workers) take over the care of their own children, in a successive chain of "exporting" emotional and care labor. This reproduces the economic inequality between North and South at the level of emotion and direct human care itself, not only at the level of traditional material goods and services.

## Historical Context

Hochschild, a sociologist at the University of California, Berkeley (now professor emerita), developed this analysis from her early research on "emotional labor" (Emotional Labor) in her book *The Managed Heart* (1983), in which she studied how flight attendants and service employees are required to manage their emotions and display artificial smiles as part of their paid job. Her later analysis of the second shift and global care chains came in the context of the economic transformations of globalization in the late twentieth century, when the migration of women from the Global South to work in the domestic care sector in the North increased. She also drew on field studies by researchers such as Bridget Anderson and Rhacel Salazar Parreñas on migrant domestic workers.

## Influence

This analysis linked the field of the sociology of emotions, to which Hochschild was a leading contributor, to the sociology of migration, globalization and gender, turning the question from "how are feelings managed individually?" to "how is the cost of emotional care distributed globally, by class and by gender?". The concept of "global care chains" became a basic analytical tool in studies of migrant domestic labor worldwide, and influenced the development of later concepts about the "international division of reproductive labor" (International Division of Reproductive Labor) in the sociology of gender and migration. Nancy Fraser later drew on it in her analysis of what she called the structural "crisis of care" in contemporary capitalism, which holds that the capitalist system depends on unpaid or poorly paid care labor that threatens its social sustainability in the long run.
