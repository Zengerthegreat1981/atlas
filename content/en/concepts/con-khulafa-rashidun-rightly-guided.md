# The Rashidun (Rightly-Guided) Caliphs

A term used by the majority of Sunni Muslims for the first four caliphs who succeeded the Prophet Muhammad in leading the Muslim community after his death in 11 AH / 632 CE: Abu Bakr al-Siddiq, Umar ibn al-Khattab, Uthman ibn Affan and Ali ibn Abi Talib. This period ends with the assassination of Ali in 40 AH / 661 CE.

## Definition and Meaning

"Al-Rashidun" is the plural of "rashid," meaning one who is guided and wise in managing his affairs. The term carries a normative rather than purely descriptive meaning: it describes these caliphs as having followed the guidance of prophecy in their rule, drawing their legitimacy from the community's consensus and choice (*bay'a*, the pledge of allegiance), not from hereditary succession or direct prophetic claim. This contrasts with the hereditary character the caliphate would later take on in the Umayyad and Abbasid periods. In Sunni tradition this period is traditionally regarded as the model "golden age" of Islamic government. The legitimacy of the method by which each caliph was chosen rests on a different mechanism: consultation (*shura*) among senior Companions for Abu Bakr, nomination by his predecessor for Umar, a limited consultative council for Uthman, and a mass pledge of allegiance for Ali.

## Major Events of the Period

The caliphate of Abu Bakr (11–13 AH) saw the Ridda Wars, which re-unified the Arabian Peninsula after several tribes broke their political allegiance following the Prophet's death, as well as the beginning of conquests beyond the Peninsula. The state expanded enormously under Umar ibn al-Khattab (13–23 AH), whose armies conquered the lands of the Levant, Iraq and Egypt. He also established the administrative and financial registers (*dawawin*) of the state and the Hijri calendar. The reign of Uthman ibn Affan (23–35 AH) saw continued expansion, but was marked by internal political tensions that escalated until his killing at the hands of rebels from the provinces. This event opened the door to the "Great Discord" (*al-fitna al-kubra*) under Ali ibn Abi Talib (35–40 AH), whose reign witnessed civil wars (the Battles of the Camel and Siffin) and ended with his assassination and the establishment of the Umayyad caliphate after him.

## Critical Historical Reading

Contemporary scholars distinguish between the ideal picture that later Sunni sources established of this period as a complete model of rightly guided rule, and a more complex critical historical reading that highlights the real political tensions accompanying each transfer of power, including tribal disputes and the economic interests of the newly conquered provinces. Islamic sects also differ radically in their assessment of this period: while the Sunnis regard it as a complete normative model, the Shia hold that the rightful caliphate belonged to Ali ibn Abi Talib alone immediately after the Prophet, and regard the caliphate of his three predecessors as a departure from the correct divine order.

## Historical Sources

Historians reconstructing this period relied on sources written a century or more after the events, the most prominent of which are the history of al-Tabari (*Tarikh al-rusul wa'l-muluk*, History of Prophets and Kings) and the works of al-Baladhuri (*Ansab al-ashraf* and *Futuh al-buldan*). These sources sometimes show multiple editorial layers that reflect later disputes between political and religious schools more than they reflect pure facts. This calls for the modern historian (as Fred Donner has shown) to read them critically, in order to distinguish the historical layers of the narrative from the later interpretive layers.
