# Revolution: The Overthrow of a Social Order

A **revolution** is the rapid, fundamental, and often violent overthrow of a political regime and the social order that sustains it, followed by the attempt to build a new order in its place. It is distinguished from a coup, which changes the rulers but not the system, and from reform, which changes the system gradually and within the rules. Revolutions are among the most studied and most contested phenomena in the social sciences, because they combine the deepest questions of politics — what makes people rise up, what makes regimes fall, and what makes a new order possible — with the most dramatic events in human history.

## Definition and Development

The modern concept of revolution was formed by the French Revolution of 1789, which gave the word its current meaning: not a return to a previous order, as in the astronomical sense of a turning wheel, but a break with the past and the creation of something new. The American Revolution, the Russian Revolution of 1917, and the Chinese Revolution of 1949 became the classic cases against which all others were measured, and the study of revolution became the study of how such breaks happen and what they produce.

Crane Brinton, in *The Anatomy of Revolution* (1938), proposed that revolutions follow a common pattern: a regime in crisis, a moderate phase, a radical phase of terror, and a Thermidorian reaction in which the revolution settles into a new order. Brinton's anatomy was influential because it suggested that revolutions, for all their differences, share a morphology — a sequence of stages that can be recognized across cases. Later scholars criticized the idea of a universal pattern, but Brinton's work established the comparative study of revolution as a field.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Theda Skocpol, in *States and Social Revolutions* (1979), transformed the field by arguing that revolutions are not made by revolutionaries alone but by the breakdown of states. Comparing France, Russia, and China, Skocpol showed that social revolutions occurred where agrarian bureaucracies faced international military pressure, where dominant classes could not reform, and where peasant revolts coincided with the collapse of the state. Her structural account challenged voluntarist theories that attributed revolutions to ideology or leadership: the revolutionaries, she argued, often did not intend the revolution that resulted, and the outcome depended on state structures and international context more than on anyone's plans.

Samuel Huntington, in *Political Order in Changing Societies* (1968), offered a different account, emphasizing the gap between mobilization and institutionalization. Revolutions, in his view, occur when social mobilization outruns political institutions: when new groups enter politics faster than parties, courts, and bureaucracies can absorb them. His work connected the study of revolution to the study of development, showing why rapid modernization so often produces instability.

Jack Goldstone, in *Revolution and Rebellion in the Early Modern World* (1991), added a demographic dimension. Population growth, he argued, produces fiscal crisis, elite competition, and a large cohort of young people with limited prospects — conditions that make states vulnerable to breakdown. His work linked the great revolutions of the early modern world to long-term demographic and economic cycles, and it helped explain why revolutions cluster in particular periods.

## Ethnographic Examples

Anthropologists have studied revolution from the ground up, showing how it is experienced by ordinary people. In Nicaragua, Cuba, and Iran, fieldwork has shown how revolution is lived as a moral drama: the old order is experienced as corrupt and unjust, the revolution as a moment of purification and hope, and the aftermath as a test of whether the new order can deliver on its promises. In many cases, the revolution's ideals survive even when its institutions fail, and people continue to measure the present against the hopes of the revolutionary moment.

Fieldwork has also shown how revolutions transform everyday life. New calendars, new forms of address, new rituals, and new dress all mark the break with the past. At the same time, anthropologists have documented the continuities that survive revolution: kinship networks, religious practices, and local solidarities that persist beneath the new political language. Revolution, in this view, is never total: it remakes the state, but it does not remake society from scratch.

## Contemporary Debates

Current scholarship debates whether the age of revolution is over. Some argue that globalization, the spread of democracy, and the decline of the peasantry have made classic social revolutions unlikely, and that contemporary uprisings — the Arab Spring, the colour revolutions — are better understood as democratic transitions or protest movements than as revolutions. Others argue that revolution has taken new forms: digital mobilization, transnational networks, and the politics of climate change may produce upheavals that do not look like the revolutions of the past but are revolutions nonetheless. A further debate concerns the outcomes of revolution: why do so many revolutions produce authoritarian regimes, and under what conditions do they produce democracy? Anthropology's contribution is to keep the focus on lived experience: revolution is not only a change of regime but a transformation of how people understand themselves, their neighbours, and their future.
