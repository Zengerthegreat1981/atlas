# Taboo

Taboo is a prohibition on certain actions, objects, words, or people, backed by social or supernatural sanctions. The word entered English from the Polynesian tapu, recorded by Captain James Cook in Tonga in 1777, where it denoted things set apart as sacred or forbidden — food that chiefs could not eat, places that could not be entered, people who could not be touched. In anthropology, taboo has become a general concept for the ways societies mark boundaries between the sacred and the profane, the pure and the polluted, the permitted and the forbidden. Taboos are not arbitrary rules: they express a society's deepest values, and violating them is felt as dangerous, disgusting, or morally wrong.

## Definition

A taboo differs from an ordinary rule in its force and its rationale. A speed limit is a rule that can be debated and changed; a taboo against incest or cannibalism is experienced as a matter of nature rather than convention, and its breach provokes horror rather than mere disapproval. Émile Durkheim argued in 1912 that the sacred is defined by prohibition: sacred things are those set apart and forbidden, and taboo is the mechanism that protects them. Mary Douglas developed this insight in 1966, arguing that taboos about pollution — dietary restrictions, bodily taboos, rules about contact — are ways of ordering experience and maintaining categories. Things that do not fit the categories, that are ambiguous or anomalous, are treated as dangerous. Sigmund Freud, in Totem and Taboo of 1913, offered a psychological account, tracing taboo to ambivalent desires and the repression of forbidden impulses. Franz Steiner, whose study of taboo was published in 1956, emphasised its social character: taboo is fundamentally a system of social restrictions on behaviour, varying enormously in content but universal in form.

## Origins and Development

The study of taboo was one of the founding topics of anthropology. James Frazer's The Golden Bough, first published in 1890, gathered examples of taboo from around the world — kings who could not touch the ground, menstruating women secluded from the village, names that could not be spoken — and treated them as evidence of a universal stage of magical thinking. Later anthropologists rejected Frazer's evolutionary scheme but kept his material. Durkheim's theory of the sacred gave taboo a sociological foundation: prohibitions express and maintain the moral unity of the group. The mid-twentieth century saw a shift from asking why taboos exist to asking what they do. Douglas showed that food taboos, such as the Levitical prohibitions on pork and shellfish, make sense as part of a system of classification rather than as hygiene or superstition. Steiner's comparative work demonstrated that taboo systems are as rational as any other part of culture, once their categories are understood.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns whether taboo is primarily religious, social, or psychological. Durkheim emphasised the social, Freud the psychological, and Douglas the cognitive; most contemporary accounts combine all three, treating taboo as a phenomenon in which social order, moral feeling, and classification reinforce each other. A second debate concerns universality. Are there universal taboos, such as the incest taboo, or is every taboo culturally specific? The incest taboo is found in every known society, but its scope varies enormously: what counts as incest depends on how a society defines kinship. A third debate concerns the relationship between taboo and power. Taboos often protect the privileges of chiefs, priests, and men, and they can be instruments of domination as much as expressions of shared values. Yet taboos also constrain the powerful: a king bound by taboo is a king whose power is limited by sacred law.

## Contemporary Relevance

Taboo remains relevant because prohibition remains a central feature of social life. Modern societies have their own taboos: topics that cannot be discussed in polite company, words that cannot be spoken in public, and behaviours — from drug use to certain forms of sexuality — that are treated with disgust as much as with legal sanction. Food taboos persist and multiply, from religious dietary laws to ethical vegetarianism. Anthropologists study how taboos change: how the taboo on discussing death, mental illness, or sexuality weakens in some contexts and strengthens in others, and how new taboos emerge around emerging technologies. The study of taboo also informs debates about censorship, free speech, and cultural sensitivity, where the question of what may not be said or shown is as contested as ever. Taboo endures as a concept because it names something societies cannot do without: the power of prohibition to define what is sacred, what is dangerous, and who we are.
