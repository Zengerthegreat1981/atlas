# Sadness

Sadness is the emotional response to a perceived loss — of a person, a relationship, a possession, a hope, or a valued part of life itself. It is one of the most universal human feelings, recognised across cultures and across the lifespan, from an infant's distress at separation to an elder's grief at the passing of a world. Where fear mobilises the organism against threat, sadness slows it down: energy falls, attention turns inward, and the person withdraws to take stock of what has been lost and what remains.

## Sadness in the Prototype Taxonomy of Emotion

In the prototype approach to emotion, sadness is one of the small set of basic-level emotion concepts — alongside love, joy, anger, fear, and perhaps surprise — that people use most readily to make everyday distinctions among feelings. In their landmark study of the emotion lexicon, Shaver, Schwartz, Kirson, and O'Connor (1987) found that ordinary speakers organise emotion words into a hierarchy, and that "sadness" anchors a large family of related terms. The sadness family, as they mapped it, includes suffering and agony; deep sorrow, grief, and melancholy; disappointment and dismay; guilt, shame, regret, and remorse; and loneliness, alienation, and rejection. These are not synonyms but neighbours: they share a common core — the experience of loss or of falling short — while differing in intensity, object, and duration.

Richard Lazarus, whose appraisal theory shaped modern emotion psychology, treated sadness as the emotion that follows an irrevocable loss. In his framework, each emotion arises from a distinctive pattern of appraisal: sadness is evoked when a person judges that something valued has been lost and cannot be restored, and that no action can undo the damage. This appraisal explains sadness's characteristic passivity: the loss is accepted as final, so its action tendency is withdrawal and rest rather than attack or flight.

## Sadness, Grief, and Loss

Sadness shades into grief when the loss is the death of a loved one or the rupture of a deep attachment. John Bowlby's third volume of *Attachment and Loss*, *Loss: Sadness and Depression* (1980), remains the foundational account of this territory. Drawing on decades of observation, Bowlby described mourning as a phased, biologically grounded response: numbing, yearning and searching for the lost person, disorganisation and despair as the loss becomes undeniable, and finally reorganisation, in which the bereaved person rebuilds a life that no longer includes the one who died.

Sigmund Freud had drawn a similar line much earlier in "Mourning and Melancholia" (1917): in normal mourning the world becomes poor and empty, whereas in melancholia the self itself becomes poor and empty.

## The Body and Brain of Sadness

Sadness has a visible signature. Charles Darwin, in *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872), described the downturned mouth, the raised inner corners of the eyebrows, and the slackening of the muscles that mark the sad face — expressions he regarded as widely recognisable, suggesting an innate basis. Modern cross-cultural research finds that the sad face is recognised at above-chance levels across very different societies, though recognition is not uniform and the rules governing when it may be displayed vary widely.

Beneath the expression, sadness involves a distinctive bodily state: low arousal, reduced motor activity, a heaviness in the limbs, and often tears. Jaak Panksepp, in *Affective Neuroscience* (1998), traced the neural systems underlying separation distress in mammals, showing that the panic-grief system — activated by separation from attachment figures — is one of the brain's primary emotional circuits, shared across species and modulated by opioids and oxytocin.

## Cultural Variation and the Arabic Tradition of Loss

How sadness is expressed, and what it is taken to mean, differs sharply between cultures. Batja Mesquita and Nico Frijda, reviewing cross-cultural evidence in 1992, documented systematic cultural differences in how emotions are appraised, expressed and regulated. Such differences are often discussed in terms of individualist and collectivist cultural models: where the self is seen as independent, sadness tends to be treated as a private experience to be managed by the individual, whereas where the self is seen as interdependent it may be more openly shared and ritualised. Whether sadness is displayed or concealed, whether it is spoken of or endured in silence, and whether it is treated as weakness or as a dignified response to loss, all depend on the cultural scripts a person has learned.

In the classical Arabic and Islamic tradition, sadness (*huzn*) and grief were examined both philosophically and poetically. In clinical and philosophical psychology, Abu Zayd al-Balkhi (d. 934) devoted a pivotal chapter of *Masalih al-Abdan wa al-Anfus* (*Sustenance of the Soul*) to distinguishing between sadness resulting from an identifiable external loss and sadness arising without an apparent external trigger (analogous to endogenous depressive states), offering early cognitive-behavioral strategies for reframing loss. Earlier, Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi (d. c. 873) composed *Risalah fi al-Hilah li-Daf' al-Ahzan* (*On the Art of Dispelling Sorrows*), presenting a rationalist-ethical framework that interpreted grief as the consequence of misplaced attachment to impermanent worldly possessions.

In Arabic literary heritage, the elegiac genre (*ritha'*)—most famously exemplified in pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry by al-Khansa' mourning her brothers Sakhr and Mu'awiya—served not as a privatized pathology but as a communal, metricized ritual that provided public catharsis, formalized communal solidarity, and structured the articulation of profound sorrow.

## Sadness and Depression

Sadness is a normal emotion; depression is a clinical condition. In DSM-5-TR, major depressive disorder requires, over at least two weeks, depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure together with several other symptoms (such as sleep or appetite change, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, psychomotor agitation or retardation, difficulty concentrating, and recurrent thoughts of death), causing significant distress or impairment. Similarly, ICD-11 classifies depressive disorders (e.g., single episode depressive disorder, code 6A70) noting persistent depressed mood or diminished interest alongside neurovegetative changes. Sadness that is proportionate, tied to a loss, and comes in waves is not by itself a disorder, and clinical judgement is needed to tell the two apart. Anyone with persistent low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of self-harm should seek professional help.

## Sadness and Meaning

Sadness is also entangled with questions of meaning. The loss of a person can be borne, but the loss of meaning — the sense that life no longer makes sense — can turn ordinary sadness into despair. Existential thinkers have long noted that grief forces a confrontation with finitude: the death of another reminds us of our own mortality. Yet sadness is not only destructive. It slows the person down, signals to others that help is needed, and creates the conditions for reflection. In this sense sadness is not the opposite of a meaningful life but one of its conditions: only beings who love and value can lose, and only beings who lose can feel sad.
