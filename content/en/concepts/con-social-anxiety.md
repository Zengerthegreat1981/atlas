# Social Anxiety Disorder

## Definition

**Social anxiety disorder** (also known as **social phobia**) is a psychiatric disorder characterized by intense and persistent fear or anxiety (lasting typically six months or more according to DSM-5-TR) about one or more social situations in which the person may be observed and evaluated by others, such as speaking in front of an audience, eating in front of strangers, or initiating a conversation. The sufferer fears acting in a way that causes embarrassment, rejection or humiliation. The fear is disproportionate to the actual danger of the situation. It usually leads to avoidance of these situations, or to enduring them with severe distress, in a way that impairs social, occupational or functional performance.

## Classification and Prevalence

The disorder is classified in DSM-5-TR among the **anxiety disorders**, and in ICD-11 (code 6B04) under anxiety or fear-related disorders. DSM-5 offers a "performance only" specifier for cases in which the fear is restricted to speaking or performing in public; the earlier "generalized" subtype of DSM-IV was removed. US epidemiological data (the National Comorbidity Survey Replication) estimated a lifetime prevalence of roughly 12%, which makes it one of the most common anxiety disorders, with typical onset in early to mid adolescence. Prevalence estimates differ considerably across countries and survey methods.

## Psychological and Neural Basis

Cognitive-behavioral models link the disorder to attentional biases toward negative social cues, a tendency toward excessively negative post-event processing of the social situation, and rigid beliefs about the necessity of performing socially flawlessly. Neuroimaging studies show hyperactivity in the **amygdala** in response to threatening faces or social cues, along with abnormalities in prefrontal cortex circuits responsible for regulating emotion.

## Treatment

- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)**: relies on correcting distorted beliefs about negative evaluation, alongside gradual **exposure therapy** to anxiety-provoking situations. Individual cognitive therapy based on the Clark and Wells model is among the best-supported psychological treatments, and CBT is recommended as a first-line option in most clinical guidelines.
- **Pharmacological treatment**: selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs, such as sertraline and paroxetine) and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs, such as extended-release venlafaxine) are among the medications approved by the US Food and Drug Administration for this disorder, and they usually take weeks to begin working.
- **Combined approach**: medication and behavioral therapy are combined in more severe cases or when there is only a partial response to monotherapy.

## Impact

Research on social anxiety disorder contributed to developing influential cognitive-behavioral models (notably the 1995 Clark and Wells model), which became a reference for the treatment of anxiety disorders in general. Its wide global prevalence also led to its inclusion among priorities of school and university mental health programs for early intervention.

## Limits

The distinction between normal shyness and a clinical disorder remains a matter of clinical judgment. Reported prevalence rates vary with culture and assessment criteria, which raises debate about the extent to which DSM criteria, developed in the United States, can be generalized across different cultural contexts.
