# Debt and Obligation

Debt is an obligation to repay, made socially real by the expectation that a gift, loan, or favour will be returned. In anthropology, debt is not only a financial transaction but a fundamental social relation: to be in debt is to be bound to another person, and the history of a relationship can often be read in the history of what is owed. The study of debt connects the anthropology of exchange with questions of morality, power, and inequality, because debts can create solidarity, sustain hierarchies, and, when they cannot be repaid, destroy livelihoods and lives.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish several kinds of debt. A gift creates an obligation to reciprocate, but the timing and the form of the return are left open, and the debt is as much moral as material. A loan, by contrast, specifies what is owed and often when it must be repaid, sometimes with interest. Bridewealth, compensation payments, and fines create debts between groups rather than individuals. Marcel Mauss, in his classic essay on the gift, argued that in many societies gifts are never free: they carry the obligation to give, to receive, and to repay, and these three obligations hold society together. C. A. Gregory later distinguished between gift economies, in which things are exchanged to create personal relationships, and commodity economies, in which things are exchanged to sever them. Debt sits uneasily between the two: it is a personal bond, but it can also be quantified, transferred, and enforced, and in that respect it resembles a commodity.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of debt grew out of the study of gift exchange in the early twentieth century, but it became a distinct field much later. Mauss's The Gift, first published in French in 1925 and in English in 1954, established the idea that exchange is a total social phenomenon, binding together economic, legal, moral, and religious life. Mid-century ethnographers documented systems of credit and obligation in peasant and pastoral societies: rotating credit associations, in which members contribute regularly and take turns receiving the pooled sum; bridewealth debts that bound families together for generations; and the debts of clients to patrons. David Graeber's Debt: The First 5,000 Years, published in 2011, brought these ethnographic insights to a wide audience, arguing that debt has been central to human history for far longer than money or markets, and that the moral language of debt — the idea that debts must always be repaid — has been used to justify relations of domination, from ancient slavery to modern structural adjustment. Gustav Peebles, in a review of the field published in 2010, argued that credit and debt should be studied as paired concepts, since every debt is someone else's credit.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns the morality of debt. In many societies, some debts are honourable and others shameful; being a good debtor can be a source of standing, while being unable to pay can mean social death. Anthropologists ask how the moral evaluation of debt varies, and how it changes when debts are taken over by banks, states, or international institutions. A second debate concerns debt and personhood. In some contexts, a debtor's person is at stake: historically, debts could be repaid with one's body, through slavery or bonded labour, and in the present, credit scores and debt collection shape who a person can be in the eyes of institutions. A third debate concerns the relationship between debt and freedom. Debt is often described as a burden, but ethnography shows that people also seek debts: a loan can start a business, a bridewealth debt can found a marriage, and the ability to borrow is a sign of trust and standing. The question is not whether debt is good or bad, but under what conditions it creates mutual obligation and under what conditions it creates domination.

## Contemporary Relevance

Debt is at the centre of contemporary economic life, and anthropology's contribution is to show that it is never merely technical. Household debt — mortgages, student loans, credit cards — now structures the lives of billions of people, and ethnographers study how families manage it, how they moralise it, and how it shapes decisions about marriage, migration, and care. Sovereign debt has become a political battleground, with creditor nations and institutions imposing conditions on debtor states, and with social movements arguing for debt cancellation as a matter of justice. Microcredit programmes, which lend small sums to poor borrowers, were once celebrated as a solution to poverty and are now fiercely debated: do they empower borrowers, or do they trap them in cycles of repayment? Remittances from migrants create new debts and obligations between households across borders. In each of these cases, the anthropological study of debt insists on a simple point: a debt is a relationship, and to understand it one must ask who owes whom, what is owed, and what happens when the debt cannot be paid.
