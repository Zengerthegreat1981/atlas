# Polyandry

Polyandry is marriage in which one woman has more than one husband at the same time. It is the rarest of the major marriage forms, and one of the most misunderstood. Where it occurs, it most often takes the form of fraternal polyandry, in which a group of brothers share a single wife, though non-fraternal forms also exist. Polyandry is distinct from polygyny, in which one man has several wives, and from polygamy, the general term for plural marriage. Its classic cases are found in the Himalayas, among Tibetans and neighbouring peoples, and in parts of South India, but anthropologists have documented it in Africa, Oceania, and the Americas as well. Understanding polyandry means asking why a society would organise marriage this way, and what it does for the people involved.

## Origins and Development

The comparative study of polyandry began with Edward Westermarck's *The History of Human Marriage* (1891), which gathered reports of the practice from travellers and colonial officials and treated it as a survival of earlier, less differentiated forms of marriage. Early evolutionists assumed polyandry was a primitive stage on the way to monogamy, but fieldwork soon complicated that view. Prince Peter of Greece and Denmark produced an influential cross-cultural study in *A Study of Polyandry* (1963), which distinguished fraternal from non-fraternal forms and treated the practice as tied to particular economic and demographic conditions rather than as a relic.

Melvyn Goldstein's research among Tibetans, summarised in "Pahari and Tibetan Polyandry Revisited" (1978), gave the classic explanation. In the high Himalayas, arable land is scarce and families risk fragmenting their holdings if each son marries separately and divides the estate. By marrying all brothers to one wife, the family keeps its land intact, limits the number of children, and concentrates labour. Nancy Levine developed this argument in *The Dynamics of Polyandry: Kinship, Domesticity, and Population in the Nepalese Himalaya* (1988), showing, for the Nyinba of Nepal, how polyandry fitted a household strategy of keeping land and labour together in a harsh environment.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The central debate concerns the causes of polyandry. The economic explanation stresses land and labour: where resources cannot be divided, polyandry prevents division. A demographic explanation stresses the shortage of women: where female infanticide, out-migration, or a skewed sex ratio leaves too few women, polyandry absorbs the surplus of men. A third explanation stresses male cooperation: brothers who share a wife also share work and inheritance, and their solidarity strengthens the household. Katherine Starkweather and Raymond Hames, in "A Survey of Non-Classical Polyandry" (2012), complicated the picture by documenting polyandry in societies without land scarcity or female shortage, including cases where women take multiple partners for their own reasons, suggesting that no single cause explains every case.

A second dimension concerns the position of women. Polyandry is often described as the mirror image of polygyny, but the symmetry is false: in fraternal polyandry, the wife's authority is real but constrained, since she is outnumbered by her husbands and their kin, and her children are counted as belonging to the household rather than to a particular father. Paternity is typically not a matter of dispute, because the children inherit from the household as a whole. Ethnographers also note that polyandrous wives may hold considerable domestic power, managing the household economy and mediating between brothers. The arrangement therefore raises questions about female agency that cannot be answered by analogy with polygyny.

A third dimension is the relationship between polyandry and other institutions. Polyandrous households are often embedded in systems of arranged marriage, caste or rank, and religious obligation. In Tibet, the practice was reinforced by the custom of sending younger sons to monasteries, which reduced the number of husbands, and by the inheritance rules that kept estates whole. Where land reform, education, and migration have weakened these supports, polyandry has declined rapidly, showing how tightly it was tied to a particular economic order.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Polyandry has declined sharply in the Himalayas, driven by land reform, the growth of wage labour, and the influence of state law, which generally recognises only monogamous marriage. Yet it continues to attract attention for what it reveals about marriage itself. It shows that marriage is not only about love or reproduction but about property, labour, and the survival of households. It challenges the assumption that one husband and one wife is the natural arrangement, and it reminds us that the rules of marriage are shaped by ecology and economy as much as by culture or religion.

Polyandry also matters for current debates about plural marriage and family law. As societies reconsider who may marry and in what combinations, polyandry provides a documented case of a stable, functioning alternative, one that persisted for centuries and served the interests of the families who practised it. Anthropologists caution against romanticising it: polyandry, like every marriage form, involved constraint as well as choice, and its decline was welcomed by many of those who lived under it. Its value as an object of study is precisely that it is neither a curiosity nor an ideal, but a working solution to a real problem, now largely abandoned as the problem itself changed.
