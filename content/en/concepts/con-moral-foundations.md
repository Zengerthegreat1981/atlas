# Moral Foundations

Moral foundations are the bases on which moral obligation rests and from which the standards that distinguish right from wrong are derived. Is the source of this obligation pure reason, divine law, human utility and experience, or the emotional structure shared by human beings? Most of the disagreement among the major ethical schools branches out from this fundamental question.

In Islamic thought, several foundational frameworks have existed. The Ash'ari theological school made divine law (*shar'*), that is, revelation, the sole basis of moral judgment, since there is no independent rational good or evil apart from divine command and prohibition. The Mu'tazilites made reason an independent basis by which humans perceive the goodness and badness of actions, and for them divine law confirms what reason perceives and supplements it in details that reason alone cannot reach. Theorists of the higher objectives of the law (*maqasid al-shari'a*), foremost among them al-Shatibi in *al-Muwafaqat*, grounded ethics in divine law on the preservation of the five universal interests (religion, life, intellect, progeny, and property), offering a purposive framework that is sometimes compared to the idea of "human flourishing" in Western philosophy. Ibn Miskawayh linked the moral foundation to the soul's teleological orientation toward perfection, influenced by both Aristotle and Plotinus.

In Western philosophy, four major foundations compete. The religious foundation (divine command theory) makes God's will the source of obligation. The rationalist Kantian foundation derives ethics from the law of practical reason, independent of any outcome or desire, expressed in the "categorical imperative." The utilitarian foundation, associated with Bentham and Mill, reduces right to the maximization of overall happiness and the minimization of pain. The emotional foundation, developed by Hume, holds that reason is "a slave to the passions" and that sympathy is the true basis of moral judgment rather than abstract inference. In recent decades, the psychologist Jonathan Haidt added an empirical theory known as the "five moral foundations" (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, and sanctity), reporting that different cultures and political orientations weight the foundations differently.

Each of these foundations faces classic objections. The Euthyphro dilemma poses a challenge to the religious foundation (is an act good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good in itself?). Kantianism is accused of formal rigidity in the face of real conflicts. Utilitarianism is charged with the possibility of sacrificing the rights of a minority to maximize the happiness of the majority. The emotional foundation is accused of excessive relativism.

Example: when asked for the foundation of the prohibition of lying, the Ash'ari answers that divine law forbade it; the Kantian answers that it cannot be universalized as a law without contradiction; and the utilitarian answers that it undermines social trust and thereby reduces overall utility. The three answers differ despite agreeing on the apparent verdict. This example shows that differences in foundations do not necessarily lead to different practical results in many cases, though their effects become clearly visible in borderline cases where principles conflict with one another, such as a conflict between an individual's right and the interest of the community. This is why the study of moral foundations is a necessary preliminary step to understanding any particular ethical system, rather than a theoretical luxury detached from practice.
