# Status: Position, Prestige, and the Rank Order of Social Life

**Status** is a position in a social structure — a place in a rank order that carries a set of rights, obligations, and a measure of prestige. To be a teacher, a mother, a citizen, or a chief is to occupy a status; the status names the position, while the role names what a person is expected to do in it. Status is one of the basic building blocks of sociology because it connects the individual to the structure: people occupy statuses, statuses are arranged in hierarchies, and those hierarchies distribute honour, wealth, and power.

## Definition and Development

Max Weber, in *Economy and Society* (1922; English edition 1968), made status — *Stand* — one of the three dimensions of stratification, alongside class and party. Class is about economic position, party about political power; status is about honour and prestige. Status groups, Weber argued, are communities defined by a shared style of life and by the recognition of others: they eat, dress, marry, and speak in ways that mark them as members, and they defend the boundaries of the group against outsiders. A wealthy merchant might have high class and low status, while an impoverished aristocrat might have high status and low class — the two dimensions do not always move together.

Ralph Linton, in *The Study of Man* (1936), gave the concept its standard sociological form. A status, Linton wrote, is a position in a social system; a role is the dynamic aspect of that status, the behaviour expected of its occupant. Every person occupies many statuses at once — ascribed statuses, such as sex and age, which are assigned at birth, and achieved statuses, such as occupation, which are earned. Linton's distinction made it possible to analyse how individuals move between statuses and how societies assign duties and privileges.

## Key Contributions: Status Sets and Stratification

Robert K. Merton, in his work on the role-set (1957), refined Linton's idea by showing that a single position carries a whole set of related roles, and he spoke of the **status set** — the array of statuses a person holds at once, which may pull in different directions. A woman may be simultaneously a professor, a mother, and a migrant, and the expectations attached to each status can conflict. Gerhard Lenski, in a 1954 article on status crystallization, analysed status inconsistency: when a person's statuses rank differently — high education but low income, for example — the mismatch can become a source of strain and, in some findings, of political dissatisfaction.

Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore, in "Some Principles of Stratification" (1945), offered an influential functionalist account of why status hierarchies exist at all. Societies, they argued, must motivate people to fill their most important and demanding positions, and they do so by attaching greater rewards — income, prestige, and power — to those positions. The Davis–Moore thesis made status hierarchy seem functional and necessary; its critics replied that it justified existing inequalities by describing them as the outcome of a fair competition for talent.

## Examples

Status operates in the smallest interactions. In a hospital, the status of doctor and nurse carries different rights to speak, to decide, and to be listened to, even when the nurse knows more about the patient. In a school, the status of teacher and pupil shapes who may interrupt whom. In everyday life, forms of address — titles, surnames, first names — signal status and mark its boundaries. To call a judge by their first name is not a mistake of etiquette; it is a challenge to the status order.

Status also explains patterns that income alone cannot. People will defend a status — a professional title, a family name, a national identity — even at great material cost, because status is about recognition, not only reward. Revolutions are often driven as much by humiliated status as by economic deprivation.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

The Davis–Moore thesis has been sharply criticized for assuming that the most rewarded positions are the most important, and for ignoring how power shapes which positions are rewarded in the first place. Feminist scholars have shown that status systems devalue the work traditionally done by women — care, domestic labour, emotional labour — even when that work is essential. And the distinction between ascribed and achieved status has been questioned: in practice, supposedly achieved statuses are strongly shaped by the ascribed statuses of birth, race, and class.

Status remains a core concept. It now frames research on the gig economy, where workers hold ambiguous statuses — neither employee nor independent — with real consequences for their rights; on digital platforms, where followers, badges, and verification confer new forms of status; and on status inconsistency in societies where education has expanded faster than the jobs that reward it. The rank order of social life has not disappeared; it has only taken new forms.

## Status in Egalitarian Societies

Societies without institutional ranking of strata still distinguish among people. Age, gender, skill and kinship position carry differing expectations and esteem. Morton Fried, in *The Evolution of Political Society* (1967), characterised an egalitarian society as one that does not limit the number of valued positions: roughly as many people can gain prestige as are able to earn it. James Woodburn (1982) studied hunting and gathering peoples, including the Hadza of Tanzania, whose immediate-return economies, mobility and sharing obligations limit the means of building lasting dominance. Richard Lee described how the !Kung (Ju/'hoansi) of the Kalahari belittled a hunter's kill, a custom that checks boasting. Christopher Boehm argued that such equality is actively maintained, as group members use ridicule, avoidance and stronger sanctions against would-be dominators. Egalitarian therefore does not mean that status is absent, and equality among adult men in a given case need not imply equality between men and women or between generations.
