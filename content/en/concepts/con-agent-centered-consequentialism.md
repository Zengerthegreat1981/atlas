# Agent-Centered Consequentialism

## Meaning and Definition

Agent-centered consequentialism is a form of consequentialist ethics built on an idea that seems at first to contradict the essence of traditional consequentialism: that each moral agent has ends or constraints particular to himself, rather than general ends that are neutral among agents. Agent-neutral consequentialism holds that each person should maximize the overall good in the world regardless of who brings it about. Agent-centered consequentialism holds that each agent has a value function of his own, which weighs his actions and relationships asymmetrically relative to the actions and outcomes of others. Thus an agent may be bound not to kill an innocent person himself, even if his killing would prevent five other people from killing five other innocents, because his own value function gives greater weight to his being the direct perpetrator of harm.

## Historical and Philosophical Context

This trend arose in contemporary analytic philosophy as an attempt to adapt the computational structure of consequentialism so that it could accommodate moral intuitions previously considered the preserve of deontological ethics. A key text is Samuel Scheffler's *The Rejection of Consequentialism* (1982), which proposed an "agent-centered prerogative" permitting the agent not always to sacrifice his own interests for the sake of maximizing the general good (a hybrid view rather than a consequentialism proper). Amartya Sen ("Rights and Agency," 1982) argued that consequentialist evaluation can be made agent-relative, and later authors such as Douglas Portmore (*Commonsense Consequentialism*, 2011) developed agent-relative consequentialism in detail. The American philosopher Peter Railton's "Alienation, Consequentialism, and the Demands of Morality" (1984) addressed related worries. Much of this was a response to the critique that Bernard Williams leveled against consequentialism in *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (1973), namely that it undermines the agent's personal integrity. Consequentialism subjects all of his decisions to an external calculation that does not recognize the particularity of his commitments and personal projects.

## Principal Positions

There are two main patterns. The first is "agent-centered prerogatives," which permit the agent to weigh his own interests and those of the people he loves above the general good, without this being a duty for him. The second is "agent-centered restrictions," which prevent the agent from committing certain acts himself, even if committing them would produce better outcomes overall. Among the important discussants of such restrictions are Thomas Nagel, who analyzed agent-relative reasons in *The View from Nowhere* (1986), and Philippa Foot, in her essays on doing and allowing harm. This formulation attempts to offer a hybrid system that combines the computational flexibility of consequentialism with the strictness of moral boundaries that characterizes deontology.

## Objections and Criticism

The trend is objected to on the grounds that it forfeits the logical consistency that was the original attraction of consequentialism. If moral goodness is measured by how much of it is realized in the world, it is hard to see why an agent should be permitted to prefer an overall worse outcome merely because he is the one who brings it about. Some critics, such as Shelly Kagan in *The Limits of Morality* (1989), argue that the case for such prerogatives and restrictions is weak and that defenders offer no systematic explanation that justifies the difference between the position of the agent and that of the spectator.

## Illustrative Example

Suppose a physician could kill one healthy patient in order to take his organs and save five patients who need organ transplants. Agent-neutral consequentialism holds that saving five is better than saving one. A view with an agent-relative restriction holds that the physician must not be the killer, even if the overall outcome is worse; agent-relative consequentialism tries to capture this by building the agent's own act of killing into the evaluation of outcomes.
