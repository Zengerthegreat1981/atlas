# Ecstasy

## Definition

Ecstasy, in anthropology, is a state in which a person is felt to stand outside themselves — the Greek *ekstasis* literally means "standing outside" — typically in a ritual context, and typically involving an encounter with a god, a spirit, or a transformed condition of the soul. The ecstatic may speak in tongues, prophesy, heal, or journey to other worlds while the body remains behind. Ecstasy is closely related to trance, but the two are not identical: trance describes a broad family of altered states, while ecstasy in the classic literature refers specifically to the soul's departure from the body, most famously in shamanism. What counts as ecstasy, who may experience it, and what it authorises are culturally variable — but everywhere it is treated as a powerful, ambiguous, and socially consequential state.

## Origins and Development

The comparative study of ecstasy begins with Mircea Eliade's *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* (1964, English translation of the 1951 French original). Eliade argued that shamanism, found from Siberia to the Amazon, is defined precisely by ecstasy: the shaman is a specialist who enters a trance at will, sends the soul to the sky or the underworld, negotiates with spirits, and returns with knowledge or healing power. Eliade treated this as an archaic, universal technique of the sacred — a claim that made the book enormously influential and also drew sustained criticism for flattening the differences between very different traditions.

I. M. Lewis, in *Ecstatic Religion* (1971), recast the subject in social terms. He distinguished two broad patterns. Central, morally approved ecstasy — the shaman's or the prophet's — served the community and reinforced its values. Peripheral ecstasy, by contrast, was associated with people marginal to power: in the spirit-possession cults Lewis studied in Somalia and elsewhere, it was often women, the poor, or the oppressed who were seized by spirits, and their affliction gave them a voice they otherwise lacked. Ecstasy, in Lewis's account, could be a form of protest: the spirit spoke what the person could not say. His work made ecstasy a key concept for the anthropology of power, gender, and resistance.

Gilbert Rouget's *Music and Trance* (1985, English translation of the 1980 French original) added a crucial element: sound. Across the world, music — drumming, chanting, singing — is the principal technology for inducing and shaping ecstasy, and Rouget showed that the relationship is not mechanical. The same rhythm may produce ecstasy in one cultural setting and nothing in another; what matters is the learned association between particular sounds, states, and meanings. Erika Bourguignon's cross-cultural research mapped how widespread such states are: in a sample of 488 societies reported in a 1973 volume she edited, institutionalised forms of altered states of consciousness appeared in roughly ninety per cent of them. Her *Possession* (1976) examined spirit possession comparatively.

## Key Debates

The first debate concerns explanation. Is ecstasy a psychological universal with a neurobiological basis — a capacity of the human brain triggered by rhythmic stimulation, fasting, or psychoactive substances — or is it wholly constituted by culture? Felicitas Goodman, in *Ecstasy, Ritual, and Alternate Reality* (1988), argued for a universal religious physiology, proposing that specific postures combined with rhythmic sound reliably produced visionary experience. Most anthropologists now take a middle position: human beings share the capacity for such states, but what the states mean, how they are entered, and what may be done in them are learned. The same drumming that summons a god in one tradition may be mere entertainment in another.

A second debate concerns authenticity and authority. Who gets to be ecstatic, and whose ecstasy counts? In many societies, ecstasy is a privilege of specialists — shamans, priests, prophets — whose claims are tested by the community: the shaman who fails to heal loses standing. In others, ecstasy is open to anyone, and its democratisation can be socially disruptive, as when revival movements produce prophets who challenge established authority. The line between the inspired and the mad, the holy and the possessed, is drawn differently in different places, and it is often a matter of fierce dispute.

A third debate concerns the relationship between ecstasy and possession. In ecstasy, classically, the soul leaves the body; in possession, a spirit enters it. But ethnography shows the two often combine, and the distinction may reflect the observer's categories more than the experience itself. What matters in practice is the social career of the state: whether it heals or harms, whether it is sought or suffered, and whose interests it serves.

## Contemporary Relevance

Ecstasy remains central to the anthropology of religion, but it has also moved into new domains. The study of Pentecostalism — one of the fastest-growing forms of Christianity worldwide — has revived Lewis's questions about ecstasy and marginality, since glossolalia, prophecy, and healing are central to Pentecostal worship. The medical anthropology of altered states has examined how ecstasy is managed, pathologised, or treated in clinical settings, and the debate over psychedelic substances has reopened old questions about the relationship between chemically induced states and religious experience.

For anthropology, ecstasy is a reminder that human experience is not uniform. The boundaries of the self — where "I" ends and the world begins — are culturally drawn, and ecstasy is the moment when those boundaries are crossed. Studying it teaches us not only about religion, but about the variability of consciousness itself.
