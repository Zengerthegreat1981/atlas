# Qur'anic Interpretation: Tafsir and Ta'wil

**Tafsir** is the discipline of explaining the Qur'an's wording, meanings, occasions of revelation, and legal and linguistic implications. **Ta'wil** usually names a move from the immediate sense to a further or symbolic sense. Early authors did not keep the two words strictly apart. Al-Tabari's commentary is titled a work of *ta'wil* and is classed by later scholars as *tafsir*.

## Origins

Muslim tradition traces explanation of the Qur'an to the Prophet Muhammad, then to Companions such as 'Abd Allah ibn 'Abbas (d. 68 AH / 687 CE), later called "the interpreter of the Qur'an," and then to the Successors. As the community spread beyond Arabic-speaking cities, commentary became a teaching discipline. Early activity is associated with Mecca, Medina, Basra, and Kufa. Written commentaries survive from the second/eighth century, including the tafsir of Muqatil ibn Sulayman (d. 150 AH / 767 CE). The classical literature is cumulative. It does not begin with a single book.

## Major orientations

- **Tafsir bi-l-ma'thur (exegesis by transmission)** explains the Qur'an by the Qur'an, then by reports from the Prophet, Companions, and Successors. Abu Ja'far al-Tabari (d. 310 AH / 923 CE), in *Jami' al-bayan 'an ta'wil ay al-Qur'an*, produced the major early encyclopedic commentary that survives complete. He gathers reports and often prefers one reading on linguistic or legal grounds. The book is a principal source for transmitted exegesis. It is not the earliest commentary to survive. Muqatil's is earlier, and it is neither as full nor as central to later Sunni teaching.
- **Tafsir bi-l-ra'y (exegesis by reasoned judgment)** uses grammar, rhetoric, and theology alongside reports. The Mu'tazili al-Zamakhshari (d. 538 AH / 1144 CE), in *al-Kashshaf*, made linguistic analysis a vehicle for Mu'tazili doctrine. The Maliki al-Qurtubi (d. 671 AH / 1273 CE) organized *al-Jami' li-ahkam al-Qur'an* around legal rulings. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi (d. 606 AH / 1210 CE), in *Mafatih al-ghayb*, wrote a long theological commentary in the later Ash'ari tradition.
- **Allusive exegesis (*tafsir ishari*)** seeks an inner sense beyond the public linguistic meaning. Sufi works of this kind include Abu 'Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami's (d. 412 AH / 1021 CE) *Haqa'iq al-tafsir* and al-Qushayri's (d. 465 AH / 1072 CE) *Lata'if al-isharat*. Authors who accepted such readings generally required that they not cancel the established outward sense.

## Ambiguous verses

Qur'an 3:7 distinguishes firm verses from *mutashabihat*, often understood as ambiguous verses, including passages that seem to describe God in bodily terms. Hanbali and Zahiri authors tended to affirm the wording without asking how (*bila kayf*) and without figurative reinterpretation. Mu'tazili authors applied *ta'wil* in order to protect divine transcendence. Ash'ari doctrine was not of one piece. Early Ash'arism stayed close to affirmation without modality. Many later Ash'aris, including al-Razi, allowed a limited *ta'wil*. The disagreement is one of the lasting divisions in Islamic theology.

## Auxiliary studies, and modern commentary

Exegetes drew on studies later grouped as the sciences of the Qur'an (*'ulum al-Qur'an*): occasions of revelation (*asbab al-nuzul*), theories of abrogation (*naskh*), the canonical recitations (*qira'at*), and rare vocabulary (*gharib al-Qur'an*), for which pre-Islamic poetry was cited as linguistic evidence. These tools support interpretation. They do not settle disputes by themselves. Both the scope of abrogation and the reliability of particular "occasions" are contested.

Modern Arabic commentary added further programs. Muhammad 'Abduh's lectures were edited and continued by Muhammad Rashid Rida as *Tafsir al-Manar*, published from *al-Manar* in the early twentieth century. Rida carried the work past 'Abduh's death in 1905. It does not cover the whole Qur'an. Amin al-Khuli (d. 1966) called for a literary study of the Qur'an as an Arabic text. His student 'A'isha 'Abd al-Rahman, known as Bint al-Shati' (1913–1998), practiced that program in *al-Tafsir al-bayani li-l-Qur'an al-karim*. Thematic commentary (*tafsir mawdu'i*) and so-called scientific commentary (*tafsir 'ilmi*), which seeks contemporary natural science in the text, also circulated. What counts as a controlled reading, especially on social questions, remains disputed.
