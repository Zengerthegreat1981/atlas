# Qur'anic Interpretation: Tafsir and Ta'wil

**Tafsir** is the science of explaining the words and meanings of the noble Qur'an, the occasions of its revelation, and its linguistic and juristic implications. **Ta'wil** usually refers to extracting the deeper, inner or symbolic meaning of the text beyond its direct literal sense, although the two terms were sometimes used interchangeably by early exegetes such as al-Tabari.

## Origins

The Islamic tradition traces the beginning of tafsir to the Prophet Muhammad himself, as the first to explain the Qur'an to people, clarifying its meanings and the occasions of its revelation and resolving what they found difficult to understand. The task then passed to the Companions (such as Ibn Abbas, who was called "the interpreter of the Qur'an"), and then to the Successors. It developed as the Islamic state expanded and as Muslims who did not speak Arabic needed a detailed explanation of the text. Schools of exegesis arose in scholarly centers such as Mecca, Medina and Iraq.

## The major schools of exegesis

- **Tafsir bil-ma'thur (exegesis by transmission)**: explains the Qur'an by the Qur'an itself, then by the sayings of the Prophet and his Companions. Its most prominent representative is al-Tabari's (d. 923 CE) *The Comprehensive Clarification of the Interpretation of the Verses of the Qur'an* (*Jami' al-bayan 'an ta'wil ay al-Qur'an*), regarded as the earliest comprehensive commentary to survive in full and one of the most important sources of transmitted exegesis.
- **Tafsir bil-ra'y (exegesis by reasoned opinion)**: relies on intellectual and linguistic effort to derive meaning. It includes several orientations, from the rationalist Mu'tazili (such as al-Zamakhshari's *al-Kashshaf*) to the juristic (such as al-Qurtubi's commentary) and the philosophical.
- **Tafsir bil-isharah (allusive exegesis)**: a Sufi interpretation that seeks the symbolic inner meanings of the text behind its outward linguistic sense.

## The debate over exegesis and interpretation

The distinction between tafsir and ta'wil raised a central theological and juristic debate, especially about the **ambiguous verses** (*mutashabihat*) of the Qur'an, such as those describing divine attributes. Should one stop at the apparent meaning of the wording without interpretation, as the Hanbalis and the Zahiris held? Or may what seems to imply anthropomorphism be interpreted in a manner consistent with God's transcendence, as the Ash'aris and the Mu'tazilis held? This disagreement became one of the major lines of division among the great Islamic theological schools.

## Tools of the science of exegesis

The exegete relies on several tools accumulated over the centuries: the occasions of revelation (the historical circumstances of a verse's revelation), abrogation (*naskh*), knowledge of the multiple widely transmitted (*mutawatir*) recitations, and the science of rare Qur'anic words (*gharib al-Qur'an*), which draws on pre-Islamic poetry and classical Arabic to explain uncommon terms.

## Impact

The science of exegesis formed the core of the classical Islamic system of knowledge, and independent disciplines branched from it (the sciences of the Qur'an and the principles of jurisprudence). It continued to develop into the modern era with new exegetical orientations, such as literary exegesis (as in Amin al-Khuli and Aisha Abd al-Rahman, Bint al-Shati), thematic exegesis and scientific exegesis.

## Caveats

The disagreement over the criteria for distinguishing legitimate exegesis from arbitrary interpretation remains a live debate, particularly in reading verses related to contemporary social questions, where traditional and various reformist readings contend without a final decisive ruling.
