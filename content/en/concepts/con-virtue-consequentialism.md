# Virtue Consequentialism

## Meaning and Definition

Virtue consequentialism is a hybrid ethical theory that tries to combine the logic of consequentialism with the content of virtue ethics. Like utilitarianism, it evaluates in terms of consequences, but it gives the virtues a central place. Versions differ: some define the "good" to be promoted partly in terms of virtue or human flourishing, while others (such as Julia Driver's) keep an independent standard of good and define the virtues as the character traits that tend to promote it. In place of "Which act maximizes happiness?", such views ask which traits, or which acts, best promote virtue or flourishing.

## Echoes in Islamic Moral Thought

This hybrid conception comes close to the project of Miskawayh in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, where virtue is pursued not merely for its own sake, but because it leads to "happiness", which is the ultimate aim of the human being within the blended Greek-Islamic philosophical view of the cosmos. Miskawayh explicitly links the realization of the four virtues (wisdom, courage, temperance, and justice) with their practical fruit in the goodness of both the soul and society. For him, virtue is both a cause and a result of a state of real good, not merely a formal commitment to a rule. The concept of "public interest" (*maslaha*) in al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi, by which the goodness of an act is measured by the real good it brings to the individual and the community rather than by its formal conformity to a rule, also reflects a consequentialist orientation that intersects with the logic of this theory.

## Roots in Western Philosophy

Critics of virtue ethics, and defenders such as Rosalind Hursthouse in *On Virtue Ethics* (1999), have long debated whether it offers adequate guidance for action and for resolving conflicts between virtues. Julia Driver, in *Uneasy Virtue* (2001), proposed a consequentialist account on which a virtue is, roughly, a character trait that systematically produces more good than bad. This reverses the Aristotelian order of explanation, which ties virtue to practical wisdom and the rational mean rather than to outcome alone. Related hybrid proposals have been made by other philosophers, such as Michael Slote and Philip Pettit, while Philippa Foot's naturalism, which ties virtue to the flourishing of a kind of living thing, is a distinct, non-consequentialist position that is sometimes discussed alongside them.

## Main Objections

Traditional Aristotelians object that tying virtue to outcome empties virtue of its internal meaning as a state of character and turns it into an external measure of success. A person may act from a non-virtuous motive (such as luck or cold calculation) and produce a better outcome than a truly virtuous person who misjudged the situation. Does the first person's act count as morally better merely because of its outcome? The theory also faces the difficulty of measuring "human flourishing" quantitatively, as classical utilitarianism does with pleasure.

## Illustrative Example

Consider a hypothetical doctor who withholds the full seriousness of an elderly patient's illness because he judges that reassurance will improve the patient's final days. A virtue-consequentialist view assesses the act, and the trait behind it, by how much flourishing it promotes, not merely by whether the statement is true or false. Critics would respond that this overlooks honesty and respect for the patient's autonomy.
