# Stratification

## Definition

Social stratification is the hierarchical ordering of individuals and groups into ranked layers, or strata, according to their differential access to valued resources. In stratified societies, wealth, income, power, prestige, education, and health are not distributed randomly: they cluster together, so that advantages in one domain tend to reinforce advantages in others, while disadvantages accumulate in the same way. Stratification is therefore a property of whole societies, not merely of individuals. A person may be talented or industrious, but the stratum into which they are born shapes the schools they attend, the jobs open to them, the neighbourhoods they can afford, and even their life expectancy. Sociologists distinguish open systems, in which movement between strata is possible, from closed systems, such as caste or slavery, in which rank is fixed at birth. Most modern societies combine formal openness with substantial inherited advantage, a tension that has made stratification one of sociology's central and most contested topics.

## Origins and Classical Formulations

The concept took shape in the classical tradition. Karl Marx argued that the fundamental division in capitalist society is between those who own the means of production and those who must sell their labour power, and that this relation generates both material inequality and political conflict. Max Weber accepted the importance of economic class but insisted that stratification has three analytically distinct dimensions: class, defined by market position; status, defined by social honour and lifestyle; and party, defined by power within organisations. This multidimensional view remains influential because it explains why groups with similar incomes can enjoy very different prestige, and why political influence does not always track wealth. In the mid-twentieth century, Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore offered the functionalist theory of stratification, arguing that unequal rewards are necessary to motivate talented people to fill the most functionally important positions. Talcott Parsons incorporated stratification into a broader theory of the social system, treating rank as an expression of shared values about what society needs and rewards.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The central debate opposes functionalist and conflict accounts. Functionalists treat inequality as a by-product of the division of labour and a mechanism for allocating talent; conflict theorists, following Marx and Weber, treat it as the outcome of domination, in which powerful groups secure disproportionate rewards and then legitimise them. A second debate concerns how many dimensions matter. Weber's three dimensions were later joined by gender, race, and ethnicity, and by Kimberlé Crenshaw's concept of intersectionality, which holds that these axes do not simply add up but interact, so that, for example, the experience of a Black working-class woman cannot be understood by summing separate disadvantages. A third debate concerns measurement. Erik Olin Wright's class schema classifies positions by ownership, authority, and scarce skills, capturing the contradictory locations of managers and professionals who are simultaneously employees and exercisers of authority. Goldthorpe's occupational class schema, by contrast, classifies people by employment relations, and has underpinned decades of research on social mobility. A fourth question is whether achievement has displaced ascription: modern societies claim to reward merit, yet parental class remains one of the strongest predictors of educational attainment and earnings in most countries.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Stratification research remains urgent because inequality has widened in many countries since the 1980s. Thomas Piketty's analysis of long-run data showed that when the rate of return on capital exceeds the rate of economic growth, inherited wealth concentrates, undermining the meritocratic promise of open systems. Contemporary research extends the concept in new directions: global stratification compares life chances across nations, where country of birth is a major determinant of a person's expected income; studies of the digital divide show that unequal access to technology and digital skills creates a new axis of advantage; and research on the gig economy asks whether precarious, platform-mediated work is producing a new stratum with neither the security of employment nor the autonomy of self-employment. Critics of the concept argue that it can reify categories, treating classes as real groups when people may identify more with occupation, locality, or consumption. Others argue that stratification research has been too focused on paid work and too inattentive to unpaid care work, much of it done by women, which sustains the economy without being counted in it. Despite these critiques, stratification endures as the framework through which sociology connects individual biography to the structure of the whole society.
