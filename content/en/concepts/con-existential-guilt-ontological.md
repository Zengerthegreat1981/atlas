# Ontological Guilt

In Heidegger: a condition of indebtedness or lack (not an emotion) that does not arise from a specific moral wrong, but from the very structure of human existence: we are always incomplete and unable to realize all our possibilities at once. Heidegger treats it in *Being and Time* (1927), sections 54-60, within his analysis of conscience (*Gewissen*) and resoluteness (*Entschlossenheit*).

## Terminology

The German word *Schuld* means both guilt and debt, and Heidegger exploits this ambiguity to show that "being-guilty" (*Schuldigsein*) is prior to any particular obligation or fault. He defines *Dasein* as the "basis of a nullity" (*Nichtigkeit*). On one side, *Dasein* is thrown into an existence it did not choose and cannot fully master, including its own origin. On the other, it is a projection onto possibilities, choosing one and thereby letting the others go. Every choice is a negation of other possibilities, and this negativity belongs to the structure of existence itself.

## The Difference from Ordinary Moral Guilt

Moral guilt, tied to a specific act that can be corrected or apologized for, is fundamentally different from ontological guilt, for which there is no specific act that can be taken back, because it results from the structure of finite existence and not from an individual error. Heidegger argues that ordinary moral guilt is itself possible only because *Dasein* is already guilty in this basic sense. He explains the call of conscience as the summons of *Dasein* from the everyday "they-self" to its own authentic self, which invites it to accept this guilt instead of fleeing into the anonymity of "the They." Authentic resoluteness is then a "wanting to have a conscience," that is, taking over one's own guilt.

## The Link to Other Conceptions of Guilt

The concept extends the Atlas entry on guilt (con-guilt) with a distinctly ontological dimension that does not appear with the same clarity in other thinkers. Karl Jaspers, in *Die Schuldfrage* (1946), distinguished criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, the last being nearer to the Heideggerian sense. Paul Tillich, in *The Courage to Be* (1952), linked the anxiety of guilt and condemnation to one of three forms of existential anxiety. Martin Buber, in "Guilt and Guilt Feelings" (1957), distinguished real existential guilt, incurred in relation to others, from neurotic guilt feelings, and the existential psychiatrists, notably Rollo May in the volume *Existence* (1958), described ontological guilt as arising from separation from nature, from others, and from one's own potentials. Emmanuel Levinas located responsibility in the claim of the other and not in the structure of the self.

## Comparison with the Islamic Tradition

Islam has no doctrine of original sin: the verse "no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another" (Qur'an 6:164) rejects inherited guilt, so Heidegger's ontological guilt is not close to the doctrine of inherited sin. The nearest themes are the description of the human being as "most unjust and ignorant" (*zalum jahul*) in bearing the trust (33:72) and the creaturely neediness expressed in "you are the poor in need of God" (35:15). These approach structural deficiency, not legal guilt, and resemble Heidegger's idea in their verdict rather than in their history.

## Criticisms and Limits

Some critics object that the concept empties guilt of its moral content, since if all existence is guilty in the same way there is no room to distinguish the wicked from the upright. Others argue that Heidegger carries Christian theological categories into philosophical language while denying that he does so. The discussion remains open about whether this existential notion can be reconciled with concrete moral responsibility. Clinically, the idea should be handled with care: ontological guilt is a philosophical lens, not a diagnosis, and it should not be used to dismiss the real guilt or guilt feelings of clients.
