# Currency Systems

Currency systems are the organised ways in which a society produces, values, and circulates the things it uses as money. Anthropologists study currency not only as a medium of exchange but as a social institution: money carries meanings, marks social boundaries, and embodies relations of power. Shells, beads, salt, cattle, cloth, metal coins, paper notes, and digital balances have all served as currency, and the anthropology of money asks what makes any of them work — not the material they are made of, but the trust, authority, and social agreement that stand behind them.

## Definition

A currency is something that is widely accepted in exchange, serves as a measure of value, and can be stored for later use. In many societies, however, these functions are divided between different kinds of money. Paul Bohannan, studying the Tiv of Nigeria, described a system of "spheres of exchange" in which subsistence goods, prestige goods such as brass rods and cloth, and rights in people — above all rights in wives and children — circulated in separate spheres, and movement between them was restricted and morally charged. Money that could buy yams could not buy a wife. Other societies use special-purpose money: shell valuables that are displayed at feasts and stored as wealth but rarely used to buy food, or currencies that are appropriate for ritual payments but not for market trade. Such cases challenge the economic assumption that money is a neutral, universal measure. Money, anthropologists argue, is always marked by its social context: who may use it, for what, and with whom.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of money developed in opposition to two influential stories. The first, inherited from classical economics, held that money emerged naturally from barter as societies became more complex, and that modern money was simply a more efficient version of the same thing. The second, associated with early evolutionary anthropology, treated non-Western currencies — shells, beads, and the like — as primitive money, stages on the road to coinage. Ethnography undermined both. Work in the substantivist tradition, including that of George Dalton, editor of Tribal and Peasant Economies (1967), argued that money in stateless and peasant societies was embedded in social relations rather than standing apart from them. Jonathan Parry and Maurice Bloch's edited volume Money and the Morality of Exchange, published in 1989, showed that the moral meaning of money depends on how it moves between transactional orders: one concerned with short-term individual gain and another with the long-term reproduction of the social and cosmic order. Jane Guyer's study of Atlantic Africa, published in 2004, documented how people in Cameroon and Nigeria managed multiple currencies and scales of value, converting between them with skill and strategic intent.

## Key Debates

A central debate concerns whether the introduction of general-purpose money — state-issued coin and paper — transforms social relations or is absorbed into existing ones. One view holds that money is corrosive: it dissolves kinship obligations, commodifies what was once shared, and introduces calculation into relations once governed by trust. Another view holds that people domesticate money, giving it new meanings and uses: earmarking particular notes for particular purposes, using cash to sustain kinship obligations rather than escape them, and maintaining separate spheres even when a single currency is available. A second debate concerns the state. Modern currency rests on state authority, and the history of colonialism is in part a history of imposing colonial currencies, demanding taxes in cash, and thereby forcing people into wage labour and markets. A third debate concerns value itself: whether the value of money is a matter of scarcity and supply, as economists hold, or of the social relations and moral evaluations that money mediates. Anthropologists generally insist that both matter, but that the social and moral dimensions are the ones economics most often overlooks.

## Contemporary Relevance

Currency systems are changing rapidly, and the anthropology of money is changing with them. Mobile money in East Africa, where the M-Pesa system has become a central part of everyday economic life, shows how a new currency technology can be adopted, adapted, and woven into existing relations of kinship and obligation. Cryptocurrencies promise money without the state, and their rise and crises have become a subject of ethnographic study. At the same time, older forms persist: shell valuables still circulate in Papua New Guinea, cattle still serve as a store of value and a medium of marriage payments across Africa, and community currencies — local scrip, time banks, and mutual credit systems — are promoted as alternatives to national money. Debates over cashless economies, central bank digital currencies, and the financial exclusion of those without bank accounts all turn on questions that the anthropology of currency has long explored: who controls the means of payment, whose trust a currency depends on, and what happens to people who are left outside the system.
