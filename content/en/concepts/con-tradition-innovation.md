# Tradition and Innovation: How the Old Is Remade into the New

**Tradition and innovation** name a pair that anthropology treats not as opposites but as partners in a single process: every tradition was once an innovation, and every innovation succeeds only by finding a place within what already exists. Tradition is what is handed down — practices, beliefs, techniques, and institutions received from the past — while innovation is the introduction of something new, whether a tool, an idea, a ritual form, or a way of organising life. The anthropological question is not which of the two a society has, since all societies have both, but how they are related: how new things are adopted, resisted, disguised as old, or celebrated as breaks with the past, and how old things are kept alive precisely by being changed.

## Definition and Development

Edward Shils, in *Tradition* (1981), argued that tradition is the substance of every society: language, beliefs, practices, and institutions are all things handed down, and no society could exist without them. But Shils also insisted that traditions are never simply repeated. Each generation receives what the past offers and remakes it — adding, dropping, reinterpreting — so that tradition is always a process of selective transmission. Innovation, in this view, is not the enemy of tradition but its normal companion: a tradition that never changed would be a dead one, unable to meet new circumstances.

The study of innovation as a social process was transformed by Everett Rogers in *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962). Rogers showed that new ideas spread through populations in a regular pattern: a few innovators adopt first, then early adopters, then a majority, then laggards, with the speed of spread depending on the innovation's advantages, its compatibility with existing values, and whether it can be tried out on a small scale.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, in *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), showed that the boundary between tradition and innovation is often deliberately blurred. Many practices presented as ancient — the Highland kilt, royal ceremonies, the folk dances of new nations — were invented in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and then given an antique pedigree to legitimise new institutions. The point is not that these traditions are fake, but that their authority depends on being seen as old: innovation succeeds by wearing the clothes of tradition. Conversely, genuine old practices are often maintained by innovation, as when a ritual is filmed, a craft is sold online, or a language is taught through an app.

Anthony Giddens, in "Living in a Post-Traditional Society" (1994), argued that modern life has changed the balance between the two. In a post-traditional society, traditions no longer command unquestioned obedience; they must justify themselves, and individuals choose among them reflexively. But Giddens insisted that this does not mean the end of tradition: traditions persist, now as options that people actively choose, defend, and remake. Richard Dorson, in *Folklore and Fakelore* (1976), made a related point from the study of folklore: the line between genuine folk tradition and commercially manufactured "fakelore" is often hard to draw, because folk materials have always been borrowed, adapted, and sold.

## Ethnographic Examples

Fieldwork is full of tradition and innovation in combination. When steel axes reached Aboriginal communities in Australia, they were adopted rapidly — not because people abandoned their traditions but because the axes fitted existing values of efficiency and generosity, and were incorporated into established patterns of sharing. When Christianity spread in the Pacific, converts often recast old rituals in Christian form, keeping the social purposes of feasting and exchange while changing their theological language. In West Africa, new religious movements blend older spirit practices with Pentecostal Christianity, producing forms that are at once recognisably traditional and strikingly new.

The same pattern appears in material life. Potters in many societies adopt the wheel or new glazes while keeping old shapes and decorative motifs; weavers take up synthetic dyes while preserving patterns that mark clan and village identity. Musicians blend ancestral instruments with electric guitars and recording studios, creating genres that are claimed both as heritage and as contemporary art. In each case, innovation is not a break with the past but a way of carrying the past forward under new conditions — and the people doing the carrying are usually aware of both the continuity and the change.

## Contemporary Debates

Current scholarship debates the politics of the pair. Heritage policies, from UNESCO's lists of intangible cultural heritage to national folklore programmes, reward practices that can be presented as traditional and authentic, which can discourage innovation: performers may be expected to repeat an approved form rather than experiment. At the same time, communities use claims of tradition strategically, presenting new demands — for land, for recognition, for rights — in the language of ancestral custom, because that language carries moral weight. Innovation, too, is political: who gets to introduce something new, and whose innovations count as progress rather than as loss, depends on power.

A further debate concerns the pace of change. Globalisation, migration, and digital media accelerate the circulation of new ideas, and some observers predict the erosion of local traditions. Anthropologists reply that the evidence points the other way: people do not simply lose traditions when they encounter novelty; they select, combine, and reinvent, producing new traditions at least as fast as old ones fade.
