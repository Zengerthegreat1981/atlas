# Poetry

Poetry is language heightened by pattern — rhythm, metre, rhyme, imagery and parallelism — used to move, persuade, remember and honour. It is found in every documented society, from epic songs sung over many nights to short praise poems, laments, riddles and love lyrics. For anthropology, poetry is not a decorative extra to social life but a central medium through which people store history, negotiate status, court lovers, mourn the dead and challenge authority. Because poems are memorable, they carry knowledge across generations without writing; because they are performed, they are social events in which relationships are made and remade.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of poetry began with oral tradition. Milman Parry's fieldwork in the 1930s among Yugoslav epic singers, continued and published by his student Albert Lord in *The Singer of Tales* (1960), demonstrated that long epics such as the *Iliad* could be composed in performance, without writing, using a stock of formulaic phrases fitted to the metre. Lord's account showed that oral poetry is not a failed version of written literature but a distinct technology of composition: the singer thinks in formulas, and each performance is at once traditional and new.

Roman Jakobson, in "Linguistics and Poetics" (1960), gave the study of poetry a structural foundation. He defined the poetic function of language as the projection of the principle of equivalence from the axis of selection into the axis of combination: poetry makes pattern out of choice, so that sound and structure carry meaning alongside words. A line is poetic not because of its subject but because of how its sounds, rhythms and parallelisms are organised.

Walter Ong, in *Orality and Literacy* (1982), placed poetry in a media history. In primarily oral cultures, he argued, knowledge must be formulated in memorable, rhythmic, formulaic language — proverbs, genealogies, epic — or it is lost. Poetry is thus a mnemonic technology: the patterns that make a poem beautiful are the same patterns that make it retainable. Writing frees thought from the demands of memory, and with it poetry changes, becoming a private, silent art as well as a public performance.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns performance. Richard Bauman, in *Verbal Art as Performance* (1977), argued that poetry must be studied as an event, not a text: the audience, the occasion, the singer's voice and gestures, and the social stakes of the moment are part of the poem. A praise poem at a wedding does different work from the same words printed in a book. This performance turn shifted anthropology from collecting texts to ethnographies of speaking, in which the poem is inseparable from its social life.

A second debate concerns translation. Poetry's meaning lives in sound, rhythm and culturally specific imagery, all of which are damaged in translation. Anthropologists have responded with interlinear translation, attention to indigenous poetics — the local terms and theories people themselves use for good verse — and, increasingly, audio and video documentation that preserves performance rather than reducing it to words on a page.

A third debate concerns authorship and ownership. Oral epics are collectively owned traditions, yet individual singers are recognised as masters; praise poetry may be commissioned and paid for; in the contemporary world, indigenous poetry circulates on radio, recordings and social media, raising questions about copyright, cultural property and who profits from heritage. John Miles Foley, in *How to Read an Oral Poem* (2002), argued that oral poetry must be read on its own terms — its formulas and registers are meaningful to audiences who know the tradition, and the outsider's job is to learn that competence rather than judge the poem by literary standards.

A fourth dimension is poetry's political power. Praise poetry legitimates chiefs and presidents; satirical verse mocks them; laments protest deaths that official accounts would forget; love poetry negotiates marriages. In South Africa, praise poets (*izimbongi*) address rulers to their faces with criticism no ordinary subject could utter; in Umayyad-era Arabic poetry, the exchanges (*naqa'id*) between Jarir and al-Farazdaq were contests of honour. Poetry is thus a political institution, not merely an aesthetic one.

## Contemporary Relevance

Poetry remains socially central. Slam poetry and spoken word give voice to urban youth, migrants and protest movements; rap, itself a poetic form, is a global medium of commentary. In endangered-language communities, poetry is often the most valued verbal art, and its documentation is a priority of language revitalisation. Digital platforms have created new performance contexts: poems circulate as video, are remixed and commented on, and reach audiences their authors never meet. For anthropology, poetry continues to demonstrate a core lesson — that language is not only a means of communicating information but a way of acting in the world, and that the most patterned speech is often the most powerful.
