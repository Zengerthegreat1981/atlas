# Worldview

A worldview is the shared set of assumptions, values, and images through which a people understands the world and their place in it: what exists, how it is ordered, what is good, and how human beings should act. The term translates the German *Weltanschauung*, and in anthropology it names something broader than a belief system or a cosmology. A worldview includes ideas about nature, time, the person, and the supernatural, but also habits of perception and feeling — what counts as obvious, what is feared, what is hoped for. It is the background against which particular beliefs and practices make sense, and it is usually taken for granted by those who hold it.

## Origins and Development

The concept entered anthropology through the study of so-called primitive thought. Robert Redfield gave it its classic formulation in *The Primitive World and Its Transformations*, published in 1953. Redfield argued that every society has a worldview — a way of seeing the world as a whole — and that the great transformations of history, from folk society to urban civilisation, could be understood as changes in worldview: from a world that is small, sacred, and personally known to one that is large, secular, and impersonal. The folk worldview, in his account, was moral and concrete, organised around kinship, the land, and the gods; the urban worldview was abstract and technical, organised around markets, states, and science.

Clifford Geertz developed the idea in a different direction. In *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), he treated culture as a system of symbols and meanings, and worldview as the cognitive and existential side of that system — the picture of the way things are — paired with ethos, the moral and aesthetic side, the picture of how things ought to be. For Geertz, a worldview is not a private philosophy but a public system, expressed in rituals, myths, art, and everyday speech, and it can be read by the anthropologist like a text. His studies of religion in Java and Bali showed how a worldview is enacted: in a funeral, a cockfight, or a temple ceremony, people display and renew their understanding of the world.

Michael Kearney offered a more systematic account in *World View* (1984). He argued that worldviews vary along a small number of universal dimensions — how a people understands the self, the other, causality, time, and space — and that comparing worldviews along these dimensions allows anthropologists to describe cultural differences precisely rather than impressionistically. Kearney's work connected the concept to cognitive anthropology, which studies how people categorise, reason, and remember, and to the study of language, which shapes and reflects the categories through which the world is seen.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns whether worldviews are coherent systems or loose collections of ideas. Redfield and Geertz tended to present worldviews as integrated wholes, in which beliefs about nature, society, and the supernatural fit together. Critics argue that this overstates their coherence: people hold contradictory beliefs, borrow ideas from neighbours and from the media, and live with inconsistencies that never trouble them. A worldview, on this view, is less a system than a repertoire — a set of resources people draw on as circumstances require.

A second debate concerns change. If a worldview is the deep background of a culture, how does it change? The history of missions, colonisation, and schooling shows that worldviews can change rapidly and painfully: conversion to Christianity or Islam, for example, often involves a wholesale reordering of assumptions about the world. But ethnography also shows continuity: people adopt new beliefs while keeping old ones, blending them into a worldview that is neither traditional nor modern but both. Anthropologists now speak of worldviews in the plural, as layered and contested, rather than as single, uniform systems.

A third debate concerns the relationship between worldview and power. Whose worldview counts as the worldview of a society? Elites, priests, and men may hold different assumptions from the poor, the young, and women, and the dominant worldview may be imposed on those who do not share it. Feminist and postcolonial anthropologists have argued that the concept of a single worldview can hide these differences, and that the task is to study competing worldviews within a society and the struggles between them.

## Contemporary Relevance

Worldview remains a useful concept because it names the level at which the largest differences between peoples lie. Debates about climate change, for example, are not only about facts: they involve different worldviews about humanity's relationship to nature, about the future, and about responsibility. Debates about medicine involve different worldviews about the body, healing, and the causes of illness. Anthropologists use the concept to explain why people who share the same information can reach different conclusions, and why arguments that seem purely technical are often, underneath, arguments about what kind of world we live in. In a globalised world where people with different worldviews live side by side, understanding the concept is a practical necessity as well as an academic one.
