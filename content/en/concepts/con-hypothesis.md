# Hypothesis: A Testable Claim About Social Life

A **hypothesis** is a specific, testable claim about the relationship between two or more variables — a provisional answer to a research question, stated clearly enough that evidence could show it to be wrong. "Education increases earnings" is a hypothesis; "education is good" is not, because no observation could refute it. The hypothesis is the bridge between theory and research: theory suggests what might be true, the hypothesis states it precisely, and empirical investigation tests it. It is one of the central concepts of the scientific study of society.

## Definition and Development

A good hypothesis has several qualities. It must be **specific**, naming the variables and the direction of the relationship; **testable**, so that data can be gathered for or against it; and **falsifiable**, so that it could in principle be shown false. Hypotheses are usually derived from theory, but they can also emerge from observation: a researcher notices a pattern and formulates a claim to test. In quantitative research, the hypothesis is typically tested statistically, with a null hypothesis — the claim of no relationship — serving as the benchmark against which the evidence is judged.

The logic of hypothesis testing was shaped by the philosophy of science. The older view, associated with verification, held that evidence accumulates to confirm a theory. The falsificationist view reversed this: a theory is scientific precisely because it risks being wrong, and research progresses by eliminating false claims rather than by confirming true ones. Sociology adopted this logic unevenly — many sociologists work with theories too broad to test in a single study — but the hypothesis remains the standard unit of empirical testing in quantitative work.

## Key Contributions

**Karl Popper** (1902–1994) provided the philosophical foundation in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (first published in German in 1934; English edition 1959). Popper argued that a claim is scientific only if it is falsifiable — if some possible observation would count against it. A hypothesis that explains everything explains nothing. For Popper, science advances not by verifying theories but by subjecting bold conjectures to severe tests and discarding those that fail. His criterion gave sociologists a standard for judging whether a claim is a genuine hypothesis or merely a vague assertion.

**Robert K. Merton** (1910–2003) connected hypotheses to sociological theory in *Social Theory and Social Structure* (1949), arguing for theories of the middle range — neither grand systems nor isolated findings, but theories specific enough to generate testable hypotheses. Merton showed how hypotheses guide research design: they determine what data to collect, which variables to measure, and what would count as evidence. He also analyzed the unintended consequences of social action, a theme that itself generates hypotheses about why policies and institutions produce results nobody intended.

**Earl Babbie** (born 1938) presented hypothesis testing as part of the everyday logic of social research in *The Practice of Social Research* (first edition 1975). Babbie emphasized the distinction between the research hypothesis and the null hypothesis, the role of statistical significance, and the difference between a finding that is statistically significant and one that is substantively important. His textbook made the hypothesis accessible to students as a practical tool rather than an abstract philosophical doctrine.

**Gary King, Robert O. Keohane, and Sidney Verba** restated the logic of hypothesis testing for the social sciences in *Designing Social Inquiry* (1994), arguing that qualitative and quantitative research share a single logic of inference. A hypothesis, in their account, must be stated so that it can be evaluated against observable implications, and the best research design is the one that maximizes the leverage of the evidence — testing as much of the theory as possible with the data available.

## Examples

Hypotheses drive sociological research. Durkheim's claim that suicide rates vary with social integration generated testable predictions about religion, marital status, and economic crises. A contemporary researcher might hypothesize that neighbourhood diversity increases trust, that parental education predicts children's test scores, or that unemployment raises the risk of divorce — and then test each claim against survey or administrative data. In each case the hypothesis specifies the variables, the expected direction, and the population to which the claim applies.

Hypotheses also appear in qualitative research, though in a different form. An ethnographer may enter the field with a working hypothesis — that a factory's informal norms protect workers from management, for example — and revise it as observation accumulates. The hypothesis here guides attention rather than dictating a statistical test, but it performs the same function: it makes the researcher's expectations explicit and therefore open to correction.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics argue that the hypothesis-testing model fits some kinds of sociology better than others. Interpretive sociologists object that social meanings cannot be reduced to variables and that hypotheses can blind researchers to what people actually do. Others note that the pressure to find statistically significant results has produced selective reporting and a replication crisis, in which many published findings do not hold up when retested. The response has been pre-registration — stating hypotheses publicly before collecting data — and a renewed emphasis on falsifiability. The hypothesis remains central to sociological research, but its use is now more self-conscious: a good hypothesis is valued not because it is confirmed, but because it is clear enough to be wrong.
