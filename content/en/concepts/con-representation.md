# Political Representation

Political representation is the process by which some people stand for, speak for, and act on behalf of others in public life. It is the mechanism that allows large, complex societies to govern themselves without every citizen participating in every decision. A representative — whether an elected legislator, a delegate at a council, or a spokesperson for a community — is expected to carry the interests, views, and will of those they represent into arenas where binding decisions are made. Yet representation is never a simple mirror. It involves selection, interpretation, and judgement, and the question of what it means to represent someone well has been one of the most enduring problems of political thought.

## Definition

At its simplest, representation means making present something or someone who is absent. In politics, this absence is literal: citizens cannot all sit in the assembly, so others sit there in their place. Hanna Pitkin, in the classic study of the concept, distinguished several meanings of representation that still frame debate. Formal representation concerns the rules by which representatives are authorised and held accountable, above all through elections. Descriptive representation concerns resemblance: a body represents a population when it looks like it, sharing its social composition. Symbolic representation concerns the feelings and meanings a representative evokes, the sense that a leader embodies a nation or a cause. Substantive representation concerns action: whether the representative actually advances the interests of the represented. These dimensions can pull in different directions, so a parliament may be formally legitimate yet descriptively unrepresentative, or symbolically powerful yet substantively unresponsive. Anthropologists add a further caution: representation also operates in everyday political life, in chiefs who speak for villages, elders who negotiate on behalf of lineages, and brokers who mediate between communities and the state, often without any election at all.

## Origins and Development

The idea of representation has deep roots, but its modern form emerged with the rise of representative government in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. In England, the House of Commons gradually claimed to speak for the realm, and the Glorious Revolution of 1688 entrenched the principle that government required the consent of a representative body. In his address to the electors of Bristol in 1774, Edmund Burke defended the representative as a trustee who owes constituents his judgement rather than blind obedience to their instructions, a position that still anchors one side of the mandate debate. Across the Atlantic, the American founders designed a republic of representatives precisely because direct popular rule seemed impossible over a large territory; James Madison argued in Federalist No. 10, published in 1787, that an extended republic would refine public views through elected representatives and control the dangers of faction. In the nineteenth century, the expansion of suffrage transformed representation from a privilege of property owners into a claim of citizens, and the twentieth century added new demands: that legislatures reflect women, workers, and minorities, not merely aggregate their votes.

## Key Debates

The central debate concerns the representative's mandate. The delegate model holds that representatives should follow the expressed wishes of their constituents; the trustee model holds that they should exercise independent judgement in the constituents' best interests. Neither model survives contact with reality in pure form: representatives negotiate, compromise, and anticipate reactions, operating in what Pitkin called a relationship of responsiveness. A second debate concerns descriptive representation. Anne Phillips argued in 1995 that a politics of ideas alone is insufficient, and that the presence of marginalised groups in decision-making bodies changes what gets decided and how. A third debate concerns the limits of electoral representation. Anthropological studies of states in Africa, Melanesia, and Latin America show that people often relate to the state through intermediaries — big men, patrons, religious leaders — whose authority rests on personal ties rather than ballots. Representation, in this wider sense, is a social relationship of mediation, and elections are only one of its forms.

## Contemporary Relevance

Representation is under strain in many democracies today. Falling trust in parties and parliaments, the rise of populist leaders who claim to embody the people directly, and the growth of digital platforms that promise unmediated voice all challenge the representative model. At the same time, new forms of representation are emerging: citizens' assemblies chosen by lot, participatory budgeting, and online consultations that supplement elected bodies. Debates about quotas, reserved seats, and minority representation continue, and the question of who can legitimately speak for whom — raised sharply by Indigenous movements and feminist politics — remains unresolved. Representation endures because no large society can govern without it, but its forms are constantly renegotiated. Understanding it means asking, in every case, who is represented, by whom, through what mechanisms, and with what consequences for those whose voices are left out.
