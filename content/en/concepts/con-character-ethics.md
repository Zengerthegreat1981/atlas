# Character Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Character ethics is the approach that places the stable traits of the agent (his or her character and dispositions) at the center of moral evaluation, rather than evaluating isolated acts or their immediate consequences. The central question here is not "What is the right act in this situation?" but "What kind of person should I be?", on the basis that the right act is what naturally issues from the virtuous character. This approach is closely tied to virtue ethics in general, but it focuses specifically on how character is formed and how it remains stable over time.

## Foundations in Islamic Moral Science

The refinement of character and the reform of "morals" (*akhlaq*, in the sense of settled dispositions) formed the core of an entire tradition of Arabic writing known as the "science of morals" or "the literature of the soul" (*adab al-nafs*). Ibn Miskawayh, in his foundational work *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq wa Tathir al-A'raq* (The Refinement of Character and the Purification of Dispositions), defined character as "a state of the soul that calls it to its actions without deliberation or reflection." In other words, it is a stable disposition from which action issues spontaneously once it has been firmly established through habituation, similar to Aristotle's definition of virtue as a *hexis* (a stable acquired state). Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' Ulum al-Din*, distinguished between innate character and character acquired through striving (*mujahada*) and training, affirming that most virtues can be acquired through repeating virtuous acts until they become second nature. Ibn Hazm, in *al-Akhlaq wa al-Siyar fi Mudawat al-Nufus* (Character and Conduct in the Treatment of Souls), took a method closer to personal introspection, analyzing vices as psychological symptoms that must be diagnosed and treated as the body is treated.

## Historical Context in Western Philosophy

The deepest roots of character ethics go back to Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, where he distinguished between intellectual virtue (acquired through teaching) and moral virtue (acquired through habituation and repeated practice), holding that we become just by doing just acts and temperate by doing temperate acts. After interest in this approach declined in modernity in favor of Kantianism and utilitarianism, Elizabeth Anscombe revived it in her well-known essay "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958), criticizing the absence of an adequate theory of moral psychology in modern ethics. Alasdair MacIntyre, in *After Virtue* (1981), called for restoring a framework of virtue and character as an alternative to the fragmentation of contemporary ethics.

## Main Objections

The main objection to character ethics comes from experimental social psychology, known as "situationism." It holds that experiments such as Milgram's obedience study and Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment, though the latter has itself been heavily criticized methodologically, showed that individuals' behavior is affected by situational circumstances far more than by supposedly stable personality traits, which casts doubt on the existence of a stable "virtuous character" across situations (a line pressed by Gilbert Harman, 1999, and John Doris, *Lack of Character*, 2002). Proponents of virtue ethics reply that genuine virtue is rare and difficult to acquire in the first place, and that the scarcity of experimental examples of it does not negate its possibility.

## Further Primary Sources

Beyond the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle's *Eudemian Ethics* examines how character is formed through habit, and Theophrastus's *Characters* offers thirty short sketches of faulty types. David Hume gave character a central role in Book III of *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-1740) and in *An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751), treating actions as signs of durable qualities that observers approve or disapprove. In the twentieth century, Iris Murdoch's *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970) stressed the slow work of attention that shapes what a person sees before choosing. Philippa Foot's *Virtues and Vices* (1978) described virtues as correctives to common temptations and deficiencies. Nancy Sherman's *The Fabric of Character* (1989) reconstructs Aristotle's account of moral education, and Rosalind Hursthouse's *On Virtue Ethics* (1999) develops a neo-Aristotelian theory in which right action is defined by what a virtuous agent would characteristically do.
