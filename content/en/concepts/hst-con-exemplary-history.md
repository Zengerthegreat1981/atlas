# Exemplary History and Moral Instruction

In classical Greco-Roman culture, historiography was understood primarily as an ethical and pedagogical enterprise. Historical writing was not pursued as an abstract, value-neutral accumulation of past data, but as a dynamic reservoir of moral, political, and military examples (*exempla* in Latin, *paradeigmata* in Greek). Historians designed their works to provide tangible models of virtue (*virtus*, *arete*) for imitation and warnings of vice (*vitium*, *kakia*) to be shunned by statesmen, citizens, and future generations.

## Philosophical Foundations and Greek Precursors

The pedagogical conception of the past originated in the epic poetry of Homer, where heroes cited ancestral precedents to instruct and admonish their peers, as when Phoenix urged Achilles to emulate the ancient warriors in Book 9 of the *Iliad*. In fifth-century BCE Athens, rhetorical education under the Sophists and Attic orators such as Isocrates formalized the use of historical paradigms (*paradeigmata*) as deliberative arguments in civic assemblies and forensic courts.

While Thucydides rejected simplistic moralizing and sensationalism, he shared the foundational conviction that history possessed lasting instructive utility. In Book 1 (ch. 22), Thucydides famously declared that his *History of the Peloponnesian War* was composed not as a transient piece for applause, but as a "possession for all time" (*ktema es aiei*). Because human nature (*to anthropinon*) remains constant, Thucydides argued that future statesmen who study his work would understand the patterns governing political conflict, imperial overreach, and civic collapse.

In the Hellenistic period, Polybius made exemplary instruction the central justification for pragmatic political history (*pragmatike historia*). In the opening chapter of his *Histories*, Polybius asserted that the study of history is the soundest education and training for political life, because learning from the misfortunes of others is the most effective and least painful way to cultivate fortitude against the sudden reversals of fortune (*tyche*).

## The Roman Tradition: Mos Maiorum and Exempla

In Rome, exemplary history found its most fertile cultural ground. Roman aristocratic identity was deeply rooted in ancestral custom (*mos maiorum*). Noble households surrounded themselves with the physical presence of past exempla: wax portrait masks (*imagines maiorum*) of distinguished ancestors were displayed in the *atrium* and worn by actors during solemn funeral processions, accompanied by public funeral orations (*laudationes funebres*) enumerating the deceased's magistracies, triumphs, and civic deeds.

Cicero provided the classic formulation of this pedagogical vision in *De Oratore* (2.36), celebrating history with a famous sequence of epithets: "History is the witness of the times, the light of truth, the life of memory, the teacher of life (*magistra vitae*), and the messenger of antiquity." For Cicero and his contemporaries, past events provided an authoritative normative standard for present political conduct.

Livy articulated the programmatic manifesto of Roman exemplary historiography in the preface (*Praefatio* 10) to his monumental *Ab Urbe Condita*: "What chiefly makes the study of history wholesome and fruitful is that you behold the lessons of every kind of experience set forth as on a conspicuous monument; from these you may choose for yourself and for your state what to imitate, and mark for avoidance what is shameful in the inception and shameful in the issue." Livy populated his early books with iconic moral heroes—such as Lucretia's chastity, Horatius Cocles' courage at the bridge, Mucius Scaevola's endurance of pain, and Cincinnatus's humble relinquishment of dictatorial power—presenting them as archetypes of republican virtue designed to counteract contemporary moral decline.

## Systematic Collections and Imperial Historiography

During the early Roman Empire, exemplary historiography developed into specialized reference genres. Under Tiberius, Valerius Maximus composed *Factorum et dictorum memorabilium libri IX* (*Nine Books of Memorable Deeds and Sayings*). Valerius systematically cataloged hundreds of historical anecdotes from Roman and foreign history under thematic moral headings—such as piety, moderation, conjugal love, bravery, cruelty, and perfidy—providing orators and declaimers with ready-made moral illustrations.

Plutarch applied the exemplary method to comparative biography in his *Parallel Lives* (*Bioi Paralleloi*). Plutarch famously noted that he studied the lives of great men as though looking into a mirror (*katoptron*), in order to conform and adorn his own life to their virtues.

Even the critical and disillusioned Tacitus reaffirmed the exemplary purpose of historical writing. In *Annals* 3.65, Tacitus defined history's supreme duty: "to ensure that virtues are not forgotten, and that evil words and deeds fear the reprobation of posterity."
