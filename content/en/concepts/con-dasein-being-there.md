# Dasein: The Being for Whom Its Own Being Is an Issue

Heidegger's central term in *Being and Time* (Sein und Zeit, 1927): "Dasein" (Da-sein, literally "being-there") denotes the human being insofar as its very being is an issue for it. It understands that it exists, asks about the meaning of its being, and has possibilities from which it chooses. It is neither the Cartesian "I" nor Husserl's transcendental "subject," but a mode of being that appears only in its openness to the world.

## In the Arabic reception

Abd al-Rahman Badawi introduced the Arab reader to existential philosophy and Heidegger in *al-Zaman al-Wujudi* (Existential Time, 1943). Arab translators have rendered the German term in several ways, among them "al-wujud huna" (being-here) and "al-kaynuna" (a coinage from *kayna*, "to be"), and no single rendering is universally accepted. Fathi al-Meskini published the complete book under the title *al-Kaynuna wa al-Zaman* (Being and Time, 2012). The expression "being-there" recalls the expressions of the mystics about "presence," but this is a linguistic comparison that goes no further than analogy: Heidegger does not speak of divine presence, and in *Being and Time* he noted that the existential analysis of Dasein decides nothing for or against a possible relation to God.

## Philosophical meaning

By Dasein Heidegger aims to dismantle inherited dichotomies: subject and object, mind and body, consciousness and world. Dasein is always "being-in-the-world" (In-der-Welt-sein), dealing with things first as equipment at hand (Zuhandenheit), rather than as abstract objects to be contemplated (Vorhandenheit). Others are not things but share the world with it ("being-with," Mitsein). Therefore the states of Dasein, such as anxiety, care, and death, are defined as existential structures that precede psychological explanation. Hubert Dreyfus, in *Being-in-the-World: A Commentary on Heidegger's Being and Time, Division I* (1991), offered an influential reading of the first division of the book, which has also been taken up by philosophers of psychology and cognitive science.

## Existential structures

Dasein's being is characterized as **care** (*Sorge*), and its structures include being-in-the-world, **thrownness** (*Geworfenheit*: finding oneself already in a situation one did not choose), projection onto possibilities, and **falling** (*Verfallen*: absorption in the anonymous "they," *das Man*). Dasein can exist **authentically** (*eigentlich*), owning its possibilities, or inauthentically. Its relation to **death** (*Sein-zum-Tode*) is, for Heidegger, the ground of its finitude, and anxiety is the mood in which this finitude is disclosed. In Division II of *Being and Time*, the ontological meaning of care is shown to be **temporality** (*Zeitlichkeit*): Dasein is stretched between having-been, the present and the future, in contrast to the ordinary notion of time as a sequence of "nows."

## Psychological and therapeutic meaning

The Swiss psychiatrist Ludwig Binswanger brought the concept into the clinic. He developed his "Daseinsanalysis" in *Grundformen und Erkenntnis menschlichen Daseins* (Basic Forms and Knowledge of Human Existence, 1942), and described his patients' cases through their "worlds," meaning structures of lived time, space, and others. Medard Boss accompanied Heidegger in the Zollikon seminars (1959-1969), and then, in *Grundriss der Medizin und der Psychologie* (Outline of Medicine and Psychology, 1971), developed a formulation closer to Heidegger's thought, treating illness as a disturbance in the human being's openness to the world rather than a fault in a "psychic apparatus." Both approaches have been used to describe the experience of psychosis and anxiety; Binswanger's cases were phenomenological descriptions, not controlled treatments, and the clinical impact of Daseinsanalysis (including the work of Boss's colleague Gion Condrau) has been narrower than that of other currents. These are historical descriptions, not treatment recommendations.

## What distinguishes it from neighboring concepts

Dasein differs from the "ego" in psychoanalysis: Freud made the ego a structure mediating between the id and the superego, whereas Dasein is an inseparable mode of being. It differs from the "Self" in Jung, which is the central psychic whole, and from "the human being" in the anthropological sense, because Heidegger confines the question to the ontological structure and rejects having his analysis treated as a "philosophical anthropology."

## Limits of the concept

Sartre and Levinas faulted Heidegger for neglecting the ethical dimension and the relation to the face of the other. Daseinsanalysis has been criticized for giving little weight to diagnosis and to individual history. The clinical application of the concept remains contested because its dense philosophical language does not easily yield measurable therapeutic procedures. In addition, Heidegger's membership in the Nazi Party in 1933 keeps alive the debate about the relationship between his philosophy and politics.
