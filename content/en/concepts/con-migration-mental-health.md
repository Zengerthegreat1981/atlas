# Migration & Mental Health

## Definition

This concept studies the relationship between migration, asylum, and mental health. It asks whether relocation increases the risk of disorder, how the effects vary between economic migrants, refugees, and asylum seekers, and what roles cultural loss, discrimination, post-arrival pressures, and pre-departure trauma play.

## Longing and Estrangement in Arabic Literature

Arabic literature knew the theme of estrangement and longing for homelands early on. It includes the treatise *Longing for Homelands* (al-Hanin ila al-Awtan), traditionally attributed to al-Jahiz (d. 255 AH / 869 CE), which gathered sayings and poetry on a person's yearning for their homeland. Johannes Hofer, a Swiss medical student, coined the word *nostalgia* in his 1688 dissertation to describe the homesickness of soldiers and others far from home. This is not a modern diagnosis, but it shows that the impact of uprooting on emotional life has long been known.

## Modern Formulations

In 1932 Ødegaard published "Emigration and Insanity" in *Acta Psychiatrica et Neurologica Scandinavica*. He found higher rates of psychiatric hospital admission among Norwegian immigrants in Minnesota, which he explained by patients' selection for migration and by the effect of uprooting. In 1997 John Berry, in *Applied Psychology*, presented a model of acculturation based on four strategies (integration, assimilation, separation, and marginalization). Integration is often associated with better adaptation where the receiving society allows cultural maintenance and participation; it is not uniformly "best" in every setting. In 1991 Eisenbruch, in *Social Science & Medicine*, proposed the concept of "cultural bereavement" as a complement to, and a caution about, the diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder for the suffering of Southeast Asian (notably Cambodian) refugees, to name the loss of homeland, symbols, and relationships. Bhugra and Becker, in *World Psychiatry* in 2005, extended the concept to cultural identity. Fanon's theory also addresses the experience of uprooting and discrimination (see the entry on Fanon).

## Evidence from Studies

Cantor-Graae and Selten, in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 2005, conducted a meta-analysis. They found that the risk of schizophrenia was about 2.7 times higher among first-generation migrants and about 4.5 times higher among second-generation migrants (compared with the native-born), and that the risk was higher among migrants from developing countries and among Black migrants. This suggested the role of social adversity and discrimination over selection. Bhugra, in *Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica* in 2004, reviewed migration and mental health, emphasizing that post-migration stressors (unemployment, housing, discrimination, isolation) contribute more than is assumed. Fazel, Wheeler, and Danesh, in *The Lancet* in 2005, reviewed studies of refugees resettled in Western countries, covering about 7,000 people, and estimated that about 9% had post-traumatic stress disorder and about 5% had major depression. In *JAMA* in 2009, Steel and colleagues reported that exposure to torture and other potentially traumatic events was associated with higher rates of post-traumatic stress disorder and depression among populations exposed to mass conflict and displacement.

## Arab Refugees

Wars in Syria, Iraq, Palestine, and Sudan have caused widespread displacement and asylum, and refugee mental health has become part of the humanitarian response. Assessment requires using the patient's language and the presence of a cultural mediator, and an understanding that distress may be expressed in physical language (see the entry on cultural competence). Reception conditions vary. Camps, unstable residence, and lack of the right to work are post-migration factors that may contribute substantially to distress, alongside past trauma.

## Limits

Studies use different definitions of a migrant, and diagnostic tools that may not apply culturally, and they sometimes confuse trauma with social suffering. Critics of the "trauma model" warn against excessively medicalizing political suffering (see the entry on mental illness in non-Western contexts). Migration is not in itself a disorder, and many migrants show clear resilience.
