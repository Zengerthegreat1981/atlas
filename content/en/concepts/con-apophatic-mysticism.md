# Apophatic Theology and the Negative Way

A philosophical-theological tradition that treats the divinity as transcendent, exceeding all the categories of human language and understanding. It cannot be described affirmatively (*kataphatic*), but only by negating every limited attribute from it (*apophatic*, from the Greek *apophasis*, meaning "denial" or "negation"): God is not this, and not that, until every limited conceptual image of him is removed. This negative way is contrasted with the affirmative (kataphatic) way, which describes the divinity through positive attributes such as goodness, wisdom and power.

## Historical Origin

The most influential source of this tradition within Christianity is the author known as "Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite", a Greek-writing author, probably Syrian, active in the late fifth or early sixth century CE, whose writings were mistakenly attributed to an Athenian converted by the Apostle Paul, and so for centuries carried an authority close to apostolic. In his treatise *Mystical Theology*, Pseudo-Dionysius presents affirmative theology as a descent from the most general divine names to particular images, while apophatic theology moves in the opposite direction: it ascends through the negation of limited attributes toward the "divine darkness" that surpasses light, in which the intellect is united with what lies beyond all knowing.

## Intellectual Structure

- **The interplay of affirmation and negation**: Pseudo-Dionysius holds that the two ways are both necessary: affirmation (*kataphasis*) says something about God through his divine names (the Good, Being, Life), while negation (*apophasis*) denies him every determination because he exceeds every name. In his account, apophatic theology is not mere ignorance but a going beyond conceptual knowledge ("unknowing") toward a union that surpasses the intellect.
- **Deification (*theosis*)**: In the Eastern Orthodox tradition, apophatic theology is linked to the path of "deification", understood as human participation in the divine life, and to the Hesychast emphasis on the uncreated divine light, while the divine essence is held to remain unknowable.
- **Contemporary hermeneutical debate**: Scholars such as Denys Turner and Bernard McGinn caution against reading Pseudo-Dionysius's ancient texts through a modern lens that assumes they describe a subjective "mystical experience" in the sense defined by contemporary psychology. In their view, the classical apophatic tradition is a negative dialectic in the structure of language and knowledge, not a description of an exceptional emotional state.

## Extension Across Religious Traditions

- **Christianity**: Pseudo-Dionysius influenced Western thought through the Latin translations of John Scottus Eriugena in the ninth century, and reached its height in the monastic theology of the English author of *The Cloud of Unknowing* in the fourteenth century, and in Byzantine Hesychasm.
- **Islam**: In Islamic mysticism, a partial parallel is found in the emphasis on divine transcendence (*tanzih*) and the denial of resemblance (*tashbih*) to creatures in some theological and Sufi currents.
- **Judaism**: Some currents of Kabbalah speak of *Ein Sof* (the Infinite) as beyond all description.
- **Buddhism**: It is sometimes compared with the concept of *shunyata* (emptiness) in the philosophy of Nagarjuna, although the essential difference is that Buddhism does not presuppose a personal divine existence that is denied description.

## Limitation

- Some scholars object to the quick comparison between Christian apophatic theology, Islamic *tanzih*, and Buddhist emptiness, because of the difference in the metaphysical framework underlying each tradition (whether or not there exists a personal God).
- The debate over the true identity of "Pseudo-Dionysius" has remained unresolved despite more than a century of philological research.
