# Bilateral Kinship

Bilateral kinship is a system in which a person traces descent and reckons kinship through both parents — mother and father alike — rather than through one line only. In such systems, maternal and paternal relatives are both recognised as kin, inheritance may pass through either side or among children of both sexes, and there are no enduring, corporate descent groups such as lineages or clans. Marriage links two kindreds without merging them into one descent group. Bilateral kinship is the dominant pattern in much of Europe, the Americas, Southeast Asia, and many cities elsewhere, and it is the system most readers of this entry will recognise from their own family lives. States often register birth, citizenship, and property through rules that assume parentage on both sides even when they do not use the anthropological term. Anthropologists study it to understand how kinship works without lineages, and how families are organised around networks of relatives rather than bounded groups.

## Definition

Kinship systems are usually classified by how descent is traced. In unilineal systems, descent passes through one sex only: patrilineal systems trace it through fathers, matrilineal systems through mothers, and membership in a descent group is fixed at birth. In bilateral systems, by contrast, each person stands at the centre of a personal network of kin — parents, siblings, grandparents, aunts, uncles, and cousins on both sides — and this network is different for every individual. Siblings share most of the same relatives, but cousins do not: one person's maternal cousins are usually not, by descent, kin to that person's paternal cousins. George Peter Murdock, in his comparative study of 1949, called such networks "kindreds" to distinguish them from corporate descent groups. Because kindreds overlap and shift with each marriage, bilateral societies typically lack the large, enduring kin groups that organise politics, landholding, and ritual in unilineal societies. Instead, the key unit is often the nuclear or extended family, and kinship ties are activated selectively — for help, loans, funerals, inheritance, or care — rather than being automatic memberships. Kindreds rarely hold land or office as one body.

## Origins and Development

From Lewis Henry Morgan onward, nineteenth-century kinship study paid close attention to unilineal clans, partly because the classic ethnographies were conducted in Africa, Native North America, Oceania, and Aboriginal Australia, where lineages and clans often organised politics and ritual. Bilateral arrangements, close to the family patterns of many ethnographers, were long treated as the unmarked background rather than as a system worthy of study. Claude Lévi-Strauss, in 1949, shifted attention by arguing that kinship is fundamentally about alliance — the exchange of spouses between groups — rather than about descent alone, a framework that applied to bilateral as well as unilineal systems. In the 1960s, David Schneider's study of American kinship challenged the assumption that kinship everywhere rests on blood and shared substance; he showed that in the United States, kinship is defined by a cultural code that combines shared substance ("blood") with a code for conduct, centred on what he called diffuse, enduring solidarity. Robin Fox's synthesis of 1967 brought bilateral systems into the comparative mainstream. More recently, Marilyn Strathern and others have shown that in Melanesia, kinship is actively produced through feeding, care, and exchange, and that bilateral reckoning can coexist with strong ideas about gendered contributions to personhood.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns whether bilateral kinship is "simpler" than unilineal kinship. Earlier theorists treated the absence of lineages as a lack; later scholars argued that bilateral systems are not failed lineages but a different way of organising social life, one that is flexible, individual-centred, and well suited to mobile, commercial societies. A related argument is terminological. "Bilateral" names tracing kin through both parents. "Cognatic" is often used for that broad class, while "ambilineal" names a different practice in which a person chooses one parental group, as in parts of Polynesia. Collapsing the terms hides differences in land rights and political allegiance. A second debate concerns inheritance: in bilateral systems, property is often divided among all children, which can fragment holdings across generations — a process that has shaped the history of peasant Europe. A third debate concerns the new reproductive technologies and new family forms of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries: surrogacy, donor conception, adoption, and same-sex parenthood have all raised the question of what makes someone kin when biology, law, and care point in different directions. Finally, feminist anthropologists have argued that bilateral kinship, by recognising both sides of the family, may offer women more scope than patrilineal systems — though the recognition of maternal kin does not by itself guarantee equality. Care, child-rearing, and the work of keeping in touch often fall unevenly, so a formally symmetrical kindred can still privilege one line in practice. Folk models that equate kinship with "blood" do not by themselves settle cases in which genetic, legal, and everyday parenthood come apart.

## Contemporary Relevance

Bilateral kinship is the system within which most of the world's urban and industrial populations now live, and its study is increasingly relevant as families change. Rising rates of divorce and remarriage create blended families in which children have kin on many sides; migration scatters kindreds across continents, maintained through remittances, phone calls, and video links; and legal debates over inheritance, custody, and the definition of family turn on bilateral assumptions that are rarely made explicit. The anthropology of kinship has also been transformed by the "new kinship studies," which treat kinship as something people make through everyday practices of care rather than something given by birth. Understanding bilateral kinship is thus not only a matter of classifying societies: it is a way of understanding how people build the relationships that sustain them.
