# Property

Property is the set of rights that people hold over things — land, animals, tools, houses, knowledge — and, more importantly, the set of relationships between people that those rights create. To own something is not simply to hold it in your hand; it is to be recognised by others as having the right to use it, to exclude others from it, to give it away, or to sell it. Anthropology studies property comparatively, because what counts as ownable, who can own it, and what ownership means vary enormously: a plot of land may be owned by an individual, a family, a clan, or a spirit, and the same object may be owned in different ways at different times.

## Definition

Anthropologists understand property as a bundle of rights rather than a single thing. The bundle can be divided: one person may hold the right to farm a field, another the right to graze animals on it after the harvest, a third the right to the fruit of its trees. In many societies these rights are layered and overlapping, so that ownership is less like a fence around a plot and more like a web of claims. This is why the English word "property" is misleading: it suggests a simple relationship between a person and a thing, when property is really a relationship between people about things.

A classic distinction is between private property, held by individuals or families; common property, held by a group with rules about its use; and open access, where no one holds rights at all. Elinor Ostrom's research on common-pool resources showed that communities can manage shared forests, fisheries, and irrigation systems sustainably for centuries without privatisation or state control.

## Origins and Development

The modern theory of property begins with John Locke's *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), which argued that a person acquires property by mixing their labour with nature — by tilling a field or gathering acorns — and that government exists to protect property. Locke's theory became the foundation of liberal ideas about ownership, and it was used to justify the enclosure of common land in England and the appropriation of indigenous land in the Americas, on the grounds that land not improved by labour was waste.

Henry Maine's *Ancient Law* (1861) offered a historical alternative, arguing that early societies knew property as a family or corporate possession, and that individual ownership developed only gradually, as societies moved "from status to contract." Lewis Henry Morgan, in *Ancient Society* (1877), linked the development of property to the development of the family and the state.

In the twentieth century, ethnographers documented property systems that confounded Western assumptions. Among the Trobriand Islanders, garden land was held by matrilineal subclans, and men gave much of their yam harvest to their sisters' households; among the Nuer of Sudan, cattle were owned by individuals but loaned, inherited, and sacrificed in ways that made ownership a matter of kinship and politics. Property, it turned out, was inseparable from social organisation.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One major debate concerns the relationship between property and personhood. Marilyn Strathern, in *The Gender of the Gift* (1988), argued that in Melanesia people and things are not sharply separated: a gift carries part of the giver, and a person is composed of the relationships that make them. Property in such a context is not the extension of an autonomous individual over things, but a way of acting upon relationships. C. B. Macpherson, in the essays collected in *Property* (1978), made a related point from political theory: property is not a thing but a right, and rights are always social and political.

A second debate concerns land. In many parts of the world, land is not owned in the Western sense at all: it is held in trust for ancestors and descendants, it cannot be sold, and its use is governed by custom. Colonial and postcolonial states have repeatedly tried to convert such systems into individual title, often with disastrous results — dispossession, conflict, and the loss of livelihoods. Research on post-socialist Eastern Europe, including work in *Property Relations* (1998), edited by Chris Hann, showed that the privatisation of land after the fall of communism often produced not efficiency but confusion, as people found that the new titles did not match the old webs of use and obligation.

A third dimension is intellectual property. Who owns a song, a story, a seed, or a medicine? Indigenous communities have argued that their knowledge is collective property, not to be patented by corporations, and international law has begun, slowly, to recognise these claims. Property, once a question about land and cattle, is now a question about culture itself.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Property is at the centre of contemporary struggles: over land in Africa and Latin America, over housing in the world's cities, over water, forests, and minerals, and over the data that companies collect from our lives. The anthropology of property offers a crucial corrective to the assumption that private, individual, alienable ownership is natural or inevitable. It shows that human beings have owned things in many different ways, and that the choice of a property system is a political choice, with winners and losers.
