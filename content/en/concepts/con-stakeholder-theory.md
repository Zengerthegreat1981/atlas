# Stakeholder Theory

## Meaning and Definition

Stakeholder theory is a theory in business ethics and corporate management. It holds that a company should not be run solely to serve shareholders by maximizing profit, but to serve the interests of all "stakeholders" affected by its activity: employees, customers, suppliers, the surrounding community, and the environment, alongside shareholders. The theory stands in direct opposition to the thesis of the economist Milton Friedman that "the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits" (New York Times Magazine, 1970), while conforming to the basic rules of society.

## Roots in Islamic Jurisprudence on Shared Interests

Islamic jurisprudence in the domain of transactions carries an original orientation that parallels the spirit of this theory, though it does not express it in modern terms. The Prophetic maxim "no harm and no reciprocating harm" obliges the owner of property to consider the effect of his conduct on neighbors and partners, not on himself alone. Jurists built an entire section on property-related rights upon it, such as the right of neighborhood and the prohibition of harm in the use of shared water and roads. Islam likewise imposed clear rights on the worker in his relationship with the employer, from the Prophet's saying "give the hired worker his wage before his sweat dries" to the principle that a worker should not be assigned tasks beyond his capacity. In *al-Hisba fi al-Islam*, Ibn Taymiyya set out a broad principle of market oversight to protect consumers from fraud and monopoly, holding that the merchant is accountable to society and not to his private profit alone. The institution of the *waqf* (endowment) is also an institutional embodiment of the idea of distributing the returns of wealth among multiple categories of beneficiaries beyond the original owner of the capital.

## The Modern Origin: Freeman

The American Edward Freeman formulated the theory in its contemporary form in his book *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach* (1984). He offered a definition that became a reference point: a stakeholder is "any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives." Freeman argued that viewing the firm as a network of reciprocal relationships among these parties, rather than merely as a tool for maximizing shareholder wealth, is the more accurate understanding both economically and ethically. Neglecting the interests of an employee or a customer over the long term harms even the shareholder's own interests.

## Applications and Objections

The theory has been applied in developing standards of corporate social responsibility (CSR) and sustainability reporting. Some corporate-governance rules, such as section 172 of the UK Companies Act 2006, ask directors to have regard to employees, the community and the environment as well as members' interests. Critics in the Friedmanite tradition object that a multiplicity of parties to be satisfied deprives management of a clear standard of accountability: for what is the manager to be held accountable if the interests of the parties conflict? Freeman's supporters reply that maximizing profit alone is a misleading criterion, one that tempts firms to sacrifice long-term interests for immediate gains. An illustrative example is a company's decision to close a polluting factory despite its profitability, for the benefit of the residents of the area. This is a direct application of stakeholder logic against the logic of pure profit maximization.
