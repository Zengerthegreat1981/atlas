# Motivation

Motivation refers to the processes that start, direct and sustain goal-directed behaviour. It addresses why people do what they do, why they choose one activity over another, and why they persist or give up. Because motivation is inferred from behaviour and self-report rather than observed directly, theories differ widely in what they treat as its core.

## Drive and homeostasis

Early twentieth-century theories borrowed from biology. Clark Hull's drive-reduction theory proposed that physiological needs such as hunger create tension, or drive, which motivates behaviour that reduces it; reinforcement was understood as drive reduction. The theory explained basic needs well but struggled with behaviour that seems to increase stimulation, such as exploration and play. Studies showing that animals and people seek novelty and manipulate objects without any obvious bodily need led researchers to propose additional motives, such as curiosity.

## Incentives and arousal

Incentive theories shift attention from internal states to external goals: behaviour is pulled by rewards and avoided penalties, an idea closely connected with operant conditioning. Arousal theories propose that people seek an optimal level of stimulation, and the Yerkes-Dodson law, from 1908, is often cited to describe a relation between arousal and performance in which moderate levels favour performance on many tasks.

## Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

Intrinsic motivation means doing an activity for its own inherent satisfaction, whereas extrinsic motivation means doing it for separable outcomes. Edward Deci and Richard Ryan developed self-determination theory, which proposes three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence and relatedness. Environments that support these needs tend to foster more self-determined forms of motivation and wellbeing. Related experiments, including Deci's studies in the early 1970s, suggested that rewards could reduce intrinsic interest under some conditions, which is often called the undermining or overjustification effect. A later meta-analysis by Deci, Koestner and Ryan reached similar conclusions for tangible, expected rewards, though critics argued that the effect depends on how rewards are framed and delivered, and that it is less consistent than sometimes presented.

## Expectancy, goals and self-belief

Expectancy-value theories, including Victor Vroom's work on workplace motivation and John Atkinson's on achievement, hold that effort depends on the expectation of success and on the value placed on the outcome. Edwin Locke and Gary Latham's goal-setting theory found, across many studies, that specific and challenging goals generally lead to better performance than vague goals such as "do your best", provided people accept them and receive feedback. Albert Bandura's concept of self-efficacy, a person's belief in their capacity to carry out a course of action, has also been linked to effort and persistence.

## The hierarchy of needs

Abraham Maslow proposed in 1943 that human needs are arranged in a hierarchy, from physiological and safety needs up to love, esteem and self-actualization, and that higher needs become prominent as lower ones are met. The idea is influential in education, management and popular culture, and it has been valuable in drawing attention to growth motives.

## Critiques and debates

- **Maslow's hierarchy.** Research has found little support for the rigid ordering of needs; people pursue several needs at once, and cross-cultural work suggests the order varies. The pyramid image common in textbooks was not drawn by Maslow himself. Critics also note that his data on self-actualised people came from a small and selected group of examples.
- **Undermining effect.** Whether rewards weaken intrinsic motivation, and when, is still debated. Reward effects depend on whether the reward is expected, contingent and perceived as controlling or as informative.
- **Conscious and unconscious.** Many accounts emphasise explicit goals, but researchers also study implicit motives, which people may be unable to report, and habits that operate with little conscious intent.
- **Cultural assumptions.** Autonomy and individual achievement carry different weight in different societies, and critics argue that some theories reflect the values of the Western societies in which most research was conducted.
- **Evolutionary and biological accounts.** Neuroscience links motivation to dopaminergic reward systems, and often distinguishes wanting from liking, but a simple "pleasure centre" account is now regarded as too crude.
- **Applications.** Incentive schemes in schools and workplaces can backfire when they narrow attention to what is measured, which is a reason for caution when applying laboratory findings.

## Why it matters

Understanding motivation informs education, workplace design, health behaviour change and clinical work with conditions such as depression, where motivation can be severely disrupted. Most contemporary accounts combine several mechanisms rather than relying on a single master motive.
