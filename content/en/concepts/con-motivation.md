# Motivation

Motivation encompasses the psychological and physiological processes that initiate, direct, energize, and sustain goal-directed behavior. It addresses the fundamental inquiries of behavioral science: why organisms choose specific actions, with what intensity they pursue them, and why they persist or surrender in the face of obstacles. Because motivation cannot be observed directly, psychology evaluates it through behavioral vigor, persistence, choice latency, physiological indices, and self-report.

## Drive-Reduction and Homeostatic Theories

Early twentieth-century biological models conceptualized motivation as tension-reduction. Introduced by Robert Woodworth (1918) and formalized mathematically by Clark Hull (1943) in his mathematico-deductive system, **drive-reduction theory** posited that biological deprivations (such as food or water deficits) disrupt homeostasis (Cannon, 1932), generating an internal state of physiological tension known as **drive** ($D$). Behaviors that reduce this drive are reinforced through habit formation ($sHr$).

While drive-reduction explained primary homeostatic motives, it proved unable to explain exploratory behavior, curiosity, sensation-seeking, and play, where organisms voluntarily increase physiological arousal and seek novel stimulation. The **Yerkes-Dodson Law** (1908) demonstrated an inverted-U relationship between physiological arousal and performance, establishing that moderate levels of arousal optimize performance across diverse tasks.

## Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivation: Self-Determination Theory

Edward L. Deci and Richard M. Ryan (1985, 2000) transformed the study of human motivation by formulating **Self-Determination Theory (SDT)**. SDT differentiates between intrinsic motivation (engaging in an activity for its inherent satisfaction, interest, and spontaneous joy) and extrinsic motivation (engaging in an activity to attain a separable outcome or avoid external sanctions).

SDT posits that optimal human functioning and psychological vitality require the satisfaction of three innate, universal basic psychological needs:
1. **Autonomy**: The experience of being the author of one's own actions and acting with volition.
2. **Competence**: Feeling effective in interacting with the social and physical environment.
3. **Relatedness**: Feeling connected to, cared for, and secure with significant others.

Through **Organismic Integration Theory**, a sub-theory of SDT, Deci and Ryan conceptualized extrinsic motivation not as a single monolith, but along a developmental continuum of internalization: from *external regulation* (compliance driven by rewards or punishment), to *introjected regulation* (driven by guilt, ego-involvement, or internal pressure), to *identified regulation* (conscious valuing of the goal), to *integrated regulation* (full congruency with the person's values and self-identity).

The **overjustification effect** (Deci, Koestner, & Ryan, 1999; Lepper, Greene, & Nisbett, 1973) demonstrated that offering tangible, expected rewards for inherently interesting activities can undermine intrinsic motivation by shifting the perceived locus of causality from internal to external.

## Cognitive and Goal-Directed Models

- **Expectancy-Value Theory**: Victor Vroom (1964) proposed that motivation is a multiplicative function of Expectancy (effort leads to performance), Instrumentality (performance leads to reward), and Valence (the subjective value of the reward).
- **Goal-Setting Theory**: Edwin Locke and Gary Latham (1990, 2002) conducted extensive laboratory and field experiments demonstrating that specific, challenging goals lead to systematically higher performance than vague directives like "do your best," provided individuals accept the goal, receive performance feedback, and possess high goal commitment.
- **Self-Efficacy**: Albert Bandura (1977, 1997) established that an individual's belief in their capability to execute behaviors necessary to produce specific performance attainments determines whether they initiate effort, how much effort they expend, and their resilience in the face of setbacks.

## Maslow's Need Hierarchy and Growth Motivation

Abraham Maslow (1943) introduced humanistic motivation through his hierarchy of needs, progressing from deficiency needs (physiological, safety, belongingness, esteem) to growth needs (self-actualization, and later self-transcendence). While Maslow's framework highlighted human growth potential, subsequent empirical studies (Wahba & Bridwell, 1976; Tay & Diener, 2011) revealed that human needs do not follow a rigid, invariant chronological hierarchy; individuals in diverse cultural contexts frequently pursue self-actualization and social connection even when physiological and safety needs are severely compromised.

## Neurobiology of Motivation: Wanting versus Liking

Modern affective neuroscience, led by Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson (1998, 2003), dissociated the neural substrates of motivation into two distinct components:
- **Incentive Salience ("Wanting")**: Mediated by mesolimbic dopamine projections extending from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) to the nucleus accumbens. On this account, dopamine energizes craving and approach behavior but does not mediate pleasure itself.
- **Hedonic Impact ("Liking")**: Mediated by localized subcortical "hedonic hotspots" utilizing endogenous opioid, endocannabinoid, and GABAergic signaling within the nucleus accumbens shell and ventral pallidum.

This dissociation explains how addiction and compulsive behaviors occur: individuals can experience intense compulsive "wanting" driven by sensitized dopaminergic pathways, even when the substance or activity produces diminished hedonic "liking."
