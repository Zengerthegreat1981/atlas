# Somatic Memory

## Definition

A central concept in Sensorimotor Psychotherapy and in somatic trauma therapy generally. It assumes that memory is not stored only in the conscious, linguistic mind, but is also recorded in the body itself: in muscle tension, breathing patterns, standing and movement postures, and even in the functions of the internal organs, so that, on this view, traumatic events leave lasting bodily imprints that may appear later as chronic tension, pain without a clear organic cause, or recurring defensive behavioral patterns. "Somatic memory" is a clinical idea, not an established neuroscientific category.

## Historical Context

This concept rests on the research of the Dutch-American psychiatrist Bessel van der Kolk, who since the 1990s (for example in his 1994 article "The Body Keeps the Score") has argued that trauma is registered in bodily and neural systems in ways that may be independent of the ability to retrieve it verbally, and who presented this thesis for a general readership in his book *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014). The therapist Pat Ogden developed this framework practically in her book co-authored with Kekuni Minton and Clare Pain, *Trauma and the Body: A Sensorimotor Approach to Psychotherapy* (2006), presenting a structured therapeutic model that integrates direct bodily observation into the traditional psychotherapy session.

## Main Ideas or Approach

This framework distinguishes several forms of somatic memory: muscle memory (a clinical usage that differs from the everyday sense of practised motor skill), which is sustained tension functioning as a defensive readiness for a danger that no longer exists; visceral memory (Visceral Memory), which may appear as chronic pain not explained organically; movement memory, which is recurring defensive movements or bodily postures whose source one is unaware of; and state memory (State Memory), which is the return of a complete emotional-physiological state (such as a freeze or mobilization state) upon a stimulus reminiscent of the original trauma. Scientifically, this conception rests on a theoretical distinction between explicit memory (Explicit Memory), which is linguistic and can be consciously narrated, and implicit memory (Implicit Memory), which includes the body's habits and its unconscious movement and emotional patterns, and which is thought to depend on partly different brain systems (for example the amygdala for conditioned fear responses) from those that support conscious narrative memory (the hippocampus and related structures). The proposed role of such systems in trauma remains an active research question.

## Notable Works or Representatives

Alongside van der Kolk and Ogden, this framework intersects with Peter Levine's "Somatic Experiencing" approach, which uses tracking of bodily sensation to complete frozen physiological defense cycles, and also with Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR), the technique developed by Francine Shapiro in 1989, an approach with good evidence for PTSD; how it works, including the role of bilateral stimulation, is still debated.

## Somatic memory and implicit memory

Implicit (non-declarative) memory is a broader category in cognitive psychology that includes procedural skills, priming and conditioned responses, which are expressed in performance without conscious recollection. Somatic memory, as used in trauma therapy, is a narrower clinical notion that stresses bodily and emotional states that return in response to reminders. The two terms overlap but are not equivalent.

## Neural basis

There is no established evidence that memories are stored in muscles or organs themselves. Bodily reactions to reminders are generally understood as produced by the brain and autonomic nervous system, drawing on conditioned responses (such as those involving the amygdala), interoceptive processing (for example in the insula) and learned motor and postural habits. How far these processes explain the clinical phenomena described here is still being researched.

## Impact and Criticism

This framework opens an alternative explanation for bodily disorders resistant to conventional treatment, such as chronic pain not explained organically, as sometimes a bodily expression of unprocessed trauma, and it pushes toward integrating psychotherapy with direct bodily tools such as movement, breathing, and therapeutic touch instead of confining treatment to talk therapy, up to a broader integration with somatic medicine in dealing with chronic illnesses. Among the critical observations directed at this field is that the term "somatic memory" remains a useful clinical metaphor more than a neural construct defined with full empirical precision, and that the sharp separation between "implicit memory" and "somatic memory" needs more terminological precision to avoid confusing them with the concepts of general implicit memory in cognitive psychology.
