# Reliability and Validity

Reliability and validity are the two basic quality criteria of psychological measurement. Reliability asks whether a measure gives consistent results; validity asks whether it measures what it is supposed to measure. Because most psychological attributes, such as anxiety, intelligence or attitude, cannot be observed directly, the quality of the instruments used to infer them determines the quality of almost everything else in the field.

## Reliability

A measure is reliable to the extent that it is free from random error. Classical test theory expresses this by treating an observed score as a true score plus error; reliability is the proportion of score variance attributable to true differences between people. Several kinds are distinguished:

- **Test-retest reliability** is the correlation between scores from the same people on two occasions. It assumes the trait itself is stable over the interval.
- **Internal consistency** asks whether the items of a scale measure the same underlying construct. It has traditionally been summarised by Cronbach's alpha ($\alpha$, 1951) or split-half methods; contemporary psychometrics increasingly recommends McDonald's omega ($\omega$, 1999) because alpha assumes tau-equivalence (equal factor loadings across items), an assumption frequently violated in practice.
- **Inter-rater reliability** is the agreement between independent observers or coders, often summarised by Cohen's kappa for categorical judgements or by intraclass correlations.
- **Parallel-forms reliability** compares two equivalent versions of a test.

Reliability is usually reported as a coefficient between 0 and 1. Conventional thresholds are rules of thumb rather than laws, and appropriate levels depend on the stakes: a decision about an individual demands more than a comparison of group averages. Reliability places a ceiling on validity, because a measure dominated by noise cannot correlate strongly with anything. Reliability is also not a fixed property of a test; it is a property of scores in a particular sample and setting.

## Validity

Modern usage treats validity as a property of the interpretations and uses of test scores, not of the test as an object. The traditional taxonomy includes several aspects:

- **Content validity**: do the items adequately sample the domain of interest?
- **Criterion validity**: do scores relate to an external criterion, either concurrently or as predictions of future outcomes (predictive validity)?
- **Construct validity**: do scores behave as theory about the underlying construct says they should? Cronbach and Meehl's 1955 paper set out this idea, and later writers such as Samuel Messick folded the other types into a unified view of construct validity.
- **Convergent and discriminant validity**: scores should correlate with other measures of the same construct and not with measures of different constructs; Campbell and Fiske proposed the multitrait-multimethod matrix in 1959 to examine this.
- **Face validity**: whether a test looks relevant to respondents. It is useful for cooperation but is not evidence of real validity.

Outside measurement, researchers also distinguish **internal validity**, the extent to which a study supports a causal conclusion, from **external validity**, the extent to which results generalise to other people, settings and times. Experimental control tends to raise the first and may lower the second.

## The relationship between the two

A useful analogy compares measurement to shooting at a target. Reliable but invalid measures cluster tightly away from the bullseye; unreliable measures scatter; valid measures cluster on the target. A measure can be reliable without being valid, for example a consistently miscalibrated scale, but cannot be valid unless it is reasonably reliable. Some constructs also create a tension: very narrow, repetitive items raise internal consistency while narrowing the construct that is actually covered, a problem sometimes called the attenuation paradox.

## Limits and debates

Validation is an ongoing argument, not a one-off certificate. Evidence accumulates, and a test can be valid for one purpose and population and not for another. Cross-cultural use is a recurring problem: translated tests may not measure the same construct, and measurement invariance must be checked before groups are compared. Response biases such as acquiescence and social desirability threaten self-report scales. Some critics, notably Denny Borsboom and colleagues, argue that psychology has leaned too heavily on correlational patterns and has neglected whether an attribute really exists and causes variation in test responses. Others stress fairness and consequences, the idea that the social effects of testing belong inside validity. Modern approaches such as item response theory and structural equation modelling give richer ways to examine reliability that varies across the score range, but they do not remove the need for theoretical justification.

The replication crisis has also drawn attention to measurement: flexible use of poorly validated scales is one route by which unreliable findings arise.

## Why it matters

Clinical diagnosis, educational selection, hiring and research all depend on scores being interpreted correctly. When reading a study, useful questions include what the instrument was, what evidence exists that it measures the intended construct in this population, how reliable the scores were in this sample, and whether the conclusion goes beyond what the measure can support.
