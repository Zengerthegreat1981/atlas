# Marxist Alienation (Entfremdung)

"Alienation" (German *Entfremdung*, with the closely related *Entäusserung*) is a central concept in the writings of the young Karl Marx. It designates the condition in which workers under capitalism are estranged from the product of their labour, from the activity of labouring, from their human "species-being", and from other people.

## Definition

Marx developed the concept in the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844* (*Ökonomisch-philosophische Manuskripte*), written in Paris and not published until 1932. In the section on "Estranged Labour" he describes four forms of estrangement.

### 1. From the product

The worker produces goods that belong to the capitalist and that confront the worker as an alien power. The more the worker produces, the greater the alien world of objects that dominates him.

### 2. From the activity of production

Labour is external to the worker: it is forced, belongs to another, and is experienced as a means of survival rather than a free expression of capacities.

### 3. From species-being (Gattungswesen)

Following Feuerbach, Marx holds that human beings are distinguished by conscious, creative, universal production. When labour is reduced to a means of subsistence, the worker is estranged from this distinctively human life-activity.

### 4. From other human beings

If the product and the activity belong to another, then the worker stands in an alien relation to those who control them, and social relations take on the form of competition and exchange.

### Alienation and Objectification

Scholars distinguish alienation (*Entfremdung*) from objectification (*Vergegenständlichung*). Objectification, the embodiment of human powers in products, is for Marx a necessary and positive aspect of all human labour. Alienation is the specific distortion of objectification under private property and wage labour, in which the objects of labour appear as hostile and independent powers. Marx's critique of Hegel in the *1844 Manuscripts* turns partly on this point: he argues that Hegel treats objectification itself as alienation. How sharply Marx himself keeps the terms apart is debated.

## Philosophical Origins

- **Hegel**: in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* spirit externalises itself in the world (*Entäusserung*, *Entfremdung*) and recognises itself in what it has made, returning to itself in absolute knowing. Marx criticised this as treating alienation as a condition of consciousness rather than of real social relations.
- **Feuerbach**: in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841) he argued that humans project their own essence onto God and then submit to it, so that religion is an alienation of human nature.
- **Marx**: in the *Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (1843-44), the *1844 Manuscripts* and *The German Ideology* (written 1845-46) alienation becomes material and social, rooted in property and the division of labour.

## Later Course

### The Left Hegelians

Feuerbach, Bruno Bauer and Max Stirner developed critiques of religion and of the state from Hegelian premises. Marx broke with them in the mid-1840s.

### Later Marxism

- **The mature Marx**: in *Capital* (1867) the vocabulary of alienation is less prominent, and the analysis centres on surplus value, exploitation and commodity fetishism. Whether the theme remains implicit in the later works is a long-standing dispute between "humanist" and "structuralist" readers.
- **Lukács**: *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) developed the concept of reification (*Verdinglichung*), the treatment of social relations as things, drawing on Marx's analysis of commodity fetishism and on Weber. It was written before the *1844 Manuscripts* were published.
- **The Frankfurt School**: Adorno and Horkheimer, and later Marcuse, extended the critique of alienation to mass culture and technological rationality, for example in *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (1944-47) and Marcuse's *One-Dimensional Man* (1964).
- **Althusser**: in *For Marx* (1965) argued for an "epistemological break" between the humanist young Marx and the scientific mature Marx, and treated alienation as an ideological notion.

## Contemporary Legacy

- **Erich Fromm**: *The Sane Society* (1955) and *Marx's Concept of Man* (1961) reformulated alienation as a psychosocial condition in which people experience themselves as commodities and lose a sense of agency.
- **Axel Honneth**: *The Struggle for Recognition* (*Kampf um Anerkennung*, 1992) reworked critical theory around recognition, and his later work on reification (2005) returned to Lukács.
- **Jürgen Habermas**: *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981) speaks of the "colonisation of the lifeworld" by economic and administrative systems, a successor theme to alienation.
- **Existential psychotherapy**: writers such as Irvin Yalom address isolation and meaning in ways that overlap thematically with alienation, but they do not derive these themes from Marx.

## Critique

- **Karl Popper** criticised Marx's historicism and what he saw as the unfalsifiable character of Marxist predictions (*The Open Society and Its Enemies*, 1945; *The Poverty of Historicism*, 1957).
- **Max Weber** located modern disenchantment and loss of freedom in bureaucratic rationalisation across capitalist and socialist societies, not in private property alone.
- **Émile Durkheim** analysed the pathologies of the modern division of labour as anomie (*The Division of Labour in Society*, 1893), a different framework from Marx's.
- **Poststructuralist critics** such as Foucault and Lyotard questioned the humanist premise of an authentic human essence from which people could be alienated, and the totalising narrative of emancipation.
