# Dimensional vs Categorical Diagnosis

## Definition

This distinction refers to two competing models for classifying mental disorders. The **categorical model** treats each disorder as a separate and distinct class that either exists or does not (such as a diagnosis of "affected" or "not affected" by major depressive disorder). The **dimensional model** treats psychological traits and symptoms as continuous on a scale of severity (from complete absence to maximum intensity), without strict dividing lines between "normal" and "pathological."

## Historical Context

From **DSM-III** (1980) onward, the classification adopted an almost entirely categorical system, for practical reasons related to ease of clinical and administrative communication (insurance and epidemiological research). Since the 1990s, however, experimental evidence has accumulated that many disorders, particularly personality, mood, and anxiety disorders, do not in reality distribute in a sharp binary way. Instead they lie on a gradual continuum with "healthy" populations, which has fueled growing calls to adopt more realistic dimensional models.

## Key Dimensions

- **Section III of DSM-5-TR**: in response to this debate, the fifth edition of the manual (2013) included in its Section III the **Alternative Model for Personality Disorders** (AMPD) as a model for further study, and the text revision (DSM-5-TR, 2022) retained it there. It combines an assessment of the degree of impairment in self and interpersonal functioning with an assessment of five pathological trait domains (negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism) on a continuous scale.
- **ICD-11 and the full dimensional shift**: the **International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision** (ICD-11) of the World Health Organization (2019/2022) adopted a more radical change in personality disorders. It replaced the separate categorical personality disorder diagnoses (such as narcissistic or antisocial personality disorder) with a single diagnosis of personality disorder graded by the **degree of severity** of impairment in self and interpersonal functioning (mild, moderate, or severe), A separate, subthreshold description, personality difficulty, is listed for milder trait-related problems and is not itself a personality-disorder diagnosis. Descriptive trait domain qualifiers (negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, and anankastia) are added, along with an optional "borderline pattern" qualifier that preserves a link to the older borderline category.
- **Arguments of supporters of the dimensional model**: they argue that it reflects clinical reality more accurately (symptom severity really does vary among patients), reduces the problem of artificial **comorbidity** arising from strict categorical boundaries (see the entry on Diagnostic Comorbidity), and captures "subthreshold" cases of real clinical significance.
- **Arguments of supporters of the categorical model**: they argue that clear categories are easier for physicians to communicate about and more practical for treatment and administrative decisions (such as insurance criteria and eligibility for services), and that a full shift to a dimensional model requires complex standardized infrastructure that has not yet been tested on a large scale.
- **The RDoC initiative**: this debate runs parallel to the **Research Domain Criteria** (RDoC) effort of the National Institute of Mental Health, launched around 2009, which proposes studying mental disorders through basic neurobehavioral functional dimensions rather than DSM and ICD categories. Its matrix crosses domains (for example negative valence systems, positive valence systems, cognitive systems, systems for social processes, and arousal/regulatory systems; a sensorimotor domain was added later) with units of analysis ranging from genes and molecules to circuits, behavior, and self-report. RDoC is a research framework, not a clinical diagnostic system.
- **Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP)**: a consortium-based dimensional model (Kotov et al., 2017) that organizes symptoms and disorders into spectra such as internalizing, thought disorder, and externalizing, based on the observed statistical structure of psychopathology.
