# Stations & States (Maqamat & Ahwal)

Sufis distinguished between the **station** (*maqam*), which is what the servant attains through his own effort and striving and in which he becomes established, and the **state** (*hal*), which comes upon the heart without deliberate intent and passes away. On this distinction the maps of the spiritual path were built: the station is acquired and the state is bestowed. Al-Qushayri, in *al-Risala*, says that the station is what the servant attains of good conduct through seeking and effortful striving, and that the state is a meaning that comes upon the heart without artifice.

## Al-Sarraj and al-Kalabadhi

The earliest major systematisation is found in Abu Nasr al-Sarraj (d. 378 AH / 988 CE), in *al-Luma'*. His stations are seven: repentance, scrupulousness, asceticism, poverty, patience, reliance on God and contentment. His states are ten: watchfulness, nearness, love, fear, hope, longing, intimacy, tranquillity, contemplation and certainty. Al-Kalabadhi (d. c. 380 AH / 990 CE), in *al-Ta'arruf li-Madhhab Ahl al-Tasawwuf*, also treated stations and states, with a somewhat different arrangement. Arberry edited his book (Cairo, 1934).

## The Stages of the Travellers

Abdullah al-Ansari al-Harawi (d. 481 AH / 1089 CE) wrote *Manazil al-Sa'irin* (*The Stages of the Travellers*), arranging one hundred stations in ten sections. The first are the beginnings, followed by the doors, the transactions, the ethics, the foundations, the valleys, the states, the authorities, the realities and the ends, and the last is unity (*tawhid*). Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 751 AH / 1350 CE) commented on it in *Madarij al-Salikin*, and in places criticised what he saw as an inclination toward annihilation. Serge de Laugier de Beaurecueil published a critical edition of *Manazil al-Sa'irin* in 1962.

## Al-Ghazali: Linking Stations to Knowledge and Action

In *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, al-Ghazali rearranged the stages in the final quarter, "the Quarter of Saving Things" (*al-Munjiyat*): repentance, patience, gratitude, fear, hope, poverty, asceticism, unity (*tawhid*), reliance on God, love, longing and contentment. He linked each station to the triad of "knowledge, state and action", so that the stations became an educational method rather than a description of experiences.

## Disagreement over the Order

The paths did not agree on one order. Some made repentance the first station and others asceticism; some counted patience as a station and then as a state. They also disagreed over whether some of them are stations or states, such as love, contentment and reliance on God. Those who counted them as states spoke of them as a gift, while those who counted them as stations spoke of them as acquired. This disagreement shows that the schemes are descriptive maps of the path rather than a single fixed sequence.

## Limits of the Scheme

The Sufi maps are not successive stages that the traveller leaves behind upon completing each one. Repentance and patience, for example, remain with the traveller at every station that follows, and even the most advanced do not do without them. The comparison between these maps and the ladders of spiritual life in Eastern Christianity and in Hinduism is made by some orientalists, but no direct historical relation has been demonstrated.
