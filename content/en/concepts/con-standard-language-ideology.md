# Standard Language Ideology

A concept used to describe the implicit system of beliefs that assumes a single "correct," "pure" or "ideal" linguistic form of a language (such as "Standard English" or *fusha*, the standard written Arabic), while other linguistic varieties (regional or social dialects) are classified as deviations or errors from this presumed standard. James Milroy and Lesley Milroy analysed this ideology of the standard in *Authority in Language* (1985). Rosina Lippi-Green gave the label "standard language ideology" wide currency in *English with an Accent* (1997), especially in relation to accent discrimination in the United States.

## The Nature of the Social Construction

From the perspective of descriptive linguistics, no linguistic variety has an objective structural superiority over another: all linguistic varieties are equally rule-governed. Standard language ideology, however, conceals this linguistic fact by presenting a specific social variety (usually the dialect of the educated or politically dominant classes) as "the language itself" in its correct form, while the other varieties are reduced to "dialects" of lower status.

## Social Consequences

This ideology is used to justify actual social discrimination: evaluating speakers of non-standard dialects as "less educated" or "less intelligent" on the basis of their dialect alone, although there is no objective linguistic basis for such a judgment. It is a central mechanism in the reproduction of social inequality through the education system and the institutions of work.

## Fusha and Colloquial Arabic: A Special Case

The Arabic case presents a complex example of standard language ideology. *Fusha* (classical and standard written Arabic), historically tied to the Quran and to the classical literary and scientific heritage, is granted a high symbolic status as "correct Arabic," while the colloquial vernaculars spoken daily (although they are the actual mother tongue of hundreds of millions of people) are described as degraded or corrupted "dialects" of the original *fusha*. This ideology ignores a basic linguistic fact: Arabic colloquial varieties are linguistic systems that are rule-governed in their own right, not random deviations from *fusha*.

## Institutional Mechanisms of Reproduction

This ideology is reproduced through specific institutions: schools that teach only "correct grammar," language academies that issue normative judgments, media that exclude non-standard varieties from formal contexts, and dictionaries that record one variety as "the language" to the exclusion of other varieties actually in use.

## The Difference between "Standardization" and "Ideological Standardism"

James and Lesley Milroy distinguished between the process of **standardization**, a relatively neutral historical process (the unification of spelling and vocabulary for practical purposes such as education, administration and printing), and "standard language ideology," which bestows a false normative sanctity on the product of this process and transforms it from a practical instrument into a moral and cultural criterion for evaluating the speakers themselves. This distinction allows one to accept the practical benefits of standardization while rejecting the social judgments built upon it that have no linguistic justification.
