# Ahliyya (Legal Capacity)

**Ahliyya** in usul al-fiqh is a person's eligibility for rights to be established for them and obligations to bind them under Sharia (**capacity of obligation**, *ahliyyat al-wujub*), or for their actions to be considered valid and effective under Sharia (**capacity of performance**, *ahliyyat al-ada'*). The Hanafi usul tradition, including **al-Sarakhsi** (d. c. 483 AH / 1090 CE), set out this concept in systematic detail, and the other schools treat the same topics with differences of emphasis.

## Capacity of Obligation and Capacity of Performance

**Capacity of obligation** is a human being's eligibility for rights and duties to accrue to them and to bind them. It is held by every person merely by virtue of being human, and it is even partly held by the fetus in the womb of its mother for specific rights (such as the right of inheritance and bequest, if it is born alive). It lasts until death. **Capacity of performance**, by contrast, is a person's eligibility for their words and actions to be considered valid under Sharia. It is tied to the degree of **discernment** (the ability to understand the meaning of an act and its consequence), and it passes through graduated stages: complete absence (the child who lacks discernment, and the insane person), partial capacity (the discerning child, whose purely beneficial transactions are valid, whose purely harmful ones are void, and whose transactions of mixed effect depend on the guardian's consent), and full capacity (upon puberty together with rational maturity and sound mind).

## Impediments to Capacity

Jurists elaborated on the **impediments to capacity** that disrupt or diminish it, dividing them into heavenly impediments (outside a person's will, such as insanity, minority, fainting, sleep and forgetfulness) and acquired impediments (arising from a person's own act or that of another, such as intoxication, coercion, error, jest and profligacy). Each impediment has a different effect on the validity and enforceability of transactions and on the attribution of criminal and civil responsibility to its bearer. These elaborations draw on a well-known prophetic report, transmitted in the Sunan collections, that the pen is lifted from the sleeper until he wakes, the child until he reaches puberty, and the insane person until he recovers.

## Comparison with Modern Legal Concepts

The Islamic concept of capacity intersects with modern concepts in comparative law, most notably **competency to stand trial** in modern criminal law, which requires a certain cognitive ability on the part of the accused to bear procedural responsibility, and the **insanity defense** and the famous **M'Naghten rule** in English and American law, under which an accused is not criminally responsible if, through a defect of reason arising from disease of the mind, they did not know the nature and quality of the act or that it was wrong. The decisive difference is that Islamic jurisprudence does not propose a single unified procedural test (like the M'Naghten test). It rather offers a graduated system of impediments according to the nature of each case, derived from a general prophetic report and refined in juristic detail over centuries, not from a single judicial decision of a fixed date, as with the M'Naghten rule (1843).

## Impact and Critique

The Islamic system of capacity is among the areas of usul al-fiqh closest to the theory of "legal personality" or "legal capacity" in modern civil law. Modern Arab civil codes, which were drafted with substantial input from jurists such as Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri, draw on the fiqh of capacity for rules such as the age of majority and interdiction. Researchers in comparative law note that the detailed juristic elaboration of impediments to capacity offers greater analytical flexibility than the strict cognitive test that the M'Naghten rule historically presented in the common law tradition. Yet both systems face the same theoretical challenge: how to measure an internal mental or psychological state by an external legal standard that can be applied in courts.

## School Differences on Age, Discernment and Maturity

Discernment is often associated with about seven years of age, echoing the report on teaching children to pray, but many jurists make the test actual understanding. The schools divide over the discerning child's transactions of mixed effect. Hanafis and Malikis generally treat them as concluded but dependent on the guardian's approval; the Hanbalis accept them when the guardian permits; the Shafi'is generally hold the child's sale void even with permission.

Puberty is established by physical signs, and failing these by age. The majority position, including the Shafi'is, the Hanbalis and Abu Hanifa's companions Abu Yusuf and al-Shaybani, sets fifteen lunar years. Abu Hanifa is reported to have set eighteen for males and seventeen for females, and the well-known Maliki position is eighteen.

Puberty alone does not release property: Qur'an 4:6 also requires sound judgment (*rushd*). Most jurists continue interdiction until it appears, whereas Abu Hanifa held that property must be handed over at twenty-five. The Shafi'is include religious probity as well as financial competence in *rushd*, while the majority stress the latter. Malikis add further conditions for a woman, traditionally including consummated marriage.

## Ages of Majority in Selected Civil Codes

Classical fiqh does not fix one birthday for full capacity of performance. Puberty, often presumed at fifteen lunar years by the majority, or at the later ages of eighteen for males and seventeen for females reported from Abu Hanifa, is joined to sound judgment before property is released. Modern codes replace that pair with a statutory age, and they do not agree with one another or with the fiqh figures.

Article 44 of the Egyptian Civil Code sets full capacity at twenty-one complete Gregorian years, provided the person has mental faculties and is not under interdiction. A comparative note on the Saudi Civil Transactions Law records majority there at eighteen complete Hijri years. In England and Wales the Family Law Reform Act 1969 reduced the age of majority from twenty-one to eighteen. French law has likewise fixed majority at eighteen since the statute of 5 July 1974. In the United Arab Emirates the older Civil Transactions Law used twenty-one lunar years. A new civil transactions law, reported in January 2026, lowers majority to eighteen Gregorian years and keeps a lower age, fifteen Gregorian years, at which a minor may ask a court for leave to manage assets.

These statutes share the fiqh idea that childhood limits performance while rights can still vest. They drop the physical signs of puberty, the separate test of *rushd*, and Abu Hanifa's outer age of twenty-five for handing over property. Voting age, marriage age and criminal responsibility often sit at different numbers again, so "majority" in a civil code is not the whole of legal adulthood.
