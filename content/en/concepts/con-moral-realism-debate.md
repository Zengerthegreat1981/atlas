# Moral Realism Debate

## Meaning and Definition

Moral realism is the thesis that there are objective moral facts that exist in their own right, independent of individuals' beliefs, feelings, or agreement. That is, the statement "torturing innocents is wrong" is true in the same sense in which "water freezes at zero degrees" is true. Its opponents, the anti-realists, deny the existence of such independent facts.

## The Islamic Theological Position

The debate over *al-husn wa-l-qubh* (good and evil) between the Mu'tazila and the Ash'ari is one of the earliest systematic treatments of this question, comparable in scope to the discussion prompted by Plato's *Euthyphro*. The Mu'tazila adopted an explicit rational moral realism, holding that the goodness and badness of actions are intrinsic properties of them, perceived by reason independently of revelation, just as reason perceives the truth of mathematical propositions. The Ash'ari, by contrast, adopted a position closer to religious voluntarism. They made goodness and badness dependent on God's command and prohibition, not on an intrinsic property of the act perceived by pure reason. Yet this position remains realist in another sense, since it affirms an objective moral truth whose source is revelation, not individual taste. The Maturidi school attempted a middle path, affirming a partial rational perception of the goodness and badness of some actions, while the detailed reward and punishment remain dependent on the law.

## The Contemporary Philosophical Context

In contemporary analytic philosophy, the debate is divided between naturalist realism, which reduces moral properties to natural properties observable by science, as in the work of Philippa Foot, and non-naturalist realism, such as the position of G. E. Moore, who regarded "good" as a simple property that cannot be reduced to anything else, warning against what he called the "naturalistic fallacy." On the opposing side stand the anti-realists of various kinds: the error theory of J. L. Mackie, which holds that all moral judgments are false because they presuppose "queer" properties that do not exist in the universe, and emotivism, which regards moral judgments as emotional expressions rather than propositions. Realists argue from the "argument from moral convergence" (the agreement of humans on the prohibition of injustice across cultures), while their opponents argue from the wide "moral disagreement" observed in practice.

## An Applied Example

The near-universal condemnation of the Nazi genocide is cited as an example of an objective moral truth that transcends the local laws that permitted it at the time. Realists use it as a strong argument against cultural relativism.

## Quietist and Minimal Views

Some contemporary philosophers, such as Ronald Dworkin and T. M. Scanlon, have tried to move beyond the sharp dichotomy between realism and its denial by treating moral claims as capable of truth in an ordinary, first-order sense without committing to a distinctive metaphysics of moral properties (sometimes called quietism or minimal realism).
