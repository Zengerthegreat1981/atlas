# Ottoman Codification in the Tanzimat Era

This concept refers to the Ottoman state's broad project to codify its legislation in the form of written, numbered laws on the European model, within the **Tanzimat** reforms, which began formally with the Edict of Gülhane (Hatt-i Sharif of Gülhane) in 1839. The reforms covered the civil domain (through the Majalla al-Ahkam al-Adliyya, a code of 1,851 articles prefaced by 99 legal maxims) as well as other domains such as criminal, commercial and land law.

## The Dual Legislative Path

Ottoman codification followed two parallel paths that differed according to the domain. In the civil domain (transactions, contracts and property), the state chose to codify Hanafi jurisprudence itself, in the form of the Majalla al-Ahkam al-Adliyya (1869–1876), without importing a foreign law. In other domains, such as commercial law (the Commercial Code of 1850) and criminal law (the Penal Code of 1858), the Ottoman state adopted texts substantially modeled on French law. The Land Code of 1858 was an Ottoman enactment, not a French import. This dual arrangement produced a hybrid legal system that combined local Sharia-based codification in one domain with European-derived borrowing in another. The new codes were applied mainly through the "Nizamiye" courts, established in the 1860s, which operated alongside the Sharia courts.

## The Ottoman Law of Family Rights (1917)

A further important step in this path was the **Ottoman Law of Family Rights** (1917), an early codification of family provisions (marriage, divorce and maintenance) in modern Islamic history. It differed from the Majalla al-Ahkam al-Adliyya in adopting the method of **selection** (*takhayyur*) among the four schools, rather than restricting itself to the Hanafi school alone. In each issue it selected the opinion best suited to the conditions of the age from any school. For example, it adopted a Maliki opinion expanding a wife's right to seek separation for harm.

## Its Extended Impact after the Fall of the Ottoman State

After the fall of the Ottoman state, several successor states (such as Syria, Lebanon, Iraq, Jordan, Palestine and Kuwait) continued to apply the Majalla, or parts of it, for decades. They gradually replaced it with modern codes, for example Lebanon's Code of Obligations and Contracts (1932) and, in other states, civil codes influenced by the Egyptian Civil Code (drafted by al-Sanhuri and in force from 1949). The Republic of Turkey, for its part, broke with this legacy in 1926 by adopting a civil code modeled on the Swiss Civil Code.

## The Principal Codes and Their Sources

In criminal law, penal codes of 1840 and 1851 preceded the Code of 1858, which followed the French Penal Code of 1810 closely in structure and content while preserving private claims under the Sharia, such as blood money. The Commercial Code of 1850 drew on the French Commercial Code of 1807; an addendum of 1860 organized commercial tribunals, and codes of commercial procedure (1861) and maritime commerce (1863) followed, again on French models. Codes of criminal and civil procedure were issued in 1879 under French influence. The Land Code of 1858 systematized existing Ottoman categories, distinguishing private (*mulk*), state (*miri*), endowed (*waqf*), publicly reserved (*matruka*) and dead (*mawat*) land, and was linked to a system of title registration.

## Replacement of the Majalla by State

The Majalla gave way at different dates. Turkey replaced it in 1926. Lebanon displaced much of it with the Code of Obligations and Contracts of 1932. Syria adopted a civil code in 1949, and Iraq enacted one in 1951, in force from 1953, that blended Majalla material with the Egyptian-influenced model. Jordan replaced it with its civil code of 1976, and Kuwait with legislation culminating in the civil code of 1980. Israel removed its provisions piecemeal and repealed it formally in 1984. In the Palestinian territories parts of it continued to apply much longer. Egypt, autonomous in legislation, never adopted it.

## Its Significance for Legal Theory

This codification is an important case study in comparative legal theory of how a flexible, multi-opinion traditional juristic system can be converted into a single, binding modern legal text. It produces, at the same time, gains (legal certainty, ease of judicial application) and losses (a contraction of traditional juristic flexibility).
