# Presence and Absence

Presence, meaning wholehearted engagement with the moment and with another person, is central to existential therapy. Yet presence is always shadowed by absence: the awareness of what is not here, whether the dead, the lost or the unlived possibilities, and the awareness that this moment will pass. The two terms are best read together, since each gives the other its depth.

## Definition

Presence is a quality of attention and availability in which a person is actually there, rather than hidden behind roles, defenses or distraction. It differs from simple attention: one can attend closely while remaining emotionally withdrawn. Absence has two senses in this tradition. It can be a failure of presence (being elsewhere in mind), and it can be a structural feature of life, the felt gap left by what is missing or gone.

## Origins

Martin Buber's *I and Thou* (1923) described two basic attitudes: I-It, in which the other is an object of use or knowledge, and I-Thou, a mutual meeting of whole beings. Gabriel Marcel likewise spoke of presence and availability as the heart of genuine relation. Heidegger's analysis of "being-there" in *Being and Time* (1927) gave the philosophical background for thinking of human existence as an engaged presence in a world. On the side of absence, Sartre's famous café example in *Being and Nothingness* (1943), in which a friend's absence is vividly felt as the absence of that particular person, shows that absence can itself be an experienced reality and not merely a lack.

## Presence in Therapy

Existential and humanistic therapists regard the therapist's presence as a primary therapeutic resource. James Bugental's *The Art of the Psychotherapist* (1987) gave a sustained account of presence as the therapist's full, undefended engagement, and Carl Rogers in later writing spoke of the value of simply being present. Yalom (*Existential Psychotherapy*, 1980) and van Deurzen (*Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy in Practice*, 2012) likewise place the quality of the relationship at the center of the work. The client's own gradual capacity to be fully present is both a means and a goal of therapy.

Many difficulties can be understood as problems of presence. Clinicians describe dissociation as a kind of psychological absence, rumination as mental absence from the present moment, and anxious projection as living in imagined futures rather than engaging with what is. Existential work does not treat these as simple symptoms to remove; it explores what the absence protects the person from. Contemplative and mindfulness-based approaches share a related emphasis on returning attention to the present, although they differ in method and theoretical background.

## Absence as Structural

Absence is not only failure or pathology. A person is shaped partly by what is missing: the unlived life, the person who has died, the possibility foreclosed. Grief research has noted that many bereaved people maintain an enduring bond with the deceased (Klass, Silverman and Nickman, *Continuing Bonds*, 1996), a form of presence within absence. Acknowledging absence, rather than defending against it, is part of authenticity and is closely tied to awareness of mortality and loss.

## Criticisms and Limits

Presence is difficult to define and measure, and claims about its effects rest largely on clinical experience rather than controlled research. Some critics, from a different philosophical camp, have questioned the "metaphysics of presence" itself, the assumption that immediate, undistorted presence is available at all. Therapists also caution that demanding presence from a client in acute distress can be counterproductive; sometimes distance or numbing is a necessary protection, and respect for it is part of good practice.
