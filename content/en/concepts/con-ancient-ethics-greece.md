# Ancient Greek Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Ancient Greek ethics refers to the body of ethical theories developed by Greek philosophers from the fifth century BCE until the end of the Hellenistic period. It is characterized by posing the question of "the good life" (Eudaimonia) as the central question of ethics, rather than confining itself to the question of "what is the right act." For the Greeks, ethics was not so much a system of binding rules as an inquiry into the nature of human flourishing and the ways of achieving it through virtue (Arete).

## Reception and Influence in Islamic Thought

The ethics of the Greeks, especially those of Aristotle and Plato, passed into Islamic civilization through the translation movement in Baghdad, and merged with Arabic ethical thought in what became known as the philosophical "science of ethics." Ibn Miskawayh, in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq wa Tathir al-A'raq* (*The Refinement of Character and the Purification of Dispositions*), relied directly on Aristotle's theory of virtue as a mean between two vices (for example, courage as a mean between cowardice and rashness), and transferred the concept of the good life into an Islamic framework that links the perfection of the soul to happiness in the hereafter as well as in this world. Al-Farabi, in *Attaining Happiness* (*Tahsil al-Sa'ada*) and *Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City* (*Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila*), absorbed the Platonic model of the virtuous city and linked individual happiness to the optimal political system, following the pattern of Plato's *Republic*. Al-Ghazali benefited from the Platonic-Aristotelian classification of the powers of the soul (appetitive, irascible and rational) in *The Balance of Action* (*Mizan al-Amal*) and *The Revival of the Religious Sciences*, although he subordinated it to a purely religious purpose.

## Historical Context

Systematic ethical inquiry in Greece is commonly traced to Socrates, who made virtue a form of knowledge (whoever knows the good necessarily does it). Plato developed this in his dialogues, especially the *Republic*, in which he linked justice to the harmony of the three powers of the soul (rational, spirited and appetitive) under the rule of reason. Then came Aristotle in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, who established the tradition of virtue ethics in its most systematic form. He defined virtue as a stable disposition of choice that lies in the mean between excess and deficiency, and made the goal of human life "happiness" (eudaimonia), achieved through the exercise of reason in virtuous activity over a complete life. In the Hellenistic period, the Stoic school (beginning with Zeno of Citium, and continued in the Roman period by Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius) proposed an ethics based on living in accordance with nature and reason, while Epicureanism called for maximizing pleasure accompanied by tranquility (ataraxia), and Cynicism (Diogenes of Sinope) presented an ascetic model that rejected social conventions.

## Applications and Uses

These ethics have had a profound influence on the revival of contemporary virtue ethics, especially in the work of Alasdair MacIntyre in *After Virtue* (1981), as well as Philippa Foot and Elizabeth Anscombe ("Modern Moral Philosophy," 1958), who found in the Aristotelian concept of flourishing an alternative to modern duty-based ethics and utilitarianism. An illustrative example: a physician faces a difficult decision that no explicit rule resolves. The virtue ethicist asks: "What would the wise physician with practical wisdom (*phronesis*) do in this situation?" rather than applying a rigid rule.

## Further Primary Sources

Socrates wrote nothing, so his ethics is reconstructed mainly from Plato, especially the *Apology*, *Crito*, *Protagoras*, and *Gorgias*, and from Xenophon's *Memorabilia*. Besides the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle's *Eudemian Ethics* gives a second treatment of happiness and virtue, and his *Politics* continues the inquiry at the level of the city. For the Hellenistic schools the evidence is more fragmentary. Epicurus's *Letter to Menoeceus* and *Principal Doctrines* survive in the tenth book of Diogenes Laertius's *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, whose sixth and seventh books are also major witnesses for the Cynics and Stoics. Early Stoic writings are largely lost and known through later reports, including those of Stobaeus and Cicero; Cicero's *On Ends* sets out and criticizes Epicurean, Stoic, and Antiochean accounts of the highest good. Roman Stoicism is better preserved in Seneca's *Letters*, the *Discourses* and *Handbook* of Epictetus recorded by Arrian, and the *Meditations* of Marcus Aurelius. A. A. Long and D. N. Sedley's *The Hellenistic Philosophers* (1987) collects key texts.
