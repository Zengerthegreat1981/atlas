# Narrative

Narrative is the telling of events in sequence: a story with a beginning, a middle, and an end, told by someone to someone for a purpose. Anthropologists study narrative not only as folklore and myth but as a basic way people make sense of their lives. Life histories, illness accounts, political speeches, rumours, and gossip all take narrative form, and through them people explain who they are, what happened, and what should happen next. To study narrative is to study how experience is ordered into meaning, and how that ordering is itself shaped by culture, power, and genre.

## Origins and Development

The formal study of narrative began with the Russian folklorist Vladimir Propp, whose *Morphology of the Folktale* (1928) showed that Russian wonder tales, despite their surface variety, were built from a small set of recurring functions, such as the hero's departure, the test, and the return. Propp's insight was structural: stories could be analysed like sentences, with a grammar of their own. Claude Levi-Strauss extended this approach to myth in *Structural Anthropology* (1958), arguing that myths work by mediating contradictions, such as life and death or nature and culture, through the arrangement of their elements. A myth, in his view, is less a tale told for entertainment than a machine for thinking.

A second strand of theory treated narrative as a mode of knowing. Paul Ricoeur argued in *Time and Narrative* (1983–1985) that human time itself becomes intelligible only through stories: we understand our lives by emplotting them, fitting events into beginnings, crises, and resolutions. Jerome Bruner made the point central to psychology and anthropology in "The Narrative Construction of Reality" (1991), arguing that narrative is not a decoration on thought but a distinct mode of cognition, alongside logical argument, through which people construct selves and worlds. People do not merely tell stories about their lives; they live their lives as stories, revising them as circumstances change.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the universality of narrative form. Propp's functions and Levi-Strauss's oppositions suggest deep structures shared across cultures, and indeed stories of journeys, tricksters, and transformations appear worldwide. Yet ethnographers insist that narrative genres differ profoundly: what counts as a true story, who may tell it, when it may be told, and what it may claim are all culturally specific. Among the Kaluli of Papua New Guinea, for example, song and sung weeping carry an emotional force distinct from that of spoken narrative, while in many West African societies, praise poetry and epic recitation are the work of specialists whose performances confer legitimacy on rulers. Form and social function cannot be separated.

A second dimension is the politics of narrative. Michael Jackson's *The Politics of Storytelling* (2002) argues that storytelling is a way for people to reclaim agency: in telling what happened to them, refugees, the ill, and the dispossessed assert a self against forces that would reduce them to cases or statistics. But narrative can also dominate. Official histories, national myths, and development reports impose their plots on events, silencing rival versions. Anthropologists therefore attend to whose story is heard, whose is dismissed as rumour or gossip, and how the same events can be emplotted as tragedy by one side and farce by another.

A third dimension is narrative and the self. Cheryl Mattingly's *The Paradox of Hope* (2010) follows families caring for seriously ill children and shows how they sustain hope by telling and retelling stories in which small improvements become turning points. Illness narratives, studied by Arthur Kleinman and others, reveal how suffering is given meaning, how moral responsibility is assigned, and how healing itself depends on finding a plot that makes pain bearable. The self emerges here not as a fixed essence but as a narrative achievement, continually revised.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Narrative has become a central concern far beyond anthropology. In medicine, narrative-based practice asks clinicians to listen to patients' stories rather than reducing them to symptoms. In law, testimony is judged by its narrative coherence as much as by its facts. In politics, competing narratives of national history, migration, and climate change shape elections and policy. Digital media have multiplied narrative forms: social media threads, podcasts, and video diaries let ordinary people publish life stories that once needed a publisher, while algorithms decide which stories travel and which vanish.

Critics warn against narrative imperialism: the assumption that all experience should be rendered as a coherent story, and that lives without a clear plot are somehow failed or inauthentic. Not all experience fits narrative form, and some suffering resists emplotment altogether. Trauma, chronic illness, and displacement often produce fragmented, unfinished accounts, and anthropologists have learned to value those fragments rather than forcing them into shape. The study of narrative therefore ends with a caution: stories are powerful tools for making meaning, but meaning can also be made in silence, in repetition, and in refusal to tell.
