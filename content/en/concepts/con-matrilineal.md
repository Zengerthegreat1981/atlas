# Matrilineal

Matrilineal descent is a principle of kinship reckoning in which membership in a descent group, rights to property, and social identity are traced through the mother's line. A person's lineage and clan pass from mother to child, and the child belongs to the mother's group rather than the father's. Matrilineal systems are found in many parts of the world — among the Akan of Ghana, the Minangkabau of Sumatra, the Khasi of north-east India, the Mosuo of China, and the Trobriand Islanders of Melanesia — and matrilineal descent is one of the two unilineal principles, alongside patrilineal descent through the father, and is contrasted with cognatic (bilateral) descent through both sides. Each principle produces a different map of who counts as kin, who inherits what, and who holds authority.

## Definition

In a matrilineal system, the core descent group is composed of a woman, her brothers and sisters, and her sisters' children; a man's own children belong to their mother's lineage, not to his. This has a distinctive consequence for male authority: the most important male figure in a child's life is often not the father but the mother's brother, who holds authority over his sister's children and manages the lineage's property on their behalf. Residence varies: in some matrilineal societies a husband moves to his wife's household, while in others spouses live separately, each remaining in the household of their own matrilineage. Inheritance typically passes from a man to his sister's son rather than to his own son. These arrangements are frequently misunderstood as "matriarchy," or rule by women. Matrilineal descent does not mean that women rule: authority is often held by men — maternal uncles and lineage elders — even though descent and property pass through women.

## Origins and Development

Matrilineal descent became a major topic of anthropology through the study of the Trobriand Islanders by Bronislaw Malinowski, whose book The Sexual Life of Savages, published in 1929, described a society in which children belonged to their mother's clan, fathers were regarded as kindly helpers rather than procreators in the biological sense, and the mother's brother was the authority figure. Audrey Richards's study of the Bemba of Northern Rhodesia, published in 1939, showed how matrilineal descent shaped land rights, chieftainship, and the organisation of labour. Matrilineal Kinship, a volume edited by David Schneider and Kathleen Gough and published in 1961, brought together comparative studies and argued that matrilineal systems posed a theoretical puzzle: if descent passes through women but authority is held by men, how are the tensions between a man's loyalty to his own lineage and his obligations to his wife and children resolved? Annette Weiner's study of the Trobrianders, published in 1976, transformed the debate by showing that women controlled the wealth in banana-leaf bundles and skirts that was central to mortuary exchanges, and that male prestige depended on women's productive and ceremonial work.

## Key Debates

A long-standing debate concerns the supposed instability of matrilineal systems. Early theorists suggested that matrilineal descent created an inherent tension — the "matrilineal puzzle" — because a man's authority over his sister's children conflicted with his attachment to his own children, and predicted that matrilineal systems would give way to patrilineal ones under the pressures of cash cropping, wage labour, and state administration. Later research showed that matrilineal systems are neither inherently unstable nor doomed: they adapt, persist, and in some cases expand. Another debate concerns gender and power. Feminist anthropologists have argued that the classic literature asked the wrong question when it asked whether matrilineal societies are matriarchies; the more productive question is how matrilineal descent shapes women's access to land, their autonomy in marriage and divorce, and their authority in ritual and exchange. A third debate concerns change: in many matrilineal societies, colonial and postcolonial land policies that registered land in the name of male household heads, and the spread of patrilineal norms through religion and schooling, have eroded women's rights — an erosion that is political and historical rather than a natural outcome of matrilineal descent itself.

## Contemporary Relevance

Matrilineal principles continue to matter in law, politics, and everyday life. The Minangkabau of Sumatra, the world's largest matrilineal society, maintain matrilineal landholding and lineage organisation within a modern Indonesian state, and their system is a focus of debates over custom, Islam, and national law. The Mosuo of China, often described as a "walking marriage" society, have become the subject of intense scholarly and tourist attention, much of it based on misunderstanding. In Ghana, matrilineal inheritance rules govern succession to chieftaincy and the distribution of family property, and they feature regularly in court cases and constitutional debates. Development programmes that ignore matrilineal tenure — registering land to husbands, for example — can dispossess women whose rights rest on their membership in a matrilineage. Understanding matrilineal descent on its own terms, rather than as the mirror image of patrilineal descent or as a stage on the road to something else, remains essential for grasping how kinship organises property, authority, and identity in a substantial part of the world.
