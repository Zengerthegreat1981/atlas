# Moral Development

Moral development is a concept in moral psychology that investigates how a person's capacity for moral reasoning and moral decision-making grows across the stages of life, from childhood to maturity. This entry is linked in particular to Carol Gilligan's critique of Lawrence Kohlberg's theory of moral development, and to her alternative known as the "Ethics of Care."

## Meaning and definition

In the 1960s Lawrence Kohlberg developed an influential theory describing moral development through six stages arranged in three levels: preconventional (judgment rests on avoiding punishment), conventional (rests on conformity to social rules), and postconventional (rests on abstract universal principles of justice). In his study Kohlberg relied on hypothetical dilemmas (the most famous being the Heinz dilemma) to measure the level of moral reasoning.

## Gilligan's objection

In her book *In a Different Voice* (1982), Carol Gilligan, who had worked with Kohlberg at Harvard, objected that his theory had been built originally on a sample of boys, and that girls and women, when their development was measured by this scale, often appeared at a lower level, not because they were less morally mature but because their style of moral reasoning differs qualitatively: whereas men, in Kohlberg's samples, tend to reason by the abstract logic of "justice and rights," women tend to reason by the logic of "care and relationships," that is, by taking into account the concrete context of human relationships and responsibility toward specific others rather than abstract rules. Gilligan did not claim the superiority of one voice over the other, but called for recognition of both. Later research, including Lawrence Walker's 1984 review, found few consistent sex differences in Kohlberg-scale scores, and Gilligan herself later stressed that the care and justice orientations are not strictly tied to gender.

## In the Islamic tradition

Moral development was not posed in the Islamic tradition in a comparable empirical psychological form, but a similar idea is present in Miskawayh's conception of gradual moral education in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (The Refinement of Character), where he held that virtue is not born complete but is acquired gradually through habituation and practice, beginning with the discipline and upbringing of the child and ending with the transformation of virtue into a firmly rooted disposition that a person performs willingly and not out of fear. This parallels Kohlberg's path from external conformity to internal principle, though the conceptual framework differs fundamentally.

## Applications and uses

Gilligan's work had a profound influence on moral education, as it led educators to avoid measuring moral maturity by a single abstract standard, and it contributed to the building of the "ethics of care" later developed by Nel Noddings, which has been influential in nursing, education and social work, where concrete relational concern is valued as much as abstract rules.

## Example

In the Heinz dilemma, in which a man steals a drug to save his sick wife, Gilligan reported that an eleven-year-old girl in her interviews, Amy, did not answer with an abstract judgment based on the right of property, but considered talking with the pharmacist or finding another solution that preserved the relationships involved, a relational style of reasoning that Kohlberg's scoring did not capture well.
