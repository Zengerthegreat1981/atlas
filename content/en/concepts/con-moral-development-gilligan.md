# Moral Development (Gilligan)

Moral development refers to the psychological process through which individuals acquire moral reasoning, ethical judgment, and prosocial obligations across the lifespan. In modern moral psychology, the concept is indelibly associated with Carol Gilligan's seminal critique of Lawrence Kohlberg's cognitive-developmental model and her formulation of the **Ethics of Care**.

## Kohlberg's Justice Framework

In the 1960s, Lawrence Kohlberg formulated a six-stage cognitive-developmental model of moral reasoning, structured into three overarching levels:
1. **Preconventional Level**: Moral reasoning is egocentric, anchored to avoiding punishment (Stage 1) and pursuing personal instrumental rewards (Stage 2).
2. **Conventional Level**: Morality is grounded in interpersonal conformity to please others (Stage 3) and maintaining social order through adherence to law and duty (Stage 4).
3. **Postconventional Level**: Morality transcends specific legal frameworks, resting on social contracts (Stage 5) and universal ethical principles of justice and human rights (Stage 6).

Kohlberg assessed moral maturity using hypothetical moral dilemmas, most notably the **Heinz dilemma**, in which a husband must decide whether to steal an unaffordable drug to save his dying wife.

## Gilligan's Objection: *In a Different Voice*

In *In a Different Voice* (1982), Carol Gilligan, a former research associate of Kohlberg at Harvard University, identified a profound methodological and theoretical limitation in Kohlberg's paradigm: his original 1958 longitudinal sample comprised exclusively 84 boys. When female participants were assessed on Kohlberg's scale, they disproportionately scored at Stage 3 (interpersonal harmony), appearing morally deficient compared to males who routinely achieved Stage 4 or Stage 5 (abstract legal and universal principles).

Gilligan argued that this apparent deficit reflected measurement bias rather than moral inferiority. Women and girls were not less mature; rather, they spoke in a distinct moral voice:
- **Ethics of Justice (Masculine Orientation)**: Evaluates moral dilemmas through abstract, universal principles, formal rights, equality, and impartial rules.
- **Ethics of Care (Feminine Orientation)**: Evaluates dilemmas through relational responsibilities, contextual compassion, prevention of harm, and sustaining interpersonal connections.

### The Contrast of Jake and Amy

Gilligan illustrated this divergence using two eleven-year-old children responding to the Heinz dilemma:
- **Jake** viewed the situation as an intellectual math problem with human actors: life is worth more than property, so Heinz should steal the drug, and a judge would logically acquit him.
- **Amy** viewed the problem as a narrative fracture in human relationships: if Heinz steals the drug, he may go to prison, leaving his sick wife unsupported. She proposed that Heinz talk with the pharmacist, explain the dilemma, and negotiate a collaborative solution.

Under Kohlberg's scoring rubric, Jake's rule-based analysis placed him at a higher developmental stage, whereas Amy's relational reasoning was scored as lower-stage interpersonal conformity.

## Gilligan's Developmental Sequence of Care

Gilligan outlined an alternative three-level developmental trajectory for the ethics of care:
1. **Preconventional: Orientation to Individual Survival**: The self is the sole object of concern; choices are governed by immediate survival and self-interest.
   - *Transition from Selfishness to Responsibility*: Recognition of connection to others.
2. **Conventional: Goodness as Self-Sacrifice**: The individual equates goodness with caring for others at the expense of their own needs, subordinating selfhood to social expectations.
   - *Transition from Goodness to Truth*: Realization that neglecting oneself is unethical and destructive to genuine relationship.
3. **Postconventional: Morality of Nonviolence**: The individual recognizes the interdependence of self and other. Moral judgment integrates care for oneself with care for others, guided by the universal injunction against causing harm.

## Empirical Reassessments and Influence

Subsequent empirical research demonstrated that moral reasoning orientations are not rigidly segregated by biological sex. Meta-analyses by Lawrence Walker (1984, 1986) demonstrated that when educational attainment, socioeconomic status, and occupational roles are controlled, gender differences on Kohlberg's dilemmas are largely negligible. Gilligan herself later clarified that justice and care represent two universal moral frameworks accessible to both sexes, though socialization influences which orientation is emphasized.

The ethics of care exerted profound influence across diverse disciplines:
- **Philosophy and Pedagogy**: Nel Noddings (*Caring*, 1984) developed care ethics into a comprehensive moral and educational philosophy, centering relational receptivity and responsiveness.
- **Bioethics and Nursing**: Care ethics transformed medical ethics, validating patient-centered narrative empathy alongside principle-based bioethics.

## Comparative Perspective: Virtue Cultivation in Islamic Thought

While the classical Islamic tradition did not construct psychometric dilemma stages, a parallel trajectory of moral refinement appears in classical philosophical ethics. In *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (The Refinement of Character), Miskawayh (c. 932–1030 CE) articulated a gradual developmental process of character education. Moral virtue is not innate; it begins with childhood discipline, social habituation, and parental instruction, gradually maturing into internalized virtue (*malaka*) where ethical action is performed autonomously from rational conviction and love of the good.
