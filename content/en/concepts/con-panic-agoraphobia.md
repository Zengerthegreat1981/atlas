# Panic Disorder & Agoraphobia

**Panic disorder** and **agoraphobia** are two anxiety disorders that are closely related clinically, but are today classified as two separate diagnoses.

## Definition

A **panic attack** is a sudden period of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes, accompanied by acute physical symptoms: palpitations, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, and sometimes a sense of losing control or of impending death. **Panic disorder** is diagnosed when unexpected panic attacks recur, and at least one of them is followed by a month or more of persistent anxiety about having further attacks, or by a significant behavioral change (such as avoiding certain activities) out of fear of a new attack.

**Agoraphobia** is intense anxiety about being in situations where escape is difficult or where help may not be available if symptoms resembling a panic attack appear. Examples include public transport, open spaces, enclosed spaces, standing in a queue or in a crowd, or leaving home alone. DSM-5 requires marked fear or anxiety about two or more of five situation types (public transport, open spaces, enclosed spaces, queues or crowds, being outside the home alone), lasting typically six months or more. The person either avoids these situations or endures them with severe distress or with the need for a companion.

## Diagnostic Evolution

In **DSM-IV-TR**, the relationship between the two disorders was hierarchical. Agoraphobia was classified either as occurring with panic disorder or without it, meaning that agoraphobia was often understood as a later consequence of panic disorder. **DSM-5** (2013) changed this framework fundamentally. It separated the two disorders into entirely independent diagnoses, based on cumulative evidence that a considerable proportion of people with agoraphobia never experience full-symptom panic attacks at all. It is now possible to record both diagnoses together when both are present, rather than merging them into a single category.

## Prevalence

DSM-5 reports a 12-month prevalence of panic disorder of roughly 2 to 3 percent in adults in the United States and several European countries, with women affected about twice as often as men; a 12-month prevalence of agoraphobia of about 1.7 percent in adolescents and adults is also cited. Figures vary by country and survey method. Onset is most often in early adulthood.

## Theoretical Framework

Several models intersect in explaining the two disorders. The **cognitive-behavioral model** focuses on the "catastrophic interpretation" of ordinary bodily sensations (for example, a slight palpitation interpreted as a warning of a heart attack). This creates a feedback loop that escalates anxiety into a full attack. The **biological model** links panic to hypersensitivity of fear circuits in the amygdala and to an exaggerated response to carbon dioxide. The **avoidance conditioning model** explains agoraphobia as learned avoidance behavior that is reinforced each time escape reduces anxiety in a situation.

## Treatment

Evidence-based treatment options include **cognitive behavioral therapy**, especially protocols of graded exposure to interoceptive sensations (interoceptive exposure) and to avoided situations. Pharmacological treatment with **selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors** (SSRIs) and related antidepressants is a commonly used drug option in guidelines. Benzodiazepines are used less often because of concerns about dependence. This entry is educational and does not constitute clinical advice.
