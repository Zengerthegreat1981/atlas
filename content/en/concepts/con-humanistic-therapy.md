# Humanistic-Existential Therapy

A psychotherapeutic current that emerged in the United States and Europe in the middle of the twentieth century, historically known as the **"Third Force"** in psychology, in contrast to Freudian psychoanalysis (the First Force) and behaviorism (the Second Force). Its leading figures share an emphasis on **human agency**, the innate human capacity for growth and self-actualization, and a rejection of reducing the person to unconscious conflictual drives or mechanical conditioned responses.

## Rogers and Maslow

**Abraham Maslow** was a leading organizer of humanistic psychology. His hierarchy of needs, set out in papers from 1943 and in *Motivation and Personality* (1954), places **self-actualization** at the top of a motivational scheme. The most influential therapeutic application came through **Carl Rogers** and **person-centered therapy** (also called client-centered therapy). In 1957 Rogers argued that six conditions were necessary and sufficient for personality change. The three that the therapist must provide are **accurate empathy**, **unconditional positive regard**, and **congruence** (genuineness). The others concern a relationship and a client who is incongruent and in contact with the therapist.

## The Existential Dimension: Rollo May

**Rollo May** introduced European existentialism and phenomenology into this American current. He emphasized darker dimensions than the purely optimistic Rogers-Maslow outlook, such as human free choice, existential anxiety, and the inevitable tragic aspects of human existence (death, isolation and loss of meaning). He was influenced by thinkers such as Søren Kierkegaard, Martin Heidegger, and Paul Tillich. These European sources added philosophical depth to the American humanist movement, which was more optimistic in its original tendency.

## The Old Saybrook Meeting (1964)

In 1964 a conference at Old Saybrook, Connecticut, brought together leading figures of the movement (including Maslow, Rogers, May, and others) to clarify its shared principles and identity as the "Third Force." The meeting took place shortly after the founding of the movement's organization, the American Association for Humanistic Psychology, in the early 1960s.

## Three Therapeutic Branches

Three main therapeutic directions branched from this general current: **client-centered therapy** (Rogers); **Gestalt therapy**, developed by Fritz Perls together with Laura Perls and Paul Goodman, with greater focus on immediate sensory awareness and direct experience in the "here and now"; and **existential therapy** (May and, later, Irvin Yalom), which directly confronts the great existential questions (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness) as a basic source of human psychological anxiety.

## Impact and Contemporary Assessment

The current has deeply influenced contemporary therapeutic practice in general. Its emphasis on the empathic therapeutic relationship is widely reflected in other schools, and research on the therapeutic alliance and empathy supports the importance of relationship factors across approaches. Meta-analytic reviews (for example Elliott and colleagues, 2013) find that person-centered and other humanistic-experiential therapies are associated with substantial pre-post change, with outcomes broadly comparable to other therapies, although they have been less often tested in large trials. Academic presence of humanistic therapy declined from the 1980s in favor of more manualized approaches. Interest later revived, including in emotion-focused therapy and in connection with positive psychology.

## Limits

The humanistic-existential current has been subject to a methodological critique: its central concepts (such as self-actualization and existential meaning) concern subjective dimensions that are difficult to operationalize in rigorous experimental designs, and its non-directive stance has led to fewer controlled trials than for behavioral and cognitive approaches. Self-actualization, in particular, has been criticized as culturally specific and weakly measurable.
