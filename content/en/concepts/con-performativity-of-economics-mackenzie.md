# The Performativity of Economics in MacKenzie

An influential thesis developed by Donald MacKenzie arguing that mathematical economic theories do not merely describe financial markets objectively from the outside (a "camera") but actually shape and produce them from within (an "engine").

## The Central Idea

Contrary to the common view that mathematical economic theories (such as the Black-Scholes model for pricing financial options) are merely neutral descriptive tools that describe an objective economic reality existing beforehand independently of them, MacKenzie argued that they actually operate as an active productive force (an "Engine," not a "Camera"). When enough traders and financial institutions adopt a particular mathematical model for pricing a financial asset, they actually begin trading and setting prices according to the expectations of that very model. Actual market behavior then moves gradually into line with the model's predictions, regardless of the model's original "objective" accuracy considered apart from its actual use. In a formulation associated with Michel Callon, from whom the idea of the performativity of economics derives, economic theory does not simply describe an external economy that exists beforehand independently of it, but helps to perform that economy, thereby creating the very phenomena that it claims merely to describe in an objective, neutral and prior way. He documented the thesis historically, notably in work with Yuval Millo on the Chicago Board Options Exchange (founded 1973), showing that option prices came increasingly to fit the Black-Scholes-Merton model after it was adopted and used by traders, rather than the model merely describing prices that arose independently of it.

## Degrees of Performativity: From Generic to Counterperformativity

MacKenzie later distinguished between varying degrees of the phenomenon of performativity. "Generic performativity" is when an economics is simply used in an actual economic practice. "Effective performativity" is when the use of the theory makes an actual difference in practice as a result of its use specifically. "Barnesian performativity" (self-fulfilling) is the strongest case, in which use of the theory makes reality closer to its assumptions (as in the case of Black-Scholes). Finally, "counterperformativity" is when the wide-scale use of a particular theory undermines the very conditions that it assumes, so that the theory invalidates itself by its spread (MacKenzie's main illustration concerns the October 1987 crash, which portfolio insurance, a technique grounded in option theory, may have worsened; afterwards a "volatility skew" appeared in option prices that departed from the Black-Scholes-Merton assumption of constant volatility).

## Impact

This thesis made a major contribution to the field of the "social studies of finance." It offered a deeply influential theoretical framework that changed the sociological understanding of the relation between economic theory and actual economic reality, from a neutral external descriptive relation to an active constitutive-productive one. The thesis also influenced later research on the performativity of economic knowledge in other markets, such as carbon markets.

## Other Applied Cases

The approach has been applied well beyond options. Marie-France Garcia-Parpet's study of a computerised strawberry auction at Fontaines-en-Sologne, in France, first published in 1986, showed how an adviser versed in economic theory helped design a market whose rules and physical setting brought actual trading closer to the textbook model of competition; it is reprinted in English in *Do Economists Make Markets?* Francesco Guala examined the spectrum auctions run by the United States Federal Communications Commission in the 1990s, where game theorists worked with experimental economists and others to build and test an institution under which bidders would behave in the intended way. In carbon markets, MacKenzie studied how different greenhouse gases are made exchangeable through conventions that express them as equivalents of carbon dioxide, and how accounting rules shape what is traded. These cases show economic knowledge entering the design of markets, though how far theory itself, as opposed to law, politics and practical adjustment, explains the outcome remains debated.
