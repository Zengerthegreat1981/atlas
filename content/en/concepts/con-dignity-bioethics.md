# Dignity in Bioethics

## Meaning and Definition

Human dignity, in the context of bioethics, is the intrinsic and non-negotiable value that every human being possesses simply by being human, regardless of health status, mental capacity, or social usefulness. It serves as a governing criterion in questions of end-of-life care, experimentation on human beings, cloning, and disability. It is invoked to prevent treating a human being as a "mere means," whatever the nobility of the goal.

## Roots in Islamic Thought

The Holy Qur'an laid down an explicit foundation for this concept in the verse "We have certainly honored the children of Adam." This is a dignity that belongs to the whole human species, believer and unbeliever, healthy and sick alike, and is not conditioned on reason, usefulness, or religion. Jurists built detailed rulings on this principle in medicine. They forbade mutilating the corpse even after death, required covering the body's private parts even during medical examination except in cases of necessity, and differed on the ruling on dissection for medical teaching, weighing the sanctity of the dead against the benefit to the living. Contemporary bodies such as the International Islamic Fiqh Academy have issued resolutions on questions such as organ transplantation and cloning, and arguments in this field often appeal to the idea that human dignity is not lost in incapacity or coma and that preserving life includes respect for the body, alive and dead.

## Western Roots

Immanuel Kant provided the deepest modern philosophical foundation for this concept, distinguishing between "price" (*Preis*), which can be replaced by an equivalent, and "dignity" (*Würde*), which has no substitute and cannot be measured against anything. In the second formulation of the categorical imperative, he established the duty to treat humanity, both in ourselves and in others, always as an end in itself and never merely as a means. Dignity is invoked in the preamble of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), and the Nuremberg Code (1947) laid foundations for research ethics on human subjects after the Nazi medical experiments, in which prisoners were used as mere research instruments. Leon Kass argued, in his essay on the "wisdom of repugnance" (1997), that revulsion at practices such as human cloning can signal a violation of human dignity.

## Objections and Examples

Critics such as Ruth Macklin ("Dignity Is a Useless Concept," 2003) object that dignity is vague and adds nothing to respect for autonomy, and that it may be invoked on both sides of debates such as euthanasia. As an illustration: a patient in the final stage of illness has lost consciousness and is no longer capable of any beneficial act. Nevertheless, the principle of dignity requires treating him with gentleness and respect, and forbids using his body for an experiment without the consent of his relatives, because his dignity does not depend on his consciousness or usefulness.
