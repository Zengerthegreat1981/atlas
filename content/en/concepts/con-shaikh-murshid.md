# The Master and Sufi Guidance

The guiding master (*shaykh murshid*) in Sufism is the one who takes the disciple by the hand along the path, teaching him the etiquette, watching over his spiritual states and revealing to him the defects of his soul. The idea derives from an old experience. In the *ribats* and *khanqahs* that arose from the third and fourth centuries AH, disciples gathered around a master, and the etiquette of this companionship was set down by al-Sulami (d. 412 AH/1021 CE) in *Adab al-Suhba* and by Abu al-Najib al-Suhrawardi (d. 563 AH/1168 CE) in *Adab al-Muridin*.

## The Disciple Before the Master

Menahem Milson translated *Adab al-Muridin* into English under the title *A Sufi Rule for Novices* (1975). It describes life in the khanqah: service, surrender of one's will, seeking permission in matters, adherence to litanies (*awrad*), and attendance at spiritual audition (*sama'*) only with the master's permission. The Sufis compared the disciple before the master to the corpse before the one who washes it. Al-Qushayri, in his *al-Risala*, mentions that whoever has no master does not prosper, and he cites Abu Yazid al-Bistami's saying: "Whoever has no master, his imam is Satan."

## The Functions of the Master

The master is expected to be a scholar of the Sharia, one who has realized the path, and one who is perceptive about souls. *'Awarif al-Ma'arif* by 'Umar al-Suhrawardi (d. 632 AH/1234 CE) sets out what is required of the master and his etiquette toward the disciple. Clarke translated it into English under the title *A Dervish Textbook*, published in Calcutta in 1891. Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, enumerated the conditions of the guide and distinguished between one who acquires morals through companionship and one who is content with books.

## The Chain and Authorization

The master is tied to the chain (*silsila*). Every master's chain connects back to the Prophet through the masters of the order, and the disciple wears the *khirqa* (the Sufi patched cloak) as a sign of his affiliation. In this way the individual relationship turned into an institution. This development was studied by J. Spencer Trimingham in *The Sufi Orders in Islam* (1971), which traces the transformation of companionship into organized orders.

## Criticism

The principle was not free from objection. Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 597 AH/1201 CE), in *Talbis Iblis*, warned against disciples' exaggeration regarding their masters and regarded some of these practices as deception (*talbis*). Ibn Taymiyya criticized absolute obedience to the master where it contravenes the Sharia. Modern reformist scholars such as Muhammad Abduh and Rashid Rida saw it as a cause of social stagnation. Nor is it correct, according to authentic Sufis, to regard the master as an intermediary between the servant and his Lord: they say he is a guide, not an intercessor.

## Limits of the Concept

The form of guidance varies between orders. It is stricter in some and less so in others, and some schools permit more than one master. In comparative studies, the master is compared with the Indian *guru* or the Orthodox *starets*, but the comparison does not presuppose historical influence.
