# The Master and Sufi Guidance

The guiding master (*shaykh murshid*) in Sufism is the one who takes the disciple along the path, teaching the etiquette, watching over spiritual states and pointing out the defects of the soul. From the third/ninth century, disciples gathered around masters in circles and in some *ribats*. The *khanqah* as a residential lodge became common later. The etiquette of this companionship was set down by al-Sulami (d. 412 AH/1021 CE) in *Adab al-Suhba* and by Abu al-Najib al-Suhrawardi (d. 563 AH/1168 CE) in *Adab al-Muridin*.

## The Disciple Before the Master

Menahem Milson translated *Adab al-Muridin* into English as *A Sufi Rule for Novices* (1975). The rule covers service, surrender of one's own preference, asking leave, keeping to litanies (*awrad*), and attending spiritual audition (*sama'*) only with the master's permission. A later and widespread saying compares the disciple before the master to the corpse in the hands of the washer. Another, widely attributed to Abu Yazid al-Bistami, says that he who has no master has Satan as his leader. That sentence is not securely placed in al-Qushayri's *Risala*. The *Risala* does treat the shaykh's direction as part of the novice's discipline.

## The Functions of the Master

The master is expected to know the Sharia, to have travelled the path, and to be perceptive about souls. *'Awarif al-Ma'arif* by Umar al-Suhrawardi (d. 632 AH/1234 CE) sets out what is required of the master and his conduct toward the disciple. H. Wilberforce Clarke's English version, *The 'Awarifu-l-Ma'arif* (Calcutta, 1891), was made from Mahmud al-Kashani's Persian rendering and covers part of the book. A 1980 reprint is titled *A Dervish Textbook*. Al-Ghazali, in the *Ihya' ulum al-din*, discusses the need for a guide and the limits of learning the path from books alone.

## The Chain and Authorization

The master is tied to a chain (*silsila*). Orders present that chain as running back to the Prophet, and the disciple may receive the *khirqa* (the patched cloak) as a sign of affiliation. Historians treat these chains as the orders' own claims about transmission. The individual relationship thus became an institution. J. Spencer Trimingham, in *The Sufi Orders in Islam* (1971), traced the passage from companionship to organized orders.

## Criticism

The principle drew objection. Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 597 AH/1201 CE), in *Talbis Iblis*, warned against disciples' exaggeration of their masters and treated some of these practices as deception (*talbis*). Ibn Taymiyya criticized absolute obedience where it contravenes the Sharia. Rashid Rida, in *al-Manar*, attacked the authority of the orders' shaykhs as a support of uncritical custom. Muhammad Abduh criticized popular practices that grew up around masters, while still speaking of Sufism as ethical discipline. Sober manuals describe the master as a guide whose direction remains subject to the Sharia. Other currents have treated him as a spiritual intermediary (*wasita*).

## Limits of the Concept

The form of guidance varies between orders. It is stricter in some and less so in others, and some schools permit more than one master. In comparative studies, the master is compared with the Indian *guru* or the Orthodox *starets*, but the comparison does not presuppose historical influence.
