# Creative Destruction

**Creative destruction** is a concept popularised by the economist **Joseph Schumpeter** in his book *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942), where it is the title of a chapter. Schumpeter credited Karl Marx with treating capitalism as an evolutionary process. Related images of destruction accompanying creation appear in Werner Sombart's *Krieg und Kapitalismus* (1913), but Sombart did not use Schumpeter's later phrase, and Schumpeter's emphasis on the entrepreneur as innovator (developed in *The Theory of Economic Development*, 1911, in German) differs from Marx's analysis of capital accumulation and the displacement of older means of production. It describes a mechanism inside capitalist economies: successive waves of technological and organizational innovation that displace existing industries, skills and firms as they create new ones. Capitalist dynamics therefore cannot be understood through a single, fixed, isolated price moment, but through a continuous "storm" of simultaneous destruction and construction.

## A critique of static analysis of competition

Schumpeter argued that the standard neoclassical model of perfect competition errs when it evaluates the efficiency of a market by a static criterion (does price equal marginal cost at a given moment?). It thereby sets aside what Schumpeter took to be a main source of long-run gains: **radical innovation** that reshapes an industry, not small marginal adjustments within a stable industrial structure. From this perspective, markets that appear "inefficient" by the static standard of perfect competition (because they contain large firms with temporary market power) may in fact be more dynamic and more productive of genuine innovation than fragmented markets that lack sufficient financial incentives to invest in costly research and development.

## The mechanism: temporary extraordinary profit

Creative destruction works through a recurring cycle. An innovative entrepreneur introduces a new product or production method that gives him an extraordinary competitive advantage and a temporary profit exceeding the prevailing normal return. This profit attracts competitors who imitate the innovation or develop a better alternative. The original competitive advantage is then gradually eroded by this growing imitation, and the old firms, technologies and skills that failed to keep up with the new innovation disappear, while resources (capital and labour) move toward the new emerging sector. This cycle never fully stops in a vibrant capitalist economy, because another innovation begins that in turn destroys the dominance achieved by the previous one.

## Illustrative historical examples

Schumpeter cited examples from the history of capitalism, such as the railway, electric power and the mechanised factory, which displaced industries and occupations based on earlier technologies, while creating new industries and jobs on a large scale that had not existed before. The concept was later applied to more recent technological waves (the personal computer, the internet, the smartphone) as repeated examples of the same pattern of simultaneous destruction and construction.

## From Schumpeterian insight to the evolutionary model

**Richard Nelson** and **Sidney Winter** reformulated Schumpeter's insight about creative destruction within a rigorous analytical mathematical framework in their book *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change* (1982). They borrowed a biological analogy: less efficient organizational "routines" are replaced by more successful ones through a process of competitive selection in the market. This translated Schumpeter's original intuition into models that can be simulated computationally. The idea also underlies the Schumpeterian growth models of Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt (1992).

## Impact and reception

The term "creative destruction" is widely used outside academic economics, including in descriptions of labour-market disruption caused by automation and digital transformation. Some researchers have academic reservations about its occasional use to justify real social costs (unemployment and occupational displacement) as an acceptable inevitable price of progress, without adequately addressing how these costs and gains are distributed.
