# Secondary Group

## Definition

A secondary group is a relatively large, impersonal, and formally organised social group whose members interact on the basis of specific roles and purposes rather than intimate personal ties. People join secondary groups to achieve particular goals — to work, to study, to obtain services, to pursue a shared interest — and their relationships within the group are typically segmental: each person is known by their function, not by their whole personality. A corporation, a university, a political party, a trade union, a hospital, and a government agency are all secondary groups.

The concept is defined in contrast to the primary group — the family, the circle of close friends, the childhood play group — where relationships are personal, enduring, and valued for their own sake. In a primary group, people interact as whole persons; in a secondary group, they interact as occupants of roles. Secondary groups can still inspire loyalty, pride, and deep commitment, and friendships often form inside them. But the group's organising principle is not personal attachment: it is a shared task, a set of rules, and a division of labour. Membership is usually voluntary and often temporary, and it is governed by formal procedures rather than by affection or custom alone.

## Origins and Development

The concept of the primary group was introduced by Charles Horton Cooley in *Social Organization* (1909); its counterpart, the secondary group, became a standard label among later sociologists for the larger, more formal associations that Cooley contrasted with it. Cooley observed that in small, intimate groups — above all the family and the neighbourhood play group — the individual develops a sense of self, a "looking-glass self" formed through the imagined judgements of others. Secondary groups, by contrast, are the larger associations of modern life, held together by interest rather than intimacy. The distinction gave sociology a vocabulary for describing what modernisation had done to social life: as societies grew larger and more complex, secondary relations multiplied.

Cooley's distinction built on an earlier contrast drawn by Ferdinand Tönnies in *Gemeinschaft und Gesellschaft* (1887). Tönnies distinguished community (*Gemeinschaft*), bound by natural will, kinship, and custom, from association (*Gesellschaft*), bound by rational will, contract, and self-interest. The secondary group is the sociological heir of *Gesellschaft*: the form of association characteristic of urban, industrial, bureaucratic society.

Later sociologists elaborated the concept. Louis Wirth, in "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938), argued that the city is the natural habitat of secondary relations: size, density, and heterogeneity make impersonal, segmental contact the normal mode of urban life. Talcott Parsons, in *The Social System* (1951), incorporated the distinction into his theory of social action, treating the shift from particularistic, affective relations to universalistic, affectively neutral ones as a defining feature of modernisation. The concept also fed into the study of bureaucracy and formal organisations, where the impersonality of the secondary group is both a virtue — it allows fair, rule-governed treatment — and a source of alienation.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns whether secondary groups are becoming dominant at the expense of primary groups. Early sociologists worried that urbanisation and industrialisation were dissolving intimate ties, leaving individuals isolated in a world of strangers. Later research qualified this fear: people in modern cities maintain rich primary relations, often by choice rather than by birth, and secondary groups frequently serve as the settings in which new primary ties form. The workplace, the university, and the voluntary association are not alternatives to friendship but among its most important sources.

A second dimension is the question of control and conformity. Secondary groups govern their members through formal rules, hierarchies, and sanctions rather than through the informal pressure of face-to-face opinion. This makes them powerful instruments of coordination — a hospital can organise hundreds of strangers into a functioning team — but also raises questions about bureaucracy, dehumanisation, and the loss of autonomy. Max Weber's analysis of bureaucracy remains the classic statement of this ambivalence: rational organisation is efficient precisely because it is impersonal, and impersonal precisely because it is rational.

A third dimension is the internal diversity of secondary groups. Some are almost purely instrumental, such as a queue or a market; others, such as a political movement or a religious congregation, demand deep commitment and generate strong collective identities. Sociologists therefore distinguish between the form of the group — large, formal, role-based — and the intensity of attachment it can sustain. A secondary group need not be a shallow one.

## Contemporary Relevance

Secondary groups dominate contemporary social life, and their study has taken on new urgency with the rise of digital platforms. Online communities, professional networks, and gig-work platforms are secondary groups of a novel kind: large, formal, and role-based, yet capable of generating intense identification and rapid collective action. The workplace itself is changing, as remote work, short-term contracts, and platform labour make secondary relations more transient and more mediated. At the same time, the COVID-19 pandemic revealed how much ordinary life depends on secondary groups — schools, hospitals, delivery networks — and how fragile they can be when their formal routines break down. Understanding secondary groups remains essential to understanding modern society: they are the structures through which most of our collective life is now organised, and the quality of that organisation — fair or arbitrary, humane or alienating — is one of the central questions of contemporary sociology.
