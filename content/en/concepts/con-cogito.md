# The Cogito: I Think, Therefore I Am

"I think, therefore I am" (Latin *cogito, ergo sum*; French *je pense, donc je suis*) is the best-known formulation of René Descartes. It first appears in French in the *Discourse on the Method* (1637, Part IV), in Latin in the *Principles of Philosophy* (1644, I.7), and in the *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641) in a different form, "I am, I exist" (*ego sum, ego existo*), in the Second Meditation.

## Definition

The Cogito is the first certain truth in Descartes's philosophy, reached by way of methodical doubt. Descartes doubts the senses, then the difference between waking and dreaming, and then even mathematics, supposing a powerful deceiver who might mislead him about everything. But even a deceived person must exist in order to be deceived, so the proposition "I am, I exist" is true whenever he states it or thinks it. Whoever doubts is thinking, and so the doubter cannot coherently doubt his own existence.

## Formulations and Interpretation

The *Meditations* do not contain the words *cogito, ergo sum*, and Descartes in the Second Replies and elsewhere denies that it is a syllogism deduced from a major premise ("whatever thinks exists"). He presents it as a simple intuition of the mind. Scholars still debate whether it is an inference, an intuition or a performative truth established by the act of thinking (the interpretation associated with Jaakko Hintikka). The Cogito also establishes only that a thinking thing exists; the further claim that the self is a distinct immaterial substance is argued separately.

## Philosophical Dimensions

- **Certainty**: the Cogito is marked by clarity and distinctness, the features Descartes later makes the criterion of truth.
- **Subjectivity**: the first-person standpoint becomes the starting point of knowledge, which is why the Cogito is often taken as a beginning of modern epistemology.
- **Independence from authority**: the first truth is reached by individual reason rather than from tradition or Church authority.

## Precedents

Augustine argued against the skeptics that "if I am mistaken, I am" (*si fallor, sum*; *City of God* XI.26; also *On the Trinity* X). Antoine Arnauld recalled an Augustinian parallel in the Fourth Objections; and in a letter of November 1640 to Andreas Colvius, who had drawn his attention to a passage of Augustine, Descartes noted that he used the point for a different purpose, to show that the thinking self is immaterial. Ancient and medieval skepticism, including the Academic and Pyrrhonian traditions, formed part of the background against which the doubt was framed.

## Objections and Criticism

- **Gassendi and Hobbes** objected in the Objections to the *Meditations* that the argument does not show what kind of thing the "I" is; Hobbes held that thinking presupposes something material that thinks.
- **Malebranche** held that we know the mind less clearly than Descartes claimed and that we see ideas in God.
- **Hume** found in introspection only a bundle of perceptions and no simple self (*A Treatise of Human Nature*, 1739-40).
- **Kant** argued in the Paralogisms of the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787) that "I think" accompanies all representations but gives no knowledge of the soul as a substance.
- **Lichtenberg and Nietzsche** questioned the grammar of "I think"; Nietzsche (*Beyond Good and Evil*, section 17) says a thought comes when "it" wishes, not when "I" wish.
- **Freud** held that the ego is "not master in its own house", since much of mental life is unconscious.
- **Heidegger** argued that the Cartesian subject leaves unasked the question of the being of the thinker, and treated the Cogito as a key step in the modern metaphysics of the subject.
- **Foucault** (*History of Madness*, 1961) read Descartes as excluding madness from reason, a reading challenged by Derrida in "Cogito and the History of Madness" (1963). Later poststructuralist thinkers, including Deleuze, criticised the Cartesian subject.

## Influence

The Cogito set the agenda for much of early modern philosophy, from the rationalists (Spinoza, Leibniz) to the empiricists (Locke, Hume) and Kant. William James, in *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), proposed that "the thoughts themselves are the thinkers", replacing the substantial self with the passing stream of thought. Debates on self-knowledge and the first-person perspective continue in contemporary philosophy of mind.
