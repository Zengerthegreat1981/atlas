# Being and Becoming

Sartre's dictum "existence precedes essence" means humans are not fixed essences but ongoing processes of self-creation. We are never merely a "self"; we are always becoming. Yet we also exist now, in a given state, with a past we cannot undo.

## Definition and Origins

The contrast between being and becoming is one of the oldest in philosophy, running from Parmenides' stable being and Heraclitus' perpetual flux to Hegel's dialectic, in which being and nothing are united in becoming. Existentialism gives it a personal form. Kierkegaard described the self as a relation that must relate itself to itself and to possibility; Heidegger described *Dasein* as always ahead of itself, projecting possibilities while thrown into a situation it did not choose; Sartre, in *Being and Nothingness* (1943), described human reality as "what it is not and not what it is," because consciousness is never identical with a fixed state.

## The Paradox

The existential paradox is that we are both being (present facticity) and becoming (future possibility). In therapy, this tension appears when someone says "I am not the type of person who..." (claiming a fixed essence) or "I could never change" (denying possibility). The opposite error also occurs: believing one can become anything and ignoring one's past, body, and circumstances. The existential response is to hold both: your past is real (being) and your future is open (becoming).

## In Practice

This distinction matters clinically. Someone stuck in depression may need to acknowledge their present state without denial, while also recognizing that different choices remain possible. Someone defensive may need to accept their habitual patterns and explore what lies beyond them. Rollo May emphasized "becoming" in his discussions of will and intentionality, and Carl Rogers' notion of the person as a process rather than a product is a close humanistic relative. Existential therapists often attend to the language clients use, noticing where "I am" statements harden into labels and where "I will" statements are avoided.

## Becoming as Authentic Process

Authenticity is not achieving a final true self but an ongoing process of consciously engaging in self-creation. You are always in the process of becoming; the question is whether that becoming is conscious and chosen or unconscious and reactive. Van Deurzen and others describe this as a lifelong task of taking up one's possibilities in the light of one's limits, including mortality.

## Criticisms and Limits

Critics argue that the strong version of "existence precedes essence" underplays biological, developmental, and social determinants of personality, and that the rhetoric of constant self-creation can become a burden or even an ideology of self-improvement. Others note that psychological research finds considerable stability in temperament and personality alongside change, so that the balance of being and becoming is an empirical as well as a philosophical matter. A balanced existential view does not deny stability; it denies that stability is destiny.
