# Being and Becoming

Jean-Paul Sartre's foundational principle "existence precedes essence" encapsulates the existential proposition that human beings possess no static, predetermined nature. Instead, human life is an ongoing process of self-definition and becoming. At the same time, human beings exist at any given moment in a concrete, situated condition—with an unalterable history, a biological embodiment, and an immediate social context. The dialectic between being (present facticity) and becoming (future possibility) constitutes a core tension of human agency.

## Philosophical Foundations and Origins

The contrast between being and becoming ranks among the oldest debates in Western philosophy:
- **Parmenides** posited that true reality is unchanging, immutable, and unitary being (*einai*).
- **Heraclitus** argued that permanence is an illusion and that reality consists of continuous flux and dynamic becoming (*panta rhei*).
- **G. W. F. Hegel** synthesized these poles in *Science of Logic* (1812), showing that abstract Being (*Sein*) and Nothing (*Nichts*) pass dialectically into one another through the dynamic category of Becoming (*Werden*).

Existential philosophy translated this metaphysical dispute into an existential description of human selfhood:
- **Søren Kierkegaard** defined the self in *The Sickness Unto Death* (1849) not as a static entity, but as a dynamic relation that relates itself to itself and to possibility.
- **Martin Heidegger** in *Being and Time* (1927) described *Dasein* as "ahead-of-itself" (*sich vorweg*), projecting into future possibilities while already thrown into a factual, historical world (*Geworfenheit*).
- **Jean-Paul Sartre** in *Being and Nothingness* (1943) captured the tension by characterizing human consciousness as "a being which is what it is not and which is not what it is," meaning that consciousness is never reducible to its past or current state.

## The Existential Paradox

The existential dilemma is that human beings are simultaneously rooted in being (unalterable past, physical constraints, historical situatedness) and propelled into becoming (the open horizon of future choice).

In psychological and clinical contexts, psychological distress often reflects a collapse into one of two defensive extremes:
1. **Fixation on Being (Essence)**: The individual treats themselves as fixed, static, and powerless to change ("I am naturally depressed," "I could never do that," "This is how I was raised"). This defense denies future possibility and disowns agency.
2. **Denial of Facticity (Unbounded Becoming)**: The individual fantasizes that they can become anything at will, disowning physical limitations, biological age, interpersonal obligations, and the historical consequences of previous choices.

Existential psychotherapy insists on holding both poles in dynamic balance: the past is unchangeable and real (being), yet how one relates to and acts from that past remains an open, creative task (becoming).

## Clinical and Therapeutic Significance

This distinction carries immediate implications in psychotherapeutic practice:
- **Overcoming Rigid Self-Labels**: Therapists help clients notice where rigid "I am" formulations (e.g., "I am an anxious person") harden into deterministic identities that forestall change, gently reframing them into open-ended questions of intentionality.
- **Humanistic Parallels**: Carl Rogers similarly defined the healthy individual as a "person in process" rather than a finished product (*On Becoming a Person*, 1961), emphasizing that personal growth requires letting go of static self-concepts to experience continuous flow.
- **Rollo May and Intentionality**: In *Psychology and the Human Dilemma* (1967), Rollo May argued that intentionality bridges being and becoming, transforming passive experiencing into active commitment.

## Becoming as an Authentic Process

Authenticity is not the discovery of a pre-existing "true self," but an ongoing, reflective engagement in self-creation. Emmy van Deurzen frames this as a lifelong task: taking up possibilities within the inescapable limits of human existence (including physical vulnerability and finitude). Human beings are perpetually in the process of becoming; the central question is whether that trajectory is lived with conscious ownership or endured as an unreflective reaction to external circumstance.

## Criticisms and Contemporary Perspectives

Philosophical and psychological critics note that radical assertions of "existence precedes essence" can downplay biological, developmental, and socio-economic determinants of human behavior. Longitudinal personality psychology demonstrates substantial temporal stability in temperamental traits (such as the Five-Factor Model), illustrating that becoming occurs within biologically and culturally bounded parameters. A contemporary existential perspective does not deny personality stability; it insists that biological and psychological traits provide the palette, not the final composition, of a person's life choices.
