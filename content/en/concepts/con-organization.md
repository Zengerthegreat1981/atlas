# Organization

An organization is a formally structured group of people coordinated to pursue explicit goals. Corporations, hospitals, schools, government agencies, armies, and voluntary associations are all organizations, and together they dominate modern life: most people are born in one, educated in another, work in a third, and are governed by many more. The sociology of organizations asks how such entities are put together, how they exercise authority, why they behave in ways no single member intends, and how they change — or resist change.

## Definition

Sociologists define an organization by several recurring features. It has a defined membership with boundaries that distinguish insiders from outsiders. It has a division of labour: tasks are specialised and assigned to roles rather than to particular persons. It has a hierarchy of authority, with rules governing who may decide what. And it pursues goals that are stated, at least officially, and that justify its existence. Max Weber's concept of bureaucracy captures the ideal type: administration by written rules, files, salaried officials, and impersonal procedures. Real organizations rarely match the ideal type exactly — they have informal networks, unwritten norms, and goals that drift — but the gap between formal design and lived practice is itself one of the field's central findings.

Organizations must also be distinguished from neighbouring forms. A crowd is a group without structure; a network is structured but lacks formal membership and hierarchy; an institution is a broader pattern of rules and meanings, such as the family or the market, within which many organizations operate. What makes an organization distinctive is the deliberate attempt to coordinate action through formal design.

## Origins and Development

The study of organizations grew out of the management revolution of the early twentieth century. Frederick Winslow Taylor's *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911) promised to rationalise factory work through time study and standardised tasks, treating the worker as an element of a production system. Weber, writing at the same time, analysed bureaucracy as the administrative expression of rational-legal authority and predicted its spread across the modern world; *Economy and Society*, published posthumously in 1922, remains the foundational text.

Mid-century scholarship complicated this picture. Chester Barnard's *The Functions of the Executive* (1938) argued that organizations survive only by securing the willing cooperation of their members, making informal communication and moral leadership central. Philip Selznick's study of the Tennessee Valley Authority, *TVA and the Grass Roots* (1949), showed how organizations are "infused with value" and co-opted by their environments, drifting from their official missions. James March and Herbert Simon's *Organizations* (1958) replaced the image of the perfectly rational actor with bounded rationality: decision-makers satisfice within limited information, and organizations develop routines and standard procedures to cope with uncertainty.

## Key Debates

One enduring debate concerns rationality versus informality. Formalists stress rules, structure, and design; others stress the informal organization — the friendships, shortcuts, and tacit bargains through which work actually gets done. Elton Mayo's Hawthorne studies suggested that attention and group norms mattered as much as formal incentives, and later research confirmed that informal relations can either sustain or sabotage official goals.

A second debate concerns power. Are organizations neutral tools for achieving goals, or instruments of domination? Critical sociologists argue that hierarchies reproduce class, gender, and racial inequality, concentrating decisions at the top and disciplining those below. Others note that organizations also empower: they allow large-scale cooperation, provide livelihoods and identities, and can be arenas of democratic participation, as in cooperatives and professional associations.

A third debate, institutional theory, asks why organizations in the same field come to look alike. Paul DiMaggio and Walter Powell's classic argument (1983) is that organizations adopt similar forms not because they are efficient but because of coercive pressure from regulators, imitation of successful peers under uncertainty, and professional norms. The result is isomorphism: schools, hospitals, and firms in very different places converge on the same structures. John Meyer and Brian Rowan (1977) added that organizations often adopt formal structures ceremonially, to signal legitimacy, while decoupling them from daily work.

## Contemporary Relevance

Organizations today face pressures their classical theorists did not anticipate. Digital platforms coordinate labour without employing it, blurring the boundary of the organization itself. Remote work disperses members across space and time, weakening the informal ties that once held firms together. Algorithmic management replaces human supervisors with software that assigns tasks and monitors performance, reviving Taylor's dream of scientific control in a new form. At the same time, organizations are pressed to answer for their social and environmental consequences, from climate emissions to supply-chain labour. The sociology of organizations remains the toolkit for understanding these shifts: it reminds us that behind every platform, policy, and performance metric stands a structure of roles, rules, and power — and that structure, designed by people, can be redesigned.
