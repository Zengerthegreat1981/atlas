# Resilience

Resilience, existentially, is not bouncing-back but integrating suffering and loss into an ongoing life. It is born of having been broken: recognizing one can sustain meaning despite—and sometimes through—adversity.

## Resilience Through Meaning

Frankl's concentration camp research identified a consistent marker: resilience correlated with ability to find meaning in suffering. Those maintaining purpose (spiritual, familial, vocational) were more likely to survive.

This suggests resilience is not a fixed trait but existential practice—the daily choice to engage with life despite awareness of suffering and finitude. Someone continuing relationships, work, or creativity despite genuine confrontation with loss demonstrates resilience.

## The Difference from Optimism

Resilience is not optimism or positivity. Someone who has confronted loss and despair and continues nonetheless—not from false belief but from conscious choice about what matters—is more truly resilient than someone who has never faced that confrontation.

Van Deurzen emphasizes resilience requires accepting limitations. The person who resilently manages chronic illness does not deny it but incorporates it and finds ways to live fully within constraints.
