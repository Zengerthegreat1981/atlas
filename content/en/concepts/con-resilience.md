# Resilience

Resilience, in the existential sense, is not simply bouncing back but integrating suffering and loss into an ongoing life. It grows out of having been shaken: the recognition that one can sustain meaning despite, and sometimes through, adversity. This differs from, and complements, the psychological research tradition that studies how people adapt well after hardship.

## Definition

In developmental and clinical psychology, resilience usually means positive adaptation despite significant adversity. Ann Masten's well-known phrase "ordinary magic" (2001) captures the finding that resilience typically arises from common protective factors, such as supportive relationships, competence and a sense of agency, rather than rare personal gifts. The existential reading adds a question the research does not directly ask: what does the person make of what has happened, and what do they commit to afterward?

## Origins

Empirical interest grew from studies of children at risk. Norman Garmezy and others examined children who thrived despite adversity, and Emmy Werner and Ruth Smith followed a Kauai (Hawaii) birth cohort across decades (*Vulnerable but Invincible*, 1982), finding that a substantial portion of high-risk children developed into competent adults, often with the help of caring adults and other protective relationships. George Bonanno's research on bereavement and trauma (2004) found that many people show stable, healthy functioning after loss, which challenged the assumption that everyone must go through prolonged distress. In parallel, Tedeschi and Calhoun described "posttraumatic growth," in which some people report new appreciation, deeper relationships or changed priorities after struggle.

## Resilience Through Meaning

Viktor Frankl, drawing on his experience as a concentration camp prisoner and his later clinical work, argued that the ability to find meaning in suffering helps people endure (*Man's Search for Meaning*, first published in English in 1959 as *From Death-Camp to Existentialism*). He observed that those who kept a sense of purpose, whether spiritual, familial or vocational, seemed better able to bear extreme conditions. This was a witness's and clinician's observation rather than a controlled study, and it should be read as such.

On this view resilience is not a fixed trait but an existential practice: the repeated choice to engage with life while aware of suffering and finitude. Continuing relationships, work or creative effort after real confrontation with loss is an expression of it.

## The Difference from Optimism

Resilience is not optimism or positive thinking. A person who has faced loss and despair and continues, not from false belief but from a considered choice about what matters, may be more deeply resilient than someone who has never been tested. Emmy van Deurzen emphasizes that resilience requires accepting limitation: a person living with chronic illness does not deny it but incorporates it and finds ways to live fully within constraints. Yalom and May likewise treat the confrontation with limits as a source of depth rather than only a threat.

## In Practice

Therapists working from an existential perspective tend to help clients name what has been lost, explore what they still value, and identify commitments that remain possible. Practical approaches from resilience research, such as strengthening supportive relationships and building coping skills, are compatible with this and often used alongside it.

## Criticisms and Limits

Critics argue that the language of resilience can place the burden on individuals, implying that those who struggle lack character, while ignoring poverty, violence and other structural conditions. Evidence on posttraumatic growth is debated, partly because self-reported growth may not match actual functioning. Frankl's account is vulnerable to survivor bias, since those who did not survive cannot report. Resilience also varies across time and circumstances rather than being a stable possession, and people who are not coping well are not failing a test. Persistent distress after hardship deserves professional support, not an exhortation to be resilient.
