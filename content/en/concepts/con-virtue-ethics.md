# Virtue Ethics and the Realization of Human Happiness (Virtue Ethics / Eudaimonia)

A doctrine in normative ethics that holds that the first moral question is "what kind of person should I be?" and not "what action should I do?" Morality is measured neither by compliance with duty (Kant) nor by the calculation of consequences (utilitarianism), but by the building of character and the refinement of settled virtues, such as justice, courage, wisdom and temperance, as a path to human flourishing (eudaimonia), as Aristotle set it out in the *Nicomachean Ethics* (c. 350 BCE).

## The Aristotelian origin

For Aristotle virtue is a "settled state" (hexis) of character that arises by habit and practice, and directs choice toward the appropriate mean between two vices: courage is a mean between cowardice and rashness, and generosity between stinginess and extravagance. The mean is relative to the agent and the situation, not an arithmetic average. The moral virtues are completed by practical wisdom (phronesis), the capacity for sound judgment in particular situations. The ultimate end is flourishing (which was translated in Arabic as happiness), which is the activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life.

## In the Arab-Islamic heritage

Virtue ethics entered Arabic with the translation of Aristotelian ethics and the books of Platonism. Miskawayh (d. 1030 CE) wrote *Tahdhib al-akhlaq* on the model of the four virtues (wisdom, courage, temperance and justice) and the mean between extremes, and Constantine Zurayk translated it into English (1968). Nasir al-Din al-Tusi completed (1235) the *Akhlaq-i Nasiri* in a similar form. Al-Ghazali in the *Ihya' ulum al-din* merged the virtues with the purification of the soul and with the religious end, making otherworldly happiness the ultimate end. The Arab heritage is thus a living example of virtue ethics with a religious framework.

## The contemporary revival

Elizabeth Anscombe in "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) called for abandoning the concept of "duty", tied to a divine conception of law after the disappearance of its foundation, and for recovering the language of virtue. Alasdair MacIntyre developed in *After Virtue* (1981) the view that the crisis of modern morality arises from its separation from the "practices" and traditions that give the virtues their meaning. Rosalind Hursthouse presented in *On Virtue Ethics* (1999) a normative theory: the right action is what the virtuous person would do in the same circumstances. The idea turned into positive psychology: Peterson and Seligman (2004) classified six virtues (wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance and transcendence) and twenty-four "character strengths" after a survey of philosophical and religious traditions, among them Confucian, Aristotelian, Buddhist and Islamic. This list is a research tool for which questionnaires have appeared, but its predictive power is a matter of dispute.

## Limits and critique

- Practical guidance: "do what the virtuous person does" does not answer someone who does not yet know the virtuous person.
- Situationism: John Doris (*Lack of Character*, 2002) and Gilbert Harman (1999) held that social psychology shows the influence of the situation on behavior, which casts doubt on the existence of stable moral traits.
- Cultural relativity: lists of virtues differ between cultures.
- Competition: Kantianism and consequentialism offer answers to questions of justice and duties toward strangers that virtue ethics may not offer.
