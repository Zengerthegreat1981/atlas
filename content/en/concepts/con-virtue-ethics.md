# Virtue Ethics and the Realization of Human Happiness (Virtue Ethics / Eudaimonia)

A major tradition in normative moral philosophy which holds that ethics is fundamentally concerned with character and moral agency—answering the foundational question "What kind of person should I be?"—rather than primarily adjudicating rule-based obligations (Kantian deontology) or calculating outcome utility (consequentialism). Virtue ethics evaluates morality through the cultivation of enduring virtues (*aretai*)—such as justice, practical wisdom, courage, and temperance—as the indispensable path to human flourishing (*eudaimonia*), as canonically formulated by Aristotle in the *Nicomachean Ethics* (c. 350 BCE).

## The Classical Aristotelian Foundation

For Aristotle, ethical virtue (*ēthikē aretē*) is an acquired "settled disposition" or habitus (*hexis*) that arises through habituation (*ethos*) and practice, rather than being innate or purely intellectual:

- **The Doctrine of the Mean (*Mesotēs*)**: Moral virtue is a deliberate disposition choosing the mean between two corresponding vicious extremes of deficiency and excess. Courage (*andreia*) is the mean between cowardice (deficiency of confidence) and rashness (excess of confidence); temperance (*sōphrosynē*) is the mean between insensibility and self-indulgence. Crucially, the mean is not an arithmetic average, but is relative to the agent and the specific situation ("at the right times, about the right things, towards the right people, for the right end, and in the right way").
- **Practical Wisdom (*Phronēsis*)**: Moral virtues cannot function without the intellectual virtue of *phronēsis* (practical judgment), the capacity to discern what is good and fitting in concrete, variable circumstances.
- **Flourishing (*Eudaimonia*)**: The supreme human good (*telos*), traditionally translated as "happiness" but more accurately understood as objective human flourishing or living well. Aristotle defines *eudaimonia* as "an activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are several virtues, in accordance with the best and most complete, over a complete life."

## In the Arab-Islamic Philosophical Heritage

Classical Islamic philosophy enthusiastically integrated and refined Aristotelian virtue ethics:
- **Miskawayh (d. 1030 CE)**: In *Tahdhīb al-akhlāq* (*The Refinement of Character*), Miskawayh structured Islamic philosophical ethics around the four cardinal virtues: wisdom (*ḥikma*), courage (*shajā'a*), temperance (*'iffa*), and justice (*'adl*), systematically analyzing moral vices as deviations of excess or deficiency from the rational mean.
- **Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1235)**: In the *Akhlāq-i Nāṣirī* (*The Nasirean Ethics*), al-Tusi synthesized Miskawayh's Peripatetic ethics with Ibn Sina's psychology, providing a comprehensive taxonomy of virtue cultivation.
- **Abu Hamid al-Ghazali**: In the *Iḥyā' 'ulūm al-dīn* (*The Revival of the Religious Sciences*), al-Ghazali integrated the Peripatetic virtues with Quranic spiritual discipline (*tazkiyat al-nafs*). While adopting the schema of cardinal virtues and the mean, al-Ghazali subordinated worldly flourishing to otherworldly beatitude (*al-sa'āda al-ukhrawiyya*), grounding moral transformation in divine love and piety.

## The Contemporary Revival

- **G. E. M. Anscombe ("Modern Moral Philosophy", 1958)**: In a catalytic essay, Anscombe argued that modern deontological concepts of moral "obligation" and "duty" are incoherent survivors of a divine-law framework whose theological foundation has been largely abandoned in secular philosophy, urging a return to Aristotelian virtue and philosophical psychology.
- **Philippa Foot (*Natural Goodness*, 2001)**: Developed neo-Aristotelian ethical naturalism, arguing that moral virtues are natural perfections of human rational life, just as deep roots and health are natural perfections of plants and animals.
- **Alasdair MacIntyre (*After Virtue*, 1981)**: Diagnosed the crisis of modern moral discourse, arguing that moral concepts make sense only within teleological social "practices," the narrative unity of a single human life, and a living moral tradition.
- **Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*, 1999)**: Formulated virtue ethics as a complete normative theory of action: an act is morally right if and only if it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances.
- **Positive Psychology and the VIA Classification**: In empirical psychology, Christopher Peterson and Martin E. P. Seligman (*Character Strengths and Virtues*, 2004) classified six core virtues (wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence) and 24 character strengths based on an international survey of historical traditions. While widely used in psychological research, its cross-cultural universality remains an active area of empirical evaluation.

## Limits and Critiques

- **Action Guidance and Circularity**: Critics argue that virtue ethics fails to offer specific algorithmic decision procedures in complex moral dilemmas, and that defining right action by reference to the virtuous agent borders on circularity.
- **The Situationist Critique**: Drawing on empirical social psychology (such as the Milgram obedience experiments and the Darley–Batson Good Samaritan study), philosophers like John Doris (*Lack of Character*, 2002) and Gilbert Harman (1999) argued that human behavior is predominantly driven by subtle situational pressures rather than stable, global character traits. Virtue ethicists respond that Aristotelian *phronēsis* has always recognized the supreme importance of situational sensitivity.
