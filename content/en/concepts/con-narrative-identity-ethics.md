# Narrative Identity

"Narrative identity" refers to the idea that a person's moral identity is not understood as a fixed essence or a set of properties detached from time. It is understood instead as a "story" that the individual tells about himself, linking his past with his present and his expected future in a unified whole with meaning and a coherent purpose. On this view, a person's understanding of who he is, and of what his moral commitments are, cannot be separated from the narrative through which he constructs the meaning of his life and his place among actions and events.

## Roots in Islamic Thought: Self-Reckoning, Biography, and Accountability

Although the term itself was not formulated in the Islamic heritage, its content is strongly present in the tradition of moral self-reckoning (*muhasaba*) in the work of al-Harith al-Muhasibi. He made the daily examination of one's past actions, and their linking to future intentions, a fundamental pillar of the purification of the soul. In this way a person builds the "story" of his moral life through a connected narrative review. The art of biography (*sira*) and of biographical dictionaries (*tarajim*) in Islamic culture is likewise a practical embodiment of this idea. The life of a scholar or a companion is not narrated as scattered events, but as a story with a moral pattern that begins with origin and ends with conclusion, from which lessons are drawn ("so take warning, O you with insight"). This also connects with the concept of repentance (*tawba*), which presupposes rewriting the earlier chapter of the story within a new narrative that begins from the point of rupture and moves toward a later uprightness.

## The Origin of the Modern Concept: MacIntyre and Ricoeur

The Scot Alasdair MacIntyre is a leading figure in developing this concept, in *After Virtue* (1981). He argued that understanding any single human action requires placing it within a broader "narrative unity," namely the life of the agent. He held that virtue itself is realized only within a narrative tradition that gives actions their meaning and defines the ends that human life as a whole pursues. The Frenchman Paul Ricoeur developed the idea philosophically in *Oneself as Another*. He distinguished between "idem" identity, which is fixed sameness, and "ipse" selfhood, which is formed through narrative. He proposed that "emplotment" is the tool that makes the individual cohesive despite his changes over time.

## Its Moral Importance and Objections

The importance of this framework lies in explaining how an individual determines his own particular moral commitments. A person keeps a promise to a friend not only by applying a general rule, but because keeping faith is part of "my story," the story I want to tell about myself as an honest person. A significant objection has nevertheless been raised: is the capacity for narration itself conditioned on cognitive and linguistic abilities that some humans lack, such as those with severe dementia or infants? Would that deprive them of their moral identity? Proponents reply that narration here is not necessarily purely individual. Family and community may carry it on behalf of an individual unable to tell it, so that his identity remains preserved within the narrative of the group that surrounds him.

## Illustrative Example

When a person engages in ongoing charitable giving, he does not do so in isolation from his story. He may see himself as continuing his family's tradition of generosity, or as atoning for an earlier period of his life in which he felt selfish. The act of giving thus becomes a meaningful chapter within a broader moral narrative, not merely a response to an abstract duty.
