# Discrimination

Discrimination is unequal treatment on the basis of group membership — of race, sex, age, religion, disability, sexuality, or class. A landlord who refuses tenants because of their ethnicity, an employer who pays women less for the same work, a school that tracks minority children into lower streams: all are instances of the same pattern. Sociology treats discrimination not only as an act of prejudice by individuals but as a property of social arrangements: it can be produced by rules that never mention group membership, and it can persist even when prejudice declines.

## Definition

The classic distinction is between prejudice and discrimination. Prejudice is an attitude — a negative belief or feeling about a group. Discrimination is behaviour: the unequal treatment that follows, or that occurs without any explicit attitude at all. A second distinction separates direct from indirect discrimination. Direct discrimination treats people differently because of who they are. Indirect discrimination applies a rule that appears neutral but systematically disadvantages one group — a height requirement that excludes most women from a job, or a hiring test that reflects the cultural knowledge of one group only.

A third distinction separates individual, institutional, and structural discrimination. Individual discrimination is the act of a person: a slur, a refusal, a biased decision. Institutional discrimination is embedded in the policies and routines of an organisation: a bank's lending criteria, a police department's stop-and-search practices, a university's admissions formula. Structural discrimination is the accumulation of disadvantage across institutions and over time: the child of a redlined neighbourhood attends an underfunded school, earns less, and inherits less wealth, each step reinforcing the last. These levels interact: institutions are staffed by individuals, and structures are built from institutions.

## Origins and Development

The sociological study of discrimination began with the study of prejudice. Gordon Allport's *The Nature of Prejudice* (1954) synthesised the psychology of intergroup hostility, showing how categorisation, stereotyping, and scapegoating produce hostile attitudes, and proposing the "contact hypothesis": that prejudice declines when groups meet under conditions of equal status, common goals, and institutional support. Economists added a second strand. Gary Becker's *The Economics of Discrimination* (1957) modelled discrimination as a "taste" — employers, workers, or customers who prefer not to associate with a group pay a price for that preference, and competition should, in theory, drive it out. The persistence of discrimination despite competition suggested that taste was not the whole story, and later economists developed models of statistical discrimination, in which decision-makers use group membership as a proxy for unobserved qualities.

Field experiments transformed the study of discrimination from theory to measurement. Devah Pager's audit study (2003) sent matched pairs of job applicants — identical except for a criminal record, and differing by race — to apply for entry-level jobs in Milwaukee, showing that a criminal record penalised Black applicants far more than white ones, and that Black applicants without a record fared no better than white applicants with one. Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan (2004) sent thousands of fictitious résumés to employers, varying only the name: applicants with white-sounding names received fifty per cent more callbacks than those with Black-sounding names. These experiments made discrimination visible in a way that surveys could not, because they measured behaviour rather than attitudes.

## Key Debates

A first debate concerns causes. Is discrimination primarily a matter of individual prejudice, or of institutional design? Those who emphasise prejudice point to the persistence of hostile attitudes and the power of stereotypes. Those who emphasise institutions point to audit studies showing discrimination in settings where decision-makers sincerely deny prejudice, and to the role of rules, algorithms, and networks that reproduce inequality without anyone intending it. Most sociologists now hold both: prejudice and structure reinforce each other.

A second debate concerns measurement. Discrimination is hard to observe directly, because decision-makers rarely announce their motives. Audit studies, statistical analysis of disparities, and qualitative research each capture part of the picture, and each has limits: audits cannot reveal motive, statistics cannot fully control for all relevant differences, and qualitative accounts cannot establish scale. The strongest findings triangulate across methods.

A third debate is about remedies. Anti-discrimination law targets individual acts, but is ill-suited to structural disadvantage. Affirmative action and positive discrimination seek to correct accumulated inequality, but are contested on grounds of fairness and effectiveness. More recent proposals — blind recruitment, algorithmic audits, reparations, and investment in segregated neighbourhoods — reflect the shift from an individual to a structural understanding of the problem.

## Contemporary Relevance

Discrimination remains a central fact of social life, and its forms continue to evolve. Algorithmic decision-making — in hiring, lending, policing, and welfare — has created new vectors of discrimination, as models trained on biased data reproduce and amplify historical patterns. The language has also expanded: ageism, ableism, and discrimination against sexual minorities and migrants are now recognised alongside race and sex. At the same time, legal protections have widened in many countries, and the measurement tools developed by sociologists are now used by regulators and courts. Understanding discrimination sociologically means looking past the question of who is prejudiced to the question of how inequality is organised — and that is the question that must be answered if it is to be undone.
