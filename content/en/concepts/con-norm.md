# Norm

A **norm** is a shared expectation about how people ought, or ought not, to behave in a given situation, backed by approval, disapproval, or other sanctions. Norms range from small matters of etiquette, such as how to greet a stranger or wait in a queue, to deeply held prohibitions. They make conduct predictable and coordinate action, but they also distribute advantage and mark the boundary between those who belong and those treated as deviant. Sociologists distinguish a norm from a mere regularity of behaviour: many people may drink coffee in the morning without anyone expecting it of them. A norm is present when departure from the pattern is treated as something that calls for correction or justification. Norms are also usually distinguished from values, which are general ideas of what is desirable, whereas norms specify conduct in particular settings.

## Theoretical Genealogy

William Graham Sumner's *Folkways* (1906) distinguished folkways, customary ways of acting whose breach draws mild disapproval, from mores, which are held to be essential to group welfare and are enforced more strongly. Émile Durkheim treated rules of conduct as social facts that are external to individuals and exercise constraint over them (*The Rules of Sociological Method*, 1895), and argued that the type of law enforcing them varies with the form of social solidarity (*The Division of Labour in Society*, 1893). In *Suicide* (1897) he used the term anomie for a condition in which social regulation of desires breaks down.

Talcott Parsons, in *The Structure of Social Action* (1937) and *The Social System* (1951), made internalised norms and values central to his answer to the problem of social order: socialisation leads actors to want to do what is expected of them. Robert K. Merton's "Social Structure and Anomie" (1938) showed that strain can arise when culturally prescribed goals are not matched by access to legitimate means.

## Comparing Approaches

Dennis Wrong's article on the "oversocialized conception of man" (1961) criticised the functionalist picture for assuming that internalisation produces conformity almost automatically. Interactionist and ethnomethodological work shifted attention to how norms are interpreted and applied in practice: Harold Garfinkel's breaching experiments, reported in *Studies in Ethnomethodology* (1967), exposed taken-for-granted expectations by disrupting them. Howard Becker's *Outsiders* (1963) asked who makes rules and against whom they are enforced. Conflict theorists likewise stress that prevailing norms may reflect the interests of more powerful groups. Rational choice writers, such as James Coleman in *Foundations of Social Theory* (1990), ask how norms emerge when actors have an interest in controlling the behaviour of others, and who bears the cost of sanctioning.

## Empirical Examples

Erving Goffman's *Behavior in Public Places* (1963) analysed the unwritten rules governing attention among strangers, including what he called civil inattention. Norbert Elias's *The Civilizing Process* (1939) traced long-term changes in European standards of manners and self-restraint. In social psychology, Muzafer Sherif's experiments with the autokinetic effect, reported in *The Psychology of Social Norms* (1936), showed that people judging an ambiguous stimulus together converge on a common standard.
