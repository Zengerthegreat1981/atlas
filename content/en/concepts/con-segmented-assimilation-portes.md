# Segmented Assimilation

The idea that the children of contemporary immigrants face multiple, divergent paths of incorporation into the host society, and not a single inevitable linear path toward the middle class.

## Central Idea

Alejandro Portes, with Min Zhou in "The New Second Generation" (1993), argued that the children of immigrants (the second generation) face multiple paths of incorporation depending on structural factors: race (non-white immigrants face discriminatory barriers that traditional European immigrants did not), the economic context of reception (a labor market with shrinking opportunities for the middle class), and the structure of the immigrant community itself (the presence of a strong supportive community network can provide an alternative path to economic success without full cultural assimilation). This means that some children of immigrants assimilate upward into the middle class, while others assimilate "downward" into a racially marginalized urban underclass, and not a single inevitable path as the classical linear model assumed.

## The Protective Factor of "Ethnic Social Capital"

Among the most important factors Portes and Zhou identified is an unexpected protective one: the presence of a cohesive and economically strong ethnic community (such as the Cuban community in Miami or the Vietnamese community in some American cities) may protect the children of immigrants from the path of "downward assimilation." The community provides social capital, family supervision and alternative economic paths (such as working within the community's ethnic economy) that compensate for weak opportunities in the general economy, even while the family remains relatively distant from full cultural assimilation into the host society.

## The Classical Model Being Criticised

The classical account of assimilation, associated with the Chicago school of Robert Park and with Milton Gordon's *Assimilation in American Life* (1964), expected immigrants and their descendants to move progressively toward the cultural and economic mainstream over generations; this expectation is often called "straight-line" assimilation, a label discussed notably by Herbert Gans. Portes and Zhou argued that this picture fitted the experience of earlier European immigrants better than that of post-1965 immigrants, who arrived in an economy with a more polarised labour market and, in many cases, encountered racial discrimination.

## A Competing Theory: Alba and Nee

In *Remaking the American Mainstream* (2003), Richard Alba and Victor Nee offered a more optimistic view competing with Portes's thesis, arguing that assimilation, redefined as a gradual process that also changes the mainstream, remains a powerful pattern across generations for contemporary immigrants and their descendants.

## Impact

This theory became a central framework in contemporary American migration sociology, and informed debates on the integration of immigrants' children.

## Case Study Examples

Portes and Zhou drew on empirical research on the American second generation, illustrated by examples such as the difference between the children of Cuban immigrants in Miami, who benefited from a strong, cohesive community economy that enabled them to advance economically while remaining culturally close to their community of origin, and the children of some Haitian and Mexican immigrants who integrated into poor urban neighborhoods with a youth culture opposed to conventional school success (Oppositional Culture), which sometimes led them toward a path of downward assimilation despite their parents' original aspirations.
