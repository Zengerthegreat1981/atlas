# The Anti-Cult Movement (ACM) and Institutional Responses to NRMs

A term describing a heterogeneous coalition of voluntary organizations, affected families and professionals (some of them clinical psychologists) that arose in the United States and Western Europe from the late 1960s and the 1970s, in response to the spread of new religious movements (the Unification Church of Sun Myung Moon, ISKCON, Scientology). It generally holds that these movements use techniques of "brainwashing" or "mind control" that require intervention. Sometimes this took the form of forced "deprogramming" of adult members without their consent.

## Its Place in the Academic Debate

This current represents the opposing side to what the leading academic researchers in the field of new religious movements (Eileen Barker and James Beckford) concluded. They found that systematic empirical evidence does not support the deterministic "brainwashing" model (see `con-brainwashing-conversion-debate`). This does not mean that the motives of the anti-cult movement are insincere; many of them arose from families that had in fact suffered a sharp breakdown of communication with children who had joined these movements. Rather, the explanatory framework ("brainwashing" as a mechanism that wholly nullifies the will) is the object of academic dispute, not the sincerity of the suffering itself.

## Comparative Institutional Response in Beckford

In his comparative study of societal responses to new religious movements in Britain, France, West Germany and the United States, James Beckford showed that the degree of institutional panic (including the official support some European states gave to anti-cult organizations, in contrast to the more cautious American position given the constitutional protection of religious freedom) reflects the institutional structure of each society (the centralization of the state, the relationship of the established church to the state) more than it reflects the characteristics of the movement under dispute.

## Legal Outcome

Forced "deprogramming" in the United States gradually declined from the 1980s, after successful lawsuits brought by former members against those who had abducted them in order to deprogram them. The major American organization CAN (Cult Awareness Network) was dissolved in 1996 after going bankrupt following a series of lawsuits, including a large civil judgment in a deprogramming case (the Jason Scott case); Scientologists were prominent among the plaintiffs and litigants. Its name and assets were afterwards acquired by a Scientology-linked buyer. This institutional transformation is documented in the literature of the field.
