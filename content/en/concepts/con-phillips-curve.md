# Phillips Curve

The **Phillips curve** is a graphical analytical tool named after the New Zealand economist **William Phillips**, who published a statistical study in 1958. It showed a relatively stable inverse historical relationship in British data between the rate of growth of money wages and the unemployment rate: when unemployment fell, wage growth rose, and vice versa. The curve later became a central tool in the analysis of macroeconomic policy during the 1960s.

## An attractive trade-off for policymakers

Keynesian economists, notably **Paul Samuelson** and **Robert Solow** (1960), who restated the relationship in terms of general price inflation rather than wages alone, received the curve with great enthusiasm. It appeared to offer policymakers a clear "menu of choices": one could select a point on the curve offering a combination of higher inflation and lower unemployment, or the reverse, according to the government's political and social priorities at a given moment. This seemed a relatively simple practical solution to the dilemma of managing the macroeconomy.

## Friedman's critique: no stable trade-off in the long run

In his presidential address to the American Economic Association in December 1967, published in 1968, **Milton Friedman** criticised this interpretation and proposed the concept of the **natural rate of unemployment**. Any attempt to push unemployment below this rate through deliberate inflation succeeds only temporarily, because workers initially mistake higher nominal wages for higher real wages. Edmund Phelps reached a similar conclusion independently, in work published in 1967 and 1968. Over time, however, their inflation expectations adjust to the new reality, and they demand higher nominal wages reflecting the expected inflation. Unemployment then returns to its natural rate, but at a permanently higher inflation level on which the economy has settled. The conclusion is that there is no stable trade-off between inflation and unemployment, except in the very short run, before expectations adjust.

## The harsh empirical test: stagflation in the 1970s

The oil price shock of 1973 and what followed seemed to bear out the Friedman-Phelps critique. Western economies experienced **stagflation**: high inflation and high unemployment together, in contradiction of the simple trade-off the curve had suggested (supply shocks were also a major part of the story). This sharply undermined academic confidence in the traditional Keynesian version of the curve, and strengthened the credibility of the rising monetarist and new classical schools.

## The reformulation: the expectations-augmented Phillips curve

This critique led to the development of the **expectations-augmented Phillips curve**, which includes inflation expectations as an explicit variable in the equation. The original simple curve becomes a special short-run case within this broader framework. This formulation remains the basis for analysing the relationship between inflation and unemployment in contemporary macroeconomic models, though in a more complex and more cautious form than the optimistic early confidence of the 1960s.
