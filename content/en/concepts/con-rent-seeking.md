# Rent-Seeking

**Rent-seeking** is a concept formulated by the economist **Gordon Tullock** in his article "The Welfare Costs of Tariffs, Monopolies, and Theft" (1967). The term "rent-seeking" itself was coined by Anne Krueger in a later article (1974). It refers to the resources that individuals and firms spend in trying to obtain exclusive government privileges (licences, customs protection, monopoly contracts) instead of engaging in ordinary productive competition in the market to create new value.

## A Hidden Waste That Goes Beyond Deadweight Loss

Traditional analysis of the cost of monopolies focused on the **deadweight loss** resulting from a monopolist pricing its good above the competitive price. This is a loss of social welfare that is relatively precisely measured in standard textbooks. Tullock observed that there is sometimes a larger and less visible additional cost: the resources that individuals and firms actually spend in competing to obtain the monopoly privilege itself (bribes, lobbying campaigns, financing of political campaigns, and specialised legal work to secure licences). These resources are wholly wasted in this political competition, without creating any real additional productive value for society.

## Distinction from Legitimate Profit-Seeking

The concept distinguishes between two kinds of profit-seeking economic behaviour. The first is **profit-seeking** through innovation and improved productive efficiency, which creates new economic value from which society as a whole benefits, even if the actor himself benefits more. The second is **rent-seeking** through political pressure to win exclusive privileges, which redistributes value already in existence from one party to another without creating new value, and does so at an additional social cost in the form of resources wasted in the political competition itself. Borderline cases, such as research spending to win a patent race, are debated.

## Applications to Trade Protectionism

The concept is often used to analyse the cost of trade protectionism. When a domestic industry asks for customs protection from foreign competition, the social cost is not limited to higher prices for consumers. It also includes the resources that this industry spent on lobbying and political pressure campaigns to secure this protection in the first place. Those resources could have been directed to alternative productive activities had they not been drained by this political competition for the privilege.

## Its Relationship to Olson's Logic of Collective Action

The concept intersects methodologically with Mancur Olson's analysis of the logic of collective action. Small, concentrated groups with a stake in a particular issue (such as a specific industry) succeed in organising effective lobbies for rent-seeking. By contrast, the broad mass of affected taxpayers, despite their large numbers, fail to organise an opposition of comparable effectiveness, owing to the free-rider problem. This explains why many protectionist privileges persist politically despite their overall cost to society.

## Impact on the Analysis of Economic Corruption

The concept has become a standard tool in analysing economic corruption and rentier economies in many countries. It is used to explain the gap between the resources wasted in obtaining exclusive government licences and protections and the actual productive value realised. It has been invoked in debates about economies that rely in part on distributing exclusive government privileges (import licences, government contracts) instead of open, free competition.
