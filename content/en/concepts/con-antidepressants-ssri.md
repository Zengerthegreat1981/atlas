# Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors

SSRIs & SNRIs

## Definition

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are a class of antidepressants that block the serotonin transporter (SERT) in the presynaptic neuron, so that serotonin increases in the synaptic cleft. The most prominent members are fluoxetine, sertraline, paroxetine, citalopram, escitalopram and fluvoxamine. Serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), such as venlafaxine and duloxetine, block both transporters.

## History

The era of antidepressants began by chance in the 1950s. Mood elevation was noted in tuberculosis patients given iproniazid, and Nathan Kline then promoted it as an antidepressant. Roland Kuhn in Switzerland introduced the tricyclic imipramine in 1957. In 1965 Schildkraut formulated the "catecholamine hypothesis" of mood disorders in the *American Journal of Psychiatry*. Then came rational design: Arvid Carlsson and colleagues derived zimelidine in Sweden, and a team at Eli Lilly, including David Wong, Bryan Molloy, and Robert Fuller, developed fluoxetine, which the US Food and Drug Administration approved in December 1987 under the name Prozac. Venlafaxine was approved in the United States in 1993 and duloxetine in 2004.

## Efficacy and Controversy

Cipriani and colleagues, in a network meta-analysis published in *The Lancet* in 2018, compared twenty-one antidepressants for acute major depression in adults. All were more efficacious than placebo, differences among drugs were modest, and a few agents, including escitalopram, ranked relatively well on both response and dropout. The ranking is sensitive to which trials are included. However, Kirsch and colleagues showed in *PLoS Medicine* in 2008 that the difference from placebo shrinks in mild to moderate depression and grows in severe depression. The STAR*D study, whose results Rush and colleagues published in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 2006, followed outpatients through up to four sequential treatment steps and reported cumulative remission rates; later reanalyses have argued that the headline figures were too optimistic. An umbrella review published by Moncrieff, Cooper and colleagues in *Molecular Psychiatry* in 2022 provoked debate by stating that the evidence does not support explaining depression by a serotonin deficiency. This does not mean the drug is ineffective, since a drug's efficacy is a separate question from its mechanism of action.

## Uses and Effects

The indications are not limited to depression. They are among the first-line medications in generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, social anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder and bulimia nervosa. The clinical effect usually takes two to four weeks to appear. Common effects include nausea, insomnia, sexual dysfunction and, over the long term, weight gain. Risks include serotonin syndrome when combined with monoamine oxidase inhibitors, withdrawal symptoms upon abrupt discontinuation, and bleeding risk with antiplatelet drugs. In 2004 the US Food and Drug Administration added a boxed warning about the increased risk of suicidal thoughts in children and adolescents, a warning that must be weighed against the benefit of treatment in severe cases.

## Cultural Considerations and Limits

Approaches to healing vary across cultures, and this affects acceptance of medication and adherence to it, a topic addressed in the entries on stigma and healing traditions. The limits of these drugs are clear. A considerable proportion do not respond (see the entry on treatment-resistant disorders), and the psychosocial impact requires accompanying psychological treatment. Moreover, the commercial noise around medications and the expansion of diagnosis (as Horwitz and Wakefield criticize in *The Loss of Sadness*) call for caution in prescribing them for ordinary sadness.
