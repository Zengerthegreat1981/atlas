# Marriage Rules

Marriage rules are the norms that govern who may marry whom, and on what terms. Every society has them: some marriages are prescribed or preferred, some are prohibited, and some are permitted but discouraged. The rules may concern kinship — whether one should marry a cousin, and which cousin — or rank, religion, ethnicity, or wealth. Anthropologists study marriage rules not as a list of customs but as a system: a way in which societies organise alliance between groups, regulate sexuality and reproduction, and reproduce themselves across generations.

## Definition

The classic distinction is between positive rules, which say whom one should marry, and negative rules, which say whom one must not marry. Positive rules include prescribed or preferred marriage with a particular relative, such as a mother's brother's daughter or, more rarely, a father's sister's daughter, and rules of endogamy, which require marriage within a given group. Negative rules include exogamy, which requires marriage outside one's own group, and the prohibition of incest, which is universal in some form, though the definition of incest varies: the prohibition may cover close kin only, or extend to an entire clan or lineage. Marriage rules are also bound up with exchange: bridewealth, paid by the groom's family to the bride's, and dowry, brought by the bride, are not simply payments for a wife but transactions that create and publicly confirm the alliance between two families. The rules also specify who arranges the marriage, what ceremonies are required, and what rights each spouse gains over the other, over children, and over property.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological theory of marriage rules was transformed by Claude Lévi-Strauss's The Elementary Structures of Kinship, first published in French in 1949 and in English in 1969. Lévi-Strauss argued that the incest prohibition is not a biological instinct but the foundation of social life: by forbidding marriage within the group, it compels groups to exchange women and thereby creates alliance. Marriage, on this view, is fundamentally about relations between groups rather than between individuals. Rodney Needham, in Structure and Sentiment (1962), insisted on distinguishing prescriptive from preferential marriage, a distinction at the centre of a long dispute over the scope of Lévi-Strauss's theory; Needham's edited volume Rethinking Kinship and Marriage (1971) went on to question whether "kinship" and "marriage" were coherent comparative categories at all. Meanwhile, ethnographers documented the enormous variety of marriage systems: polygyny, polyandry, and monogamy; arranged marriage and marriage by choice; marriage as a single event and marriage as a gradual process completed only after the birth of children or the payment of bridewealth in full.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns the relationship between rules and practice. Rules state an ideal; people frequently marry otherwise. Anthropologists now attend to the gap between rule and behaviour, asking when rules are followed, when they are bent, and what people say when they break them. A second debate concerns gender. Early theories of marriage treated women as objects of exchange between male-headed groups. Gayle Rubin's essay "The Traffic in Women," published in 1975, argued that this exchange system was itself a structure of male dominance, and feminist anthropologists have since examined how women negotiate, resist, and benefit from marriage rules. Jane Collier and Sylvia Yanagisako showed that marriage cannot be understood apart from the wider system of gender and power in which it is embedded. A third debate concerns whether "marriage" is a single cross-cultural category at all. Some societies lack a clear equivalent of marriage, recognising instead a continuum of unions with different rights; others recognise same-sex marriages or marriages between women and women for purposes of inheritance, as documented in parts of Africa. Such cases have pushed anthropologists to define marriage by its social effects — the creation of recognised rights and alliances — rather than by its form.

## Contemporary Relevance

Marriage rules remain politically charged. Debates over same-sex marriage, interfaith marriage, and the legal recognition of customary and religious marriages turn on questions that anthropology has long studied: what counts as a marriage, who decides, and what rights follow from it. In many countries, customary marriage rules coexist uneasily with state law: a marriage valid under custom may not be recognised by the state, with consequences for inheritance, child custody, and citizenship. Migration adds further complexity, as families negotiate marriage choices across borders and between different legal and moral systems. Meanwhile, the decline of arranged marriage and the rise of "love marriage" in many parts of the world are often described as a simple move from tradition to modernity; ethnography shows a more complicated picture, in which young people combine romantic choice with family negotiation, and in which marriage rules adapt rather than simply disappearing. Understanding marriage rules as living systems, not as survivals of the past, is essential for making sense of these changes.
