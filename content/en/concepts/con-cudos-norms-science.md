# The CUDOS Norms of Science (Merton)

A set of ideal institutional norms formulated by Robert Merton to describe how the scientific community ought to operate as a social institution with its own internal system of moral control.

## Content

In a 1942 paper first published as "Science and Technology in a Democratic Order" (later retitled "A Note on Science and Democracy" and, in the 1973 collection, "The Normative Structure of Science"), Merton identified four ideal institutional norms governing the scientific community, which he wrote in response to the attack on science and its universalist ethos under Nazi Germany. The acronym "CUDOS" for the four norms is later and comes from Merton's own subsequent writing. His original term for the first norm was "communism" (in the sense of common ownership of findings), later usually rendered as "communalism." **Communalism**: the findings of scientific research, once published, become the common property of the whole scientific community and not the exclusive private property of the researcher who reached them, although he retains moral (not exclusive material) recognition of priority in the discovery. **Universalism**: scientific claims are evaluated by objective, impersonal criteria (method, evidence, logical consistency) regardless of the claimant's social, ethnic or national identity or institutional standing. **Disinterestedness**: the scientist seeks, institutionally at least, objective knowledge for its own sake and not for direct personal material or ideological gain that would distort his research results. **Organized Skepticism**: every scientific claim, regardless of its source or the standing of its author, is subjected to systematic critical scrutiny by peers before being accepted as reliable knowledge (the institutional peer review system).

## Impact and Critique

These norms founded the field of the sociology of science as an independent sub-discipline within sociology, offering a framework for analyzing science as a social institution with its own moral controls and not merely a neutral cognitive activity abstracted from social context. These ideal norms were later criticized from several directions. Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), though not a direct reply to Merton, is often read as showing that scientists commit strongly to prevailing "paradigms" in a way that sits uneasily with unrestricted organized skepticism. More directly, Ian Mitroff's study of Apollo moon scientists (1974) reported that working scientists also followed opposed "counter-norms" (such as secrecy and particularism), and Michael Mulkay (1976) argued that the norms function more as an ideology that scientists invoke than as rules that regulate their conduct. Later sociology of science, including constructivist studies, placed social, professional and power-related factors at the center of how scientific knowledge is accepted.
