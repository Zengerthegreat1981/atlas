# Dopamine Hypothesis

## Definition

The **dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia** is an influential neurobiological hypothesis holding that the symptoms of **schizophrenia** (especially the positive symptoms, such as hallucinations and delusions) are linked to a disturbance in the **neurotransmitter dopamine** system. Specifically, it involves **dopaminergic overactivity** in subcortical neural pathways (the mesolimbic pathway), alongside a possible dopaminergic deficit in other cortical areas (such as the prefrontal cortex) that is linked to negative and cognitive symptoms.

## Historical Context

The hypothesis is generally traced to the Swedish pharmacologist **Arvid Carlsson**, who with Margit Lindqvist reported in 1963 that chlorpromazine and haloperidol altered catecholamine metabolism in the brain, which led to the idea that antipsychotics act by blocking dopamine transmission. In the 1970s, work by Philip Seeman and by Creese, Burt, and Snyder showed that the clinical potency of antipsychotics correlates with their affinity for D2 receptors. A second line of evidence is that drugs that increase dopamine release (such as amphetamine) can produce psychosis-like symptoms in healthy people. Carlsson was later awarded the **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine** in 2000 (shared with Paul Greengard and Eric Kandel) for discoveries concerning signal transduction in the nervous system, including his work on dopamine as a neurotransmitter.

**Version I** held that dopamine transmission is generally excessive in schizophrenia. **Version II** (Davis and colleagues, 1991) distinguished subcortical (mesolimbic) dopamine overactivity, linked to positive symptoms, from a prefrontal cortical dopamine deficit, linked to negative and cognitive symptoms. **Oliver Howes** and **Shitij Kapur** reformulated the account in 2009 as a **third version**, titled "The Final Common Pathway." That version merged multiple genetic, environmental, and developmental risk factors into a single shared final pathway: presynaptic dopamine overactivity in the striatum.

## Key Dimensions

- **The "aberrant salience" framework**: the third version of the hypothesis links the dopamine disturbance at the level of the brain to a psychological phenomenon at the level of subjective experience. The affected brain assigns exaggerated "significance" or "salience" to ordinary stimuli and thoughts. This may explain how delusions and hallucinations arise as an attempt to make sense of this abnormal sense of significance.
- **Pharmacological support**: the strongest support for the hypothesis comes from the fact that nearly all established antipsychotics act at D2 receptors (as antagonists or, in the case of aripiprazole and similar drugs, partial agonists), and from the correlation between their clinical doses and D2 occupancy. Clozapine, which has comparatively low D2 occupancy, and newer agents acting on other targets (for example muscarinic receptors) show that D2 blockade is not the only route to antipsychotic effect.
- **Limits of the hypothesis**: current scientific literature acknowledges that the dopamine hypothesis does not adequately explain the negative symptoms (such as social withdrawal and blunted affect) or the cognitive impairment in schizophrenia. Growing evidence points to the involvement of other neurotransmitter systems, most notably **glutamate**, within a broader neurodevelopmental model.
- **Therapeutic impact**: despite its limits, the hypothesis has remained a main theoretical frame for most antipsychotic drugs, whose clinical use began with chlorpromazine in the early 1950s. The correlation of dose with D2 affinity was reported in the mid-1970s by Philip Seeman and, separately, by Ian Creese, David Burt, and Solomon Snyder.
