# Verstehen: Interpretive Understanding in Sociology

***Verstehen*** is a German word meaning "understanding," and in sociology it names the method of grasping the subjective meaning that actors attach to their own actions. Where the natural sciences explain behaviour from the outside, by causes and laws, *Verstehen* seeks to understand action from the inside, by recovering the intentions, beliefs, and values that make it meaningful to the actor. It is the founding methodological idea of interpretive sociology: human beings are not objects pushed by forces but meaning-making creatures, and social science must therefore interpret meanings as well as measure causes.

## Definition and Development

The concept comes from the German tradition of the human sciences. Wilhelm Dilthey, in his *Introduction to the Human Sciences* (1883), and later writings, argued that the study of human life requires a different approach from the study of nature. In his formulation of 1894, nature is explained (*erklären*) while psychic life is understood (*verstehen*); his later work stressed re-experiencing the inner life of others as expressed in texts, actions, and institutions. Dilthey's distinction gave the human sciences their own epistemological foundation, independent of the natural sciences.

Max Weber made *Verstehen* the centre of sociology. In *Economy and Society*, published posthumously in 1922, he defined sociology as "a science concerning itself with the interpretive understanding of social action and thereby with a causal explanation of its course and consequences." For Weber, the sociologist must first understand what an action means to the actor — why a person prays, votes, strikes, or saves money — and only then explain it causally. A purely external description, such as recording that a person raised an arm, tells us nothing until we know whether the gesture was a greeting, a signal, or a threat.

Weber distinguished two kinds of understanding. **Direct observational understanding** grasps the meaning of an act immediately, as when we understand a person chopping wood. **Explanatory understanding** grasps the motive: we understand why the person chops wood — to earn a wage, to supply his own household, or perhaps to work off anger. Sociology aims at the second, and it tests its interpretations against evidence, including statistical regularities, so that *Verstehen* is not mere intuition but a disciplined method.

## Key Contributions: Phenomenology and the Interpretation of Meaning

Alfred Schutz, in *The Phenomenology of the Social World* (German 1932; English 1967), deepened Weber's account by analysing how understanding actually works in everyday life. Schutz showed that we understand others by drawing on a stock of typifications — taken-for-granted assumptions about how people behave — and that social reality is built up from these mutual interpretations. His work became the foundation of phenomenological sociology and of ethnomethodology, which studies the methods people use to make their actions intelligible to one another.

Clifford Geertz carried the interpretive programme into anthropology. In *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), he argued that culture is a web of significance that people have spun themselves, and that the analyst's task is "thick description": interpreting the layers of meaning in a wink, a ritual, or a cockfight. Geertz's famous analysis of the Balinese cockfight showed that a single event could encode status rivalry and masculinity — meanings invisible to any observer who merely counted bets.

## Examples

Consider a religious fast. A causal explanation might correlate fasting with poverty or with social pressure. *Verstehen* asks what fasting means to those who fast: devotion, discipline, solidarity with the poor, or obedience to God. Without that meaning, the sociologist cannot distinguish fasting from hunger, though the two are physically similar and socially opposite. Or consider voting: the same ballot may express loyalty, protest, habit, or hope, and the meaning shapes how the act should be explained. In the Arab and Islamic tradition, a parallel concern with intention — *niyya* — points to a related insight: an act's significance depends on the meaning the actor gives it, and the same outward deed may be worship, custom, or hypocrisy.

## The Limits of Empathy across Cultures

Understanding is sometimes equated with putting oneself in another's place, but this is least reliable where experiences and concepts differ most. An interpreter who imagines how they would feel in a situation may simply report their own reactions. Weber had already denied that understanding requires reliving another's experience. Clifford Geertz, in an essay of 1974 on understanding from the native's point of view, argued that the anthropologist need not achieve emotional identification; the task is to examine the words, images, institutions, and behaviour through which people represent themselves, comparing local concepts, in his cases from Java, Bali, and Morocco, with the analyst's own. Peter Winch (1964) warned against judging another society's practices, such as Zande witchcraft, by standards drawn from Western science without first grasping the point they have for participants; critics replied that this risks making cross-cultural criticism impossible. Renato Rosaldo described how he failed to grasp Ilongot accounts of grief, rage, and headhunting until his own bereavement, concluding that a researcher's position and life experience limit and enable what can be understood. These cases suggest that empathy is at best a source of hypotheses, to be checked against language learning, prolonged observation, and the responses of the people studied.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics have charged that *Verstehen* is unscientific: meanings cannot be observed, and the sociologist may simply project their own assumptions onto others. Positivists argued that sociology should stick to observable behaviour and statistical regularities. Weber's answer was that interpretation must be tested — an interpretation is adequate when it fits the evidence and when it can be combined with causal explanation. Later critics added that understanding is never neutral: the sociologist's own position, gender, and culture shape what meanings they can see, so *Verstehen* requires reflexivity about the interpreter's standpoint.

The concept remains central. Qualitative interviews, ethnography, and the analysis of online communication all depend on interpretive understanding: a researcher reading social media posts must recover what users meant, not merely count their words. In a world of algorithmic prediction, *Verstehen* insists on a question that no amount of data can answer by itself — what does this action mean to the person who performed it?
