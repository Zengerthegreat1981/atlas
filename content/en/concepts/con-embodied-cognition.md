# Embodied Cognition: The Mind Extends into the Body

A thesis in cognitive psychology and the philosophy of mind holds that human cognition is not abstract information processing taking place in a separate "mind," but arises from the interaction of the body with its material and social environment. Its programmatic formulation emerged in the 1980s and 1990s: Lakoff and Johnson in *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) and then *Philosophy in the Flesh* (1999), and Varela, Thompson, and Rosch in *The Embodied Mind* (1991).

## The Comparison with the Arabic Heritage

The most famous confrontation between the self and the body in the Arabic heritage is the thought experiment of the "floating man" or suspended man in Ibn Sina (in *al-Shifa'* and *al-Isharat*): imagine a human being created all at once, suspended in the air, who does not feel his body and touches nothing, yet still establishes his own existence for himself. From this Ibn Sina concludes that the soul is a substance distinct from the body. The embodied program disputes this premise, holding that the self's sense of itself is built on signals from the body and its movement. Yet Ibn Sina himself assigned the soul inner faculties (the inner senses, including common sense, imagination and estimation) that he located in the brain, so he did not neglect the bodily basis of cognition. As for al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* he treated outward acts of worship as disciplines that shape the inner state of the heart, an idea that can be set beside embodied accounts of practice. These are comparisons of themes, not claims of historical influence.

## Phenomenological and Philosophical Origins

Varela and his colleagues drew explicitly on Maurice Merleau-Ponty, who in *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) made the "lived body" (*corps propre*) the ground of perception and meaning: the subject is not a mind that merely owns a body but is embodied through and through. This idea connects with Heidegger's analysis of Dasein as being-in-the-world and with the concept of the body schema. Lakoff and Johnson added that abstract thinking is carried out through bodily metaphors: we say "the price went up" and "I grasped the idea," because our concepts are built on images of movement, place, and fullness.

## Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

Neurological cases have been cited in support of this trend. Oliver Sacks, in *The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat* (1985), described a patient ("the disembodied lady") who lost proprioception and felt disembodied. V. S. Ramachandran, in *Phantoms in the Brain* (1998, with Sandra Blakeslee), discussed phantom limbs and the mirror-box procedure. Botvinick and Cohen, in "Rubber Hands 'Feel' Touch That Eyes See" (1998), showed that synchronous touch and sight can lead a person to feel an artificial hand as part of the body, evidence for the plasticity of body representation. Applications in neurorehabilitation and in research on psychosis and autism are being explored, but their clinical value is still under investigation.

## Distinction from Adjacent Concepts

It differs from **embedded** cognition, which focuses on cognition's dependence on the environment and tools, and from the **extended** mind of Clark and Chalmers (1998), which regards tools as part of the cognitive system itself. Wilson, in "Six Views of Embodied Cognition" (2002), distinguishes clearly among these programs.

## Four Research Programs

The label covers distinct programs. Lakoff and Johnson's cognitive linguistics argues that concepts and reasoning are structured by bodily experience and metaphor. Varela, Thompson and Rosch's **enactivism** treats cognition as the activity of a living organism that "enacts" its world through sensorimotor coupling. Andy Clark's work on situated and **extended** cognition (with Chalmers, 1998) emphasizes the use of tools and environmental structure in cognitive processes. Shaun Gallagher's phenomenological approach (*How the Body Shapes the Mind*, 2005) analyses the body schema, body image and agency. They overlap but differ on whether the body is constitutive of cognition or only a causal contributor.

## Limits of the Thesis

Critics argue that the concept of "embodiment" is loose and used in various senses, and that some well-known studies that claimed bodily states change psychological judgments failed to replicate. Moreover, abstract operations such as mathematics need an explanation other than direct bodily movement.
