# Secularization and Decline of Religious Practice

A bridging concept that connects sociology and the study of religion. It examines the hypothesis that **modernity**, with its scientific, industrial, and bureaucratic institutions, inevitably leads to a decline in the importance of religion in public and private life, and the criticism and revision this hypothesis has received over more than a century of sociological debate.

## The Weberian Root: Disenchantment of the World

The theoretical origin of this concept goes back to **Max Weber** and his notion of **"disenchantment of the world"** (*Entzauberung der Welt*). He held that **rationalization** is the central driver of secularization: technical-scientific instrumental thinking gradually replaces magical and religious orientations toward the world, so that religious institutions gradually lose their control over sectors of society and culture.

## The Classical Theory of Secularization

The sociologist **Peter Berger** formulated, in the 1960s, an influential definition of secularization as "the process by which sectors of society and culture are withdrawn from the control of religious institutions and symbols." He introduced the concept of **"plausibility structures"** to explain how the persistence of religious belief depends on groups, rituals, and shared practices that make a particular religious conception seem socially plausible. When these supporting social structures dissolve, belief weakens with them. The British sociologist **Steve Bruce** later developed this framework into a multidimensional account linking the decline of religion to processes of **institutional differentiation**, **rationalization**, and **pluralism**, and to changes in how religion is transmitted across generations.

## Critical Revision

Berger himself later retreated (from the late 1990s, notably in *The Desecularization of the World*, 1999) from generalizing the classical theory of secularization. He proposed that what characterizes modernity is not necessarily the **decline** of religious belief but its growing **pluralism**. He cited statistical data showing continued high religious vitality in large parts of the world (the United States, Africa, Latin America) despite rapid economic modernization, contrary to what the classical theory predicted.

## Divergent Statistical Evidence

By contrast, data from Western Europe show a marked, documented decline in traditional indicators of religious practice such as church attendance (a decline Steve Bruce documents for Britain). This has led researchers to distinguish between **institutional secularization** (the decline of the official church's authority) and **individual secularization** (the decline of personal faith), since the first may occur without the second necessarily following.

## A Multidimensional View

José Casanova's *Public Religions in the Modern World* (1994) separated the secularization thesis into three distinct claims: the differentiation of religious, political, legal, and economic spheres; the decline of religious belief and practice; and the privatization of religion. He argued that differentiation has largely held in modern Western societies, whereas decline and privatization are contingent and have been contradicted in many settings, so that the three need to be assessed separately for law, politics, and personal belief.

## Contemporary Impact

This framework is now used to study contemporary phenomena such as the effect of the internet and social media on patterns of religiosity, and the shift of religion from a collective institutional practice to a selective individual experience ("believing without belonging," in Grace Davie's phrase). Whether these changes amount to decline or to transformation of religion remains disputed.

## The Limit

The debate remains open over the universality of the secularization thesis. Is it an inevitable path tied to modernization regardless of cultural context, or a phenomenon specific to Western Europe that does not necessarily apply to other regions with different modernization experiences?
