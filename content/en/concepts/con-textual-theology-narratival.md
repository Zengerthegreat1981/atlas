# Textual and Narrative Theology

A concept associated with narrative theology, especially the work of Hans Frei and George Lindbeck of the Yale school. It asserts that sacred religious texts (the Torah, the Qur'an, sacred myths) are not containers that carry fixed prior meanings. Rather, the texts themselves produce meaning and theology through their linguistic and narrative structure.

## Definition

Textual theology rejects the separation between:
- the form (the text, the story, the language)
- the content (the meaning, the principle, the religious truth)

Instead, it holds that **the text and the meaning are one**. The "meaning of the theology" cannot be extracted from behind the text, because the meaning lives inside the text itself.

## The Pivotal Example

**A classic error:**
- The text of the Torah: "God said: let there be light" (the story of creation).
- The "meaning" extracted: "God the Creator, who is powerful" (an abstract theological principle).
- The assumption: that the story is merely a vessel for conveying the principle.

**The textual position:**
- The meaning does not hide behind the text; it lives within it.
- On this view, the narrative does not merely illustrate a divine attribute such as power; it is how the identity of God is rendered for the community that reads it.
- Understanding God means understanding how God acts within this story, not extracting attributes from it.

## Frei and Lindbeck

Frei's *The Eclipse of Biblical Narrative* (1974) argued that, from the eighteenth century, interpreters increasingly sought the meaning of biblical stories in historical reference or in general ideas, losing sight of their literal, narrative sense. Lindbeck's *The Nature of Doctrine* (1984) proposed a "cultural-linguistic" account of religion and doctrine, in which scripture shapes the language and practices of a community and is interpreted "intratextually," that is, within its own world of meaning.

## Implications for the Comparative Study of Religions

Each religion has different sacred texts:
- **Judaism**: the Hebrew Bible (Torah, Prophets, Writings) together with rabbinic literature.
- **Christianity**: the Old and New Testaments (multiple texts, but with a large narrative component, notably the Gospels).
- **Islam**: the Qur'an (a single text, revealed directly).
- **Buddhism**: the sutras and other canonical texts (discourses, but also narratives such as the Jataka tales).
- **Hinduism**: the Vedas, the Upanishads and the epics (texts of several kinds).

The study of each tradition requires a deep understanding of its own particular "textual fabric", rather than imposing one framework on all of them.

## The Difference from Classical Literary Criticism

Literary criticism studies texts as works of pure art. Textual theology studies them as carriers of **religious** meanings with an effect on the identity and practices of believers, and not as neutral works.

## Connection with the Arab-Islamic Heritage

This concept can be read in comparison with a classical Islamic exegetical tradition, which likewise does not easily separate the Qur'anic text from its theological meaning. The science of exegesis, in the work of exegetes such as al-Tabari in *Jami' al-Bayan* (d. 310 AH), treats the Qur'anic text as the source that produces legal and doctrinal meaning from within its linguistic and contextual structure (occasions of revelation, abrogation and abrogated verses). It does not treat it as an external shell for a prior abstract principle. Nevertheless, the difference remains essential. The Islamic tradition describes the Qur'an as direct divine speech, not authored by humans (whereas Frei's analysis treats the Gospels as realistic narratives read within the Christian community, bracketing questions of historical reconstruction). This means that applying the tools of Western textual theology to Qur'anic exegesis requires a methodological caution that has not yet been fully worked out in the comparative literature.
