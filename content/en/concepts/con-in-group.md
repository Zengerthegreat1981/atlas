# In-group

An in-group is a social group with which a person identifies and feels they belong. Its members share a sense of "we", which shapes how they see themselves and how they treat outsiders. The counterpart is the out-group: those who are seen as not belonging, and who are often judged by different standards. The in-group is one of the most basic units of social life. Families, friendship circles, teams, nations, and religious communities can all function as in-groups, and most people belong to several at once, moving between them as contexts change.

## Definition

William Graham Sumner introduced the terms in-group and out-group in *Folkways* (1906), arguing that loyalty to one's own group and hostility toward others were among the oldest features of human society. In modern usage, the in-group is defined less by hostility than by identification: members recognise one another as similar in some relevant respect, share norms and expectations, and expect solidarity and mutual support. Membership can be ascribed, as with family or ethnicity, or achieved, as with a profession or a club. It can also be purely psychological: people can feel part of a group they rarely meet, such as a nation or a fan community.

In-group favouritism is the tendency to evaluate one's own group more positively and to allocate it more resources and trust. This bias is often mild, but it becomes consequential when it shapes hiring, policing, or political loyalty, because then the boundary between "us" and "them" distributes real advantages.

## Origins and Development

Sumner's account was shaped by evolutionary thinking: groups that cooperated internally and competed externally survived, and the habits of loyalty and suspicion were passed on as folkways. Later research qualified this picture. Gordon Allport, in *The Nature of Prejudice* (1954), argued that in-group love and out-group hate are distinct: favouring one's own group does not automatically produce hatred of others, though the two often travel together. Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave experiments, reported in *Intergroup Conflict and Cooperation* (1961), showed how quickly group identity could form. Boys at a summer camp, divided into two groups, developed loyalty, nicknames, and norms within days, and competition over prizes turned that loyalty into hostility.

Henri Tajfel's minimal group experiments in the early 1970s pushed the argument further. Tajfel showed that people favoured their own group even when groups were assigned arbitrarily, for example by a coin toss, with no history, conflict, or material interest at stake. Mere categorisation was enough to produce bias. Tajfel and John Turner developed this into social identity theory in "An Integrative Theory of Intergroup Conflict" (1979), arguing that people derive self-esteem from group membership and seek positive distinctiveness for their in-groups.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the source of in-group bias. Realistic conflict theory, associated with Sherif, holds that bias arises from competition over scarce resources: groups favour their own when interests clash. Social identity theory holds that bias can arise from categorisation alone, even without competition, because people want their group to compare well with others. Later research has reconciled the two: categorisation creates a readiness for bias, and competition, threat, and inequality amplify it.

A second dimension is the strength and salience of identification. Not all in-group memberships matter equally. Some are central to a person's sense of self, while others are trivial or situational. Marilynn Brewer, in her 1979 work on in-group bias, argued that the core of in-group attachment is trust and positive regard for fellow members rather than hostility toward outsiders, a distinction that matters for interventions: reducing out-group hostility may require different strategies from building in-group cohesion.

A third debate concerns multiple and overlapping memberships. People belong to many in-groups, and these can cross-cut: a colleague may be an out-group member by nationality but an in-group member by profession. Cross-cutting memberships moderate bias, because they make a simple "us versus them" division harder to sustain. Conversely, when many boundaries align, so that ethnicity, religion, and class all divide the same people, conflict becomes more likely.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The in-group concept is widely used to explain political polarisation, nationalism, and online tribalism. Partisan identity now predicts attitudes and behaviour more strongly than policy preferences in many democracies, and social media can intensify in-group dynamics by surrounding people with like-minded voices and visible out-group opponents. In organisations, in-group favouritism helps explain why managers hire and promote people who resemble themselves, and why whistleblowers who challenge their own group face such hostility.

Critics note that the concept can be used too loosely, labelling any group an in-group without specifying what members share or how strongly they identify. Laboratory findings from minimal groups do not always translate to real groups with histories and material stakes. Others argue that the in-group/out-group binary oversimplifies social life, where boundaries are negotiated rather than fixed. These criticisms have refined rather than displaced the concept.
