# Antipsychotic Mechanisms

## Definition

Antipsychotic drugs are a group of medications that reduce hallucinations, delusions, and thought disorder in schizophrenia and related conditions. They are also used in psychotic mania and psychotic depression. All of them share, to varying degrees, the blocking of **dopamine D2 receptors**. They then differ in their binding to serotonin, histamine, adrenergic, and muscarinic receptors, which explains the variation in their side effects.

## The Discovery of Chlorpromazine and Its Interpretation

Paul Charpentier synthesized the compound chlorpromazine in the Rhône-Poulenc laboratories in 1950. It was being investigated as an antihistamine and an anesthetic adjunct. Henri Laborit introduced it into artificial anesthesia. Jean Delay and Pierre Deniker then tested it at Sainte-Anne Hospital in Paris in 1952, on patients with mania and psychosis, and it produced marked calm and emotional indifference without loss of consciousness. David Healy described this phase in *The Creation of Psychopharmacology* (Harvard University Press, 2002). In 1963 Carlsson and Lindqvist in *Acta Pharmacologica et Toxicologica* that these drugs block monoamine receptors, based on increased levels of the metabolites 3-methoxytyramine and normetanephrine (derived from dopamine and norepinephrine). Seeman and colleagues (1975-1976), and Creese, Burt, and Snyder (*Science*, 1976), showed that the clinical potency of antipsychotics correlates with their affinity for the D2 receptor. Carlsson shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2000 for discoveries concerning dopamine as a transmitter in the brain.

## The Second Generation and Multiple Receptors

Clozapine, which appeared in the 1960s, was withdrawn in the mid-1970s after cases of agranulocytosis and was reintroduced in the United States in 1989 for treatment-resistant schizophrenia. This followed the study by Kane and colleagues (*Archives of General Psychiatry*, 1988), which demonstrated its superiority to chlorpromazine in treatment-resistant schizophrenia. It is distinguished by preferring 5-HT2A receptors and by weaker blocking of D2. For this reason it was thought that the balance between serotonin and dopamine explains its low rate of motor symptoms. Kapur and colleagues, in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 2000, reported, in a PET study, that clinical response was associated with D2 occupancy above about 65%, hyperprolactinemia with occupancy above about 72%, and extrapyramidal side effects with occupancy above about 78%. Aripiprazole, approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2002, is a partial agonist at D2 and is often described as a dopamine system stabilizer.

## Evidence and Reservations

In a network meta-analysis published in *The Lancet* in 2013, Leucht and colleagues compared fifteen antipsychotic drugs. They found that differences in efficacy were smaller than differences in side-effect profiles, and that clozapine, amisulpride, olanzapine, and risperidone were at the forefront in efficacy. In *Schizophrenia Bulletin* in 2009, Howes and Kapur presented the "version III" of the dopamine hypothesis, placing presynaptic dopamine overactivity in the striatum at the center of the model. Critics argue that the hypothesis does not explain negative and cognitive symptoms, and that a substantial minority of patients do not respond adequately, for whom clozapine is the main option (see the entry on Antipsychotic Response). Glutamate hypotheses (based on NMDA-receptor hypofunction) and the muscarinic cholinergic system (for example, xanomeline-trospium, approved in the United States in 2024) open new avenues.

## Clinical and Social Aspects

Understanding the mechanisms guides drug selection. A drug that is strongly active on D2 increases the risk of extrapyramidal symptoms and hyperprolactinemia, while a drug with strong antihistamine activity increases weight gain and sedation. Success does not depend on the medication alone. Recovery from psychosis requires social support and psychological treatment (see the entry on Recovery from Psychosis).
