# Virtue and Vice as Historical Causes

In ancient Greek and Roman historiography, moral character was widely understood as an active, primary causal force that dictated the rise, triumph, and collapse of states. Rather than explaining historical transformation primarily through impersonal socioeconomic structures, ancient historians frequently attributed political outcomes to the moral excellence (*arete* / *virtus*) or moral depravity (*kakia* / *vitium*) of individual rulers, aristocratic elites, and entire civic bodies.

## Definition and Moral Causation

Moral causation posits that historical agency is deeply rooted in ethical character. For ancient historians, historical narrative was fundamentally didactic and moralistic: history was viewed as philosophy teaching by examples (*historia magistra vitae*). Under this framework, political prosperity, military victory, and domestic stability were interpreted as the direct fruit of virtues such as justice (*dikaiosyne* / *iustitia*), self-restraint (*sophrosyne* / *temperantia*), courage (*andreia* / *fortitudo*), and religious piety (*pietas*). Conversely, imperial overreach, civil discord, and state collapse were viewed as the inevitable consequences of moral degeneracy, particularly greed (*avaritia* / *philochrematia*), arrogance (*hubris* / *superbia*), and luxury (*luxuria*).

## Historical Context and Development

The ethical framing of historical causation developed from archaic poetic traditions into a sophisticated historiographical tool:

- **Xenophon (c. 430–354 BCE):** In his *Cyropaedia* ("Education of Cyrus"), Xenophon presented Cyrus the Great as an idealized moral monarch whose personal temperance, piety, and leadership cultivated an enduring empire. When Cyrus's successors abandoned his ethical regimen for effeminacy and indulgence, the Persian empire experienced immediate administrative and military decline.
- **Polybius (c. 200–c. 118 BCE):** Even while Polybius championed institutional analysis—such as the Roman mixed constitution—he identified the moral discipline instilled by ancestral customs (*mos maiorum*) as indispensable to Roman supremacy. In Book 6, he highlighted Roman funeral practices and public eulogies as civic technologies designed to inspire youth to display heroic virtue for the commonwealth.

## Sallust, Livy, and the Roman Moral Decline Tradition

In Roman historiography, the concept of moral causation reached its most intense articulation:

1. **Sallust (86–c. 35 BCE):** In *Bellum Catilinae* and *Bellum Iugurthinum*, Sallust constructed an influential causal theory of the fall of the Roman Republic based on moral pathology. He argued that as long as Rome faced external existential threats—specifically Carthage—fear of the enemy (*metus hostilis*) preserved domestic concord and civic virtue. Following the destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE, security fostered ambition (*ambitio*) and luxury (*luxuria*), which corrupted the senatorial order, eroded public liberty, and culminated in the Catilinarian conspiracy and civil wars.
2. **Livy (59 BCE–17 CE):** In the Preface to *Ab Urbe Condita*, Livy explicitly instructed his readers to pay close attention to the morals and men through whom Rome achieved greatness, and then to observe the moral decay that accompanied territorial wealth, until Rome reached a crisis where "we can endure neither our vices nor their remedies." Historical examples (*exempla*) were presented as clear models to imitate or avoid.
3. **Tacitus and Plutarch:** Tacitus defined the historian's highest calling as preserving the memory of virtue while deterring vice through the fear of posthumous infamy (*Annals* 3.65). Plutarch, writing parallel biographies of Greek and Roman statesmen, explicitly privileged character over event, noting that a minor gesture, witty retort, or ethical choice often revealed personal character (*ethos*) far more effectively than catastrophic battles involving tens of thousands of casualties.

## Historiographical Critiques and Modern Reassessment

Ancient moral causation provided a powerful narrative architecture, but modern historians recognize its analytical limits. By interpreting complex structural shifts—such as agrarian transformations, demographic pressures, provincial taxation crises, and institutional stagnation—primarily as moral failings or individual ethical lapses, ancient writers often obscured the socioeconomic mechanisms driving historical change. Modern scholarship treats ancient moralizing narratives not as transparent explanations of causation, but as elite ideological discourses reflecting contemporary anxieties about social change and political centralization.
