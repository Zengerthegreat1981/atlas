# Futuh Literature: The Genre of Conquest Historiography in Early Islam

A genre of early Islamic historical writing concerned with recording the **military and territorial expansion of the Islamic state** in the first and second centuries AH, through the narration of battles of conquest, the settlements of surrender with cities, and the terms of treaties with their original inhabitants. The genre is known technically as the "books of conquests" (kutub al-futuh).

## Definition and Derivation

In this historical context, *fath* ("conquest") means the military seizure of a region or city, either by force ('anwatan) or by agreement (sulhan), where the agreement specifies the terms of submission, such as the payment of tribute (jizya) in exchange for protection of the inhabitants and their property. This legal distinction between conquest by force and conquest by agreement was not merely a narrative detail. It had a direct jurisprudential effect on the later legal status of the conquered land (kharaji or 'ushri) and of its inhabitants. This made the books of conquest a source consulted for jurisprudence, not only for history.

## Prominent Examples of the Genre

The most famous work in this field is *Futuh al-Buldan* by Ahmad ibn Yahya al-Baladhuri (d. 279 AH / 892 CE), one of the most important primary sources for the history of the early Islamic conquests. It covers the conquest of Syria, Egypt, Iraq, Iran, North Africa and al-Andalus, documents the specific terms of agreement for each region, and records the early formation of the Islamic administration in the conquered territories. Other notable works in the genre include *Futuh Misr wa-akhbaruha* by Abu al-Qasim 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Abd Allah ibn 'Abd al-Hakam (d. 257 AH / 871 CE), the *Kitab al-Futuh* of Ibn A'tham al-Kufi (traditionally d. c. 314 AH / 926 CE), and the later popular compilation *Futuh al-Sham*, which was pseudepigraphically attributed to Muhammad ibn 'Umar al-Waqidi (d. 207 AH / 823 CE).

## Source Value and Critical Problems

The books of conquest are among the most important primary sources for the early political and administrative history of the Islamic state. They preserve details of treaty agreements that are not preserved in other sources, and they illuminate the nature of the relationship between the conquerors and the original inhabitants (Christians, Zoroastrians and Jews) of the conquered regions. However, this literature is subject to the same critical problems as the rest of early Islamic historical reports: the writing down of accounts occurred decades or centuries after the events; there are multiple conflicting accounts of the details of a single conquest (such as the conquest of Egypt or Syria); and some accounts may have been coloured by later jurisprudential or tribal purposes, such as an attempt to establish a particular tax status for a region retroactively by attributing it to the original conquest terms.

## Methodological Importance

This genre exemplifies the intertwining of history and jurisprudence in medieval Islamic heritage. Writing "what happened" in the conquests was not only an end in itself, but also an argument in later jurisprudential disputes about the legal status of the land and its inhabitants. Contemporary researchers should therefore pay attention to the possible jurisprudential motives behind the formulation of some reports, rather than accepting them as purely neutral description.
