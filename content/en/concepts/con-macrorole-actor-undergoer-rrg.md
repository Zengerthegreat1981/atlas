# Actor and Undergoer Macroroles

A central concept in Role and Reference Grammar (RRG), formulated by William Foley and Robert Van Valin. It generalizes the many detailed semantic roles (such as a real agent, recipient, instrument, location) into just two macroroles: the "Actor," the participant most active and causally responsible in bringing about the event, and the "Undergoer," the participant most affected by the outcome of the event.

## An analytical tool that crosses language types

This generalization aims to provide a single analytical tool that works equally across language types that differ radically in how they organize their grammatical cases. In languages with accusative (nominative-accusative) orientation, such as English, the Actor is by default the syntactic pivot (subject). In syntactically ergative languages, the pivot may instead be the Undergoer. Analysis of the two macroroles shows precisely how these semantic roles are distributed across grammatical categories in each language type.

## The relation to argument structure

The concept of the two macroroles provides a link between the detailed semantic argument structure of a particular verb (as represented in the lexicon) and the actual grammatical realization of the sentence, through an "Actor-Undergoer Hierarchy" that determines which argument is chosen as the "Actor" and which as the "Undergoer" when a verb carries more than one argument.

## An illustrative example across languages

In a sentence such as "The boy broke the window," "the boy" is analyzed as the Actor (the participant most causally responsible for the event), and "the window" as the Undergoer (the participant most affected by its outcome). In an accusative language such as Arabic or English, the Actor of a transitive verb is by default the subject, and the single argument of an intransitive verb is also the subject. In an ergative language (such as Basque or some Australian languages), the Undergoer of a transitive clause is marked in the same way as the sole argument of an intransitive clause (absolutive), while the Actor receives a distinct ergative marking. This is precisely what the analysis in terms of the two macroroles clarifies, by disentangling the misleading surface match between the "grammatical subject" and the "semantic subject."

## Its relation to the prominence hierarchy

Assigning the two macroroles from the detailed argument structure is tied to the "Actor-Undergoer Hierarchy," a general semantic sequence which states that if a verb carries two or more semantic arguments, the argument higher on the hierarchy (the more causally responsible and controlling one) is chosen as Actor and the lower as Undergoer, regardless of the exact number of detailed semantic roles included in the verb's lexical meaning.
