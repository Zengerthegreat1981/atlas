# Democracy

Democracy — rule by the people — is usually studied as a political system of elections, constitutions, and representative institutions. Anthropology approaches it differently: as a lived practice, a set of ideals and idioms, and a site of struggle over who counts as "the people" and what ruling together actually requires. Anthropologists study how democratic ideas travel, how they are translated into local idioms of consensus, chiefship, or assembly, and how people experience elections, citizenship, and the state in everyday life. The anthropology of democracy thus asks not only whether a regime is democratic, but what democracy means to the people who practise, demand, or resist it.

## Origins and Development

Anthropology's engagement with democracy has deep roots. Alexis de Tocqueville's *Democracy in America* (1835), though not an anthropological work in the modern sense, is an early ethnographic study of democratic mores — of how equality of conditions shapes manners, associations, and everyday life. In the twentieth century, anthropologists studying societies without states found forms of collective decision-making that unsettled European assumptions: the Nuer's acephalous politics, the lineage-based politics of the Tallensi, and the consensus-based assemblies of many Indigenous American societies have all been read as suggesting that democratic practice long predated the modern state.

Pierre Clastres's *Society Against the State* (1987, first published in French in 1974) made this point sharply. Drawing on Amazonian ethnography, Clastres argued that many stateless societies actively worked to prevent the concentration of power: chiefs were orators and mediators without coercive authority, and the community guarded itself against the emergence of a separate ruling apparatus. David Graeber developed a similar argument in *Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology* (2004), contending that anthropology's archive is full of democratic experiments — councils, assemblies, and consensus procedures — that political theory has systematically ignored because they do not fit the story of democracy as a Western invention.

The field coalesced around the turn of the millennium. Julia Paley's essay "Toward an Anthropology of Democracy" (2002) called for ethnography of democratic processes themselves: campaigns, voting, bureaucratic encounters, and the everyday work of being a citizen. James Holston's *Insurgent Citizenship* (2008), a study of urban Brazil, showed how poor residents of São Paulo's peripheries used legal claims, land occupations, and neighbourhood associations to demand rights the state had promised but never delivered — a form of democratic practice from below.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the universality of democracy. Is democracy a Western export, imposed through colonialism and development programmes, or a human capacity with many local forms? Anthropologists generally reject both the claim that democracy belongs to the West and the claim that any collective decision-making counts as democracy. Ethnography shows democratic ideals being translated — sometimes into idioms of kinship, sometimes into religious language — and transformed in the process. The question is not whether a society "has" democracy, but how its people understand and practise collective self-rule.

A second dimension is the gap between formal and substantive democracy. Elections may be held while power remains concentrated; constitutions may promise rights that citizens cannot exercise. Anthropologists document this gap from the ground up: the voter who sells her vote, the official who demands a bribe, the activist who finds the courts closed. But they also document the opposite — the moments when ordinary people use democratic institutions in unexpected ways, turning a bureaucratic procedure into a claim for dignity.

A third dimension is the state itself. Anthropology has shown that the state is not a single apparatus but a dispersed set of practices, encounters, and documents. Democracy, in this perspective, is not a property of the state but something produced, unevenly, in the interaction between citizens and officials, in queues, offices, and polling stations.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The anthropology of democracy is directly relevant to the present moment of democratic backsliding, populism, and protest. Ethnographers study how authoritarian leaders mobilise democratic language, how disinformation circulates in local networks, and how social movements — from Occupy to the Arab Spring to Indigenous land defence — experiment with new forms of collective decision-making. These studies show that democracy is never finished: it is continually made and unmade in practice.

Critics within the field warn against romanticising "local democracy" or treating consensus as inherently egalitarian — consensus procedures can silence dissent as effectively as any election can. Others note that anthropology's focus on the local can miss the transnational forces — debt, climate change, migration — that constrain what any single polity can decide. The strongest recent work therefore connects the ethnography of democratic practice to the wider political economy in which it unfolds, showing how global forces shape the possibilities of self-rule.
