# Secularism, Law, and Religion

## An early Arab debate: Islam and the foundations of rule

In Arabic discourse, Ali Abd al-Raziq ignited a major debate in 1925 with his book *Islam and the Foundations of Power*, arguing that the caliphate is not a religious pillar and that the prophetic mission did not include establishing a state in the political sense. He was stripped of his Azhar scholarly degree (*al-'alamiyya*). Many scholars responded, and the debate continued in the writings of Rashid Rida and others. At the same time, the Ottoman state moved toward modern codification (the *Mecelle*, or Ottoman Civil Code of 1876) while maintaining a jurisprudential reference. Later, Abdullahi Ahmed An-Na'im, in *Islam and the Secular State* (2008), argued that a state neutral toward religions is a condition for a Muslim to freely choose his religion, and that enforcing Sharia by state power strips it of its religious character because it cancels choice. Wael Hallaq, in *The Impossible State* (2013), holds that the concept of the modern state, by its very structure, conflicts with the moral governance that Sharia presupposes.

## Models of secularism: France, the United States, and Turkey

Ahmet Kuru, in *Secularism and State Policies toward Religion* (2009), compared the three cases of the United States, France and Turkey and concluded that they represent two patterns: an "assertive" secularism that excludes religion from the public sphere, as in France and Turkey, and a "passive" secularism that allows its presence, as in the United States. In France, the law of 9 December 1905 separated the churches from the state and ended their state funding, and *laïcité* became a republican principle. In the United States, the First Amendment (1791) prohibits establishing an official religion and protects the freedom of practice. In Turkey, Mustafa Kemal abolished the caliphate in 1924 and established the Presidency of Religious Affairs (Diyanet) to supervise religion under the state; secularism then entered the constitution in 1937. The comparison shows that secularism is not a single mold.

## Theories of secularization

Talal Asad, in *Formations of the Secular* (2003), offered a critical reading that regards the "secular" not as the mere absence of religion but as a modern political and legal category that redefines religion, reason, and pain, and produces new selves, rather than being neutral. Charles Taylor, in *A Secular Age* (2007), argued that secularization is a transformation in the conditions of belief: faith became a choice among options, after having been self-evident. José Casanova, in *Public Religions in the Modern World* (1994), proposed that secularization involves three things: differentiation of spheres, decline of religiosity, and withdrawal of religion into the private domain. He showed that the last of these is not inevitable, since religion has returned to public life.

## Legal problems

Secularism raises specific legal questions: religious symbols in schools (the 2004 French law banning conspicuous religious symbols in public schools), religious personal status laws in multi-confessional states, and the status of Sharia in constitutions (such as Article 2 of the Egyptian constitution, which establishes the principles of Sharia as the main source of legislation). Critics on both sides argue that absolute neutrality is impossible: the rules governing holidays, the calendar, and cemeteries always reflect some tradition.

## Limits of the approach

The comparison is open to the objection that the three models are specific historical cases that are hard to reproduce, and that Kuru's classification varies with subsequent events. Furthermore, the very idea of secularism is subject to political charge in the Arab world, where its use ranges from accusations of atheism to advocacy of equal citizenship.
