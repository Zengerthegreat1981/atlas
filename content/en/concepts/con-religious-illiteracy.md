# Religious Illiteracy

A concept popularized by Stephen Prothero in his 2007 book *Religious Literacy* to describe a situation he regards as paradoxical: a society (the United States specifically) that is statistically highly religious, yet unable to name the four Gospels, define the difference between Sunni and Shi'a, or explain the basic doctrine of its own religion. The concept distinguishes between two levels: **religious practice** (attendance at rites, personal faith) and **religious knowledge** (the ability to describe the doctrines and history of religions accurately, independently of commitment to them). It claims that the gap between them widened in the twentieth century, as the teaching of the Bible and of doctrine declined in American schools and churches themselves.

## The Civic Function of the Concept

Prothero does not present religious illiteracy as an individual moral failing but as a civic problem. A citizen lacking basic religious knowledge is not qualified, according to his argument, to participate knowingly in public debates in which implicit religious references are interwoven. This position generated a subsequent academic debate about measuring "religious literacy" and designing curricula to fill it. It is represented later by the research volume *Religious Literacy in Policy and Practice* (edited by Adam Dinham and Matthew Francis, 2015), which extended the debate to the British context and to public policy.

## The Statistical Evidence Prothero Relied On

Prothero relied on surveys showing that a large proportion of Americans cannot name the four Gospels, state the first book of the Bible, or explain basic terms of their other religions, even though the United States is classed among the most religious Western societies in terms of self-reported faith and attendance at rites. This statistical contradiction (high religiosity combined with low religious knowledge) led him to argue for the concept and to call for religion to be taught in American public schools, not as doctrinal indoctrination but as objective comparative knowledge that respects the constitutional separation of religion and state.

## Expansion Beyond the American Context

The debate later extended to contexts other than the American, most notably the project of Adam Dinham and Matthew Francis in Britain, which applied the concept of "religious literacy" to British public policy. In that context, policymakers face the challenge of dealing with increasing religious diversity (for example Islam, Hinduism and Sikhism) without sufficient knowledge of the basics of these traditions, which affects the quality of policies concerning education, health and social integration.
