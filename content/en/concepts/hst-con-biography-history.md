# Biography vs. History

In classical antiquity, biography (*bios*, Latin *vita*) and history (*historia*, Latin *res gestae*) were recognized as distinct, autonomous literary genres, each governed by its own rules of composition, evidentiary standards, and teleological objectives. While both forms addressed real past actions and aimed at instructing their audiences, ancient literary theorists and practitioners strictly differentiated between the grand, collective scope of political-military history and the intimate, character-centered investigation of the individual life.

## The Classical Demarcation: Plutarch's Formulation

The most famous programmatic articulation of the boundary between the two genres appears in the opening chapter of Plutarch's *Life of Alexander* (1.1–2):
> "It is not histories that I am writing, but lives (*ou gar historias graphomen, alla bious*); and in the most illustrious deeds there is not always a manifestation of virtue or vice, but a slight thing like a phrase or a jest often makes a greater revelation of character than battles where thousands fall, or the greatest armaments, or sieges of cities."

For Plutarch (ca. 46–ca. 119 CE), the distinction was fundamentally moral and psychological:
- ***Historia*** focuses on monumental public deeds (*praxeis*), statecraft, constitutional upheavals, diplomatic treaties, and military campaigns. It requires an elevated rhetorical register, strict chronological or annalistic sequencing, and the systematic analysis of political cause and effect.
- ***Bios*** focuses on the revelation of ethical character (*ethos*). It privileges domestic anecdotes, private utterances, physical appearance, education (*paideia*), and moral choices. Biography utilizes the subject's life as a philosophical mirror (*speculum*) to provide readers with positive and negative ethical exemplars (*exempla*) for emulation or avoidance.

## Generic Origins and Formal Evolution

The evolution of ancient biographical writing developed alongside but separately from mainstream historiography:
- **Fourth-Century BCE Philosophical Circles**: Biography emerged out of Athenian rhetorical encomium (such as Isocrates' *Evagoras*) and Socratic memoirs. Xenophon pioneered biographical portraiture in the *Agesilaus* (a panegyrical eulogy) and the *Cyropaedia* (a fictionalized philosophical treatise on the ideal education and leadership of Cyrus the Great).
- **Hellenistic Scholarship**: Following Aristotle, the Peripatetic school (notably Aristoxenus and Hermippus of Smyrna) developed biographical studies of poets, philosophers, and statesmen, emphasizing personality types and eccentric habits, while Alexandrian scholars compiled factual biographical sketches (*pinakes*) of canonical literary authors.
- **Plutarch's *Parallel Lives***: Plutarch perfected ethical-comparative biography (*Bioi Paralleloi*), deliberately pairing a Greek and a Roman statesman (such as Alexander and Julius Caesar, or Demosthenes and Cicero) followed by a comparative epilogue (*synkrisis*) evaluating their relative virtues, moral integrity, and political downfalls.

## Roman Biographical Traditions: Nepos, Tacitus, and Suetonius

In the Roman world, biographical writing developed along two distinct tracks:
- **Cornelius Nepos and Senatorial Exempla**: In *De Viris Illustribus* (first century BCE), Nepos introduced brief, accessible biographies of foreign and Roman commanders, emphasizing civic virtues and cosmopolitan tolerance.
- **Tacitus's *Agricola***: Cornelius Tacitus composed a biography of his father-in-law, Gnaeus Julius Agricola (98 CE), which occupied a hybrid space between funeral eulogy (*laudatio funebris*), biography, and political history. Tacitus presented how an honorable man could maintain moral dignity and serve the state under the corrupt tyranny of Emperor Domitian.
- **Suetonius and the Thematic Framework**: Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus (ca. 69–after 122 CE) established an alternative to chronological narrative in *De Vita Caesarum* (*Lives of the Caesars*). Having held imperial secretarial posts, Suetonius organized imperial biographies thematically by categories (*per species*): family ancestry, public career, military campaigns, administrative reforms, personal vices, domestic habits, physical traits, and omens of death. Suetonius's rubrics stripped away the elevated rhetorical decorum of formal history, exposing the raw, unvarnished realities of autocratic power.

## Historiographical Significance

The tension between biography and history illuminates an enduring debate in historiography: the relative significance of individual agency, moral psychology, and charismatic leadership versus structural institutions, material circumstances, and collective social movements. While nineteenth-century historicism subordinated biography to institutional history, modern historiography—through microhistory, prosopography, and political biography—has reaffirmed the critical dialectic between individual life-paths and broader historical structures.
