# Dance

Dance is patterned, rhythmic movement of the body, performed for communication, ritual, entertainment, courtship or aesthetic pleasure. In anthropology, dance is studied not as decoration added to social life but as social life itself made visible: a form of embodied knowledge through which people learn who they are, mark transitions, negotiate status and remember their history. Every known society dances, though what counts as dance, who may dance, and what dance means vary enormously. The anthropology of dance therefore asks how movement carries meaning, how bodies are trained and disciplined, and how dance both reflects and remakes the social order.

## Origins and Development

Early anthropologists treated dance as a survival of primitive ritual. Nineteenth-century writers linked it to religious ecstasy and to the origins of drama, assuming that "savage" dance was spontaneous and unreflective. The modern anthropology of dance began when scholars started to analyse movement as a structured system, comparable to language. Anya Peterson Royce's *The Anthropology of Dance* (1977) surveyed approaches to dance as a field of study, including the analysis of its structure, history and meanings. Adrienne Kaeppler, working in Tonga and Hawaii, analysed Tongan dance as a structured system of movement whose units and combinations dancers and audiences recognise, and related it to aesthetics and social rank. Judith Lynne Hanna went further, arguing that dance is a form of nonverbal communication through which dancers express emotion, assert identity and negotiate relationships, and that it deserves the same serious analysis as speech.

Dance is also inseparable from ritual. In many societies, dances accompany initiation, marriage, funerals and seasonal festivals, and the act of dancing together creates the solidarity the rite requires. Victor Turner's account of liminality — the state betwixt and between ordinary social roles — and of *communitas*, a feeling of shared humanity that transcends hierarchy, has often been applied to ritual dancing. At the same time, dance marks hierarchy: who leads and who follows, who dances in the centre and who watches from the edge, who may perform sacred dances and who is forbidden, all encode rank, gender and age. Among the Maasai of Kenya and Tanzania, for example, the jumping dance of young warriors displays strength and endurance, and is at once a courtship display, a test of manhood and a celebration of collective identity.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns whether dance is universal or culturally specific. Its defenders point to the ubiquity of rhythmic movement and to evidence that infants respond to rhythm before language, suggesting a deep human capacity. Critics reply that treating all patterned movement as "dance" imposes a Western category on practices their performers might call prayer, work or play, and that the boundary between dance, ritual and theatre is itself cultural. A second debate concerns the body and power: dance trains bodies to move in socially approved ways, so learning to dance is learning to inhabit gender, class and ethnicity. Feminist anthropologists have shown how dance can both enforce feminine ideals and provide women with a space for expression and resistance. A third debate asks how dance travels: ballet, hip-hop, salsa and Bollywood styles circulate globally, are adopted and transformed in new settings, and raise questions about authenticity, appropriation and ownership that ethnographers now study in dance studios and on social media as much as in villages.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Dance remains a vital field of anthropological research. Ethnographers study dance as therapy, as political protest — from the *haka* performed in New Zealand parliaments to flash mobs and street dance in urban uprisings — and as heritage, as communities revive dances suppressed by colonialism or missionaries. The study of dance has also reshaped anthropology itself: it forced the discipline to take the body seriously as a source of knowledge, to develop methods of movement notation and video ethnography, and to recognise that some social meanings can only be understood by moving, not merely by watching or asking. Dance, in this sense, is not a marginal topic but a window onto how culture lives in the body.
