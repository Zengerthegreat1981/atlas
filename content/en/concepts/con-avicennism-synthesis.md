# Avicennism & Ibn Sina's Synthesis

**Avicennism** is the philosophical system built by **Abu 'Ali al-Husayn ibn 'Abd Allah ibn Sina** (Avicenna, 980–1037 CE), the Persian physician and philosopher, through an original synthesis of three currents: **Aristotelian logic, physics and metaphysics**; **Neoplatonic emanationism** (as transmitted in the Arabic philosophical tradition, including al-Farabi); and **kalam and the Qur'anic text**. The system became the most influential philosophical framework in medieval Islamic philosophy, and its influence extended into Latin scholastic philosophy in Europe through the Latin translations of his works.

## Structure of the Synthesis

Ibn Sina adopted Aristotelian physics and logic as the general framework, but he clothed this framework in a Neoplatonic metaphysics of **emanation** (*fayd*). Existence proceeds from the **Necessary Existent** (God), in whom essence and existence coincide, unlike every other existent. This is the **distinction between essence and existence** (*al-tamyiz bayn al-mahiyya wa-l-wujud*), one of Ibn Sina's most important independent metaphysical innovations. Existence then proceeds through a hierarchical chain of ten separate intellects, ending with the Active Intellect, from which the terrestrial world and its forms emanate. However, Ibn Sina **rejected** some elements of classical Neoplatonism, such as the Platonic theory of "recollection" that holds the soul to exist before the body. He preferred a modified Aristotelian account of the soul's origin together with the body.

## The "Flying Man" Argument

One of his most famous independent philosophical contributions is the **Flying Man argument**, which he put forward in *al-Shifa'* and *al-Isharat wa-l-tanbihat*. Ibn Sina imagines a man created all at once, suspended in the air, with his senses completely veiled from the external world and from his own body, yet still aware of his own existence. From this he concludes that **self-awareness (the soul)** does not depend on perceiving the body or the senses, and that **the soul is a substance independent of the body**, whose existence can be established by immediate self-intuition independently of any sensory knowledge. The argument has often been compared with later discussions of the self, including Descartes', though direct influence is debated.

## Impact

Avicennism dominated medieval Islamic philosophy for centuries, both as a reference on which others built (for instance Nasir al-Din al-Tusi) and as the target of sharp theological critique. The most prominent critique was al-Ghazali's attack on the philosophers in *Tahafut al-falasifa*, which targeted specifically Ibn Sina's theses on the eternity of the world, God's knowledge of particulars, and the resurrection of bodies. His works were transmitted to medieval Europe through Latin translation (especially in Toledo) under the name *Avicenna*, and influenced Thomas Aquinas and other scholastic philosophers, especially on questions of the soul, essence and existence.
