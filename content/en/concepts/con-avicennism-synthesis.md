# Avicennism & Ibn Sina's Synthesis

Avicennism denotes the comprehensive philosophical and theological synthesis formulated by **Abu 'Ali al-Husayn ibn 'Abd Allah ibn Sina** (Avicenna, 370–428 AH / 980–1037 CE). Ibn Sina unified Aristotelian logic, physics, and metaphysics with Neoplatonic cosmological emanationism and monotheistic theological concerns. His system became a principal reference for much later Islamic philosophy, and a major source for European scholastic discussions, through twelfth-century Latin translations.

## Metaphysical Innovations: Essence, Existence, and Modality

At the core of Ibn Sina's metaphysics lies the fundamental distinction between **essence** (*mahiyya*) and **existence** (*wujud*). In all created entities, essence does not imply existence; the nature of a triangle or a human being can be fully conceived in the mind without requiring its instantiation in reality. Created things are therefore in themselves merely **contingent** (*mumkin al-wujud*), requiring an external cause to bestow existence upon them.

Existence terminates in a first uncaused cause: the **Necessary Existent by virtue of Itself** (*wajib al-wujud bi-dhatihi*), in whom essence and existence are identical. From that immutable reality, on the account in *al-Shifa'* and *al-Isharat*, the cosmos proceeds eternally through a series of intellects, commonly counted as ten, together with the souls and bodies of the spheres. The last of the series, the Active Intellect (*al-'aql al-fa''al*), is related to the sublunary world and is the source from which human minds receive intelligible forms.

## The Flying Man Thought Experiment and the Soul

Ibn Sina rejected the view that the rational soul is only the form or the harmony of the body. In *al-Shifa'* (*Kitab al-Nafs*, I.1), with a shorter version in *al-Isharat wa-l-tanbihat*, he sets the **Flying Man** argument (*al-insan al-mu'allaq fi al-hawa'*). The reader is asked to imagine a person created all at once, suspended so that no limb touches another and no external thing is perceived. Ibn Sina holds that this person would still affirm his own existence and would affirm nothing of his body, heart, or brain. He takes that self-awareness as evidence that the rational soul is an immaterial substance, distinct from the body. The soul comes into being with the body, which individuates it, and on his account it survives the body's death as an individual.

## Theological Reception and Enduring Influence

Avicennian concepts became the shared vocabulary of much later Islamic philosophy and kalam. Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH / 1111 CE), in *Tahafut al-falasifa*, charged the philosophers with unbelief on three points: the eternity of the world, the claim that God knows particulars only in a universal way, and the denial of bodily resurrection. He nevertheless used Avicennian logic. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi wrote a critical commentary on the *Isharat*; Nasir al-Din al-Tusi answered him in Ibn Sina's defense. In both cases the debate stayed inside Avicennian terms. In twelfth-century Toledo, Dominicus Gundissalinus, with a Jewish collaborator known in Latin as Avendauth, translated the psychological and metaphysical parts of *al-Shifa'* into Latin. Gerard of Cremona's corpus includes Ibn Sina's *Canon of Medicine*, not that philosophical summa. The distinction between essence and existence, and the account of the rational soul, entered the Latin discussions of Thomas Aquinas and Duns Scotus.
