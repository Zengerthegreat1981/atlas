# Anatta and the Denial of a Fixed Self in Buddhism

The dismantling of the illusion that there is a soul or fixed origin (atman) within the human being, and the demonstration that what is called the "self" is composed of five perishable aggregates (skandhas): body, sensation, perception, mental formations, and consciousness.

## Its Place among the Three Marks of Existence

Anatta is one of the three marks of existence (trilakshana) in early Buddhist teaching, alongside anicca (constant change) and dukkha (suffering): since all phenomena are changing (anicca) and nothing in them is fixed, there cannot exist within them a fixed, substantial "self" (anatta), and clinging to the illusion of this self is the source of dukkha.

## The Founding Text and the Mode of Argument

In the discourse "Anattalakkhana" (the discourse on the mark of non-self) in the Pali canon, the Buddha is reported to have addressed the first five ascetics, reviewing the five aggregates one by one. His argument is that each aggregate tends to affliction and change, and if it were a self we could command it to be as we wish, which does not happen. And what changes and causes pain cannot be said to be "this is mine, this am I, this is my self." So anatta here is not an absolute denial of the person in practical life, but a denial of a permanent independent essence standing behind experience.

Scholars disagree over how to read the early texts. Some hold that the Buddha denied any self whatsoever; others argue that he mainly taught that none of the five aggregates should be identified as a self. The Pali canon also records his silence when the wanderer Vacchagotta asked whether a self exists (Samyutta Nikaya 44.10), which interpreters have understood in different ways.

## The Chariot Example

The texts of the Milinda Panha (The Questions of King Milinda) give a famous example: the sage Nagasena asks the king about the chariot in which he came, whether it is the wheels, the axle or the frame. It becomes clear that "chariot" is a conventional name applied to an assemblage of parts in a certain arrangement. Likewise "person" is a name applied to the flow of psychological and bodily parts without an independent substance. Nevertheless the school acknowledges the continuity of actions and their fruits (karma) through the chain of causes, so the moral agent remains without the need for an enduring soul.

## Comparison with the Islamic and Western Traditions

Anatta stands at the opposite end from the conception of the soul in Islamic philosophy: for Ibn Sina and al-Ghazali the soul is a spiritual substance that remains after the body perishes, and it is the basis of reckoning and reward. On the other hand, what comes close to anatta in the West is the bundle theory of David Hume, who found in introspection nothing but a succession of perceptions, as well as Derek Parfit's analysis of personal identity. However, Buddhism makes this vision a path to liberation from attachment, not merely a theoretical position.

## Limits and Critique

Anatta was criticized by Vedantic Hinduism, which saw in the "atman" the basis of consciousness and liberation, and by Jainism. Within Buddhism itself a debate arose over how to explain memory and moral responsibility: the Pudgalavada school came with the concept of an indeterminable "person," and the later schools (Abhidharma, then Madhyamaka with Nagarjuna) presented more precise analyses of the meaning of emptiness (shunyata). Contemporary objectors ask how the unity of conscious experience is to be explained in the absence of a self, a question that remains open in Buddhist studies and comparative philosophy.
