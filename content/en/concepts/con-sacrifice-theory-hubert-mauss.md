# Hubert & Mauss's Theory of Sacrifice

A sociological-structural model for explaining religious sacrifice, developed by Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss in their joint study *Essai sur la nature et la fonction du sacrifice* (1899), published in the journal *L'Année Sociologique* founded by Émile Durkheim, and translated into English by W. D. Halls as *Sacrifice: Its Nature and Function* (1964).

## The Procedural Mechanism: Passage through an Intermediary

Hubert and Mauss propose that sacrifice is a procedural mechanism that connects the realms of the sacred and the profane through an **intermediary** (the sacrificial victim), which absorbs the danger of contact with the sacred instead of the "sacrifier" (the person or group for whom the sacrifice is performed and who receives its benefits). The victim is first consecrated (passing from the profane to the sacred), then destroyed or consumed (so that the quality of sanctity is transferred or emptied through it), and the community then returns, or enters its new state, without touching the sacred directly in a dangerous manner. This stage-by-stage procedural analysis (entry, transformation, exit) shares a structural kinship with Arnold van Gennep's model of rites of passage (*Les rites de passage*, 1909), with its phases of separation, transition and incorporation, although Hubert and Mauss developed their model independently of it and focused on a single rite.

## Henri Hubert

Henri Hubert (1872–1927) was a historian, archaeologist and sociologist of religion in his own right. Born in Paris, he studied at the École normale supérieure, qualified in history, and pursued oriental and religious studies at the École pratique des hautes études, entering the circle around Durkheim and becoming a close collaborator of Mauss on *L'Année Sociologique*. From 1898 he worked at the museum of national antiquities at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, where he applied wide archaeological knowledge to the organization of its collections, and he later taught at the École pratique des hautes études, on the early religions of Europe, and at the École du Louvre. With Mauss he also wrote the *Outline of a General Theory of Magic* (1902–1903, published in *L'Année Sociologique* in 1904). On his own he wrote an influential study of how time is represented in religion and magic (1905), which treats sacred time as qualitative, structured by festivals and critical dates, and unlike uniform measured time. His major work on the Celts appeared posthumously in 1932. His archaeological and historical learning is often overshadowed by his partner's later fame.

## Methodological Impact

Hubert and Mauss directly drew on William Robertson Smith's analysis of Semitic sacrifice (the communal sacrificial meal), but they shaped from it a general procedural model not confined to any single Semitic context. It is applicable to multiple sacrificial systems (Vedic, Greek, Hebrew). This structural generalization made their work a founding reference for every later anthropological study of ritual that is concerned with the structure of ritual action more than its doctrinal content.

## The Theological Roots Implicit in the Model

Ivan Strenski, in *Theology and the First Theory of Sacrifice*, argues that the Hubert and Mauss model, despite its claim of scientific sociological neutrality, was shaped by Christian (in particular Catholic) theological debates about sacrifice and their idea of substitution, namely that a single victim can bear, on behalf of the whole community, the cost of contact with a dangerous sacred. This idea is very close to the Christian doctrine of redemption (Christ as a victim who atones for the sins of humanity). This argument raises a broader methodological question about the extent to which early "secular" theories of religion were influenced by implicit theological frameworks that the researchers themselves did not acknowledge.

## Burkert's Competing Theory

The classical philologist Walter Burkert developed a fundamentally different theory of the origin of sacrifice. He proposed, in *Homo Necans* (German original 1972), that it originates in the ritualization of the hunting and killing practised by early humans, with the guilt and anxiety that accompany killing, rather than from an abstract sociological procedural structure as in Hubert and Mauss. He relied particularly on the analysis of ancient Greek sacrificial rituals, such as the Athenian *Bouphonia*, in which the axe itself was symbolically put on trial after the ox was slaughtered, in a ritual that dramatizes the guilt of killing.
