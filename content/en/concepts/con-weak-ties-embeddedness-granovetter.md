# The Strength of Weak Ties and Economic Embeddedness in Granovetter

Two concepts formulated by Mark Granovetter that explain, respectively, why relatively distant social relations are more informationally useful than intimate ones, and how economic action always remains embedded in a wider social structure.

## Content

In his pivotal article "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973), Granovetter presented a counterintuitive empirical finding. Based on his study of how individuals actually obtain new jobs, he discovered that "weak" ties (Weak Ties: passing acquaintances or relatively distant former colleagues) are more useful in transmitting new information (such as unannounced job opportunities) than "strong" ties (Strong Ties: close friends and family members). The reason is that strong ties tend to be closed within similar, overlapping social circles (your close friends often know the same people and information you already know), whereas weak ties act as "bridges" (Bridges) linking the individual to distant, diverse social circles that carry entirely new information not available within his direct intimate circle. Later, in "Economic Action and Social Structure" (1985), Granovetter developed the concept of "embeddedness" (Embeddedness). He argued that economic action, contrary to classical economics' assumption of an isolated "rational actor" freed from any social context, always remains embedded in an actual network of social relations that shapes the trust available, the information obtainable and the real opportunities open to the economic actor. An economic decision cannot be understood in isolation from the social network within which it is taken.

The 1973 argument also rests on a structural point about triads: if one person has strong ties to two others, those two are likely to be acquainted as well. It follows that a tie serving as the only short route between otherwise separate groups is unlikely to be a strong one. Granovetter used this reasoning to connect small-scale interaction with larger patterns, arguing that a community rich in strong ties but poor in bridges may be fragmented and find it harder to organise collectively. *Getting a Job* (1974), based on a study of professional, technical and managerial workers in Newton, Massachusetts, showed that personal contacts were a major route into employment.

The 1985 article used "embeddedness", a term earlier associated with Karl Polanyi, in its own sense. Granovetter criticised both an "undersocialized" view of action, which treats actors as isolated pursuers of self-interest, and an "oversocialized" view, which treats them as automatically following internalised norms. Both, he argued, neglect ongoing concrete relations. Networks can generate trust and discourage malfeasance, but they can also provide the means for fraud and conflict, so embeddedness guarantees no particular outcome. He applied the argument to Oliver Williamson's account of markets and hierarchies.

## Influence

The concept of "the strength of weak ties" became one of the most cited concepts in the history of sociology. Together with the concept of "embeddedness", it became a cornerstone of the network approach in sociology and of the new economic sociology, with influence reaching economics and computer science. Both ideas have been drawn on in studying digital networks and social media, though later work, for example Damon Centola's studies of complex contagion, which build on small-world network models such as Duncan Watts's, has qualified the thesis, noting that weak ties spread information well but that behaviours requiring reinforcement may spread better through clustered, strong ties.
