# Healing Practices

Healing practices are the organised ways in which societies respond to sickness, suffering, and misfortune. They range from herbal remedies and bone-setting to prayer, spirit possession ceremonies, and the high technology of the modern hospital. Medical anthropology studies these practices not as a catalogue of exotic cures but as cultural systems: each has its own ideas about what the body is, what causes illness, who has the authority to heal, and what counts as a cure. To study healing is to study how people understand their bodies, their relationships, and their place in the world.

## Definition

A healing practice is more than a treatment. It involves a diagnosis, a healer, a sufferer, and a community, and it unfolds in a sequence of actions — consultation, ritual, remedy, recovery — that give meaning to the experience of illness. Arthur Kleinman, in *Patients and Healers in the Cultural Context* (1980), distinguished between disease, the biological malfunction as biomedicine understands it, and illness, the lived experience of being unwell. Other scholars, such as Allan Young, developed a third term, sickness, the social meanings and roles attached to being ill. A healing practice may address all three at once, or it may address one while ignoring the others.

Healers take many forms: the herbalist who knows the properties of plants, the diviner who identifies the spirit or witch responsible, the shaman who journeys to retrieve a lost soul, the priest who prays, and the doctor who prescribes. In most societies these figures coexist and compete, and sufferers move between them, trying one remedy after another. Healing is therefore also a matter of choice, of trust, and of what a family can afford.

## Origins and Development

The anthropology of healing begins with W. H. R. Rivers, whose *Medicine, Magic, and Religion* (1924) argued that in many societies illness was attributed to human, spiritual, or natural causes, and that each kind of cause called for a different kind of remedy. Rivers insisted that these ideas were rational within their own terms: given a belief in sorcery, it was reasonable to seek a diviner rather than a drug.

E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande* (1937) developed this insight into a classic. Among the Azande of what was then the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan (their homeland also extends into neighbouring countries), witchcraft explained why a particular person fell ill at a particular time, while natural causes explained how the illness worked. The two explanations were complementary, not contradictory, and the oracle — a poison test administered to a chicken — provided a way of resolving disputes and deciding what to do. Evans-Pritchard showed that a healing system is also a system of knowledge and of justice.

In the second half of the twentieth century, medical anthropology became a field in its own right. Kleinman's work in Taiwan and China showed how patients and healers negotiate the meaning of illness across the boundaries of biomedicine and traditional medicine, and Thomas Csordas, in *The Sacred Self* (1994), showed how healing works through the body itself — through touch, breath, emotion, and the experience of the sacred — in charismatic Christian healing in North America.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One central debate concerns efficacy: do healing practices work, and if so, how? Anthropologists have shown that healing can be effective in ways that have little to do with the remedy itself — through the placebo effect, through the mobilisation of social support, through the relief of anxiety, and through the restoration of meaning. Jerome Frank argued as early as 1961 that all healing systems share common features — a shared worldview, a trusted healer, a ritual that arouses emotion and hope — and that these features themselves have therapeutic power. The question is not whether a practice is "really" effective but what kind of effectiveness it offers, and for whom.

A second debate concerns the politics of healing. Who gets to heal, and who gets healed? Colonial governments often criminalised indigenous healers while promoting Western medicine, and postcolonial states have sometimes done the same. Women healers, in particular, have often been marginalised — as witches, as unqualified, as unscientific — even when they were the main source of care in their communities. At the same time, traditional healing has been a resource for cultural resistance and for the assertion of identity.

A third dimension is the encounter between healing systems. In most of the world today, people use biomedicine and other forms of healing side by side, and the boundaries between them are porous: a hospital patient may also consult a herbalist, and a traditional healer may refer a patient to a clinic. Medical pluralism is the norm, not the exception, and understanding how people navigate it is a central task of medical anthropology.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Healing practices are at the centre of contemporary global health debates: the integration of traditional medicine into national health systems, the commercialisation of herbal remedies, the ethics of clinical trials in poor countries, and the response to epidemics. The COVID-19 pandemic showed how healing is also a matter of trust — in governments, in experts, in neighbours — and how quickly that trust can break down.

Recent anthropology has also turned its attention to the suffering of the healer, to the burnout of nurses and doctors, and to the ways in which biomedicine itself can be experienced as alienating or violent. The study of healing practices, once focused on distant others, now includes the hospital ward, the clinic, and the laboratory — and it continues to ask the same question: what does it mean to be healed, and who decides?
