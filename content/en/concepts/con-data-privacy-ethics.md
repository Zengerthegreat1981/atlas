# Data Privacy Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Data privacy ethics is a branch of digital applied ethics. It examines the moral foundations of an individual's right to control his personal information, and the limits of what other parties (governments, companies, or researchers) may collect, analyze, and use from such data. New problems have emerged with the rise of large-scale computing and artificial intelligence, where data can be linked and inferred on a scale far beyond what traditional legislators imagined.

## Origins of the Sanctity of Privacy in Islamic Jurisprudence

The principle of the sanctity of privacy was rooted in Islamic jurisprudence from an early period, through explicit texts. The most prominent is the Qur'anic prohibition of spying, in the words "and do not spy." On this principle, jurists built a broad domain known as the sanctity of intruding on people's private affairs. They required seeking permission before entering homes, and prohibited pursuing people's private faults. A report in the collection of Abu Dawud has Ibn Mas'ud declining to pursue a man whose wrongdoing had come to light, saying that they had been forbidden to spy. Some contemporary scholars extend these texts by analogy to the collection of personal data without the owner's consent or a compelling legitimate need.

## Historical Context in Western Thought

The modern legal concept of privacy crystallized with the article by Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy" (1890), which described privacy as the "right to be let alone." The digital ethicist Luciano Floridi reformulated this right within the framework of the philosophy of information. He argued that personal identity itself has become an "informational entity" (the infosphere) that extends digitally, and that a data breach is an assault on part of the self, not merely on external information about it. Helen Nissenbaum formulated the concept of "contextual integrity." It holds that the morality of information flows is not measured by whether information is absolutely secret or public, but by the extent to which its use conforms to the norms of the context in which it was collected. For example, medical information disclosed to a physician is not authorized to be shared with an employer.

## Applications and Contemporary Problems

A fundamental problem arises with the "presumed consent" on which lengthy digital terms of use rely, which users rarely read. This makes consent formal rather than real. A concern often raised is that health applications may collect fitness data that is passed to third parties such as insurers. The user may regard the data as harmless personal information, yet it could be used to influence premiums or coverage. This is a clear violation of the principle of contextual integrity.
