# Decolonial Data Sovereignty

A theoretical and policy framework within decolonial anthropology that demands that Indigenous peoples and marginalized communities have effective control over the data collected about them: how it is gathered, stored and used, and who has the right to access it. It stands against treating such data as an open resource that external institutions (governmental, academic or commercial) extract and exploit without genuine community oversight.

## The Concept: Indigenous Data

"Indigenous data" is defined as any data, information or knowledge, in any form, that concerns Indigenous peoples, their nations and communities at both the collective and individual levels: data about their resources and environments, data about them as individuals, and data about them as groups. "Data sovereignty" starts from the principle that a people's sovereignty over its land and traditional resources logically extends to its sovereignty over the data produced about that land and community, as a contemporary extension of the right of self-determination.

## The CARE Principles

In 2019, the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group, within the Research Data Alliance, developed a normative framework known as the **CARE Principles** (Collective benefit, Authority to control, Responsibility, Ethics). It was intended to complement the technical **FAIR** principles that dominate open data management (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which were criticized for their purely technical focus without regard to who holds the right to control the data. The CARE Principles rest on four pillars: **Collective Benefit**, designing data systems so that Indigenous peoples themselves benefit from them and not external parties alone; **Authority to Control**, enabling Indigenous communities to determine how their cultures, resources, knowledge and members are represented within any data system; **Responsibility**, the obligation of those working with Indigenous data to show how it is used to support Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit; and **Ethics**, the principle that Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing should be the primary concern at every stage of the data life cycle.

## The Colonial Context

Researchers link this demand to a broader critique of the long history of colonial "knowledge extraction," in which Western researchers and institutions gathered information, knowledge, and genetic and cultural resources from Indigenous peoples without their consent or sharing in the resulting benefit, in a manner similar to the colonial extraction of natural resources. Data sovereignty seeks to break this pattern in the digital age, when the capacity to collect and analyze data (through remote sensing, genetic databases and digital applications) is multiplying at a pace that exceeds the ability of affected communities to monitor it or negotiate its terms.

## Applications

- Including prior, informed consent requirements at the community level (not only individual consent) before collecting genetic or cultural data from Indigenous communities in research projects.
- Establishing data storage infrastructure that is managed and controlled by the Indigenous community itself, rather than by an external research or governmental institution.
- Reviewing academic and governmental "open data" policies so that they do not conflict with the right of Indigenous peoples to control their own data.

## Limitations

The tension remains between the "open data" principle that dominates contemporary science policy (which assumes that research data should be made available to all to promote scientific transparency) and the Indigenous sovereignty principle that resists this openness. The mechanisms for applying the CARE Principles also differ in practice from one institution to another, and there is not yet a unified global legal enforcement mechanism that obliges researchers and institutions to comply with them.
