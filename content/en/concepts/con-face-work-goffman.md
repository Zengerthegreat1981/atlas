# Face-Work in Goffman

A concept coined by Erving Goffman to describe the set of micro-strategies that individuals constantly use in everyday interaction to protect their own social image and that of their interlocutors from threat or embarrassment.

## Content

In the essay "On Face-Work" (1955), reprinted in *Interaction Ritual* (1967), Goffman defined "face" as the positive social value that a person effectively claims for himself or herself through the line that others assume he or she has taken during a particular interaction, built on shared assumptions that others temporarily accept about him or her. From this definition he formulated the concept of "face-work": the set of coordinated actions that participants in an interaction undertake to make what they are doing consistent with the "face" to be protected, whether their own face or that of others. These strategies include "avoidance", keeping away from situations that might threaten face in the first place; the "corrective process", a series of exchanges (apology, justification, acceptance, thanks) used to repair a threat to face that has actually occurred; and "tactful blindness", the deliberate overlooking of a small slip committed by the other in order to avoid embarrassing him or her publicly. Goffman held that these small everyday rituals perform a function similar to that of collective religious rituals in Émile Durkheim, affirming the shared moral order and social solidarity, but at the level of fleeting everyday individual interaction rather than the great periodic collective celebration.

## The Ritual Nature of Everyday Interaction

Goffman holds that every face-to-face interaction, however fleeting or trivial it may seem (a quick greeting in the corridor, a short chat in the elevator), is governed by quasi-ritual rules that protect the "sacredness" of each participant's social face, just as religious rituals protect the sacredness of shared symbols in Durkheim. When a person stumbles or commits an embarrassing slip of the tongue before others, those present implicitly recognize the "rules of the game" that oblige them either to overlook the incident tactfully or to help "repair" it through a joking remark that eases the tension, not because they necessarily care about the stumbling person's feelings personally, but because the stability of the "interaction order" itself requires continuous collective protection of the faces of all participants, since any unmanaged threat to face risks undermining the mutual trust necessary for the continuation of any future social interaction.

## The Difference between Protective and Corrective Work

Goffman noted a defensive orientation toward saving one's own face and a protective orientation toward saving the face of others. He also distinguished two complementary kinds of face-work: avoidance practices, anticipatory strategies used before any threat occurs (such as reticence in speaking about topics that might embarrass him or her, or choosing words carefully to avoid misunderstanding); and "corrective practices", the sequence of steps used after an actual threat has occurred: the challenge (drawing attention to the offence), the offering (providing an explanation or apology), the acceptance (the other party's acceptance of this explanation), and the thanks (ending the exchange with a symbolic sign of reconciliation that restores the shaken social balance).

## Impact

This analysis deeply influenced the theory of linguistic politeness (Politeness Theory) developed by Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson in sociolinguistics, which borrowed Goffman's concept of "face" directly to analyse the linguistic strategies speakers use to soften face-threatening speech acts (such as requests, refusals or criticism). This analysis also partly paved the way for Hochschild's later analysis of "emotional labor", although the two concepts differ in focus (the external social face in Goffman versus the internal emotional state in Hochschild), and the concept of "face-work" remains a basic analytical tool in studies of symbolic interaction and the sociology of emotions to this day.
