# The Coase Theorem

A condensed formulation (devised by the economist George Stigler as a summary of the argument of **Ronald Coase** in his article "The Problem of Social Cost", 1960) of a central thesis in the **economic analysis of law**.

## The Basic Formulation

The theorem states that **in the absence of transaction costs and under clearly defined property rights**, the parties concerned with an economic dispute (such as a disagreement between a polluting factory and an affected farmer) will reach, through free bargaining, the **economically most efficient allocation of resources**, regardless of **who the law initially grants the right to** (does the farmer have the right to clean air, or does the factory have the right to produce?). The final economic outcome, in terms of efficiency, is the same in both cases; only **who pays whom** in the bargaining process changes.

## The Real Lesson: The Importance of Transaction Costs

The paradox is that Coase himself formulated this point not to prove that the distribution of legal rights is practically unimportant, but **precisely the opposite**. Since the real world is full of high transaction costs (the difficulty of organizing bargaining among many parties, the costs of information, and strategic behaviour), the condition of "absence of transaction costs" is rarely met in practice. And where it is not met, **the law's initial distribution of rights becomes economically decisive**, because subsequent bargaining will not automatically correct any inefficient distribution. This is what makes the theorem an analytical tool for determining **when the legislator or the judge must intervene carefully** to allocate rights efficiently, and not an argument that law is unimportant, as it is sometimes misunderstood.

## Practical Impact

The theorem has become a standard normative tool for analysing questions of intellectual property, environmental pollution, broadcasting rights, and the allocation of radio frequencies. In these cases, economic analysts of law ask: **which distribution of rights reduces the need for costly later bargaining, given the transaction costs expected in each context?**

## Reservations and Limits

Several criticisms have been levelled at the theorem. One is that the assumption of "free" bargaining ignores disparities in purchasing power and information between the parties (for example, a factory may have bargaining resources far greater than those of an individual farmer). Another is the point, associated with Guido Calabresi and A. Douglas Melamed ("Property Rules, Liability Rules, and Inalienability", 1972), that the initial allocation of rights has a **distributional effect** that is not limited to efficiency alone but affects justice between the parties. Critics have also observed that transaction costs are not a marginal exception but the rule in most collective environmental disputes (such as the pollution of a river that harms thousands of residents). This makes the theorem, in its abstract form, closer to a thought experiment that shows why transaction costs matter than to a ready-made legislative recipe.

## Relationship to the Law and Economics Movement

The Coase theorem formed the cornerstone of the "economic analysis of law" school, associated especially with Richard Posner at the University of Chicago and Guido Calabresi at Yale. Coase himself received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1991. It transformed traditional legal questions (such as who bears the harm of pollution) into questions about economic efficiency that can be measured. Calabresi's analysis of the "cheapest cost avoider" became an influential standard in debates over tort liability rules, offering an economic alternative to moral standards of fault.
