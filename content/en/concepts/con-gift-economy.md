# Gift Economy

An economic system based on the exchange of gifts and reciprocal obligations rather than on direct monetary exchange. A gift economy differs from a market economy in that value is defined by social relationships rather than by a price set in advance, and social obligations continue over time rather than ending once goods have been exchanged.

## Definition and Theoretical Origins

In his well-known essay *The Gift* (*Essai sur le don*, 1925), Marcel Mauss argued that economic exchange in non-capitalist societies is not based on a purely utilitarian foundation, as classical economists assume, but on a network of intertwined social obligations. Mauss developed his theory by studying documented real exchange systems among different peoples: the Kula ring of the Trobriand Islands, documented by Bronisław Malinowski, and the potlatch among the peoples of the northwest coast of North America, documented above all by Franz Boas among the Kwakwaka'wakw (Kwakiutl). Mauss showed that a gift does not simply produce an equal counter-exchange. It creates a lasting social obligation that binds the exchanging parties in a relationship.

## The Three-Part Logic of the Gift

Mauss presented a basic three-part model of the exchange process: **giving**, in which a person offers a gift with a particular social meaning; **receiving**, in which the other party accepts the gift and becomes socially indebted; and **returning**, in which the recipient gives back an equal or greater gift, but not immediately, rather after a period of time. This temporal delay is decisive. It preserves the character of the social relationship and prevents it from becoming a settled transaction. If an immediate counter-exchange occurs, the process may become a purely economic exchange that ends the obligation.

## Difference from the Market and from Barter

The gift economy differs fundamentally from the market economy in several respects:

- In a market, the price is determined objectively and in advance, and has no connection with the personal relationship between seller and buyer.
- In a gift economy, value is determined by the social relationship itself, and the degree of intimacy and obligation is reflected in the exchange.
- In a market, the process ends once the exchange and payment are complete.
- In a gift economy, the process truly begins after the first exchange and continues through multiple cycles.

## Ethnographic Examples

### The Kula Ring

Documented by Bronisław Malinowski among the Trobriand people of Papua New Guinea. Men exchange white armbands (*mwali*) and red necklaces (*soulava*) in a cycle called the Kula, in which these valuable objects circulate among the different islands through specific exchange partners. These objects are not consumed and are not sold for money. The most valued pieces have names and histories of circulation, and holding them brings renown. Malinowski argued in *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) that the Kula is not a utilitarian exchange but a **social system that links the different islands together**.

### The Potlatch

Documented by Franz Boas among the Kwakiutl (Kwakwaka'wakw) and described for other peoples of the northwest coast of North America. A host, often a chief, holds a public feast in which he gives large gifts to his guests and, in some cases, destroys wealth (such as blankets or the prestigious engraved "coppers"). Rather than diminishing, the host's social prestige increases, since he has demonstrated his capacity to spend and give; the potlatch is best understood as an investment in rank and prestige rather than as waste.

### The Moka Exchange

Documented by Andrew Strathern (*The Rope of Moka*, 1971) among the Melpa of the Mount Hagen area of Papua New Guinea. Men, especially "big-men", exchange pigs and shell valuables in ceremonial gifts, and a moka gift is normally returned with an increment. Marilyn Strathern's later work on Melanesian exchange (*The Gender of the Gift*, 1988) emphasised that such gifts help to constitute persons and relations: a person becomes known through what they give and what they receive.

## Critics and Later Developments

### Marshall Sahlins

Sahlins developed Mauss's model by adding a classification of types of exchange in *Stone Age Economics* (1972): **generalized reciprocity**, in which no immediate return is expected (between close relatives); **balanced reciprocity**, in which a return of similar value is expected within a close time frame (between trading partners); and **negative reciprocity**, in which each party tries to get the better of the other (bargaining and cheating).

### Annette Weiner

Annette Weiner, who carried out her own fieldwork in the Trobriands, argued in *Inalienable Possessions* (1992) that exchange turns on keeping as well as giving. Certain possessions are treated as **"inalienable"** and are withheld from circulation as far as possible, and their retention underwrites identity and rank.

### David Graeber

Graeber criticized the standard story of economic origins in *Debt: The First 5000 Years* (2011). He rejected the "myth of barter", the idea that money and markets arose out of barter, and argued that credit and mutual obligation, not barter, structured economic life before coined money. His account treats debt, credit and moral obligation as central, and shows that gift-like and market-like relations have long coexisted.

## Contemporary Criticisms

### Anthropological Critique

Some anthropological voices objected to portraying the gift economy as a simple "opposite" of the market. They argued that this oversimplifies the complexity of real systems, and that most societies combine multiple systems.

### Colonial Critique

Some critics have noted that treating the "gift economy" as the opposite of capitalism may reproduce a colonial dichotomy: "primitive" societies that give, versus "modern" societies that trade.

## Contemporary Relevance

In recent times, contemporary studies have renewed interest in the "gift economy" in new contexts:

- **The internet and open-source software**: a modern application of gift logic, in which programmers share their work without direct compensation but gain reputation and the respect of the community.
- **The cooperative economy**: a reassessment of gift societies as an alternative model to the capitalist economy.
- **Social movements**: the use of gift principles in organizing solidarity projects.
