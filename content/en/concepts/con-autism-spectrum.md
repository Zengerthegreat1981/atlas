# Autism Spectrum Disorder

## Definition

Autism spectrum disorder is a neurodevelopmental condition that appears in early childhood. It is characterized by persistent deficits in communication and social interaction, restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior and interests, and unusual sensory sensitivity. DSM-5 (2013) diagnoses it on two domains, persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction, and restricted, repetitive patterns of behavior, interests, or activities (including atypical sensory responses). Under the umbrella term "spectrum," it replaced the separate DSM-IV categories of autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified. ICD-11 uses a similar "autism spectrum disorder" category (6A02).

## First Descriptions

Leo Kanner, an Austrian-American psychiatrist at Johns Hopkins University, described eleven children in his article "Autistic Disturbances of Affective Contact" (*Nervous Child*, 1943). In the same period, Hans Asperger in Vienna described children he called "autistic psychopaths" in an article published in *Archiv für Psychiatrie und Nervenkrankheiten* in 1944. Lorna Wing reintroduced Asperger's work to English-speaking audiences in *Psychological Medicine* in 1981. She and Judith Gould formulated the concept of an autistic "spectrum" and the triad of impairments in social interaction, communication, and imagination (1979). Edith Sheffer, in *Asperger's Children* (Norton, 2018), argued from archival records that Asperger worked under the Nazi regime and took part in its system for sorting children; other historians have disputed the extent of his involvement, and the debate has prompted reconsideration of naming the syndrome after him.

## Successive Explanations

In the 1950s and 1960s, the "refrigerator mother" theory, promoted by Bruno Bettelheim, was influential. It was later rejected, and parents were absolved of blame. A twin study published by Bailey and colleagues in *Psychological Medicine* in 1995 showed that heredity is a very large factor. In 1985, Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith proposed in *Cognition* that autistic children may lack a "theory of mind" (an idea Baron-Cohen later developed as "mindblindness"). Treatments varied from applied behavior analysis, whose results Lovaas published in 1987 in the *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* (and which is criticized for its intensity and by advocates of neurodiversity), to early naturalistic developmental interventions. No medication treats the core symptoms. Medications are used for accompanying symptoms such as irritability, sleep, and attention, under specialist supervision.

## The Vaccine Myth and Neurodiversity

In 1998, Wakefield published a paper in *The Lancet* linking the triple vaccine to autism. It was retracted by the journal in 2010 after the UK General Medical Council found ethical and procedural violations in the research, and large population studies have found no association between the vaccine and autism. In contrast, the neurodiversity movement emerged, a term associated with Judy Singer and Harvey Blume in the late 1990s. Steve Silberman documented it in *NeuroTribes* (Avery, 2015). The movement views autism as a human difference rather than merely a disease. This view is in turn opposed by families of people with severe autism and associated intellectual disability.

## The Arab Context

Medieval Arab medical heritage contains no description matching autism, and the term cannot be projected onto old texts. Today, referral and diagnosis are often delayed in many Arab countries because of the scarcity of assessment centers, the lack of Arabic-adapted measurement tools, and family stigma. In some families, the condition is attributed to the evil eye or to possession. Awareness work is therefore linked to approaches that address stigma and to healing traditions.

## Limits of the Concept

The expansion of diagnosis and the rising prevalence reported by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (ADDM) are criticized. Part of the increase is attributed to broader criteria and better detection. The great variability among cases also means that the word "spectrum" sometimes serves as an umbrella wider than its predictive value.
