# Property Rights

**Property rights** are the set of legal powers granted to an individual or entity over a particular thing: the right to use, exploit and dispose of it, and to prevent others from interfering with it. They are among the oldest legal institutions and among the most philosophically contested, since they intersect considerations of individual liberty, distributive justice and the efficiency of the economic system.

## Property in Islamic Jurisprudence

Islamic jurisprudence treated property (*milk*) as a legitimate right arising from specific causes, such as reviving dead land (*ihya al-mawat*), labour, inheritance and contract, but it constrained that right with a clear social function. In the Islamic view, wealth is God's wealth, over which humans are made vicegerents (*istikhlaf*); it entails rights for others, such as zakat; and dispositions over it are restricted by the prohibitions on extravagance and on harming others ("there shall be neither harm nor reciprocated harm"). Al-Shatibi, in *al-Muwafaqat*, listed the preservation of wealth among the five universal objectives (*al-kulliyat al-khams*) that the Sharia seeks to preserve. Jurists also developed detailed theories of joint ownership (*sharika*) and of *waqf*, a distinctive form of withdrawing private property in favour of a permanent public purpose.

## Foundational Western Theories: Locke and Hegel

In his *Two Treatises of Government* (1689), **John Locke** offered the most famous philosophical justification of private property for modern liberalism, through the **labour theory**: a person owns themselves and the fruit of their labour. When they mix their labour with a common natural material, such as cultivating unclaimed land, they acquire legitimate ownership of it, on condition that they do not spoil any surplus beyond their need and that they leave "enough, and as good, for others" (the Lockean proviso). **Hegel**, in *Philosophy of Right* (1821), linked property to the realization of the individual's free will in the external world, holding private property to be a necessary condition for the development of moral personality.

## Socialist and Distributive Critique

In *Capital* (1867), **Karl Marx** attacked private ownership of the means of production as the foundation of class exploitation. **John Rawls**, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), defended limits on property subject to the difference principle, which permits inequality in wealth only if it benefits the least advantaged. **Robert Nozick**, in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), defended an entitlement theory holding that any distribution arising from just acquisition and voluntary transfer is itself just, regardless of the pattern of inequality that results.

## A Contemporary Example

The tension between private property and the public interest appears in the principle of **eminent domain**, as in the American case *Kelo v. City of New London* (2005). In that case the U.S. Supreme Court upheld the taking of private property for an economic development project run by private entities, a ruling that provoked wide debate over the limits of state power over individual property.
