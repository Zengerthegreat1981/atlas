# Social Control

Social control is the set of mechanisms by which a society secures conformity to its norms and expectations. It is the answer to one of sociology's oldest questions: given that people have conflicting desires and interests, what holds a society together? The answer is not simply force. Much of social control is quiet and routine — the approval of friends, the expectations of family, the habits learned in school — and it works best when people comply without noticing that they are being controlled. But behind the informal pressures stand formal institutions: the police, the courts, the prison, and the psychiatric hospital, which deal with those who do not conform. Social control therefore spans the full range from a raised eyebrow to a prison sentence.

## Definition

Sociologists distinguish between informal and formal social control. Informal control operates through everyday interaction: gossip, ridicule, praise, shunning, and the internalised sense of shame and guilt that accompanies the violation of a norm. It is decentralised, continuous, and in most societies far more important than the police. Formal control is exercised by institutions that have been given an explicit mandate to define and punish deviance: the law, the criminal justice system, regulatory agencies, and the professions. A second distinction is between positive and negative control. Positive control rewards conformity — through praise, promotion, honours, and material rewards — while negative control punishes deviance. A third distinction, drawn from Michel Foucault, is between repressive and productive forms of power: control does not only prohibit behaviour, it also produces the kinds of people a society needs, through schooling, medical examination, and the classification of populations. Finally, control may be internal or external. Socialisation internalises norms so deeply that the individual polices herself; where that fails, external sanctions take over.

## Origins and Development

The concept was introduced by Edward Ross in *Social Control* (1901), which argued that order in modern societies depended less on the state than on a dense web of social institutions — public opinion, custom, religion, and education — that shaped the individual from within. Émile Durkheim provided the theoretical foundation in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), treating social facts as external and constraining, and in his studies of suicide and religion, which showed how integration into groups protects against deviance. In the mid-twentieth century, Talcott Parsons incorporated social control into functionalist theory in *The Social System* (1951), arguing that societies maintain equilibrium through mechanisms that correct deviance and resocialise the deviant, with illness and crime treated as forms of deviance to be managed. The 1960s and 1970s produced a critical turn. Labelling theorists argued that control agencies do not simply respond to deviance but create it, by applying stigmatising labels that push people into deviant identities. Foucault's *Discipline and Punish* (1975) recast the history of punishment as the spread of disciplinary techniques — surveillance, timetables, examinations — from the prison into the school, the hospital, and the factory. Travis Hirschi's *Causes of Delinquency* (1969) offered a different, influential account: people conform not because they fear punishment but because of their bonds to society — attachment to others, commitment to conventional goals, involvement in ordinary activities, and belief in shared values.

## Key Debates

One enduring debate is whether social control should be understood as a neutral mechanism of order or as an instrument of domination. Functionalists emphasise its role in maintaining stability and protecting members of society from harm. Conflict theorists emphasise that the norms enforced are those of the powerful, and that control falls most heavily on the poor, racial minorities, and other marginalised groups. A second debate concerns the balance between informal and formal control. Some sociologists argue that modern societies have become more reliant on formal, bureaucratic control as communities have weakened, while others point to the persistence and power of informal mechanisms, from workplace cultures to online shaming. A third debate is about the scope of the concept. If everything that shapes behaviour counts as social control, the term risks becoming a synonym for socialisation or for society itself. Some theorists therefore restrict it to the deliberate responses to deviance, while others retain the broader sense in which the term captures the full range of techniques by which order is produced.

## Contemporary Relevance

Social control is a central lens for understanding contemporary life. The expansion of digital surveillance — CCTV, location tracking, facial recognition, and the monitoring of online behaviour by platforms and employers — has given both states and corporations new capacities to observe and shape conduct, often without the knowledge of those observed. Social media has also transformed informal control: public shaming can now be instantaneous and global, and the fear of reputational damage disciplines speech and behaviour in ways that formal law cannot. The criminal justice system remains the most visible site of formal control, and debates about policing, mass incarceration, and racial disparities in sentencing are debates about who is controlled and why. The COVID-19 pandemic provided a vivid illustration of the concept in action, as governments relied on a mix of formal rules, social pressure, and appeals to solidarity to secure compliance with public health measures. Across all these cases, the sociological insight remains the same: order is not natural, and it is not free. It is produced, every day, by a combination of persuasion, habit, and force.
