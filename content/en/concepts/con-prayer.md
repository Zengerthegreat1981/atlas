# Prayer

Prayer is communication with superhuman beings — gods, spirits, ancestors, saints — through words, gestures, song or silence, in which the petitioner asks, thanks, praises, confesses or simply attends. It ranges from the fixed liturgies recited by millions in unison to the whispered, improvised plea of a single person in crisis, and from silent meditation to loud collective supplication. For anthropology, prayer is a fundamental religious act: it is where belief becomes practice, where the relationship between humans and the sacred is enacted, and where individuals and communities articulate their hopes, fears and moral obligations.

## Origins and Development

The first systematic sociological study of prayer was Marcel Mauss's unfinished work "On Prayer", written in 1909 and published only later in his collected works. Mauss defined prayer as a religious rite that is oral and bears directly on sacred things, and he traced its history from the magical formulae of archaic religion — words believed to compel results automatically — to the interior, spiritual prayer of the great world religions. For Mauss, prayer is a social fact: its forms are given by tradition, its efficacy is collective, and even the most private prayer is spoken in a language and a posture learned from others.

Emile Durkheim, in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912), did not make prayer his central subject, but his theory of ritual has shaped its study. He argued that in religious life the sacred is society itself, transfigured, and that the collective effervescence of shared rites binds participants into a moral community. Applied to prayer, this suggests that the person who prays is strengthened not only by belief but by the social force of the group.

Bronislaw Malinowski, in his essay "Magic, Science and Religion" (1925), drew a different distinction. Magic, for him, is practical and technique-like: it is used where outcomes are uncertain, as among the Trobriand Islanders in gardening, fishing, and sea voyaging. Religion, by contrast, centres on the crises of life, above all death, and on ends that are valued in themselves. Prayer as supplication does not fit neatly on either side of this contrast, which has been used and questioned in discussions of how petition differs from spell.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns prayer and magic. Mauss's historical scheme treated prayer as evolving out of magic; later anthropologists argued that the two coexist and interpenetrate, and that the boundary between them is drawn differently in different traditions. A Catholic novena, a Sufi *dhikr*, a Shinto *norito* and a Pentecostal prayer for healing each combine petition, praise and formula in ways that defeat any simple classification.

A second debate concerns language and efficacy. Clifford Geertz, in "Religion as a Cultural System" (1966), argued that religious symbols provide both a model of reality and a model for it. Applied to prayer, this means that prayer tells people how the cosmos is ordered and how they should feel and act within it. The words of prayer are thus performative in a double sense: they address the sacred and they shape the person who prays, cultivating dispositions such as humility, gratitude and patience. Studies of prayer as a bodily practice — kneeling, prostration, raised hands, closed eyes — extend this insight: the body learns the prayer as much as the mind does.

A third debate concerns interiority. The modern Western image of prayer as private, sincere, inward communion is historically specific, and Talal Asad, in *Genealogies of Religion* (1993), argued that the modern conception of religion as a matter of inward belief reflects a particular post-Reformation and secular history. In many traditions, prayer is primarily public, formulaic and embodied — its value lies in correct performance rather than inner feeling — and the anthropologist who looks only for interior belief misses what prayer does. Asad's critique warns against projecting one culture's idea of prayer onto all others.

A fourth dimension is prayer's social work. Prayer circles sustain the sick; intercessory prayer mobilises community support; public prayer asserts identity in contested spaces, from street prayers to parliamentary invocations; and prayer can be protest, as when the oppressed pray aloud for justice. Prayer is also economically significant — pilgrimage, offerings and prayer economies move resources across communities.

## Contemporary Relevance

Prayer remains one of the most widely practised human activities. Surveys in many countries find that large numbers of people pray, including many who do not attend religious services. The forms are changing: prayer apps, televised services, online prayer requests and social-media prayer chains extend old practices into new media, while the COVID-19 pandemic saw a surge in both private prayer and streamed worship. Medical research on intercessory prayer remains contested, but the anthropology of prayer is less concerned with whether prayer heals bodies than with how it sustains persons — giving language to grief, structure to hope, and a community to those who suffer. In a secular age, prayer persists because it does something no other act quite does: it lets people speak to what exceeds them.
