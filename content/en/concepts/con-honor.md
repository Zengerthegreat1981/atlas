# Honor

Honor is the value a person claims for themselves and the value others grant them in return. It is at once deeply personal — a matter of dignity, pride, and self-respect — and profoundly social, because honor exists only in the eyes of others. In societies organised around honor, a person's standing depends on their reputation, on the reputation of their family, and on their willingness to defend both. Anthropology has studied honor above all in the Mediterranean, the Middle East, and South Asia, but its logic — of reputation, challenge, and response — is found in many societies, including the boardroom, the street gang, and the military unit.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish two aspects of honor. The first is honor as status: the recognition that a person belongs to a group of honourable people, whether by birth, by office, or by conduct. The second is honor as virtue: the qualities — courage, generosity, loyalty, self-control — that make a person worthy of that recognition. Julian Pitt-Rivers, in a classic essay of 1966, defined honor as the value of a person in their own eyes and in the eyes of their society, and argued that it is the link between a person's ideals and their social standing.

Honor is typically gendered. In many Mediterranean and Middle Eastern societies, male honor has been tied to courage, hospitality, and the defence of the family, while female honor has been tied to modesty, chastity, and obedience — so that a woman's conduct could determine her family's honor. The concept of shame is honor's counterpart: to lose honor is to be shamed, and shame is experienced not only by the individual but by the whole family. This is why honor has been associated with practices ranging from duelling to so-called honor killings, in which a family seeks to restore its standing through violence.

## Origins and Development

A landmark in the anthropology of honor was John Peristiany's edited volume *Honour and Shame: The Values of Mediterranean Society* (1966), which brought together studies of Spain, Greece, Cyprus, Egypt, and Algeria. They found a shared vocabulary of honor and shame across the region, but also striking local differences: what counted as honourable in one village could be shameful in the next. The volume established honor as a comparative concept, not a Mediterranean essence.

Pierre Bourdieu's work in Algeria added a crucial dimension. In his essay on the sentiment of honor in Kabyle society, first published in Peristiany's volume in 1966 and developed in *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1977), Bourdieu argued that honor is not a set of rules but a practical sense — a feel for the game — that lets people know when a challenge must be answered, when an insult can be ignored, and when a gift must be repaid. Honor, for Bourdieu, was a form of symbolic capital: a resource that could be accumulated, spent, and converted into political power.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One major debate concerns whether honor is a Mediterranean phenomenon or a universal one. Some scholars argued that the honor-shame complex was specific to the Mediterranean, rooted in its history of pastoralism, segmentary politics, and the absence of strong states. Others replied that honor operates wherever reputation matters — in Japan, in the American South, in the honour cultures of the street — and that the Mediterranean was simply where anthropologists happened to look first. Frank Henderson Stewart, in *Honor* (1994), offered a comparative theory, drawing on European history and on his research among Bedouin of Sinai, that understands honor as a kind of right to respect, governed by codes whose contents differ widely.

A second debate concerns the relationship between honor and violence. Is violence the core of honor, or is it a pathological extreme? Ethnographers have shown that most honor systems are primarily about restraint: the honourable person avoids insult, settles disputes, and shows generosity. Violence is usually a last resort, when other ways of restoring honor have failed. But where the state is weak or absent, the threat of violence may be the only guarantee of honor, and feuds can last for generations.

A third dimension is change. Urbanisation, education, and the state have transformed honor in many societies: duelling has disappeared, feuding has declined, and women's honor is increasingly asserted by women themselves, as a matter of dignity and rights rather than of family reputation. At the same time, new forms of honor have emerged — professional honor, national honor, the honor of the gang or the regiment — suggesting that the logic of reputation is more durable than any particular code.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Honor remains a live issue in contemporary politics and law. Debates over honor killings, forced marriage, and the treatment of women in diaspora communities have made honor a matter of public policy in Europe and North America, and they have raised difficult questions about cultural relativism: how to respect cultural difference without condoning violence against women. Kwame Anthony Appiah, in *The Honor Code* (2010), argued that moral revolutions — against duelling, foot-binding, and slavery — succeeded not by appealing to abstract principle but by changing what counted as honourable, so that practices once required by honor became shameful.

Feminist anthropology has been the sharpest critic of the classic honor literature, showing that it was written largely from the perspective of men, and that women's experience of honor — as constraint, as burden, and sometimes as a resource of their own — was long ignored. The study of honor today is as much about who gets to define honor as about what honor is.
