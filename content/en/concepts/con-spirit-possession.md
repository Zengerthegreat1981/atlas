# Spirit Possession

Spirit possession is the belief and experience that a spirit, ancestor, deity, or other non-human being enters a person's body and takes over its actions, speech, or consciousness. It is found in societies on every continent, from the zar cults of northeast Africa to the Vodou ceremonies of Haiti, and it takes many forms: a sudden affliction that must be cured, a regular ritual in which a medium speaks with a god's voice, or a communal ceremony of music, dance, and trance. Anthropologists study possession not as a delusion to be explained away but as a social and cultural practice through which people negotiate illness, power, gender, and the unseen world.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish possession from related phenomena such as trance and mediumship, though the three often overlap. Trance is an altered state of consciousness; possession is the interpretation of that state, or of a change in behaviour, as caused by a spirit inhabiting the body. In some traditions the possessing spirit is welcomed and cultivated, as when a priestess is regularly mounted by a deity who gives advice to the community. In others it is an unwelcome intrusion, an illness that a healer must diagnose and expel or appease. The same episode may be read differently by different people: what a psychiatrist calls dissociation, a family may call the work of a spirit, and the possessed person may experience as both suffering and authority.

Possession is also a form of communication. The spirit that speaks through a medium often says things the medium could not say in ordinary life: a wife may rebuke her husband, a poor woman may demand gifts from the rich, a subordinate may criticise a chief.

## Origins and Development

Early anthropology treated possession as a survival of primitive religion or as a symptom of mental illness. Edward Tylor's theory of animism, set out in *Primitive Culture* (1871), framed spirits as early humanity's explanation of dreams and death, and possession as one of its more dramatic expressions. By the mid-twentieth century, ethnographers working in Africa, the Caribbean, and South Asia were documenting possession cults in rich detail, and the emphasis shifted from origins to social function.

I. M. Lewis's *Ecstatic Religion* (1971) was a landmark. Lewis distinguished between central possession cults, in which the possessed are priests and leaders of a dominant religion, and peripheral possession cults, in which the possessed are typically marginal people — often women, the poor, or members of subordinate groups — and the spirits are outsiders to the official religion. In the zar cult of Ethiopia and Sudan, for example, women possessed by zar spirits formed their own ceremonies, demanded goods and attention, and gained a measure of relief and status. Lewis argued that peripheral possession was a form of oblique protest: a way for the powerless to make claims on the powerful without open rebellion.

Later work complicated this picture. Erika Bourguignon's *Possession* (1976) surveyed possession beliefs across hundreds of societies, while Janice Boddy's *Wombs and Alien Spirits* (1989), based on fieldwork in northern Sudan, showed that zar possession was not simply a strategy or a symptom but a deeply embodied experience, tied to fertility, womanhood, and the history of slavery and displacement in the region.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One central debate concerns the relationship between possession and mental health. Early psychiatrists treated possession as hysteria or psychosis. Anthropologists have resisted that reduction, arguing that possession is a culturally patterned experience with its own logic, and that labelling it illness can be a form of colonial or medical power. At the same time, ethnographers recognise that possession can involve real suffering, and that healers and psychiatrists sometimes work with the same afflicted people. The question is not whether possession is "really" illness or "really" religion, but how a given society draws that line.

A second debate concerns gender. In many societies women are far more likely than men to be possessed, and this has been explained in different ways: as a consequence of women's marginalisation, as an expression of women's religious power, or as a product of the particular stresses of marriage, childbearing, and domestic life. Boddy's work in Sudan showed that zar spirits were closely tied to women's reproductive lives and to memories of slavery, so that possession carried the weight of personal and collective history at once.

A third dimension is the politics of possession. Colonial officials and missionaries often tried to suppress possession cults as superstition or disorder, while nationalist and religious reform movements have sometimes done the same. Yet possession cults have also been sites of resistance and of cultural survival: in Haiti, Vodou possession ceremonies preserved African religious traditions under slavery and colonial rule, and in Brazil, Candomble possession rituals sustain Afro-Brazilian identity to this day.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Possession has not faded with modernity. Zar ceremonies continue in Cairo and Khartoum, Vodou remains a living religion in Haiti and its diaspora, and spirit mediums draw large followings in cities from Lagos to Bangkok. In diaspora communities, possession rituals can become powerful markers of identity and belonging.

Recent anthropology has moved away from asking what possession "really is" and towards asking what it does: how it heals, how it organises social life, how it gives form to suffering, and how it changes as people migrate, convert, and encounter biomedicine. The study of possession thus remains a window onto the most enduring questions of the discipline — how bodies, spirits, and societies are made together, and how people live with powers they cannot see.
