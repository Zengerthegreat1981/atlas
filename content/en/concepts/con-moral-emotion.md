# Moral Emotion

Moral emotions are those affective states directly linked to the evaluation of the rightness or wrongness of actions, and to the welfare, suffering, or deservingness of others. Examples include guilt, shame, moral disgust, anger at injustice, gratitude, and moral admiration. These emotions raise a central philosophical question: are they cognitive tools that reveal moral truths to us, or merely non-cognitive psychological responses that reason ought to refine?

## Meaning and Definition

A distinction is usually made between emotions directed toward the self (such as guilt, shame, and remorse), emotions directed toward others (such as moral anger, contempt, and disgust), and emotions of sympathy (such as mercy and compassion) that move one to come to the aid of those who suffer. Most of these emotions presuppose an implicit cognitive judgment. The feeling of guilt, for example, presupposes the agent's belief that he has done wrong, which makes them closer to complex judgments than to raw sensations.

## In the Islamic Tradition

Muslim moral scholars were concerned with regulating psychological emotions. Miskawayh, in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, distinguished between the emotions of the irascible power (*al-quwwa al-ghadabiyya*), which produce courage when moderate, recklessness when excessive, and cowardice when deficient. He held that virtue is a mean between the extremes of excess and deficiency of emotion, not its elimination. Al-Ghazali gave special attention to the emotion of *haya'* (modesty or shame) as a moral emotion that restrains from indecencies, citing the hadith "Modesty is part of faith," and considered its absence a sign of corruption of moral nature. He also spoke of the "heart" and its direct affective knowledge of the ugliness of sins, in a manner resembling the role of an emotional conscience in detecting wrong before careful rational reflection.

## In Western Philosophy

In Greek philosophy, Aristotle regarded the emotions as an integral part of virtue rather than an obstacle to it. The courageous person is one who feels the appropriate fear in the appropriate situation, not one who has no fear at all. In contemporary moral psychology, Paul Rozin developed the concept of "moral disgust," which extends from sensory revulsion to the condemnation of moral violations of purity and dignity. Jonathan Haidt focused on the role of rapid, unconscious emotions in generating moral judgments before rational justification follows (the "social intuitionist" model, 2001). This reverses the traditional order, which presumes that reason precedes emotion. Immanuel Kant, by contrast, warned against relying on emotion as the basis of moral action, since emotion is changeable and cannot serve as a stable universal criterion. He required that moral action proceed from duty, not from emotional inclination alone.

## Objections and an Example

It is objected that total reliance on moral emotions may mislead the person who feels them. Disgust, for example, has historically been used to justify racial discrimination or the rejection of innocent minorities. As an example, a person witnesses an injustice committed against a stranger, and his anger erupts immediately, before any careful reflection. Haidt regards this as evidence for the priority of the swift moral emotion over the rational justification, which often comes later to defend the judgment rather than to produce it.
