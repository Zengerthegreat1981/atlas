# Humanity as End-in-Itself

The principle of "humanity as an end in itself" is a central formulation of the categorical imperative in Immanuel Kant's philosophy. It requires that every rational being, both oneself and others, be treated as an end in itself and not merely as a means to the ends of others. This means that human beings possess a dignity (*Würde*) that cannot be exchanged for any benefit or price, unlike things that have a "price" and can be replaced by their equivalents.

## Meaning and Definition

Kant formulated this principle in *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) as follows: "So act that you use humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means" (4:429, in Mary Gregor's translation). The intent is not to prohibit using others as a means altogether. A person who hires a plumber, for example, uses the plumber as a means, which is permissible so long as the plumber also remains treated as an end. The aim is to prevent reducing others to a mere means, that is, treating them without regard for their rational will, their consent, and their own ends.

## Historical and Philosophical Context

This principle flows from Kant's broader moral system, which rests on the autonomy of the rational will. For Kant, the human being is a law unto himself, as a free rational being, and this legislative rational activity is the source of his dignity, which surpasses every relative or utilitarian value. In this respect Kant's position differs from utilitarianism, which measures the rightness of an action by the aggregate of general benefits, even if an individual is used purely as a means to maximize the happiness of the majority, something Kant rejected categorically. The idea has been influential in modern human-rights thought, and an echo of it can be heard in the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), which affirms the inherent dignity of every human being.

It is also possible to note a partial similarity, though the metaphysical foundations differ, with earlier moral conceptions that give value to the human soul in itself, such as the affirmation by some Muslim theologians and philosophers of the dignity of the human being as honored through reason and moral responsibility. The Holy Qur'an states: "We have certainly honored the children of Adam." This is a Qur'anic basis for the idea of an intrinsic human dignity, although it differs from the purely rational Kantian foundation in that it rests on a divine honoring rather than on the autonomy of reason alone.

## Applications and Uses

This principle is used chiefly as a philosophical basis for rejecting slavery, exploitation, and the forced trade in organs, and for justifying the necessity of obtaining informed consent in medical experiments and scientific research, as in the Nuremberg Code (1947). Subjecting a person to an experiment without his knowledge is a violation of his status as an end in himself. The principle is also invoked in debates on labor ethics, which warn against treating workers as mere instruments of production without regard for their dignity and conditions.

## Relations to Other Concepts

This concept is closely tied to Kantian deontological ethics (Deontological Ethics), to the concepts of autonomy and human dignity, and it stands methodologically opposed to act utilitarianism, which permits sacrificing an individual to maximize the good of the community.

## The Metaphysics of Morals and the Question of Scope

In *The Metaphysics of Morals* (1797) the idea does practical work, especially in the Doctrine of Virtue. Kant holds there that some ends, one's own perfection and the happiness of others, are also duties. He condemns suicide, lying, and servility as failures to respect humanity in one's own person, and grounds duties of respect toward others, which forbid contempt, arrogance, defamation, and ridicule, in the dignity that every human being may claim and owes in return.

The scope of that claim is disputed. Kant locates dignity in rational nature capable of moral self-legislation, and in the same work argues that duties concerning animals and natural objects are really duties to oneself: cruelty to animals, for instance, dulls compassion that serves morality toward people. Critics object that this leaves animals, and apparently some human beings who lack developed rational capacities, without direct standing. Allen Wood (1998) proposes revising Kant so that respect for rational nature also covers beings that have it partially or potentially. Christine Korsgaard (2018) argues that Kantian reasoning commits us to treating all creatures with a good of their own as ends in themselves.
