# The Insanity Defense and the M'Naghten Rule

The **insanity defense** is an exception to criminal liability that acknowledges that the accused, **at the time the act was committed**, suffered from a mental disorder that deprived them of the mental element required for liability. It is closely tied to the principle of **mens rea** and to the idea that punishment presupposes responsibility: if mental illness deprives a person of the capacity for rational responsibility, the legitimacy of punishment falls away.

## The Foundational Standard: The M'Naghten Rule (1843)

The standard that has long prevailed across common law traditions arose from the **M'Naghten case** of 1843. Daniel M'Naghten, who killed Edward Drummond (secretary to the prime minister, Robert Peel, whom he apparently mistook for Peel), was acquitted on the ground of insanity. After public controversy, the House of Lords put questions to the judges of England, and Chief Justice Tindal of the Court of Common Pleas delivered their answers, known as the **M'Naghten Rules** (1843): every accused is presumed sane unless it is clearly proved that, at the time of the act, they were labouring under such a "defect of reason, from disease of the mind" as not to know **the nature and quality of the act**, or, if they did know it, **that what they were doing was wrong**.

This standard, sometimes called the **right-wrong test**, focuses solely on **cognitive capacity** (did the accused know what they were doing, and know that it was wrong?) without directly considering their capacity to **control their conduct voluntarily**.

## Alternatives and Later Developments

- **Irresistible impulse test**: adopted in addition to M'Naghten by some American states to cover cases in which the accused knows the act is wrong but lacks the capacity to control their behavior because of mental illness.
- **Durham Rule** (from *Durham v. United States*, 1954): a broader standard adopted by the appeals court for the District of Columbia, requiring only that the act be the "product" of a mental disease. It was later abandoned: the D.C. Circuit replaced it in *United States v. Brawner* (1972) with the Model Penal Code test, partly because of the difficulty of applying the "product" standard in practice.
- **Model Penal Code standard** (§ 4.01, 1962): combines the cognitive and volitional dimensions, exempting the accused if, owing to mental disease or defect, they lacked **"substantial capacity"** either to appreciate the wrongfulness of the act or to conform their conduct to the requirements of law.

## Philosophical Critique

The M'Naghten standard has been criticized for its exclusive focus on the cognitive dimension and its neglect of the volitional one: a psychiatric patient may know perfectly well that their act is legally wrong and yet be entirely unable to control their behavior. This led some states to adopt broader standards covering volitional incapacity, not only cognitive incapacity. In contrast, a few American states (including Montana, Idaho, Kansas and Utah) have abolished the insanity defense as a separate defense, allowing evidence of mental illness only on whether the accused had the required mens rea, or at sentencing. This position is controversial. After the acquittal of John Hinckley in 1982, the federal Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984 narrowed the federal defense to a cognitive test requiring a severe mental disease or defect and placing the burden on the defendant. In England and Wales the verdict is now governed by statute (the Criminal Procedure (Insanity) Act 1964, as amended), which keeps the M'Naghten formulation.
