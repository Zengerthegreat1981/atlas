# The Insanity Defense and the M'Naghten Rule

The **insanity defense** is an exception to criminal liability that acknowledges that the accused, **at the time the act was committed**, suffered from a mental disorder that deprived them of the mental element required for liability. It is closely tied to the principle of **mens rea** and to the idea that punishment presupposes responsibility: if mental illness deprives a person of the capacity for rational responsibility, the legitimacy of punishment falls away.

## The Foundational Standard: The M'Naghten Rule (1843)

The standard that has long prevailed across common law traditions arose from the **M'Naghten case** of 1843. Daniel M'Naghten, who killed Edward Drummond (secretary to the prime minister, Robert Peel, whom he apparently mistook for Peel), was acquitted on the ground of insanity. After public controversy, the House of Lords put questions to the judges of England, and Chief Justice Tindal of the Court of Common Pleas delivered their answers, known as the **M'Naghten Rules** (1843): every accused is presumed sane unless it is clearly proved that, at the time of the act, they were labouring under such a "defect of reason, from disease of the mind" as not to know **the nature and quality of the act**, or, if they did know it, **that what they were doing was wrong**.

This standard, sometimes called the **right-wrong test**, focuses solely on **cognitive capacity** (did the accused know what they were doing, and know that it was wrong?) without directly considering their capacity to **control their conduct voluntarily**.

## Alternatives and Later Developments

- **Irresistible impulse test**: adopted in addition to M'Naghten by some American states to cover cases in which the accused knows the act is wrong but lacks the capacity to control their behavior because of mental illness.
- **Durham Rule** (from *Durham v. United States*, 1954): a broader standard adopted by the appeals court for the District of Columbia, requiring only that the act be the "product" of a mental disease. It was later abandoned: the D.C. Circuit replaced it in *United States v. Brawner* (1972) with the Model Penal Code test, partly because of the difficulty of applying the "product" standard in practice.
- **Model Penal Code standard** (§ 4.01, 1962): combines the cognitive and volitional dimensions, exempting the accused if, owing to mental disease or defect, they lacked **"substantial capacity"** either to appreciate the wrongfulness of the act or to conform their conduct to the requirements of law.

## Philosophical Critique

The M'Naghten standard has been criticized for its exclusive focus on the cognitive dimension and its neglect of the volitional one: a psychiatric patient may know perfectly well that their act is legally wrong and yet be entirely unable to control their behavior. This led some states to adopt broader standards covering volitional incapacity, not only cognitive incapacity. In contrast, a few American states (including Montana, Idaho, Kansas and Utah) have abolished the insanity defense as a separate defense, allowing evidence of mental illness only on whether the accused had the required mens rea, or at sentencing. This position is controversial. After the acquittal of John Hinckley in 1982, the federal Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984 narrowed the federal defense to a cognitive test requiring a severe mental disease or defect and placing the burden on the defendant. In England and Wales the test of insanity remains the common law M'Naghten formulation, while the special verdict of not guilty by reason of insanity and its consequences are dealt with by statute (including the Trial of Lunatics Act 1883 and the Criminal Procedure (Insanity) Act 1964, as amended).

## Variation among United States jurisdictions

There is no single American insanity rule. A FindLaw survey of state law, last reviewed in 2023, groups jurisdictions into cognitive tests in the M'Naghten family, Model Penal Code or modified "substantial capacity" tests, and a smaller set that adds an irresistible-impulse or control prong to a cognitive test. Who bears the burden of proof also differs, and some states add a "guilty but mentally ill" or similar verdict. The same survey records that Kansas, Montana, Idaho, and Utah do not recognize a freestanding insanity defense, though several of those states still allow a special verdict or sentencing path tied to mental illness. In *Kahler v. Kansas* (2020), the United States Supreme Court held that due process does not require a state to acquit a defendant who, because of mental illness, could not tell that the act was morally wrong. The majority treated the precise link between mental illness and criminal culpability as a choice left to the states, so long as mental illness can still bear on the mental state required for the offense and on sentence.

## Leading cases on irresistible impulse and the Durham rule

The volitional supplement has a nineteenth-century pedigree. In *Parsons v. State* (1887), the Alabama Supreme Court instructed that disease of the mind may excuse if the accused did not know the nature and quality of the act or that it was wrong, or could not prevent the act. Later writers treat that formulation as a leading statement of the so-called irresistible-impulse test, a label that covers several control tests rather than one formula. In the District of Columbia, *Smith v. United States* (1929) added a control inquiry to the right-wrong test: acquittal could follow if diseased mental condition destroyed the power to resist the impulse even though the accused knew the deed was wrong. *Durham v. United States* (1954) then rejected both tests as exclusive criteria. Judge Bazelon wrote that an accused is not criminally responsible if the unlawful act was the product of mental disease or mental defect, using "disease" for a condition that can improve or deteriorate and "defect" for a more static condition. As the page already notes, the D.C. Circuit abandoned it in *United States v. Brawner* (1972) in favor of the Model Penal Code standard. A few states, notably New Hampshire in the FindLaw survey, have kept a Durham-style product test.
