# Major Depressive Disorder

## Definition

Major depressive disorder is a psychiatric condition. In DSM-5-TR a major depressive episode requires five or more of nine symptoms during the same two-week period, nearly every day, including at least one of depressed mood or markedly diminished interest or pleasure. The other symptoms are significant weight or appetite change, sleep disturbance, psychomotor agitation or retardation, fatigue or loss of energy, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, impaired concentration or indecision, and recurrent thoughts of death or suicide. The symptoms must cause clinically significant distress or impairment and not be attributable to a substance or medical condition. The Global Burden of Disease analysis published by Whiteford and colleagues in *The Lancet* in 2013 ranked depressive disorders as the largest contributor to disability among mental and substance use disorders.

## Arab Roots: Melancholia

Arab-Islamic civilization knew the disease as "melancholia" (*al-malikhuliya*), a term transmitted by Hunayn ibn Ishaq and his students from the Greeks. The physician Ishaq ibn Imran (died around 907 CE) wrote a "Treatise on Melancholia" in Qayrawan, one of the earliest standalone Arabic works on the subject. Abu Bakr al-Razi described individual cases in *al-Hawi*. Ibn Sina devoted a description of melancholia in *al-Qanun* to black-bile humors and treatment through diet, music and conversation. Ahmad ibn Sahl al-Balkhi (850–934 CE), in *Masalih al-Abdan wa-al-Anfus*, produced a classification of emotional disorders in which he distinguished between anger, fear, sorrow and obsession. Malik Badri, in his English translation *Abu Zayd al-Balkhi's Sustenance of the Soul* (International Institute of Islamic Thought, 2013), argues that al-Balkhi anticipated cognitive therapy. That is a modern reading and should not be given more weight than it can bear (see the entries on Ibn Sina, al-Razi and Islamic Psychology).

## The Modern Concept Takes Shape

Kraepelin described depression as part of "manic-depressive insanity" (sixth edition of his textbook, 1899), distinguishing it from dementia praecox. Feighner and colleagues in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 1972 proposed explicit research diagnostic criteria (the "Feighner criteria"), and the third edition of the diagnostic manual (DSM-III, 1980), developed under Robert Spitzer, introduced the category "major depressive disorder." Aaron Beck presented the cognitive framework in *Depression* (Harper & Row, 1967). Brown and Harris, in *Social Origins of Depression* (Tavistock, 1978), reported that severe life events and lack of a confiding relationship were associated with depression among women in Camberwell, London. A meta-analysis of twin studies by Sullivan, Neale and Kendler in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 2000 estimated the heritability of the disorder at around 37%. The National Comorbidity Survey Replication (Kessler and colleagues, *JAMA*, 2003) estimated a lifetime prevalence of major depressive disorder in the United States of about 16%.

## Treatment

Clinical guidelines describe both psychological treatments (such as cognitive therapy and behavioral activation) and antidepressant medication as effective options (see the entry on serotonin reuptake inhibitors). A patient-level meta-analysis (Fournier et al., 2010) found that the advantage of antidepressants over placebo increases with severity and is small in mild depression. A considerable proportion of patients do not respond to the first treatment (see the entry on treatment-resistant disorders). Assessment of suicide risk is part of the evaluation of every patient.

## Cultural Dimensions and Criticism

Depression is expressed in varied forms. In many settings physical complaints (fatigue, headaches and chest pain) predominate over named sadness. Horwitz and Wakefield, in *The Loss of Sadness* (Oxford University Press, 2007), criticized the manual's criteria for failing to separate normal sadness after loss from illness, which broadens the diagnosis. The concept remains multiform, as patients differ in their causes and in their response (see the entry on depression subtypes).
