# Deafferentation Hypothesis in Neurotheology

The specific neuro-physiological mechanism proposed by Eugene d'Aquili and Andrew Newberg (1999) to explain the sense of "loss of self-boundaries" and "union with something greater" reported by practitioners in states of intense deep meditation and prayer, understood as the product of a specific, observable neural mechanism rather than a vague subjective description.

## The Proposed Mechanism

The hypothesis holds that intense and sustained mental concentration (as in deep meditation or contemplative prayer) gradually reduces the afferent input received by a specific area of the brain, which d'Aquili and Newberg call the "Orientation Association Area" (in the superior posterior parietal lobe). This area is normally responsible for drawing the perceptual boundary between the "self" and the "external world" in space. When this part of the brain is deprived of its usual input through intense concentration, its ability to draw this boundary is disrupted, and the brain itself produces a conscious subjective experience of losing bodily boundaries. According to the hypothesis, this explains the feeling of "mystical unity" or "cosmic union" described across very different religious traditions.

## Methodological Significance: From Description to Mechanism

Before this hypothesis, scholars such as William James had contented themselves with a phenomenological description of mystical experience (loss of boundaries, unity, ineffability). What the deafferentation hypothesis added is the proposal of a **specific causal neural mechanism** that can be tested experimentally through neuroimaging, not merely a phenomenological description. This makes it a model for moving from studying religion phenomenologically to studying it neurologically and explanatorily.

## The Constraint

The hypothesis describes a neural mechanism temporally associated with the subjective experience, but it does not settle, and does not claim to settle, the metaphysical question of whether this neural mechanism produces the experience from nothing or is merely a neural "channel" for access to an actual transcendent reality that lies outside the scope of neuroimaging altogether.

## Experimental Evidence

Newberg and colleagues reported SPECT neuroimaging studies of Tibetan Buddhist meditators (2001) and Franciscan nuns during meditative prayer (2003). Both reported reduced blood flow in the posterior superior parietal region at the peak of the practice, which they related to participants' reports of a loss of the sense of self-boundaries. The finding in two different traditions supported their proposal of a general, cross-traditional mechanism. The evidence is limited, however: the samples were very small, the designs lacked controls and the imaging had low resolution, so critics regard the hypothesis as suggestive but unestablished.
