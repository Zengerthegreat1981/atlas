# Lineage

A lineage is a group of people who trace their descent from a common ancestor through a single line, whether patrilineal, through men, or matrilineal, through women. Unlike a clan, whose members claim a shared ancestor they often cannot name, a lineage reckons descent through a chain of known, named forebears, usually reaching back three to ten generations. Lineages are corporate groups: they hold land, livestock, or ritual rights in common; they act together in feuds, marriages, and ceremonies; and they provide their members with identity, protection, and a place in the social order. In many societies without centralised states, the lineage is the principal unit of political and economic life.

## Origins and Development

The concept of lineage took shape in the classic era of British social anthropology. Lewis Henry Morgan's *Ancient Society* (1877) had already treated descent groups as stages in social evolution, but it was A. R. Radcliffe-Brown who gave lineage its analytical precision. In *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952), he described the lineage as a corporate group whose continuity outlives its individual members. Edward Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) provided the most famous ethnography of lineage organisation: among the Nuer of the southern Sudan, segmentary lineages nested inside one another like branches of a tree, uniting against outside threats and splitting again once the threat passed, so that political order emerged without chiefs or a state.

Meyer Fortes developed the theory further in *The Dynamics of Clanship among the Tallensi* (1945), showing how Tallensi lineages combined descent with ancestor worship, so that the dead remained active members of the group, receiving offerings and enforcing moral rules. Fortes distinguished between the lineage as a legal and ritual corporation and the domestic family as a unit of everyday life, a distinction that became standard in kinship studies. Together, Radcliffe-Brown, Evans-Pritchard, and Fortes made lineage the centrepiece of the descent theory that dominated anthropology through the 1950s and 1960s, and that set it against alliance theory, which stressed marriage rather than descent.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The sharpest debate concerned whether lineages were real groups or analytical fictions. Adam Kuper argued in *The Invention of Primitive Society* (1988) that the elegant lineage models of the classic ethnographies owed as much to the anthropologists' own assumptions as to the societies they described, and that "segmentary lineage systems" were partly an invention of the literature. Critics of descent theory pointed out that people often manipulate genealogies, telescoping generations, inventing ancestors, or shifting affiliation to suit present interests. Lineage, in this view, is less a rigid charter than a language people use to argue about rights and belonging.

A second debate concerns the relationship between descent and other principles of organisation. Where descent is unilineal, it creates clear groups; where it is cognatic, traced through both parents, or ambilineal, allowing choice, group boundaries blur. Many societies combine principles: the Tallensi are patrilineal, the Ashanti matrilineal, and the Maori organise through descent groups that can be entered through either parent. Ethnographers also found that lineages vary in depth and function. Some are shallow, lasting only a few generations before splitting; others, like Chinese patrilineages or Arab tribal lineages, claim depths of centuries and control substantial corporate property, temples, and genealogical records.

A third dimension is gender. Patrilineal systems typically pass membership and property through men, while women marry out and their children belong to their husband's lineage. Matrilineal systems pass membership through women, but authority often still rests with men, usually the mother's brother, a pattern that puzzled early theorists and led to the classic problem of "matrilineal puzzle" discussed by Audrey Richards and others. Lineage therefore raises questions about how descent, authority, and gender intersect rather than simply coincide.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Lineage has not disappeared from modern life. In China, lineage halls and ancestral temples have been rebuilt since the 1980s, and lineage networks channel investment, charity, and political influence. In parts of Africa and the Middle East, lineage and clan identities shape elections, land disputes, and access to state resources. In diaspora communities, genealogical research and DNA testing have revived interest in tracing descent, though now often through commercial databases rather than oral tradition. Anthropologists also study how states interact with lineage organisation: colonial administrators once codified "tribal" lineages into law, freezing fluid relationships into fixed categories, and postcolonial governments have sometimes tried to bypass lineage authorities and sometimes to co-opt them.

The concept's enduring value is analytical. Lineage forces attention to how groups persist over time, how membership is defined, and how the dead continue to govern the living through ancestors, property, and memory. At the same time, contemporary anthropology treats lineages as made and remade rather than given: genealogies are negotiated, membership is contested, and the language of descent is deployed strategically. The lineage remains a powerful lens on social continuity, precisely because it shows continuity itself to be a social achievement.
