# Reference Group

A reference group is a group that an individual uses as a standard for evaluating herself, her attitudes, and her behaviour. It is the group against which a person measures what she has, what she wants, and what she ought to do. Crucially, a reference group need not be a group to which the person belongs. A factory worker may judge her wages by comparing herself with office workers she has never met; a student may model her speech, dress, and ambitions on a professional class she hopes to join; an immigrant may hold on to the norms of the community she left behind. The concept captures a fundamental fact: people do not evaluate themselves in isolation, but by looking sideways at others, and the group they choose shapes who they think they are.

## Definition

The concept was coined by Herbert Hyman in *The Psychology of Status* (1942), which showed that people's judgments of their own social standing depended on the group they used as a frame of reference. Harold Kelley, in 1952, distinguished two functions of reference groups. The normative function is the provision of standards and values: the reference group tells the individual what is expected, what is admirable, and what is shameful. The comparative function is the provision of a benchmark: the reference group supplies the point of comparison against which the individual assesses her own position. Robert K. Merton, who gave the concept its sociological form (with Alice Kitt, 1950), distinguished between membership groups, to which the individual belongs, and non-membership groups, which may serve as reference points whether the individual aspires to join them or defines herself against them. Kelley showed that different groups often perform these two functions: the group whose values a person adopts may not be the group against which she measures her success. Tamotsu Shibutani added in 1955 that reference groups are best understood as perspectives — organised outlooks through which people interpret the world — rather than concrete collections of people.

## Origins and Development

The concept grew out of the social psychology of the 1940s. Hyman's studies demonstrated that the same objective status — the same income, the same occupation — was rated very differently depending on the comparison group the respondent had in mind. During the Second World War, Samuel Stouffer and his colleagues, in *The American Soldier* (1949), found that soldiers' satisfaction depended less on absolute conditions than on comparison with other units: rapidly promoted airmen were more dissatisfied with promotion than slowly promoted military policemen, because the airmen compared themselves with those promoted ahead of them. This finding, which Merton analysed as a case of relative deprivation, brought the reference group into the centre of sociology. Merton's essays on reference group behaviour (with Alice Kitt, 1950, and later with Alice Rossi), collected in the revised edition of *Social Theory and Social Structure* (1957), systematised the concept as a classic of middle-range theory: modest enough to test, general enough to illuminate aspirations, mobility, deviance, and conformity.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One important distinction is between positive and negative reference groups. A positive reference group is one whose norms the individual accepts and whose membership she seeks or values; a negative reference group is one whose norms she rejects and against which she defines herself. Both shape behaviour, since defining oneself against a group is as powerful as aspiring to it. A second dimension is anticipatory socialisation: adopting the values and behaviour of a group one hopes to join — a student behaving like a doctor before she qualifies — smooths entry but risks alienating one's current group. Merton identified this as a source of tension, since the norms of the aspirational group may conflict with those of the membership group. A third issue is multiplicity: people orient themselves towards many groups at once — family, friends, colleagues, ethnic community, profession — which may pull in different directions, and which prevails in a given situation remains a central question. Finally, critics have asked whether the concept is too individualistic, since it treats the group as a frame in the individual's mind rather than as a structure of power and constraint. Later work has linked reference groups to social identity and to the structural positions that make some comparisons available and others unthinkable.

## Contemporary Relevance

The reference group is more relevant than ever in an age of social media, where the range of available comparisons has exploded. People now measure their appearance, income, lifestyle, and happiness against images curated by influencers, celebrities, and peers, often with damaging consequences for self-esteem and mental health. Upward comparison on platforms such as Instagram is linked to anxiety and dissatisfaction, echoing Stouffer's soldiers. In consumer research, the concept explains how advertising supplies reference groups whose consumption defines aspiration. In the study of migration, it explains why migrants may feel deprived despite objective improvements in their circumstances, because their frame of reference has shifted to the host society. In education and the workplace, it illuminates how students and employees set their ambitions by looking at those around them, and why the same achievement can feel like success in one context and failure in another. The concept's enduring lesson is that satisfaction is relative: what people want, and whether they feel they have enough, depends less on what they have than on whom they compare themselves with.
