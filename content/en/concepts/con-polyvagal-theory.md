# Polyvagal Theory

## Definition

**Polyvagal Theory** is a neurobiological and psychological framework developed by **Stephen Porges**, first presented at the Society for Psychophysiological Research in 1994 and published in 1995. It proposes that the autonomic nervous system supports **three phylogenetically ordered response systems** (social engagement, mobilization, and immobilization) that mediate physiological states corresponding to perceived environmental safety, danger, and life threat. The original theory provides a psychophysiological model, while subsequent clinical applications—notably popularized by Deb Dana through concepts like the "Polyvagal Ladder"—adapt its hierarchy to psychotherapy, trauma processing, and the regulation of the **Window of Tolerance**.

## The vagus nerve

The vagus is the tenth cranial nerve. It carries parasympathetic fibers to the heart, lungs and gut, and most of its fibers are sensory, carrying information from the body to the brain. Polyvagal Theory distinguishes two vagal pathways with different evolutionary origins and functions, which is the source of the term "poly-vagal."

## Basic principles

### 1. Evolutionary origins
- Porges proposes that the autonomic nervous system evolved in **three stages**:
  1. **The unmyelinated (dorsal) vagal system**: the oldest, shared with early vertebrates, associated with immobilization.
  2. **The sympathetic system**: associated with mobilization for action.
  3. **The myelinated (ventral) vagal system**: unique to mammals, linked to the muscles of the face and head and to social engagement.

### 2. The three states

#### A. Safety (social engagement)
- The **myelinated vagus** is dominant.
- The **social engagement system** is working: facial expression, vocal prosody, eye contact, listening.
- Calm, connection and curiosity are possible.

#### B. Danger (mobilization)
- The **sympathetic system** is active.
- **Fight or flight**.
- Increased heart rate and release of adrenaline.

#### C. Life threat (immobilization)
- The **unmyelinated vagus** is dominant.
- **Freezing**, collapse, dissociation.
- Apparent surrender and conservation of energy.

## Mechanisms

### 1. Hierarchical ordering
- The systems are ordered phylogenetically.
- The newest system (the myelinated vagus) is proposed to restrain the older ones when conditions are safe; when it fails, the sympathetic system takes over, and if that fails, the dorsal vagal system.

### 2. Social cues
- Facial expressions, tone of voice and body movement are proposed to signal safety or threat and so to engage or withdraw the social system.

### 3. Neuroception
- Porges's term for the unconscious detection of safety or danger by the nervous system, without awareness.
- It is proposed to operate faster than conscious perception and to determine which state the person enters.

## Therapeutic importance

### 1. Explaining trauma
- Within the theory, trauma is understood as leaving the nervous system biased toward defensive states (mobilization or immobilization) and away from social engagement.

### 2. Treatment strategies
Practitioners who use the framework emphasize **cues of safety** in the therapeutic relationship, such as a calm tone of voice, gentle facial expression and a predictable setting, along with body-based regulation practices such as slow breathing and movement. These are applications derived from the theory; their effects specifically through the proposed mechanisms have not been established.

## Applications

The framework has been applied in work with trauma (PTSD, complex trauma), anxiety, attachment difficulties, emotion regulation and family therapy. Applications are generally broader than the direct evidence.

## Empirical status

- Respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), the variation in heart rate with breathing, is the measure most often used as an index of vagal influence on the heart, and Porges has treated it as a marker of social-engagement capacity.
- Research on heart rate variability and psychological functioning is extensive, but whether it indexes the specific myelinated-vagus mechanism proposed by the theory is disputed.

## Criticism and Neuroanatomical Debates

- **Comparative Neuroanatomy**: Paul Grossman and Edwin W. Taylor (2007) published a comprehensive critique in *Biological Psychology*, arguing that the theory's central evolutionary claims contradict established comparative vertebrate anatomy and physiology. They noted that unmyelinated and myelinated vagal fibers, as well as dual vagal motor nuclei (the dorsal motor nucleus and the nucleus ambiguus), exist across non-mammalian vertebrates (including fish, amphibians, and reptiles), challenging the idea of a uniquely mammalian evolutionary innovation.
- **Respiratory Sinus Arrhythmia (RSA)**: Grossman and Taylor also disputed the claim that RSA serves as a direct, unmediated index of mammalian social engagement or ventral vagal tone, demonstrating that RSA is substantially confounded by respiratory rate and tidal volume.
- **Translational Gap**: Many neuroscientists caution that while the theory has gained widespread popularity in clinical trauma therapy, its physiological mechanisms are often accepted metaphorically rather than demonstrated empirically.

## Therapeutic Legacy

- Polyvagal theory remains an exceptionally influential conceptual model in trauma-informed care and somatic therapies.
- It has informed body-oriented modalities such as Peter Levine's Somatic Experiencing and Pat Ogden's Sensorimotor Psychotherapy, offering a physiological language for clients to understand autonomic trauma responses (hyperarousal and hypoarousal).
