# Hedonistic vs Preference Utilitarianism

## Meaning and Definition

This concept sits at the core point of disagreement within utilitarianism about what the "good" to be maximized is. Hedonistic utilitarianism (the hedonistic version) makes pleasure and the absence of pain the sole ultimate good with intrinsic value. Preference utilitarianism holds that the good is the realization of what individuals actually prefer, or what they would prefer if they were rational and fully informed, regardless of whether that realization is accompanied by sensory pleasure.

## Historical Context

This distinction is tied to the school of consequentialism and utilitarianism (sch-consequentialism-utilitarianism). Jeremy Bentham's formulation in *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* is the purest model of hedonistic utilitarianism, in which pleasure and pain are the "sovereign masters" governing every human act. John Stuart Mill then introduced a qualitative distinction between pleasures (higher and lower), which opened the way beyond pure hedonism. Preference utilitarianism is associated chiefly with R. M. Hare (*Moral Thinking*, 1981) and, in his earlier work, Peter Singer (*Practical Ethics*, 1979), and with the economist John Harsanyi. Singer held in that work that the criterion of moral rightness is the maximal satisfaction of the actual preferences of beings with interests, rather than the production of a particular state of consciousness. There is no ethical tradition in Islamic thought that matches this modern distinction exactly. However, the criticism by Ibn Miskawayh and al-Ghazali of sensual pleasure as a lowly, bestial end, and their preference for intellectual and spiritual pleasure connected with knowledge of God, partly parallels Mill's critical stance toward pure hedonism.

## Positions and Debates

Hedonistic utilitarianism faces the "experience machine" objection formulated by Robert Nozick in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974). If we were offered a choice between connecting to a machine that produced perpetual illusory sensory pleasure and a real life that was less pleasurable, most people would reject the machine. This suggests that there are values other than sensory pleasure, such as truth and actual achievement, that we value for their own sake. Preference utilitarianism faces the objection of "distorted or evil preferences": a person might prefer to harm others, so must that preference be satisfied simply because it is a preference? Preference utilitarians such as Hare and Harsanyi typically respond by counting only informed, rational (idealized) preferences or by excluding malicious ones. Singer himself later moved toward a hedonistic position, together with Katarzyna de Lazari-Radek.

## Applications

The importance of this distinction appears in health policy. The hedonistic approach evaluates the success of a medical treatment by how much pain it relieves, whereas the preference approach evaluates it by how far it accords with the patient's own wishes. The two may diverge when a patient prefers a treatment that is less comfortable but preserves their autonomy.

## The Question of Non-Human Beings

This disagreement has a practical consequence for the treatment of animals. The hedonistic approach includes animals within the circle of moral consideration simply by virtue of their capacity to feel pleasure and pain, which fits Singer's argument in *Animal Liberation* (1975) that sentience is the relevant criterion for equal consideration of interests. The preference approach, by contrast, faces a problem in determining whether animals have "preferences" in the fully rational sense that the theory requires. This makes the distinction between the two formulations practically consequential beyond the abstract theory.

## Formal Arguments: Harsanyi and Hare

Harsanyi gave two arguments. In the first (1953, 1955), a moral judge imagines having an equal chance of being anyone in society. A rational chooser who maximizes expected utility would then prefer the arrangement with the highest average utility. The second, his aggregation theorem (1955), assumes that individual preferences and social preferences both satisfy the von Neumann-Morgenstern axioms of expected utility, and that society is indifferent between options whenever every individual is. It follows that social utility is a weighted sum of individual utilities; equal weights require a further assumption of impartiality. Critics, including Amartya Sen, question whether such utility functions measure well-being, and others argue that the axioms neglect fairness.

Hare argued from the logic of moral words. Moral judgments are prescriptive and universalizable, so someone who prescribes an act must prescribe it for all relevantly similar cases, including those in which that person occupies the position of those affected. Fully knowing what it is like to be in their position involves acquiring corresponding preferences about what should happen to oneself there. The judge must then weigh all these preferences by strength, as if they were one's own, which yields maximizing their satisfaction.
