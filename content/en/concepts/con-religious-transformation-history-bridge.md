# Religious Transformation Through History

## Ibn Khaldun and the Cycle of Civilisation and Mission

In the Arab heritage, Ibn Khaldun was an influential reader of religious transformation in the light of historical movement. In the *Muqaddimah* (1377) he holds that religious mission is renewed at the margins of nomadic civilisation, where group solidarity (*'asabiyyah*) is strong; it founds a state, then its zeal wanes through luxury within roughly three generations, and the turn of another mission comes. Thus the historical presence of religion in his account takes the form of cycles, and adapts to the level of civilisation. Later historians of the spread of Islam have approached the question quantitatively: Richard Bulliet, in *Conversion to Islam in the Medieval Period* (1979), showed that conversion to Islam in Iran, Egypt, Iraq and Spain followed a bell-shaped curve extending over centuries, in that about half the population of Iran had converted by around the third century AH, and he relied on an analysis of the names in biographical dictionaries. His findings tell against the image of a sudden conversion by the sword.

## The Axial Age and Structural Transformation

On a wider scale, Karl Jaspers, in *The Origin and Goal of History* (1949), proposed the concept of the Axial Age (*Achsenzeit*), roughly 800–200 BCE, when critical thinking arose in China, India, Iran, Palestine and Greece, questioning the whole and going beyond mythology. Shmuel Eisenstadt developed this concept in *The Origins and Diversity of Axial Age Civilizations* (1986) as "transcendence," which produced a tension between the mundane order and the transcendent order. Robert Bellah, in *Religion in Human Evolution* (2011), traced the transition of religion from myth and ritual to theory and contemplation. Marshall Hodgson, in *The Venture of Islam* (1974), adds an important notion: Islam is a civilisational tradition read in dialogue with the Iranian and Byzantine heritage, not reducible to belief, and he proposed the term "Islamicate" to describe the culture established in Muslim societies that is not tied to worship.

## Mechanisms of Transformation and Adaptation

Max Weber summarised a basic mechanism, the "routinisation of charisma" (*Veralltäglichung*): when the founder dies, the authority of personal revelation turns into organisation, tradition and institution, and hence the emergence of the legal schools and Sufi orders after the generation of the founders. Other mechanisms include syncretism, such as the blending of Islam with local traditions in Africa and Southeast Asia; reform and renewal (the tradition of a renewer at the head of every hundred years in the well-known hadith); and schism. Nehemia Levtzion, in *Conversion to Islam* (1979), gathered case studies from Africa and Asia showing that the paths of conversion were many: trade, Sufism and political patronage.

## Methodological Reservations

Comparative history of conversion is open to risks. The model of the Axial Age is criticised for selecting civilisations eclectically and for assuming a decisive leap that may be artificial. Weber and Bellah are likewise faulted for arranging religions on an implicit evolutionary scale. As for Bulliet's reading, it rests on samples of names drawn from biographical dictionaries, which do not cover every environment, and it assumes that a change in naming patterns indicates a change of religion. What Ibn Khaldun offered remains a comprehensive reflection on the conditions of the Maghreb and al-Andalus, not a law valid for all societies.
