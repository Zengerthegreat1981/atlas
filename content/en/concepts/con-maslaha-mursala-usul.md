# Maslaha Mursala (Unregulated Public Interest)

An usuli proof that means acting on an interest for which no specific sharia text either validates it or invalidates it, but which **agrees with the general aims of the sharia** (the preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage and property; see **maqasid al-shari'ah**). It is called "unregulated" (*mursala*) because it is not bound to a particular proof that either supports it or cancels it.

## Distinction from Istihsan

This concept overlaps considerably with **istihsan**, but according to many usuliyyun the terminological distinction is that istihsan is a departure from an apparent analogy to a different ruling on the basis of a stronger proof, whereas unregulated public interest is an original foundation of a new ruling for which there is no text and no analogy at all, relying directly on its agreement with the general sharia purpose.

## Al-Ghazali: Strict Conditions for Its Acceptance

Al-Ghazali, in *al-Mustasfa*, stipulates strict conditions for adopting unregulated public interest as a proof: that it be **necessary** (not merely an embellishment or complement), **definitive** (not merely presumed), and **universal** (serving the community in general, not a particular individual). These restrictions aim to prevent arbitrary expansion in using public interest as a pretext for changing rulings without any control. His standard illustration is the case of an enemy using Muslim captives as human shields, where, if the army does not attack, the enemy will overrun the Muslim community; he regarded attacking as justified because the interest at stake is necessary, definitive and universal.

## Importance in Comparative Legal Theory

This concept is sometimes compared with the principle of the **legislative purpose** in modern teleological interpretation of law. Both allow the derivation of a ruling for a new situation that has no explicit text, by referring to the general purpose of the legal system, rather than to a direct literal text. It is a tool for filling legislative gaps in a manner consistent with the spirit of the system rather than its literal wording.

## The Malikis versus the Reservations of the Shafi'is and Hanbalis

The **Malikis** have historically been known for the widest adoption of unregulated public interest as an independent proof in its own right, with Maliki jurists treating it as consistent with the practice of Malik, who in many questions reasoned from considerations of public benefit. Later Maliki authorities such as al-Shatibi treated it within the theory of the objectives of the Sharia. The **Shafi'is** and many **Hanbalis**, by contrast, have historically been reserved about accepting it as an independent proof, out of concern for arbitrary legislation without a textual control. They preferred to refer it back to analogy whenever possible, or to stipulate strict conditions (such as Ghazali's triple condition of necessity, definitiveness, and universality) before recognizing it as a limited exception rather than a general rule. The Hanbali jurist Najm al-Din al-Tufi (d. 716/1316) went further than most, arguing that where a text conflicts with maslaha in the sphere of transactions, maslaha should take precedence, a view that remained controversial.

## School by School

The **Hanafis** did not usually list unregulated interest among their sources. They reached many comparable results through *istihsan*, including departures from analogy justified by necessity, and through custom.

Among the **Malikis**, al-Qarafi argued that all schools in practice rely on such interests when they distinguish or join cases without a specific textual witness, even if they reject the label. Maliki acceptance still presupposed that the interest was consonant with the aims of the law, did not contradict a decisive text, and operated mainly outside acts of worship.

In the **Shafi'i** line, al-Shafi'i's rejection of reasoning not anchored in revealed evidence encouraged caution. Al-Juwayni nonetheless admitted interests resembling those the law had already recognized, and al-Ghazali, as described above, tied acceptance to the objectives of the law.

**Hanbali** positions varied. Ibn Qudama declined to treat it as an independent proof, while Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya gave considerations of interest substantial weight in practice without granting them authority against revelation. The **Zahiris**, who rejected even analogy, rejected it altogether.

## Modern Application: Codifying Sharia and the General Interest

In the modern period the concept has often been invoked, together with the older doctrine of discretionary governance in accordance with the sharia (*siyasa shar'iyya*), in discussions of how modern regulation (for example on traffic or urban planning), for which there is no direct text, can be reconciled with the sharia. It has become one of the prominent usul concepts in contemporary discourse on the renewal of fiqh, since it offers a space for ongoing legal reasoning without claiming that every new issue has a direct text. Critics worry that, without Ghazali's strict conditions, it can be used to justify almost any measure.
