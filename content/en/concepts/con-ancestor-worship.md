# Ancestor Worship

Ancestor worship is the ritual veneration of deceased forebears, who are believed to remain active in the lives of the living. Ancestors are honoured with offerings of food, drink, and incense, consulted through divination and dreams, and feared for the misfortunes they may send if neglected. Far from being a relic of the past, ancestor worship remains a central feature of religious life in China, Japan, Korea, Vietnam, and much of Africa, and its traces appear in the memorial practices of nearly every society. Anthropologists study ancestor worship as a window onto kinship, morality, and the social order: the dead, it turns out, are powerful members of the community.

## Definition

Ancestor worship takes many forms, but its core is the belief that the dead retain agency — they can bless or punish, protect or afflict — and that the living owe them continuing care. In China, ancestors are honoured at family altars and tombs, especially during the Qingming festival, and are understood to depend on offerings from descendants while also watching over them. In West Africa, ancestors are consulted before major decisions and are believed to enforce moral rules, punishing theft, incest, and disrespect. The practice is often described as "worship," but many scholars prefer "ancestor veneration" or "ancestor cult," since ancestors are not gods: they are senior kin, and the rituals addressed to them resemble the respect owed to living elders more than the adoration owed to a deity. Ancestor worship is closely tied to descent: it is typically the ancestors of one's own lineage who matter, and the rituals reinforce who belongs to which group and who may inherit land, titles, and authority.

## Origins and Development

Edward Tylor, writing in 1871, made ancestor worship central to his theory of the origins of religion: he argued that religion began with animism, belief in souls and spirits, of which the veneration of the souls of dead ancestors ("manes-worship") was a major form. Herbert Spencer later claimed that all religion derived from ancestor worship, a view that was influential but is now rejected as too sweeping. In the twentieth century, ethnographic research replaced speculation with detailed studies. Jack Goody's work on the LoDagaa of Ghana, published in 1962, showed that ancestor rituals were closely connected to inheritance: the ancestors were the guardians of property, and disputes over land were also disputes about the dead. Meyer Fortes, in a classic 1965 essay, argued that ancestor worship in Africa was less about the afterlife than about the authority of elders and the moral order of the lineage: the ancestors embodied the values of the group. Maurice Freedman's studies of Chinese lineage organisation, beginning in 1958, showed how ancestor halls and genealogies bound dispersed kin together and legitimised the corporate power of the lineage.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns whether ancestor worship is religion at all. If religion requires gods and a supernatural realm, then the veneration of senior kin may seem like something else — a social institution dressed in ritual form. A second debate concerns its functions: does ancestor worship stabilise society by reinforcing respect for elders and the moral order, or does it serve the interests of particular groups, such as senior men, who claim to speak for the dead? A third debate concerns its relationship to other religions. In China, ancestor worship has coexisted with Buddhism, Daoism, and Confucianism for centuries; in Africa, it has often been combined with Christianity and Islam, sometimes in tension and sometimes in creative synthesis. The Catholic Church, for example, has debated whether Chinese ancestral rites are religious worship or merely civil respect — the so-called Chinese Rites Controversy of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. Finally, scholars debate how ancestor worship changes under modern conditions: urban migration, smaller families, and cremation all challenge practices built around lineage, tombs, and the family home.

## Contemporary Relevance

Ancestor worship remains a living practice for hundreds of millions of people. In East Asia, it shapes family life, festivals, and attitudes toward filial duty, and it has adapted to urban life through online memorials, digital offerings, and simplified rites. In Africa, ancestors remain important in healing, politics, and the resolution of disputes, and diaspora communities maintain ancestral practices far from home. The practice also raises important questions for heritage and law: the repatriation of human remains, the protection of burial grounds, and the rights of Indigenous peoples to care for their dead are all, in part, questions about ancestors. For anthropology, ancestor worship remains a reminder that the social world does not end at death, and that the dead continue to shape the lives of the living.
