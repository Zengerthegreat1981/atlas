# Merge

The only basic derivational operation in the Minimalist Program, formulated by Chomsky (1995) to replace the multiple phrase-structure and transformational rule systems that characterized earlier phases of generative grammar. Merge takes two syntactic elements (two words, a word and a phrase, or two phrases) and combines them into one larger syntactic unit, without either of them losing its internal identity.

## Simplicity and Recursion

Merge can be applied recursively to its own output without any theoretical limit: the product of one merge operation can be merged with a new element, and the new product merged with yet another element, and so on without end. Chomsky argues that this mechanism alone produces the unbounded generative capacity characteristic of human language (the ability to produce an infinite number of sentences from a finite set of elements), instead of requiring a diverse set of phrase-structure rules as in earlier frameworks.

## External and Internal Merge

The framework distinguishes between "External Merge," which combines two elements that were not previously part of the same structure (generating a new structure), and "Internal Merge," which takes an element already present within the derived structure and merges it again at a different, higher position in the structure. This reinterprets the grammatical phenomenon of "movement" (such as the movement of a question phrase to the front of its clause) as a special application of Merge itself, rather than as a separate mechanism as in Government and Binding theory.

## Binary Merge and the Asymmetry Requirement

The framework requires that Merge always be **binary** (combining only two elements in each application, no more), and that it yield a hierarchically asymmetric structure in which one of the two elements (the "head") determines the properties of the whole resulting structure, while the other is treated as a "complement" or "specifier" functionally subordinate to it. This provides a simple structural explanation for the head–dependent duality prevalent across the syntax of all natural languages, without the need to state a separate rule for each grammatical category, as in earlier frameworks.

## Constraints on the Application of Merge

Merge is not applied at random: it is governed by principles of derivational economy that limit its possible outputs. These include "Bare Phrase Structure," which derives the properties of a phrase (whether nominal or verbal) from its directly merged head without assuming intermediate syntactic levels in advance, as in the earlier X-Bar theory; and "Phase" constraints, which limit the ability of later syntactic operations to access structures that have been fully merged and derived in an earlier derivational stage, thereby reducing the computational burden of the derivation.
