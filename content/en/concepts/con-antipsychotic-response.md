# Antipsychotic Response

A clinical and research field that studies the extent, speed, and patterns of response of patients with **psychotic disorders**, first and foremost **schizophrenia**, to treatment with **antipsychotics**. These are medications that work mainly by blocking **dopamine D2 receptors**, in line with what the **dopamine hypothesis of psychosis** proposes.

## Patterns of Response

Patients with schizophrenia do not respond uniformly to drug treatment. The clinical literature shows that most patients achieve a clear improvement in **positive symptoms** (hallucinations and delusions) during the first weeks of treatment at an adequate dose. **Negative symptoms** (blunted affect, social withdrawal, poverty of speech) and **cognitive symptoms** (impaired working memory and executive functions) are generally less responsive to conventional antipsychotics. **Treatment-resistant schizophrenia** is defined clinically as failure to respond adequately to at least two separate trials of different antipsychotics, at adequate doses and for adequate durations. Commonly cited estimates put this at roughly 20-30% of patients, and figures differ between studies; consensus criteria (TRRIP) also require confirmed adherence and a minimum trial duration of about six weeks.

## Clozapine: The Therapeutic Exception

**Clozapine** is the antipsychotic with the strongest and most consistent evidence of effectiveness for treatment-resistant schizophrenia. It is classified as an "atypical" antipsychotic with relatively low affinity for D2 receptors and rapid dissociation from them, compared with other drugs. This suggests that its therapeutic mechanism goes beyond dopamine blockade alone, since it also affects serotonin, norepinephrine, and histamine receptors. Clozapine is used with great caution because of the risk of **agranulocytosis**, a rare but potentially life-threatening suppression of bone marrow. In most jurisdictions this is managed through regular blood monitoring.

## Predictors of Response

Clinical research has identified several factors associated with a better response, though none predicts outcome reliably in an individual patient. These include a shorter interval between the onset of psychosis and the start of treatment (duration of untreated psychosis), less severe negative symptoms at the outset, better premorbid functioning, and early improvement in the first weeks of treatment. Prominent negative and cognitive symptoms, earlier illness onset, and a longer duration of untreated psychosis are associated with a weaker response. Cigarette smoking induces the liver enzyme CYP1A2 and lowers blood levels of some drugs, notably clozapine and olanzapine. Pooled comparisons have also reported more severe positive symptoms in smokers than in non-smokers, although individual studies are mixed and the association does not by itself establish cause.

## Clinical Significance

This field guides key treatment decisions. When is a treatment truly failing and in need of a switch to another drug or to clozapine? How can true drug resistance be distinguished from poor medication adherence, which is a very common cause of apparent "treatment failure"? This distinction supports the use of long-acting injectable medications to ensure continuity of treatment before a final judgment of resistance is made.
