# Wahdat al-Wujud (Unity of Being)

A phrase that abbreviates the doctrine "there is no existence but God" in philosophical Sufism, or "theoretical gnosis", in Ibn Arabi (d. 638 AH / 1240 CE) and his pupils: the true Being is one, and whatever is other than it is manifestations of its names and attributes. The concept has a history independent of the school that carries it, and what follows traces the term itself, the structure of the idea, and the controversy it provoked.

## The term and its limits

Ibn Arabi did not use the phrase "wahdat al-wujud" in his known works, according to a number of scholars, among them William Chittick. The phrase became current in the generations after him, among followers in the circle of Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi and, importantly, among opponents of this tendency such as Ibn Taymiyya, who made it a summary description of the doctrine of the Greatest Shaykh (Ibn Arabi), Ibn Sab'in, al-Tilimsani and Ibn al-Farid. For this reason the doctrine was described in later literature as the "doctrine of the people of unity", a description that may go beyond what its proponents intended.

## The structure

1. **The Divine Essence**: "the One" who has no name, who is neither grasped nor named.
2. **The Divine Names**: the manifestations of the Essence, each name indicating an attribute (the Living, the Knowing, the Powerful).
3. **The fixed entities (al-a'yan al-thabita)**: the forms of creatures in the divine knowledge, which have no existence outside this knowledge.
4. **The sensible world**: the manifestations of the fixed entities in degrees: the world of the intellect, then the world of images (*mithal*), then the world of witnessing.
5. **The Perfect Human**: who gathers all the names in his being, and so is a mirror of the Essence.

Ibn Arabi's expression on this point is that the world is "He and not He": it is the Real in respect of the Real's appearing in it, and it is not the Real in respect of its determination and limitation. With this formula he establishes both transcendence and immanence, neither abolishing the difference between Lord and servant nor making the two independent.

## Backgrounds

- **al-Hallaj** (d. 309 AH): "I am the Real", the earliest ecstatic utterance that later figures read in a unitive way.
- **al-Junayd** (d. 298 AH): the saying attributed to him that "the color of the water is the color of its vessel", on the meaning of annihilation of the self and subsistence in God.
- **Ibn Sina**: the theory of emanation, in which existents proceed from the Necessary Being in order.
- **al-Suhrawardi**: the world of images and light, as a bridge between the Essence and creation.

## From Ibn Arabi to al-Qunawi and Ibn Sab'in

The doctrinal divergence between Ibn Arabi's original discourse and its later systematization is central to understanding the concept:
- **Ibn Arabi's dialectic**: Ibn Arabi himself focused on the paradoxical tension between the Real (*al-Haqq*) and creation (*al-khalq*). Rather than asserting a simplistic monism, he framed creation through the notion of loci of manifestation (*mazahir*), summarized in the formula "He / not He" (*huwa la huwa*). This preserved both divine transcendence (*tanzih*) and immanence (*tashbih*).
- **Al-Qunawi's philosophical systematization**: Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi (d. 673 AH / 1274 CE), Ibn Arabi's stepson and leading disciple, translated his master's visionary and symbolic language into a rigorous metaphysical system. Drawing on Avicennian philosophical ontology and terminology (distinguishing necessary existence, possible existence, and degrees of being), al-Qunawi and commentators in his lineage (such as al-Farghani and al-Qashani) established the standard conceptual architecture of *wahdat al-wujud*.
- **Ibn Sab'in's absolute unity**: In contrast, Abd al-Haqq ibn Sab'in (d. 669 AH / 1270 CE) articulated a more radical, unyielding doctrine of "absolute unity" (*al-wahda al-mutlaqa*). Where al-Qunawi maintained structured degrees of reality and manifestation (*maratib al-wujud*), Ibn Sab'in rejected even the intermediate ontological standing of multiplicity, dismissing relational distinctions as illusory or conceptually redundant.

## Ibn Taymiyya's critique

Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728 AH) counted this doctrine as a union between Creator and creature. His argument is that it abolishes creation as a distinct reality, and corrupts the meaning of worship, which requires a worshipper and a worshipped who are distinct, and for him it is closer to the statements of the philosophers than to the guidance of the Law. The defenders of Ibn Arabi replied on his behalf that his talk concerns degrees of manifestation, not indwelling or union, and that the legal rulings stand in his view in the world of witnessing as the standing of a truth.

## Unity of witnessing in al-Sirhindi

Ahmad al-Sirhindi (d. 1034 AH) held that what is perceived in the station of annihilation is an experience of witnessing, not an existential reality: the wayfarer is absent from all but God and so counts things as one, but not every existent is God in his essence. Hence "wahdat al-shuhud" (unity of witnessing) as against "wahdat al-wujud".

## Influence

- In later philosophical Sufism: Ibn Sab'in, Abd al-Karim al-Jili in *The Perfect Human*, Ibn al-Farid, and Abd al-Ghani al-Nabulusi.
- In Persian poetry: Jalal al-Din Rumi and Fakhr al-Din Iraqi, and in another mode Hafez of Shiraz.
- In later Islamic philosophy: Mulla Sadra, who took the idea of the one gradational (*mushakkik*) being and merged it into the Transcendent Wisdom.
- In modern studies: Henry Corbin, Toshihiko Izutsu, Chittick, and Annemarie Schimmel.
