# Rule of Law

**The rule of law** is a central constitutional principle holding that every authority in the state, including the ruler and the government themselves, is subject to the law and bound by it. No authority stands above the law or escapes its oversight, in contrast to the arbitrary rule of men, which rests on unrestricted personal will. This concept is among the most widely accepted constitutional principles across different legal traditions, although the details of its formulation and content differ from one tradition to another.

## The Arab and Islamic Roots: Equality Before the Sharia

The Islamic and Arab tradition holds deep roots parallel to the idea of the rule of law. The most prominent is the principle of **equality of all before the Sharia**, expressed in early juristic and judicial practice, such as the Rightly Guided Caliphs submitting to the judiciary on the same footing as ordinary people. Another is the principle that **there is no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator**, which constrains the ruler's authority by a higher legal standard than his personal will. The jurisprudence of **siyasa shar'iyya** also developed a framework for restricting the ruler's conduct by both the interest of the community and the rulings of the Sharia. These intellectual foundations meet modern rule-of-law principles in substance, although their source of authority differs.

## The Classical Anglo-Saxon Formulation: Dicey and His Three Pillars

The British constitutional jurist Albert Venn Dicey provided the most influential classical formulation of the concept of the Rule of Law in his *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* (1885). He identified three pillars. First, the supremacy of ordinary law (rather than arbitrary discretionary powers) in regulating state authority. Second, equality before the law, so that every person, including state officials, is subject to the same ordinary courts and the same rules. Third, the basic constitutional rights in the British system are not grants from an abstract constitutional document, but the cumulative product of ordinary court decisions in specific cases.

## The Continental Rechtsstaat: Rechtsstaat and État de droit

In German and French tradition, the concept of the **state of law** (*Rechtsstaat* / *État de droit*) converges with the Anglo-Saxon rule of law, but with greater focus on the formal institutional structure: separation of powers; judicial review of the constitutionality of legislation and the legality of administrative decisions through specialized administrative courts (such as the French Conseil d'État); and the subjection of the administration itself to the principle of legality. Jurists such as Georg Jellinek helped formulate the theoretical framework for this relationship between the state and the law, while maintaining a methodological distinction between the state as a legal person and the rules that govern it.

## The Contemporary Debate: Formal Criterion Versus Substantive Content

A continuing philosophical debate concerns whether the rule of law is limited to purely **formal** conditions (clarity of rules, non-retroactivity, stability of application, judicial independence), regardless of the justice of their substantive content, or whether it necessarily requires a minimum respect for **substantive rights** (such as freedom of expression or equality) in order to deserve the name at all. Jurists such as Joseph Raz (the "thin" or formal conception) hold that formal virtues alone suffice to define the concept; the contrasting "thick" conceptions are associated with writers such as Ronald Dworkin and Tom Bingham. Others link the rule of law to a minimal substantive content of rights, to prevent the description from applying to authoritarian regimes that enforce unjust laws with strict formal discipline.

## International Law and Development

The principle of the rule of law has become a central benchmark in international legal assistance programs and in reports of institutions such as the World Bank and the United Nations, which link it to indicators of good governance and economic development. This promotional use has, however, drawn criticism from jurists who see in it at times the imposition of a particular Western institutional model on different legal and cultural contexts without sufficient regard for local legal traditions.
