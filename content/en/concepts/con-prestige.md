# Prestige

Prestige is the respect, admiration, and deference accorded to a person, role, or group on the basis of achievement, reputation, or association with valued qualities. It is distinct from power, which rests on coercion, and from wealth, which rests on material resources — though the three are closely linked, since wealth and power often bring prestige, and prestige can be converted into wealth and influence. Anthropologists study prestige because it explains why people strive for recognition even at material cost, why some roles are honoured and others despised, and how hierarchy is maintained in societies without formal ranks or states. Prestige is a social currency: invisible, but spent and accumulated every day.

## Definition

Max Weber distinguished three dimensions of social stratification: class, based on economic position; status, based on honour and prestige; and party, based on political power. Prestige belongs to the dimension of status: it is the esteem in which a person or group is held, expressed through deference, titles, seating orders, forms of address, and the right to speak first. Pierre Bourdieu developed this insight into a theory of symbolic capital: prestige is a form of capital that can be accumulated, displayed, and exchanged, and that legitimises other forms of advantage. Prestige differs from honour, a broader moral order tied to the defence of reputation, and from fame, the wide recognition of a name, which does not always bring respect. Prestige is also relational: it exists only in the eyes of others, and what counts as prestigious varies — warriors and hunters in some societies, orators, healers, or generous givers in others.

## Origins and Development

The study of prestige has roots in the sociology of Thorstein Veblen, who argued in *The Theory of the Leisure Class* that the rich display their wealth through conspicuous consumption and conspicuous leisure, and that prestige is won by demonstrating that one need not labour. In anthropology, the classic studies came from Melanesia, where Marshall Sahlins analysed the "big-man" — a leader whose authority rests not on office but on personal achievement, generosity, and the ability to mobilise followers. The big-man's prestige rises with each successful feast and falls when he fails to deliver. In Polynesia, by contrast, prestige attached to chiefs whose rank was inherited, showing that prestige can rest on ascription as well as achievement. Evolutionary anthropologists later asked why humans care so much about prestige at all. Joseph Henrich and Francisco Gil-White proposed that prestige is an evolved adaptation: by deferring to skilled individuals and imitating them, humans gain access to valuable knowledge, and the prestigious benefit from the deference they receive. This "prestige bias" in social learning, they argued, helps explain the rapid spread of skills and innovations in human societies.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the sources of prestige: is it earned by achievement, inherited by birth, or conferred by association? In practice, all three operate, and the balance between them defines a society's character. Where prestige is inherited, hierarchy is stable but rigid; where it is earned, hierarchy is fluid but anxious. A second debate concerns the relationship between prestige and power. Prestige can legitimise power, making authority appear natural and deserved; but it can also challenge power, as when a respected elder or a popular hero speaks against a ruler. A third dimension is the cost of prestige. In many societies, prestige requires generosity: the big-man must give feasts, the chief must redistribute wealth, the patron must support clients. Prestige is therefore expensive, and its pursuit can drive people to ruin — a dynamic visible in the potlatch, in competitive gift-giving, and in the modern pursuit of luxury. A fourth question is gender: women's sources of prestige — in childbearing, ritual knowledge, and household management — have often been undervalued by male ethnographers. Finally, scholars debate whether prestige is universal or culturally specific: while the desire for esteem appears everywhere, the qualities that earn it vary so widely that some argue the concept must be filled in afresh for each society.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Prestige remains a powerful force in contemporary life. In the economy, brands sell prestige as much as products; in education, the prestige of a university shapes life chances; in science, prestige is measured in prizes, publications, and citations, and it determines who receives funding and attention. Anthropologists use the concept to analyse inequality: prestige systems can entrench advantage, as when the children of the prestigious inherit the networks and manners that win esteem, and they can also be challenged, as when social movements assert the dignity of stigmatised groups. Critics warn that the study of prestige can slide into a psychology of individual ambition, neglecting the structural conditions that decide whose achievements are recognised. The enduring insight is that prestige is not a frill on social life but one of its engines: people work, give, compete, and sacrifice for esteem, and the distribution of esteem shapes the distribution of everything else.
