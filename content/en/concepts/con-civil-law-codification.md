# Civil Law Codification

**Codification** is the gathering of legal rules into a single, organized, comprehensive, and coherent text (a code or statute). It replaces the scattered provisions found among judicial precedents and dispersed customs, and aims at self-sufficiency (completeness) and systematic clarity.

## Roots in Islamic Jurisprudence: The Ottoman Codification and the Majalla

The closest methodological Islamic precedent for this concept is the **Majalla al-Ahkam al-Adliyya** (issued in stages, 1869–1876 CE). It was a landmark official attempt to codify Hanafi jurisprudence on civil transactions in the form of numbered articles applicable directly by the courts. The work was prepared by a committee headed by Ahmed Cevdet Pasha. The Majalla adopted a hybrid method: its articles were drawn from authoritative Hanafi legal books and employed general juristic maxims, such as "harm must be removed," but were presented in a modern legislative form (numbered articles and an introductory section on general rules). The Majalla was a decisive precedent that later influenced the drafting of modern Arab civil codes, foremost among them the Egyptian civil code drafted by **Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri** in 1948, which drew on French law, other modern codes, and Islamic jurisprudence (it came into force in 1949).

## The European Context: Napoleon and Modern Codification

The **French Civil Code** (Code civil, 1804, known as the Napoleonic Code) is the founding model of modern codification in the continental European tradition. It sought to replace the accumulated, conflicting feudal customs with a unified rational text derived from the principles of Roman law and Enlightenment philosophy: equality before the law, freedom of contract, and individual property. Other influential codes followed, such as the German Civil Code (BGB, adopted 1896 and in force from 1900), which was distinguished by high technical precision and conceptual abstraction.

## The Core Tension: Certainty Versus Flexibility

Codification remains a subject of enduring theoretical debate. Its advocates, such as Jeremy Bentham (who coined the word "codification" and criticized the disorder of unwritten English common law), see it as a guarantee of legal certainty and ease of access to the rule. Critics, such as Friedrich Carl von Savigny, who opposed A. F. J. Thibaut's call for a German code in his pamphlet *On the Vocation of Our Age for Legislation and Jurisprudence* (1814), warn of the danger of freezing law in a rigid text that cannot keep pace with the organic development of society. Savigny's argument became a founding statement of the German Historical School.

## Practical Differences Between the Two Systems in Judicial Application

The choice of codification has a practical effect on the role of the judge. In codified systems (such as France and Egypt), the judge is expected to find the solution to every dispute within the texts of the code itself, even if by analogy from similar articles. In common law systems, by contrast, the judge has wider power to create a new rule through judicial precedent when no legislative text exists. This difference has historically affected how lawyers are trained: the emphasis in the civil law tradition falls on systematic mastery of the structure of the code, whereas the common law tradition focuses on analyzing prior court decisions.

## What the Primary Texts Say

The Code civil opens with a short preliminary title. Article 4 makes a judge who refuses to decide on the ground that legislation is silent or obscure liable for denial of justice, while Article 5 forbids judges to lay down general regulatory rulings. In the original numbering, Article 544 defined ownership as a very broad right to enjoy and dispose of things within the law, Article 1134 gave lawfully formed contracts the force of law between the parties, and Article 1382 obliged anyone whose fault caused damage to repair it. The 2016 reform renumbered the last two provisions.

The BGB begins with a General Part stating concepts used across later books. Section 138 voids transactions contrary to good morals, section 242 requires performance in good faith, and section 823 sets out liability for injury to specified protected interests.

The Majalla has 1,851 articles in sixteen books, moving from sale to evidence and adjudication. Its introduction defines jurisprudence and then states general maxims: matters are judged by their purposes (Article 2), certainty is not displaced by doubt (Article 4), hardship calls for facilitation (Article 17), harm is to be removed (Article 20) and custom has authority (Article 36).
