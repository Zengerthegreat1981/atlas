# Deontological Constraints

## Meaning and Definition

Deontological constraints are moral principles that forbid the agent from committing certain acts, such as lying, the deliberate killing of an innocent person, or betraying a trust. The prohibition is unconditional and not contingent on calculating outcomes. The act remains forbidden even if committing it would produce an overall better result than not committing it. In this sense deontological constraints form strict boundaries (thresholds) whose value is not weighed against the value of breaking them, however large the sought-after benefit. This contrasts with consequentialist reasoning, which subjects every rule to a continuous calculation of balance.

## Historical Context

The concept is fundamentally linked to the Kantian moral tradition. Immanuel Kant founded the principle of the categorical imperative in *Foundations of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785). One of its formulations is to treat humanity always as an end in itself and never merely as a means. This logically excludes any justification for exploiting an innocent individual as a tool to achieve a collective good, regardless of how large that good is.

Contemporary philosophers have reformulated this idea. Among them is Robert Nozick, in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), who framed it as a theory of rights as "side constraints." On this view, individual rights set limits that may not be crossed in pursuit of any social goal, however beneficial that goal may seem.

## Principal Positions and Distinctions

Deontologists distinguish between "causing" harm and "allowing" it to occur. Deontological constraints usually forbid directly causing harm more strictly than they forbid allowing it. On this view, killing one person to save five is typically held to be impermissible, even though letting five die without intervening may be permitted. These constraints are also distinguished from "agent-centered prerogatives," which permit the agent to give preference to their own interests. Constraints, by contrast, impose an absolute prohibition regardless of the interests of any party.

The doctrine of double effect is also discussed. Traditionally traced to Thomas Aquinas in the Middle Ages (*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 64, a. 7), it distinguishes between intending harm directly and tolerating an unintended side effect in order to achieve a proportionate good.

## Objections and Criticism

Consequentialists argue that absolute adherence to these constraints produces what is called "moral self-indulgence," in which the agent prefers to keep their own hands clean over reducing the overall evil in the world. Samuel Scheffler discussed this as the "paradox of deontology": it seems irrational to forbid an act even when doing so would minimize the total of such acts. It is also sometimes difficult to draw the precise line between "causing" harm directly and "allowing" it indirectly. This makes the application of deontological constraints in practice less clear than their theoretical rigor suggests.

## Illustrative Example

The trolley problem, introduced by Philippa Foot (1967) and developed by Judith Jarvis Thomson, illustrates the constraint. Many judge it permissible to redirect a runaway trolley so that it kills one person rather than five, but not to push a person off a bridge to stop the trolley, because the second case uses the person directly as a means. Whether this intuition reflects a genuine constraint is itself disputed.
