# The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) Axis and Stress

## Definition

The **hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis** (HPA) is the main hormonal network through which the body regulates its physiological response to psychological and physical stress. When the brain perceives a threat or pressure, the **hypothalamus** releases **CRH** (corticotropin-releasing hormone), which stimulates the **anterior pituitary gland** to release **ACTH** (adrenocorticotropic hormone). ACTH in turn stimulates the **adrenal cortex** to release **cortisol** into the blood, the main stress hormone in humans.

## Mechanism and Regulation

The axis usually works through a **negative feedback** mechanism: high cortisol later suppresses the release of CRH and ACTH, returning the system to balance once the pressure has passed. However, **chronic** exposure to stress (such as repeated trauma or ongoing strain) may disrupt this regulatory loop. This can produce either **chronic overactivity** of the axis (persistently high cortisol) or, in other cases, a **regulatory deficit** that appears as a flat or low cortisol response pattern.

## Key Features

- **Depression**: a substantial subgroup of patients with major depression show overactivity of the HPA axis (elevated cortisol, impaired suppression by dexamethasone). A meta-analysis of four decades of research (Stetler and Miller, 2011) found elevated cortisol and impaired dexamethasone suppression on average, with substantial variability. In the 1980s the dexamethasone suppression test was proposed as a diagnostic marker, but its sensitivity and specificity proved too limited for routine clinical use.
- **Effect on the hippocampus**: in animal studies, chronically elevated glucocorticoids are associated with dendritic atrophy and reduced neurogenesis in the **hippocampus**, a brain region important for memory and mood regulation, and smaller hippocampal volume has been reported in recurrent depression and post-traumatic stress disorder. How far these findings explain the cognitive disturbances that accompany chronic depression, and whether they are cause or consequence, remains debated.
- **Early trauma**: research shows that exposure to psychological trauma in childhood (abuse, neglect) is associated with lasting changes in the stress responsiveness of the HPA axis (Heim and Nemeroff, 2001), which may contribute to vulnerability to depression and post-traumatic stress disorder in adulthood.
- **Schizophrenia**: disturbances in the activity of the axis are also observed in schizophrenia, although they are less consistent and less central to its disease model than in depression.

## Significance

The concept of stress as a physiological response was developed by Hans Selye from 1936, and the discovery of HPA axis disturbances became one of the early pillars of modern **biological psychiatry**. It later led to experimental therapeutic strategies that target the axis, such as glucocorticoid receptor, CRH-1 receptor, and vasopressin V1B receptor antagonists, which have so far shown limited or inconsistent clinical benefit. It also established the biological basis for understanding the **relationship between early trauma and later mental illness**.

## Limitations

HPA axis disturbances are not specific to depression or to any single mental disorder. They appear in several physiological and psychological conditions (ordinary chronic stress, chronic physical illnesses), which makes them a non-specific biomarker that cannot be relied upon alone for differential diagnosis.
