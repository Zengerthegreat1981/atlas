# Liminality & Communitas

Two central concepts developed by Victor Turner, drawing on the early work of Arnold van Gennep on rites of passage. They describe the intermediate phase of a ritual and the particular social experience that accompanies it.

## Liminality

"Liminality" (from the Latin *limen*, meaning threshold or boundary) is the intermediate phase of a rite of passage, in which the individual is "between" two social states: no longer a child but not yet an adult, not single but not yet married. The individual is temporarily freed from the classifications and expectations of the usual social structure. This state is often accompanied by symbols that express ambiguity and contradiction (nakedness, masks, the reversal of ordinary social roles).

## Communitas

"Communitas" is the profound sense of solidarity and human equality that arises among participants in the liminal state, independent of the usual social hierarchy (status, wealth, authority) that governs their daily lives outside the ritual. Turner distinguishes between "spontaneous communitas" (arising spontaneously in exceptional moments) and "normative communitas" (organized and institutionalized, as in some monastic religious communities).

## Impact

These two concepts had a wide influence beyond the study of tribal rituals, extending to the analysis of modern phenomena (the religious pilgrimage, festivals, and even some protest gatherings) as moments of social liminality in which the ordinary structure is temporarily suspended in favor of an exceptional solidarity.

## The Origin of the Concept in van Gennep

Arnold van Gennep preceded Turner by six decades in his book *Les Rites de passage* (1909), proposing that every rite of passage (puberty, marriage, death) passes through three successive phases: **separation** from the previous social state, then **liminality** (or transition), the ambiguous middle phase, then **incorporation** into the new social state. Turner focused specifically on the middle phase and developed it theoretically in far greater depth than van Gennep had done.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The Islamic pilgrimage (*ḥajj*) can be read through this framework. The pilgrim enters the state of *iḥrām* wearing a uniform garment that cancels the class and national distinctions among pilgrims, and is temporarily freed from the marks of his ordinary social status. Among the pilgrims there arises a deep sense of equality and solidarity during the circumambulation (*ṭawāf*) and the standing at ʿArafa. This corresponds precisely to Turner's concepts of liminality and communitas, although Turner's own pilgrimage studies (with Edith Turner, *Image and Pilgrimage in Christian Culture*, 1978) concentrated on Christian pilgrimage.

## Later Critique

Later researchers criticized Turner for overgeneralizing "communitas" as always a positive state. They showed that some liminal situations (such as initiations involving coercion or violence, or situations of war) can produce harm or exclusion rather than positive solidarity, and that the nature of the liminal moment (positive or negative) depends on its specific social context rather than on its formal structure alone.
