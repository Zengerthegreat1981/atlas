# Lineage System

A central concept in the anthropology of kinship, describing the organization of a social group around a common ancestor whose name is known or whose descent is documented, such that membership is determined by a single line of descent, either paternal or maternal, rather than by both, as in bilateral kinship.

## Central Idea

A **lineage** is a kinship group whose members can trace an actual chain of descent, generation by generation, to a founding ancestor known by name. This distinguishes it from the **clan**, whose members assume descent from a common ancestor without being able to demonstrate the chain of descent step by step, and where the ancestor may be mythical or symbolic (for example, a totemic animal). Where descent is traced only through the father's line it is called **patrilineal**, and where it is traced only through the mother's line it is called **matrilineal**. Both are forms of "unilineal descent," which by its rule excludes from formal membership of the group half of the direct biological relatives.

## Social Functions

In societies lacking a central state or written bureaucratic institutions, the descent system performs functions that the modern state performs in other societies: regulating property and inheritance, specifying rules of marriage (often by prohibiting marriage within the same lineage, that is, enforcing exogamy), and distributing mutual rights and duties in disputes and in collective defense. George Peter Murdock developed systematic classifications of these systems in his broad comparative project, the *Ethnographic Atlas* (1967), showing that patrilineal descent is statistically more common than matrilineal descent across the world sample of recorded societies.

## From Descent to Segment: The Evolution of the Model

In the nineteenth century, Lewis Henry Morgan developed the first systematic attempts to classify kinship and descent systems across cultures, as part of his broader evolutionary project. The decisive development, however, came in the twentieth century with British structural functionalism. In his study of the Nuer (1940), E. E. Evans-Pritchard showed that patrilineal systems may branch into nested "segments" of varying size (a wider descent group containing smaller ones, which in turn contain smaller sections), such that alliance and hostility between groups depend relatively on the proximity or distance of kinship in each particular dispute. This was later known as the "segmentary lineage system" (see the related concept).

## Limitations

The classical models of the lineage system are criticized for portraying the boundaries of descent groups as clearer and more fixed than they are in practice. Later studies (from the Manchester School and others) show that descent affiliation is often renegotiated and deployed politically with a flexibility that goes beyond the formal tree diagram. Feminist anthropology has also drawn attention to the fact that focusing on a single "official" line of descent often overlooks the actual ties through the other line (the maternal line in a patrilineal system, for example), which remain socially and economically influential even though they are not formally recognized.
