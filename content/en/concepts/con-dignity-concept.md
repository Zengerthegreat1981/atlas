# Concept of Dignity

## Meaning and Definition

Dignity is the unconditional intrinsic value that a human being deserves simply by being human, regardless of their usefulness, social standing, or productive capacity. It is the basis for the sanctity of body and soul and for prohibiting the exploitation of a person as a mere means to another's end.

## Qur'anic and Juristic Roots

In Islamic thought, the concept of dignity rests on an explicit Qur'anic text: God's words, "We have certainly honored the children of Adam" (Qur'an 17:70). This is a general honor that encompasses all of humankind, regardless of religion or lineage, unlike other historical conceptions that tied dignity to descent or social class. On this principle, jurists built a broad range of practical rulings, including the sanctity of the dead body (as in the hadith, "breaking the bone of the dead is like breaking it while alive"), the prohibition of mutilating corpses even in war, and the obligation to treat prisoners with kindness. Jurists distinguished between "inherent dignity," which belongs to the human being by virtue of their created nature, and "acquired dignity," which comes through piety and righteous deeds, referred to in God's words, "Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of God is the most righteous of you" (Qur'an 49:13). The former is the basis of general human rights; the latter is the basis of distinction in the hereafter.

## The Western Philosophical Context

Kant gave the most influential philosophical formulation of dignity in *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785). He held that the rational human being has "dignity" (Dignity) rather than "price" (Price): such a being cannot be replaced by anything else or treated merely as a means, but must always be treated as "an end in itself." Dignity also appears in Article 1 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), which states that all human beings "are born free and equal in dignity and rights." The concept has drawn objections from critics such as Steven Pinker ("The Stupidity of Dignity," 2008), who regard it as obscure and dispensable in favor of more precise notions such as autonomy and well-being. Others, such as Leon Kass, defend it as necessary to explain the prohibition of practices that violate neither autonomy nor cause direct harm, such as human cloning.

## Applied Example

Dignity is invoked in rejecting torture even when it might yield information that could save lives (the "ticking bomb" case), since torture treats the human being as a mere instrument. This is one reason the prohibition of torture is treated as absolute in international human rights law, for example in the UN Convention against Torture (1984), despite opposing utilitarian calculations.

## Dignity and the Rights of Vulnerable Groups

The idea of inherent, stable dignity is a central argument in defending the rights of persons with disabilities, older people, and persons with mental illness, since it prevents linking a person's worth to their productivity or cognitive competence. Islamic ethical discourse can ground similar protection in the distinction that a deficiency of reason removes legal obligation (*taklif*) but not the honor owed to the person. This is a precise distinction between the basis of responsibility (reason) and the basis of respect (the original created nature of the human being), which keeps the dignity of these groups protected even though they are not under obligation.
