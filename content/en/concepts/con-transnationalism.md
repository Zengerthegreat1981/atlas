# Transnationalism

## Definition

Transnationalism refers to the social, economic, political, and cultural processes that connect people, institutions, and places across national borders. It describes a world in which migrants maintain active ties to more than one country, corporations organise production across continents, political movements coordinate internationally, and cultural forms circulate globally. The concept challenges the assumption, long built into the social sciences, that societies coincide with nation-states and that migration is a one-way move from an origin to a destination. Instead, transnationalism draws attention to the social fields that span borders: families whose members live in several countries but share income, care, and decisions; hometowns transformed by remittances; and diasporas that participate in the politics of both their country of residence and their country of origin.

## Origins and Development

The concept emerged in anthropology and sociology in the early 1990s as a response to the limits of assimilation theory, which assumed that migrants would gradually shed their origins and merge into the host society. Nina Glick Schiller, Linda Basch, and Cristina Blanc-Szanton argued in 1992 that many migrants are transmigrants, people who build social fields linking their country of origin and their country of settlement, and that this condition is not a transitional stage but a durable way of life. Alejandro Portes, Luis Guarnizo, and Patricia Landolt refined the concept in 1999, distinguishing transnationalism from above, driven by corporations and states, from transnationalism from below, driven by migrants and grassroots movements, and insisting that the concept should be reserved for regular, sustained cross-border activities rather than any contact between countries. Peggy Levitt's ethnography of Dominican villagers in Boston showed how remittances and return visits transformed both the sending and the receiving community, producing what she called a transnational village. Steven Vertovec systematised the field, identifying transnationalism as a mode of cultural reproduction, a site of political engagement, and a form of capital, and arguing that it required new methods capable of following people, money, and ideas across borders.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns novelty: critics argue that cross-border connections are not new, pointing to the trading diasporas, empires, and labour migrations of earlier centuries, and that the concept risks mistaking an intensification for an invention. Defenders reply that the scale, speed, and institutionalisation of contemporary connections are qualitatively different, sustained by cheap travel, instant communication, and dual citizenship. A second debate concerns the relationship between transnationalism and integration. Early work suggested that transnational ties might delay integration, but later research has shown that the two often reinforce each other: migrants who are securely settled are better able to sustain cross-border ties, and remittances and diaspora investment can support development in countries of origin. A third debate concerns power. Transnational social fields are not flat: gender shapes who migrates and who stays, class shapes who can travel and who cannot, and states shape the field through citizenship law, border controls, and diaspora policies. A fourth dimension concerns the expansion of the concept beyond migration: scholars now write about transnational corporations, transnational advocacy networks, transnational religion, and transnational crime, raising the question of whether the concept has become too broad to remain analytically useful.

## Contemporary Relevance

Transnationalism is now central to understanding a connected world. Remittances from migrants exceed official development aid in many countries, and diaspora communities play decisive roles in elections, conflicts, and reconstruction in their countries of origin. Digital communication has deepened transnational life: families maintain daily contact across continents through messaging and video, and political movements coordinate in real time. The COVID-19 pandemic illustrated both the strength and the fragility of transnational connections, as border closures separated families and disrupted supply chains, while digital ties endured. Climate change is producing new forms of transnationalism, as displaced communities maintain links to disappearing homelands, and as activists organise across borders to demand climate justice. At the same time, the resurgence of nationalism and the tightening of borders in many countries has made transnational life more precarious, with undocumented migrants and asylum seekers facing the sharpest constraints. The concept's enduring contribution is methodological as well as substantive: it taught the social sciences to follow people, money, and meaning across borders rather than assuming that the nation-state contains social life.
