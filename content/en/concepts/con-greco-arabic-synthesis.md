# Greco-Arabic Philosophical Synthesis

The Greco-Arabic synthesis refers to what Muslim philosophers did in merging the Greek heritage, especially Aristotle, Plato, Plotinus and the Alexandrian commentators, into a single conception that meets revelation. This was not a spontaneous merging. It began with al-Kindi (d. after 870 CE), who addressed to the caliph al-Mu'tasim his treatise *On First Philosophy*, in which he argues that one should not be ashamed to welcome truth from whatever source it comes, even from distant peoples and nations foreign to us.

## Neoplatonism in Aristotelian dress

The most consequential texts to enter Arabic in this regard were works wrongly attributed to Aristotle. The *Theology of Aristotle* (*Uthuluijya Aristatalis*, known as *al-Rububiyya*) is in fact an Arabic paraphrase of parts of Plotinus's *Enneads*. It was translated by Abd al-Masih ibn Na'ima al-Himsi and revised by al-Kindi around 840 CE. Likewise, *The Pure Good* (*al-Khayr al-mahd*), derived from Proclus's *Elements of Theology*, was known in Latin as the *Liber de causis*. Because of this confusion, philosophers received Aristotle mixed with the theory of emanation. This helps explain the emanationist cosmology, joined to the doctrine of the Active Intellect, in al-Farabi and Ibn Sina. Peter Adamson studied this circle in *The Arabic Plotinus* (2002), and Abd al-Rahman Badawi gathered the texts in *Plotinus among the Arabs* (1955).

## Al-Farabi: reconciling the two sages

Al-Farabi (d. 339 AH / 950 CE) wrote the treatise *The Harmonization of the Opinions of the Two Sages, the Divine Plato and Aristotle* (*al-Jam' bayna ra'yay al-hakimayn Aflatun al-ilahi wa-Aristutalis*), in which he affirms that the disagreement between them is only apparent. The attribution of this book to him may be questioned in some studies, but his orientation toward reconciliation is established in his other works, such as *The Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City*, in which he combined emanation with prophetic politics.

## Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd

Ibn Sina (d. 428 AH / 1037 CE) built from this inheritance a new philosophy resting on the distinction between the Necessary Existent and the possible existent. Ibn Rushd (d. 595 AH / 1198 CE) sought to return to the original Aristotle, away from Neoplatonism, and criticized Ibn Sina and al-Farabi for this confusion. Nonetheless, he maintained the belief in the possibility of agreement between wisdom and the Sharia, as in *The Decisive Treatise* (*Fasl al-maqal*).

## Limits of the synthesis

The synthesis should not be regarded as submission to the Greek heritage. Each of Ibn Sina, al-Ghazali and al-Suhrawardi changed the original structure. Al-Ghazali objected to twenty issues in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* and declared the philosophers unbelievers on three of them. Moreover, what was called "Greek" was itself a compound of late Hellenistic and Syriac elements, so the Arabs did not deal with the classical Greeks directly.
