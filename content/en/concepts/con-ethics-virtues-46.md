# Humility Ethics

Humility is an ethical virtue that consists in a person's appraisal of themselves, their abilities and their standing that is accurate, neither inflated by arrogance nor diminished by abasement, together with an honest acknowledgment of the limits of their knowledge, their capacity and their personal merit before others and before the truth. Humility ethics stands in opposition to pride and intellectual vanity, and it is a precondition for many other virtues, such as justice and fairness in dialogue.

## In the Islamic Heritage

Humility holds a high place in Islamic ethics. The Quran described the servants of the Most Merciful as "those who walk upon the earth in humility" (*hawnan*), and a saying of the Prophet states: "No one humbles himself before God except that God raises him." Al-Ghazali devoted a lengthy section of *The Revival of the Religious Sciences* to condemning arrogance and praising humility, distinguishing between a praiseworthy humility that springs from the servant's knowledge of his true self before God, and a blameworthy humility aimed at showing off or seeking praise. Within the Aristotelian mean framework adopted by Miskawayh in *The Refinement of Character*, humility is naturally understood as a mean between arrogance (exalting oneself above one's worth) and abasement (diminishing oneself below one's worth). True humility is therefore a realistic acknowledgment of one's standing, not a false belittling of oneself.

## In Western Philosophy

In Western ethics, Aristotle distinguished between "greatness of soul" (Megalopsychia), which he regarded as a virtue requiring that a person know their true worth without pretense, and blameworthy pride. He did not give humility itself a prominent place as later religious thought did. In Christian ethics, Thomas Aquinas regarded humility as a fundamental virtue that subjects the desire for personal distinction to the judgment of right reason. In contemporary philosophy, Julia Driver, in *Uneasy Virtue* (2001), analyzed modesty as a "virtue of ignorance," involving a systematic underestimation of one's own merits. Other approaches, such as Whitcomb, Battaly, Baehr and Howard-Snyder's account of intellectual humility, focus on appropriate attentiveness to and owning of one's intellectual limitations, as a virtue relevant to science and public dialogue.

## Objections and Example

It is sometimes objected that excessive humility may turn into passivity that prevents an individual from defending their rights or presenting their genuine achievements when the situation requires it, as in leadership or when applying for a job. Proponents of this concept reply that true humility does not mean denying facts but honesty of assessment: the humble scientist acknowledges their scientific achievement without claiming to know everything. Example: a distinguished scientist presents their discovery at a scientific conference, acknowledges the contributions of colleagues, and admits the shortcomings in their research. This is the kind of humility that combines honesty with fairness.
