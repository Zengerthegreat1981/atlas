# Oracle

An oracle is a source of authoritative knowledge about hidden or future things, and the institution through which that knowledge is obtained. Oracles take many forms: a priestess speaking at Delphi, a poison oracle consulted by the Azande, a diviner casting lots or reading entrails, a medium possessed by a spirit. In each case, people bring questions they cannot answer by ordinary means, whether about illness, guilt, marriage, or war, and receive an answer treated as coming from beyond the human world. Anthropologists study oracles not to judge whether their answers are true, but to understand what kinds of knowledge they provide, who controls them, and how they shape decisions.

## Origins and Development

The classic anthropological study of oracles is Edward Evans-Pritchard's *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande* (1937), based on fieldwork in what is now South Sudan. Evans-Pritchard showed that the Azande consulted oracles, above all the poison oracle, in which a chicken was fed a poison and its death or survival read as an answer, in almost every important decision. He argued that oracles were not irrational: within Azande beliefs about witchcraft, they were a rational way of testing suspicions, settling disputes, and deciding action. The oracle gave the Azande a procedure for reaching certainty where ordinary evidence was unavailable, and its verdicts were accepted because the procedure itself was trusted, even when individual results seemed contradictory.

The comparative study of divination developed from this foundation. Geoffrey Park's "Divination and Its Social Context" (1963) argued that divination must be understood through its social setting: who consults, who interprets, and what is at stake. Philip Peek's edited volume *African Divination Systems: Ways of Knowing* (1991) gathered case studies showing that divination is a system of knowledge in its own right, with its own logic, training, and standards of evidence. Greek oracles, meanwhile, were studied by historians of religion who showed that Delphi's influence depended on the prestige of Apollo's sanctuary, the ambiguity of its pronouncements, and the political uses to which city-states put them. In ancient Greece as in Africa, the oracle's authority rested on institutions, not only on belief.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns the rationality of oracles. Evans-Pritchard's Azande material launched a long philosophical discussion: if the Azande saw contradictions in oracle results and explained them away, were they irrational, or were they reasoning soundly within a different set of premises? Peter Winch's influential essay on understanding a primitive society argued that rationality itself must be judged within a form of life, not from outside it. Anthropologists have largely followed Evans-Pritchard in treating oracle consultation as reasonable given its premises, while also noting that people are not naive: clients test diviners, shop around for second opinions, and sometimes ignore the oracle's advice.

A second dimension is the social role of the oracle. Oracles resolve conflicts that ordinary courts cannot, because they speak with an authority no party can challenge. They detect witchcraft, name thieves, and assign blame, thereby channelling accusations that might otherwise explode into violence. But they also concentrate power: the diviner, the priest, and the king who controls the oracle all gain influence from its verdicts. The oracle is therefore a political institution as much as a religious one, and its history is bound up with the history of authority.

A third dimension is the nature of oracular knowledge. Oracular answers are typically ambiguous, conditional, or expressed in signs that require interpretation. This ambiguity is not a flaw but a feature: it allows the answer to be fitted to unfolding events, and it protects the oracle's authority when predictions fail. The work of interpretation falls to specialists, and to the clients themselves, who argue over what the signs mean. The oracle thus opens a space of deliberation rather than closing it, giving people a language in which to debate the uncertain.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Oracles have not vanished. Divination is practised widely across Africa, Asia, and Latin America, and in diaspora communities in Europe and the Americas, where it serves migrants facing illness, legal trouble, and family conflict. In China, oracle-bone divination is ancient history, but fortune-telling, geomancy, and the casting of lots remain part of everyday life. In the West, horoscopes, tarot, and psychics occupy a similar niche, offering guidance where expert knowledge is silent or unwelcome. Anthropologists study these practices as forms of decision-making under uncertainty, comparable in some respects to risk assessment, counselling, and even the consultation of algorithms.

The study of oracles has also shaped how anthropologists think about knowledge itself. Divination shows that knowledge is not only a matter of true statements about the world but of procedures, authority, and trust. An oracle works when people believe in the procedure, accept its verdicts, and act on them together. In that sense, the oracle is a reminder that all knowledge, including scientific knowledge, depends on institutions that certify it and communities that receive it. The difference lies in the procedures and the certifiers, not in a simple divide between reason and superstition.
