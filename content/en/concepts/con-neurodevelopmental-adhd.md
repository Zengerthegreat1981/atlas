# Neurodevelopmental Disorders & ADHD

## Definition

DSM-5 (2013) groups neurodevelopmental disorders as a set of conditions that begin during the developmental period and cause impairment in personal, social, or academic functioning. These include intellectual disability, communication disorders, autism spectrum disorder, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), specific learning disorders, and motor and tic disorders. This entry addresses ADHD and its place in the category. Autism has its own separate entry.

## History of Description

In his Goulstonian Lectures at the Royal College of Physicians, published in *The Lancet* in 1902, George Still described children with what he called "defective moral control," marked by overactivity and inattention, some of whom had a history of brain injury or disease. In 1937 Charles Bradley reported in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* that Benzedrine improved the behavior and school performance of children at the Emma Pendleton Bradley Home in East Providence, Rhode Island. That report is widely cited as an early observation of a stimulant effect in children with behavioral problems. Labels later included minimal brain damage or minimal brain dysfunction in the mid-twentieth century, "hyperkinetic reaction of childhood" in DSM-II (1968), "attention deficit disorder" in DSM-III (1980), and ADHD in DSM-III-R (1987).

## Clinical and Neural Model

DSM-5 identifies three presentations: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined. Symptoms must appear before age twelve and in two or more settings. In 1997, Russell Barkley proposed in *Psychological Bulletin* that the core of the disorder is a deficit in "response inhibition" that produces impaired executive functions. Sonuga-Barke (2003) proposed a dual-pathway model: an executive pathway and a pathway related to delay aversion. Volkow and colleagues reported in *JAMA* in 2009 lower dopamine receptor and transporter availability in reward-related brain regions in adults with ADHD, associated with symptoms of inattention. The finding is a group difference in a small imaging sample, not a diagnostic test. Heritability is estimated at about 70–80% in twin studies, but early environmental factors such as preterm birth and lead exposure also contribute.

## Prevalence and Treatment

Polanczyk and colleagues, in a meta-analysis in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 2007, estimated a worldwide prevalence of about 5.3% among children. They showed that differences in prevalence between countries are largely due to differences in methodology rather than to the phenomenon itself. In the large American MTA study (MTA Cooperative Group, *Archives of General Psychiatry*, 1999), carefully managed medication treatment (mostly stimulants such as methylphenidate) and combined medication-plus-behavioral treatment outperformed intensive behavioral treatment alone and routine community care on core symptoms after 14 months; the combination was not clearly better than medication management alone on those measures. Group differences were later reported to diminish at three-year follow-up. Non-stimulant medications include atomoxetine and guanfacine. Guidelines also describe parent training and adjustment of the school environment as part of care.

## Culture and Controversy

The diagnosis of ADHD varies across countries and social groups. Children who are youngest in their class are more likely to be diagnosed, which suggests an effect of "relative age." There is debate about overdiagnosis and overprescription in some settings, and about under-detection in girls and in other countries. In some settings, including parts of the Arab region, a very active child may be seen as "mischievous" or indulged, which may delay assessment, though systematic data are limited. The disorder should not be projected onto descriptions in medieval Arab medicine (see the entry on developmental trajectories of psychiatric illness).

## Limits

Diagnosis remains clinical, with no biological test. Symptoms overlap with anxiety, trauma, and sleep deprivation. Concerns remain about the misuse of stimulants and their cardiac and growth effects, which call for monitoring (see the entry on management of medication side effects). Many cases extend into adulthood, and detection in adults remains limited.
