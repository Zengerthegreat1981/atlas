# Equality

Equality is the idea and the social condition in which people hold the same status, enjoy the same rights, or receive the same treatment. It is one of the most powerful ideals of modern political life, invoked by revolutions, constitutions, and social movements, yet it is also one of the most contested. Does equality mean that everyone should have the same wealth, the same opportunities, the same respect, or simply the same rights before the law? Anthropology approaches the question comparatively: by studying societies that organise themselves around egalitarian ideals, societies built on rank and hierarchy, and the many arrangements in between, it shows that equality is not a single thing but a family of values, practices, and institutions that take different forms in different places.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish several kinds of equality. Political equality means an equal voice in collective decisions; legal equality means the same standing before the law; economic equality means a similar share of wealth and resources; and social equality means equal respect and dignity. A society may be egalitarian in one sense and unequal in another: a hunter-gatherer band may share meat equally while granting great prestige to a skilled hunter, and a modern democracy may guarantee equal voting rights while tolerating vast differences of wealth.

James Woodburn, in a classic essay of 1982, distinguished between immediate-return societies, such as many hunter-gatherer groups, in which people consume what they obtain each day and sharing is a constant social demand, and delayed-return societies, in which stored resources, property, and debts allow some people to accumulate power over others. Egalitarianism, in this view, is not simply the absence of hierarchy but an active achievement: it requires levelling practices — sharing, mockery of the boastful, the refusal of commands — that keep would-be leaders in check.

## Origins and Development

The modern language of equality comes from the Enlightenment. Jean-Jacques Rousseau's *Discourse on the Origin and Basis of Inequality Among Men* (1755) argued that inequality was not natural but the product of property, agriculture, and civilisation, and that in a state of nature humans had been roughly equal. Alexis de Tocqueville, in *Democracy in America* (1835), observed that the passion for equality was the driving force of democratic societies, and that it could threaten liberty as well as sustain it.

Anthropology added a comparative dimension. Early ethnographers found societies that seemed to live without chiefs, classes, or coercion, and this challenged the assumption that hierarchy was natural or inevitable. Christopher Boehm's *Hierarchy in the Forest* (1999) argued that egalitarian hunter-gatherer societies are not simply societies that have not yet developed hierarchy: they are societies in which people actively suppress dominance, using gossip, ridicule, ostracism, and even violence to bring down anyone who tries to rise above the rest. Egalitarianism, Boehm argued, is a political strategy, not a primitive condition.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One major debate concerns the relationship between equality and hierarchy. Louis Dumont, in *Homo Hierarchicus* (1966), argued that in India hierarchy was not a failure of equality but a different value system altogether, in which rank ordered the cosmos and gave everyone a place. Critics replied that hierarchy is always experienced from below as well as from above, and that even the most hierarchical societies contain egalitarian ideals, protests, and spaces of equal treatment.

A second debate concerns whether equality is a human universal or a historical invention. Some anthropologists argue that the demand for equality is found in every society, even if it is expressed differently — in the sharing of food, the rotation of office, or the right to speak in council. Others argue that modern egalitarianism, with its language of rights and citizenship, is a product of specific historical conditions: the printing press, the market, the nation-state. David Graeber and David Wengrow, in *The Dawn of Everything* (2021), challenged both views, arguing that human societies have experimented with equality and hierarchy in far more varied combinations than the old story of progress from egalitarian bands to hierarchical states allows.

A third dimension is gender and age. In many societies, equality among adult men coexists with the subordination of women and children, and feminist anthropology has shown that claims of egalitarianism often hide inequalities within the household. Equality, in other words, is always equality for someone, and the question of who counts is itself part of the politics of equality.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Equality remains at the centre of contemporary politics: debates over income inequality, racial justice, gender equality, and global inequality between nations all invoke it. Anthropology's contribution is to show that equality is not an abstract principle but a lived practice, achieved and contested in everyday life — in who shares food, who speaks in meetings, who is listened to, and who is allowed to lead. It also shows that egalitarian societies are not utopias: they can be conformist, suspicious of difference, and harsh towards those who stand out.

The critique of equality cuts both ways. Some argue that the pursuit of equality has produced uniformity and the erosion of freedom; others argue that formal equality — equal rights on paper — masks real inequalities of wealth and power. Anthropology does not settle this argument, but it widens it, showing that human beings have organised equality in many different ways, and that the choice between equality and hierarchy is a political one, made and remade in every generation.
