# Sustainability Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Sustainability ethics is a branch of environmental ethics that examines the obligations of the present generation toward future generations with respect to natural resources and the environment. It starts from the idea that depleting resources or destroying environmental balance today deprives those not yet born of a fair opportunity to live with dignity. This field raises a precise philosophical question: how can beings that do not yet exist have rights that bind us now?

## Foundations in Islamic Thought

In Islamic thought, this concept rests on the Qur'anic principle of *istikhlaf* (stewardship), stated in God's words, "He brought you into being from the earth and made you its inhabitants" (Qur'an 11:61). This stewardship requires the cultivation of the earth, not its corruption, as evidenced by God's words, "Do not cause corruption on the earth after it has been set in order" (Qur'an 7:56). Al-Shatibi, in *al-Muwafaqat*, systematized the preservation of progeny and the preservation of property as two of the five overarching objectives (*maqasid*) of the Shari'a, alongside religion, life and intellect. These two objectives implicitly call for safeguarding the resources that ensure the continuity of progeny and the growth of wealth across generations. A hadith reported in Ibn Majah forbids waste even in ablution with water, "even if you are at a flowing river"; its chain of transmission is graded weak by many hadith scholars, though the prohibition of waste (*israf*) has firm Qur'anic support (for example 7:31). Contemporary writers have relied on such texts to establish the principle of consumer moderation and the rationalization of resources as a religious obligation, not merely a political choice (a view held by some, not all, contemporary scholars).

## Contemporary Western Context

Modern sustainability ethics took shape with the 1987 report "Our Common Future" (the Brundtland Report) of the UN World Commission on Environment and Development, which defined sustainable development as "development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." In *Reasons and Persons* (1984), the moral philosopher Derek Parfit offered a precise analysis of the "non-identity problem", showing that our policies today effectively determine who will be born among future generations. This complicates the idea of "harming" people whose identities do not yet exist. Hans Jonas, in *The Imperative of Responsibility* (German original 1979; English translation 1984), developed an ethics suited to the capacity of modern technology to affect the planet's fate. He gave priority to the "heuristics of fear," a stance close to what is now called the precautionary principle, which urges caution in the face of risks whose effects cannot be reversed.

## Applications and Objections

These ethics are applied in debates on climate change, carbon pricing, and the protection of endangered species. Some economists object that temporal discounting makes the benefits to distant generations of less weight in a purely utilitarian calculation. Advocates of sustainability reply that temporal distance alone does not justify forfeiting the rights of future generations to the very resources that the present generation enjoys.

## Illustrative Example

Draining a deep aquifer of fresh water to irrigate a commercial crop, which will not be renewed for a century, so that after two generations the inhabitants of the region are deprived of their only water source, is a typical example of violating the duty of sustainability between generations.

## Further Primary Texts

Aldo Leopold's *A Sand County Almanac* (1949) preceded the vocabulary of sustainability with a land ethic that extends moral concern to soils, waters, plants and animals. In *A Theory of Justice* (1971), section 44, John Rawls asked how much each generation should save for its successors, and proposed a just savings principle chosen by parties who do not know which generation they belong to. Edith Brown Weiss's *In Fairness to Future Generations* (1989) argued in international law that each generation holds the planet in trust and should conserve the options, quality and access passed on to the next.

The Rio Declaration on Environment and Development (1992) links the right to development to the needs of present and future generations (Principle 3) and states a precautionary approach under which scientific uncertainty is no reason to postpone cost-effective measures against threats of serious or irreversible damage (Principle 15). The economics of discounting became a focus with the *Stern Review* on climate change (published in 2007), which gave very little weight to pure time preference and recommended strong early action; William Nordhaus and others disputed its discounting assumptions and favoured a more gradual path.
