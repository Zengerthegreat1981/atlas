# Al-Jurjani's Theory of Nazm

A semantic and rhetorical thesis put forward by Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani (d. 471 AH) in *Dala'il al-I'jaz* (*The Proofs of Inimitability*) and *Asrar al-Balagha* (*The Secrets of Eloquence*), holding that **the eloquence and inimitability of speech lie not in individual words, but in *nazm*** — the manner in which words are arranged and linked to one another according to the meanings of grammar (*maʿani al-nahw*).

## Content

Al-Jurjani rejects the explanation of rhetorical excellence by the quality of an individual word isolated from its context. A single word may be eloquent in one construction and banal in another. The difference, for him, lies in the grammatical-semantic relations: word order (fronting and postponement), ellipsis and mention, definiteness and indefiniteness, conjunction and separation, and the linking of each element with what precedes and follows it according to the "meanings of grammar." In the essence of the thesis, **meaning resides in the relationship between the elements, not in the isolated element**.

## Grammatical Meanings and Fine Rhetorical Distinctions

Al-Jurjani details how contrasting grammatical choices produce fine rhetorical and semantic differences even when the individual words are the same. Reordering the same words, as in *Zaydun muntaliqun* ("Zayd is departing") versus *muntaliqun Zaydun*, or choosing a nominal rather than a verbal predicate, shifts what the speaker is understood to be asserting or stressing. Indefiniteness in a context may convey magnification or belittlement depending on the contextual cue. Ellipsis (such as omitting the subject) may be more eloquent than mention when the situation calls for brevity or magnification. These precise applied examples are what distinguish al-Jurjani's work from abstract theorizing, since he linked each theoretical observation to actual poetic and Quranic evidence.

## The Relationship of Nazm to the Inimitability of the Quran

The thesis of *nazm* originally arose as an answer to a specific theological question: by what means does the Quran defeat the eloquent Arabs, despite their sharing the same words and sounds with it? Al-Jurjani answered that the aspect of inimitability lies in the Quran's own *nazm*: the precision of the alignment and proportion of its grammatical meanings in a way that humans cannot imitate. It does not lie in the strangeness of its words or in the rhetorical quality of its isolated vocabulary. This tied his linguistic project to the theological discussion of the inimitability (*iʿjaz*) of the Quran in the Islamic tradition.

## Its Place in the History of Linguistic Thought

This thesis was put forward more than eight centuries before Ferdinand de Saussure and his principle of the differential value of the linguistic sign (that the value of a sign is determined by its relation to other signs in the system, not by itself). Modern studies (such as Kamal Abu Deeb, 1979, who read al-Jurjani in light of structuralist theory) have noted points of convergence between the two theses, with a necessary methodological caveat: al-Jurjani was addressing the question of the Quranic inimitability and literary eloquence specifically, not a general theory of the linguistic sign or of human language as Saussure did. The similarity in principle therefore does not mean that the two projects or their aims are identical.
