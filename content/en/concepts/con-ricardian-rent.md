# Ricardian Rent

**Ricardian rent** is a theory developed by **David Ricardo** in *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817) to explain how the rental income earned by owners of agricultural land arises. It was formulated in the context of the sharp political debate over the protectionist British Corn Laws, and similar ideas were put forward at about the same time by Malthus, Edward West and Robert Torrens.

## The Basic Mechanism: Marginal Land and Differential Surplus

Ricardo assumed that agricultural land varies in its natural fertility, and that farmers begin by cultivating the most fertile land first. As the demand for food grows (as a result of population growth, following Malthusian reasoning), society is gradually forced to bring less fertile land into cultivation. This is the "marginal" land, which barely covers the costs of farming with no surplus at all. On this marginal land there is no rent, because output exactly equals cost. On more fertile land, however, the farmer produces a larger quantity at roughly the same cost, so the owner of that land earns a **differential rent** equal to the difference between the productivity of his land and that of the marginal land, without making any additional effort to achieve this difference.

## Rent as an Effect, Not a Cause, of Price

Ricardo reached an important theoretical conclusion that ran against the common intuition of his time: **rent does not raise the price of grain**. The reverse is true: a rise in the price of grain (due to the scarcity of fertile land relative to growing demand) is what creates the rent in the first place. Rent is therefore a consequence of the market price rather than its cause, contrary to a prevailing view that held that landowners raise prices to obtain their rent.

## The Political Use: Opposition to the Corn Laws

Ricardo used this theory as a direct political argument in the debate over the Corn Laws. Protecting domestic grain prices from cheap foreign competition forces society to continue cultivating less fertile land, which raises the rent earned by owners of fertile land at the expense of the profits of industrial capitalists (who pay higher wages to cover a dearer cost of living) and the wages of the workers themselves. In Ricardo's analysis, commercial protection thus becomes a redistribution of income in favour of the landowning class at the expense of the rest of society.

## Its Extended Influence on the Theory of Marginal Value

The idea of the "marginal land" developed by Ricardo here became an early model that neoclassical economists later drew on in the nineteenth century when developing the more general theory of **marginal productivity**, applied to all factors of production (labour and capital) rather than to land alone.

## Later Generalizations

In later neoclassical economics, "economic rent" came to mean any payment to a factor of production above the minimum needed to bring it into use, so that the concept covers scarce natural resources and other factors in fixed supply, not only agricultural land. Rent from monopoly power is a different notion. Henry George (*Progress and Poverty*, 1879) used the logic of rent to argue for taxing land values.
