# Sacrifice

Sacrifice is the ritual offering of something valuable — an animal, food, drink, a possession, or in rare cases a human life — to a god, spirit, or ancestor, in order to communicate with the sacred, to give thanks, to seek favour, or to atone for wrongdoing. In its broadest anthropological sense, sacrifice is any act of giving up something precious for a higher purpose, but the term is usually reserved for ritualised offerings made within a religious system. Sacrifice is one of the most widespread and most debated practices in the ethnographic and historical record, found in ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, and Rome, in Vedic India, in the Hebrew Bible, in African and American religions, and in the ritual life of the present day.

## Origins and Development

The classic theory of sacrifice was formulated by Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss in their essay of 1899, translated into English as *Sacrifice: Its Nature and Functions* (1964). They argued that sacrifice works through consecration: an offering is made sacred, destroyed or consumed, and thereby creates a channel of communication between the human and the divine. The sacrificer gives something of value and receives something in return — blessing, protection, forgiveness, or communion — but the exchange is indirect, mediated by the victim. Sacrifice, in this view, is a form of communication, a way of speaking to powers that cannot be addressed directly.

James George Frazer offered a different account in *The Golden Bough*, first published in 1890. Frazer saw sacrifice as an expression of magical thinking: primitive people believed they could influence the gods by offering them what they desired, or that a dying god embodied in a king or a victim could be killed and renewed to restore the fertility of the land. Frazer's evolutionary scheme, which placed magic before religion and religion before science, is now rejected, but his vast comparative collection of sacrificial practices remains a landmark of the field, and his attention to the killing of the god-king raised questions that later scholars returned to.

Later theorists shifted attention from meaning to social function. René Girard argued in *Violence and the Sacred* (1972) that sacrifice is a mechanism for containing violence: a community in conflict unites against a single victim, the scapegoat, whose death restores peace. Walter Burkert, in *Homo Necans* (1972), traced Greek sacrifice to the experience of hunting and killing, arguing that the ritualised killing of an animal, followed by communal eating, created solidarity and managed guilt about taking life. Maurice Bloch, in *Prey into Hunter* (1992), argued that sacrifice in many societies involves a symbolic reversal: the sacrificer first identifies with the victim, then triumphs over death, returning to ordinary life with renewed vitality and authority.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns what sacrifice communicates. Hubert and Mauss treated it as a message to the gods; Girard treated it as a message to the community; Burkert treated it as a way of managing the human relationship with killing and eating. These are not mutually exclusive, and recent work tends to combine them, asking in each case who sacrifices what to whom, who benefits, and what social relationships the act creates or repairs.

A second debate concerns the difference between sacrifice and other forms of offering. Is the gift of food to an ancestor a sacrifice, or simply feeding? Is the donation of money to a temple a sacrifice, or a payment? Anthropologists argue that the distinction lies in consecration and destruction: a sacrifice is set apart as sacred and then given up — killed, burned, poured out — so that it cannot be taken back. But the boundary is fluid, and many rituals combine offering, feasting, and prayer in ways that resist a single label.

A third debate concerns human sacrifice. Once widespread in the ancient world, human sacrifice is now rare and widely condemned, but its history raises difficult questions. Why did societies that valued human life kill people for the gods? Explanations range from the political — human sacrifice displayed the power of rulers and terrorised subjects — to the cosmological, in which human blood was believed necessary to feed the gods and keep the world turning. The Aztec case is the most studied: the Mexica offered thousands of captives to the sun, a practice that modern scholars explain through a combination of cosmology, warfare, and imperial politics.

## Contemporary Relevance

Sacrifice has not disappeared with secularisation. Animal sacrifice continues in many religious traditions, from the Islamic festival of Eid al-Adha to West African and Afro-Caribbean religions, where it is often a focus of legal and political controversy. At the same time, the language of sacrifice has spread far beyond religion: soldiers, athletes, and parents are said to sacrifice for their countries, teams, and children, and political debates turn on who should sacrifice what for the common good. Anthropologists argue that this language matters: it shows that the logic of giving up something valuable for a higher purpose still shapes how people understand duty, devotion, and belonging. Studying sacrifice therefore means studying not only ancient ritual but the moral imagination of the present.
