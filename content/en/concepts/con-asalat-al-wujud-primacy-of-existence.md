# The Primacy of Existence and the Mental Status of Quiddity (Primacy of Existence)

Mulla Sadra (Sadr al-Din Muhammad al-Shirazi, d. 1050 AH/1640 CE) formulated this principle in his greatest book, *al-Hikma al-Muta'aliya fi al-Asfar al-Aqliyya al-Arba'a* (begun in 1606 during his years of retreat in Kahak near Qom and completed in Shiraz in 1628), in order to overturn two earlier positions at once: the thesis of Ibn Sina, who made existence an accident added to the possible quiddity (so that for him quiddity is what is realized first and existence supervenes on it), and the thesis of al-Suhrawardi (Shaykh al-Ishraq), who held explicitly to the "primacy of quiddity" and regarded existence as a mere accidental mental consideration. Sadra reverses the order: what is realized in the external world, and the original active factor in reality, is "the one real existence admitting of gradation" (according to the principle of ontological gradation, which he built upon it), while "quiddities" (such as humanness, horseness and treeness) are merely limits, mental abstractions and conceptual considerations that mirror the degrees of intensity and deficiency of existence, not an existence independent in itself.

## Influence and continuity

On the primacy of existence Sadra built his doctrine of substantial motion (the transformation of substance itself and not only its accidents over time), and the principle remained the pillar of Transcendent Theosophy in the Iranian seminaries to this day, being taught as a compulsory introduction before gradation and then substantial motion, in texts such as Tabataba'i's *Bidayat al-Hikma* and *Nihayat al-Hikma*.

## The meaning of the question and its reason

The question the issue answers is: when we say of something that it exists, what is it that is realized in the external world, the quiddity (what the thing is) or existence (that it is)? The mind analyzes the existent into quiddity and existence, but the analysis is mental, and does not mean that there are two parts in the external world. Either the realized original is quiddity, so that existence is a consideration the mind abstracts from it, and this is the doctrine of the primacy of quiddity that al-Suhrawardi held and, among the later thinkers, Mir Damad, Sadra's teacher, supported; or existence is what is realized, so that quiddity is a limit the mind abstracts from the degree of existence. Sadra mentioned that at the beginning he held to the primacy of quiddity and then God guided him to the truth, as he relates in the *Asfar*.

## Sadra's arguments

Among his most prominent arguments is that quiddity as such is nothing but itself, that is, neither existent nor nonexistent, so it cannot be what bestows reality; for if the quiddity of the human being existed by itself it would necessarily exist in every case. Another is that existence is the principle by which things are distinguished from one another in the external world, so the difference between Zayd the existent and Zayd imagined in the mind is existence and not quiddity, since the quiddity is one in both. He also argues from the prevention of infinite regress: if existence were something added to quiddity in the external world, it too would need an existence, and so on without end. This idea in Sadra is likened to light in al-Suhrawardi's philosophy: light is one and varies in intensity, and quiddities are like shadows.

## Comparison with Sartre

The Sadrian principle is sometimes compared with Sartre's formula that "existence precedes essence", but the two differ in scope and ground. Sartre's claim concerns human beings, who have no predetermined nature and define themselves through their choices. Sadra's thesis is a general ontological claim about everything real: existence is the single reality that exists in degrees, culminating in the Necessary Being, and quiddities are mental abstractions from its limits. Sadra's view is therefore theistic and metaphysical where Sartre's is atheistic and anthropological, and a direct comparison needs care.

## Limits and criticism

The supporters of the primacy of quiddity objected that talk of "one existence in the degrees" makes it hard to explain multiplicity, and that it makes quiddities illusory things. Sadra replied that quiddities are conceptual but that they report the limits of existence, so they are not illusions. The Sadrian school was criticized by jurists and mutakallimun who feared the consequences of the doctrine of the oneness of existence and the possibility of abolishing the distinction of Creator from created. Contemporary scholars also look with caution at projecting the duality of existence and quiddity onto Western philosophy, since the Avicennan and Sadrian heritage is governed by its own terminology.
