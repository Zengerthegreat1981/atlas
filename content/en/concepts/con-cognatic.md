# Cognatic Descent

Cognatic descent is descent traced through both male and female links, so that a person belongs to the kindred of both parents rather than to a single line of fathers or mothers. The term comes from the Latin *cognatus*, a blood relative, and it contrasts with unilineal descent — patrilineal descent through men only, or matrilineal descent through women only. In a cognatic system, kinship spreads outward in both directions, creating a wide, flexible network of relatives rather than a bounded, exclusive descent group. It is the most common form of descent in the modern world, including in Europe and North America, but it is also found in many small-scale societies, from the Pacific to the Amazon.

## Origins and Development

The study of descent was one of the founding projects of social anthropology. Early scholars, following Lewis Henry Morgan, assumed that human societies had evolved from promiscuity through matrilineal to patrilineal organisation, and treated cognatic descent as a late, civilised development. This evolutionary scheme was dismantled in the twentieth century. Raymond Firth's study of Tikopia, *We, the Tikopia*, published in 1936, showed a society in which descent was traced through both parents, yet clans and chiefly lines were clearly organised: cognatic descent was not the absence of structure but a different kind of structure, in which people could claim membership in several groups and choose among them.

George Peter Murdock's comparative survey *Social Structure* (1949) established that cognatic systems were widespread and varied. He distinguished between bilateral descent, in which kin are traced equally through both parents, and ambilineal descent, in which a person affiliates with either the father's or the mother's group, choosing one line for a particular purpose. In ambilineal systems, such as those of the Maori of New Zealand and many Polynesian societies, a person could claim land and titles through either parent, and the choice was often a political one, made in light of where land was available or which group was stronger.

Claude Lévi-Strauss, in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949), treated cognatic descent as part of a general theory of alliance. Where unilineal systems create bounded groups that exchange women in marriage, cognatic systems create overlapping networks, and the problem of alliance takes a different form: not how to keep groups distinct, but how to manage the claims of a wide field of kin. David Schneider, in *American Kinship* (1968), took the argument further, showing that in the United States kinship is understood as a combination of blood and love, and that cognatic reckoning — counting relatives on both sides — is inseparable from a cultural model of the person as an individual who chooses which ties to honour.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns flexibility and its costs. Cognatic descent gives people options: a person can claim land through a mother or a father, seek support from either side, and belong to more than one group. But flexibility also creates ambiguity: when several groups can claim the same person or the same land, disputes arise. Anthropologists debate whether cognatic systems are inherently less stable than unilineal ones, or whether their flexibility is a strength, allowing people to adapt to changing circumstances, migration, and demographic pressure.

A second debate concerns the relationship between cognatic descent and the state. In many parts of the world, colonial and postcolonial states have imposed unilineal or bilateral rules for inheritance, land registration, and citizenship, reshaping cognatic practices. In Melanesia and the Pacific, for example, the registration of land under individual title has clashed with cognatic claims, in which many people hold overlapping rights through different lines. The debate turns on whether state law can accommodate cognatic kinship or whether it inevitably simplifies it, with consequences for who gains and who loses.

A third debate concerns gender. Because cognatic descent traces kinship through women as well as men, it is sometimes assumed to be more egalitarian than patrilineal systems. Ethnography complicates this picture: in many cognatic societies, men still control land, office, and ritual, and women's links are valued mainly as channels through which men claim rights. The debate asks whether cognatic reckoning changes the balance of power between the sexes or merely changes the language in which male dominance is expressed.

## Contemporary Relevance

Cognatic descent is the everyday reality for most people reading this entry. In Europe, the Americas, and much of Asia, kinship is reckoned bilaterally: we count grandparents on both sides, inherit from both parents, and choose which relatives to stay close to. The anthropological study of cognatic descent shows that this familiar arrangement is not natural or universal but one solution among many to the problem of organising kinship. It also illuminates contemporary issues: migration, which scatters kin networks across continents; assisted reproduction, which separates genetic, gestational, and social parenthood; and debates about inheritance and citizenship, which turn on whose descent counts. Understanding cognatic descent helps explain how people build families in a world where kinship is increasingly a matter of choice as well as birth.
