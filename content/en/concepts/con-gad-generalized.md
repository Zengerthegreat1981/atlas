# Generalized Anxiety Disorder

A chronic psychiatric disorder characterized by **excessive and disproportionate anxiety and tension** about a wide range of everyday life areas (work, health, relationships, finances), in a way that is difficult for the sufferer to control. The anxiety is present on more days than not for a period of at least six months, according to DSM-5-TR.

## Diagnostic Criteria

The DSM-5-TR requires, for a diagnosis of generalized anxiety disorder, **excessive anxiety and worry** about a number of activities or events, occurring more days than not for at least six months, which the person finds difficult to control. In adults it must be accompanied by at least three of six symptoms: restlessness or a constant sense of tension, easy fatigability, difficulty concentrating or mind going blank, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbance. In addition, the anxiety must cause **clinically significant impairment** in social or occupational life, and must not be attributable to a substance or medical condition, or better explained by another mental disorder. ICD-11 (6B00) describes generalized anxiety disorder similarly, as general apprehension or excessive worry about multiple everyday events on more days than not for at least several months, without a fixed six-month threshold.

## The Distinctive Feature: Floating Anxiety

Generalized anxiety disorder is distinguished from other anxiety disorders (such as panic attacks or a specific social phobia) by being **not linked to a specific stimulus**. Instead, its anxiety moves from one subject to another ("floating anxiety"). The sufferer worries today about their health, tomorrow about their job, and the day after about a family relationship, without attention settling on one specific threat that can be avoided or directly confronted. This makes it one of the most exhausting anxiety disorders over the long term, even though its individual symptoms may seem less intense than an acute panic attack.

## Causes

The evidence points to an interaction of several factors. Biological factors include genetic predisposition, dysregulation of neurotransmitters such as serotonin and noradrenaline, and hyperactivity in brain regions linked to threat processing, such as the amygdala. Cognitive-behavioral factors include catastrophic thinking patterns, attentional bias toward threatening information, and repeated avoidance and reassurance behaviors that keep anxiety going instead of resolving it. Environmental factors include early childhood experiences of instability or overprotection.

## Treatment

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is among the first-line recommended treatments. It includes cognitive restructuring techniques to challenge catastrophic thoughts, relaxation techniques, and specialized methods such as "worry postponement," which trains the patient to set a specific time each day for preoccupation with anxiety, rather than letting it intrude at every moment. Medications are used alone or combined with psychotherapy in moderate to severe cases. The main ones are selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, and sometimes pregabalin (licensed for this use in Europe) or short-term anxiolytics, which carry risks of dependence with prolonged use.

## Prevalence and Course

Generalized anxiety disorder is among the most common anxiety disorders in primary health care. It often remains undiagnosed or is inadequately treated, because its physical symptoms (muscle tension, sleep and digestive disturbances) lead patients to consult general practitioners rather than mental health specialists. Its course remains chronic and fluctuating in the absence of appropriate treatment, with a high tendency to co-occur with depression and other anxiety disorders.

## Limits

The diagnostic distinction between generalized anxiety disorder and "normal" anxiety linked to real-life stresses remains a delicate clinical matter. It relies essentially on the criteria of **proportionality** and **functioning**, which are clinical judgments that may differ between examiners. This makes generalized anxiety disorder one of the diagnoses most prone to variation among practitioners.
