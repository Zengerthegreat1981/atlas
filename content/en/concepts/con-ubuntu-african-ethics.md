# Ubuntu in African Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

"Ubuntu" is a moral and philosophical concept that arose in South Africa and sub-Saharan Africa. It is summed up in the well-known Zulu saying "Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu," meaning "a person is a person through other persons." Individual personhood and human worth are realized only through a network of reciprocal social relationships, and an individual becomes a complete human being to the extent that they live solidarity, compassion, and belonging to their community. In this sense, Ubuntu is a philosophical counterpoint to Western liberal individualism, which makes the individual a self-sufficient moral unit that precedes any social relationship.

## Cultural Foundations and Oral Thought

The roots of Ubuntu go back to ancient oral traditions among the Bantu peoples of southern and eastern Africa. The Kenyan philosopher and theologian John Mbiti gave a widely quoted formulation of this communal view of the self in *African Religions and Philosophy* (1969): "I am, because we are; and since we are, therefore I am," which is often contrasted with Descartes's individualistic "I think, therefore I am." This view rests on a conception of the individual as a node in a network of relationships that includes the living, the dead (the ancestors), and future generations together, rather than as an isolated being.

## Ubuntu in Contemporary Politics: Tutu and Mandela

The concept gained wide international renown after the collapse of the apartheid system in South Africa, when Bishop Desmond Tutu employed it as the philosophical foundation for the work of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, which he chaired. He called for restorative justice (Restorative Justice), based on acknowledgment and forgiveness to repair torn social bonds, in place of punitive justice seeking revenge. In *No Future Without Forgiveness* (1999), Tutu described the person with Ubuntu as open and available to others, secure in knowing that he or she belongs to a greater whole and is diminished when others are humiliated or oppressed. Nelson Mandela is also widely associated with the spirit of reconciliation that the term came to evoke, although he used the word less programmatically.

## Contemporary Philosophical Development: Thaddeus Metz

The philosopher Thaddeus Metz developed Ubuntu into a rigorous normative ethical theory. He proposed a systematic formulation according to which the right act is what promotes "communal relationship" (Communal Relationships), which consists of two elements: "identity" (Identity, meaning the sense of belonging) and "solidarity" (Solidarity, meaning mutual concern for the well-being of the other). Metz argues that this formulation is a promising alternative to individualistic Western theories in explaining our moral intuitions about the value of friendship and communal belonging.

## Objections and a Brief Comparison with Islamic Communal Ethics

Ubuntu is objected to on the grounds that excessive emphasis on communal harmony may marginalize the rights of the dissenting individual or one who separates from the group, and may be exploited to justify coerced conformity in the name of "unity." It is worth noting that Islamic thought contains a close counterpart to this communal orientation. The concept of the *umma* (community) and the hadith "The believers in their mutual love, mercy, and compassion are like one body" link the perfection of the individual's faith to their solidarity with the community. However, this Islamic communal bond is framed by fixed textual principles that protect the individual's juristic rights at the same time, unlike the more fluid, customary flexibility of Ubuntu.
