# Witchcraft

Witchcraft is the use — real or attributed — of hidden, supernatural powers to harm others, and the body of beliefs, accusations, and practices that surround it. For anthropology, witchcraft is one of the founding topics: the study of witch beliefs among the Azande of Central Africa, the Navajo of North America, and the peoples of early modern Europe shaped the discipline's understanding of rationality, explanation, and social conflict. Far from being a superstition of the past, witchcraft remains a live concern in many parts of the world, and its study continues to illuminate how people explain misfortune and manage social tension.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish witchcraft from sorcery, though the two terms are often used interchangeably. In the classic distinction drawn by E. E. Evans-Pritchard, a witch harms others through an innate, often unconscious capacity — a power in the body — while a sorcerer uses learned techniques, spells, and medicines. Both differ from magic in general, which may be used for good or ill, and from the role of the diviner or healer, who diagnoses and counters witchcraft.

Witchcraft beliefs answer a specific question: not how something happened, but why it happened to this person at this time. Evans-Pritchard's famous example is the granary that collapses on a man: termites explain how the granary fell, but witchcraft explains why it fell when that particular man was sitting beneath it. Witchcraft is therefore a theory of causation, one that links misfortune to human relationships — envy, anger, greed, and broken obligations.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of witchcraft began with Evans-Pritchard's *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande* (1937), based on fieldwork in what is now South Sudan. Evans-Pritchard showed that Azande witchcraft beliefs were internally consistent and rational within their own premises, and that they were woven into everyday life: oracles were consulted before journeys, marriages, and hunts, and accusations were managed through established procedures of compensation and reconciliation. This work demolished the idea that witchcraft belief was simply irrational or primitive.

In the 1960s and 1970s, Mary Douglas edited *Witchcraft Confessions and Accusations* (1970), which compared witchcraft across societies and argued that accusations flourish where social boundaries are ambiguous and relationships are strained. Witchcraft accusations, in this view, are a way of talking about social conflict — about who belongs, who is trusted, and who is resented. Later scholars, notably Peter Geschiere in Cameroon, showed that witchcraft is not a traditional survival but a modern phenomenon: it is bound up with new forms of wealth, politics, and inequality, and it adapts to each new context.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One long-running debate concerns rationality. Early anthropologists, such as Lucien Lévy-Bruhl, described witchcraft belief as "pre-logical." Evans-Pritchard rejected this, showing that the Azande reasoned carefully about witchcraft, testing oracles and revising judgements. The debate shaped the philosophy of social science: if witchcraft beliefs are rational on their own terms, what counts as rationality, and who gets to decide? Robin Horton's comparison of African thought with Western science (1967) argued that traditional religious systems and modern science are both explanatory enterprises, differing in their openness to alternatives.

A second dimension is the social function of accusations. Accusations often target people in ambiguous positions: the successful neighbour whose wealth provokes envy, the elderly widow who lives alone, the migrant who does not fit in. In early modern Europe, most of those executed as witches were women, and feminist historians have argued that witch trials were a way of policing female independence and sexuality. In contemporary Africa, accusations frequently fall on children and the elderly, raising urgent questions about protection and human rights.

A third dimension is the relationship between witchcraft and power. Witchcraft can be a levelling mechanism: the fear of being accused restrains the accumulation of wealth and the display of arrogance. But it can also be a weapon of the powerful, used to discredit rivals and justify violence. Geschiere's *The Modernity of Witchcraft* (1997) showed how witchcraft discourse in Cameroon is entangled with electoral politics, the civil service, and the struggle for access to state resources.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Witchcraft remains a matter of life and death in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Pacific, where accusations can lead to expulsion, violence, and murder. Governments, churches, and NGOs have responded with education campaigns and legal reforms, but anthropologists caution that dismissing witchcraft beliefs as ignorance misses their social logic. Accusations express real grievances — about inequality, illness, and the failure of institutions — and addressing them requires engaging with the social conflicts they encode.

At the same time, witchcraft has entered popular culture in new forms, from the global success of Wicca and neo-paganism to the representation of witches in film and television. Anthropologists study these developments as evidence that witchcraft is not a fading relic but a flexible idiom, capable of expressing anxieties about gender, power, and the unseen forces that shape modern life. The study of witchcraft, which began as an attempt to understand other people's beliefs, has become a way of understanding how all societies explain misfortune and assign blame.
