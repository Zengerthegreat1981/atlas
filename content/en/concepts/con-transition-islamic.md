# The Transition from Jahiliyyah to Islamic Thought

## Definition

This concept refers to the intellectual and social transformation that took place in the Arabian Peninsula between the beginning of the Prophetic mission (c. 610 CE) and the establishment of the first Islamic state (the mid-seventh century CE). In this period the reference framework for knowledge and value shifted from a **tribal, oral, multi-centred system** to a **centralized monotheistic religious system** that derives its legitimacy from a single text (the Qur'an) and a single prophetic authority.

## Main Features

- **From the plurality of gods to monotheism**: The system of idols and tribal gods (al-Lat, al-'Uzza, Manat and Hubal) was replaced by a strict monotheistic creed. Some older elements (such as the Kaaba and the pilgrimage) were re-employed within a new doctrinal framework.
- **From the tribe to the community (umma)**: The religious concept of the all-encompassing **umma** replaced exclusive tribal loyalty as the highest social bond. It found institutional expression in the **Constitution of Medina** (c. 622 CE), which regulated relations between the Emigrants (*muhajirun*), the Helpers (*ansar*) and the Jews of Medina.
- **From the soothsayer and the poet to the prophet**: The cognitive and social role of the soothsayer (who mediated with the world of the jinn and spirits) and of the poet (the official spokesman of the tribe) receded in favour of the authority of **prophetic revelation** as the new source of religious knowledge and moral certainty. Yet poetry continued as an important medium of expression, as in Ka'b ibn Zuhayr's ode before the Prophet.
- **From the vicissitudes of time to divine decree**: Islam reframed the Jahiliyya conception of the blind "time" (*dahr*) within the doctrine of **divine decree and destiny (qada' and qadar)**, which is subject to the will of one just and wise God, and not to a blind force without purpose.
- **From orality to writing**: The collection of the Qur'an in written form (especially in the reign of the caliph 'Uthman) marked a turning point from a purely oral culture toward a central written textual culture, although orality (memorization and recitation) remained an essential parallel instrument for transmitting the Qur'an and hadith.

## Context

This transition was not an immediate and complete rupture. Elements of Jahiliyya culture (such as the values of generosity, courage and poetic eloquence) continued within the new Islamic framework after being ethically and religiously re-adapted, while other elements (such as the worship of idols) disappeared relatively quickly and sharply.

## Impact

This transition formed the founding template for all later Arab-Islamic thought. "Jahiliyya" became a negative reference category against which the degree of "Islamicness" was measured in later religious discourse. The Jahiliyya heritage (especially its poetry) remained a linguistic and literary source of reference, indispensable in exegesis, jurisprudence and the sciences of language.

## Limitations

The temporal and conceptual boundaries of this "transition" are not as sharp historically as the late classical Islamic narratives suggest. Archaeological studies and recent inscriptions (Safaitic and Musnad) gradually reveal a greater religious and social diversity in the peninsula before Islam than the simplified narrative of a single "Jahiliyya" followed by a decisive "Islam" would imply.
