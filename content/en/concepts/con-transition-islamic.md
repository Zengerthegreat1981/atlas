# The Transition from Jahiliyyah to Islamic Thought

## Definition

This concept refers to the intellectual and social transformation in the Arabian Peninsula from the beginning of the Prophetic mission (c. 610 CE) through the mid-seventh century, when the early caliphate and the Uthmanic written Qur'an were in place. The Medinan community itself dates from the hijra of 622. Over this span the reference for knowledge and value moved from a **tribal, oral and multi-centred pattern** toward a **monotheistic religious order** that claimed legitimacy from the Qur'an and from prophetic authority. The shift was uneven, and tribal ties did not disappear.

## Main Features

- **From the plurality of gods to monotheism**: The system of idols and tribal gods (al-Lat, al-'Uzza, Manat and Hubal) was replaced by a strict monotheistic creed. Some older elements (such as the Kaaba and the pilgrimage) were re-employed within a new doctrinal framework.
- **From the tribe to the community (umma)**: The religious idea of the **umma** was set above exclusive tribal loyalty as the bond of the new community. A written agreement, usually called the Constitution (or document) of Medina and preserved in the sira, is generally placed in the early Medinan years after 622 CE. It regulates relations among the Emigrants (*muhajirun*), the Helpers (*ansar*) and Jewish groups in Medina. Scholars disagree about whether the text is one document or several.
- **From the soothsayer and the poet to the prophet**: The Qur'an distinguishes the Prophet from the soothsayer (*kahin*), who was said to deal with the jinn, and from the poet as tribal spokesman. Prophetic revelation became the new source of religious knowledge. Poetry did not stop. The sira preserves the story of Ka'b ibn Zuhayr reciting his ode before the Prophet.
- **From blind time to God's decree**: The Qur'an rejects the claim that nothing destroys people but time (*dahr*; Q. 45:24) and affirms that God wills and decrees. The later technical pair *qada'* and *qadar* belongs to kalam. It should not be read back as a Jahili idea under a new name.
- **From orality to writing**: The collection of the Qur'an in written form (especially in the reign of the caliph 'Uthman) marked a turning point from a purely oral culture toward a central written textual culture, although orality (memorization and recitation) remained an essential parallel instrument for transmitting the Qur'an and hadith.

## Context

This transition was not an immediate and complete rupture. Elements of Jahiliyya culture (such as the values of generosity, courage and poetic eloquence) continued within the new Islamic framework after being ethically and religiously re-adapted, while other elements (such as the worship of idols) disappeared relatively quickly and sharply.

## Impact

This transition supplied a pattern to which later Arab-Islamic thought returned. "Jahiliyya" became a negative reference category against which later religious discourse measured fidelity to Islam. Jahili poetry remained a linguistic source for exegesis, jurisprudence and the sciences of language, through the practice of citing verses as *shawahid*.

## Limitations

The temporal and conceptual boundaries of this "transition" are less sharp than later classical narratives suggest. Archaeological work and inscriptions in Safaitic and in Ancient South Arabian (Musnad script) show a wider religious and social variety in the peninsula before Islam than a single "Jahiliyya" followed by a clean break allows. Fred Donner has argued that the earliest community understood itself as a community of believers, and that later narratives draw sharper boundaries than the first decades support. That is his reading of the sources, not a second chronicle.
