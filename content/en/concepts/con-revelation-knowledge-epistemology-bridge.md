# Revelation and Epistemology

## Revelation as a Source of Knowledge in the Islamic Philosophers

The relation between revelation and reason has occupied Islamic thought since the age of translation. Al-Farabi, in *The Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City*, describes the prophet as one whose intellect is joined to the Active Intellect, and upon whom knowledge overflows through his imaginative faculty, so that rational truths appear in symbolic forms that the public can grasp. Ibn Sina followed this conception in *al-Shifa* and in his treatise on proving prophecies, making the prophet a person of strong intuition who receives without learning. Ibn Rushd, in *Fasl al-Maqal* (c. 1179), arranged the methods of persuasion into demonstrative, dialectical, and rhetorical, and said that the Law addresses all people through these methods; where the apparent meaning of a text contradicts demonstration, it must be interpreted. Al-Ghazali, in *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal*, opposed the philosophers' conception: he held that prophecy is a tasting (*dhawq*) that pure reason cannot grasp, just as a blind man cannot know colours, and that it is a cognitive rank above reason rather than its opposite. In *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*, Ibn Tufayl proposes that a solitary intellect can reach the very truths that revelation brought, so that the two paths run side by side.

## Modern Approaches

In the West, philosophy has treated the question of revelation in several forms. Kant, in *Religion within the Boundaries of Mere Reason* (1793), regards revelation as merely a pedagogical means of communicating what reason can grasp of the moral law. By contrast, Alvin Plantinga, in *Warranted Christian Belief* (2000), defended the view that religious belief may be "properly basic" and justified without being inferred from other beliefs or evidence, so long as the faculties of knowledge are functioning properly. William Alston, in *Perceiving God* (1991), argued that religious experience is a source of knowledge similar to sense perception. In modern Islamic studies, Fazlur Rahman, in *Prophecy in Islam* (1958), examined the history of conceptions of prophecy among philosophers and theologians, and in later works (such as *Islam and Modernity*, 1982) held that the Quran is wholly the word of God and also, in a sense, wholly the word of Muhammad's own inspired consciousness. Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd, in *Mafhum al-Nass* (1990), criticised the monolithic view that ignores the historical character of the text, and focused on the relation of revelation to the language and culture of its recipients. This drew strong objection from religious quarters.

## Points of Disagreement between Religious and Empirical Knowledge

Revealed knowledge is distinguished by relying on testimony and authority (the report of the infallible Prophet), whereas empirical science relies on repeated verification. The theologians met this by classifying the sources of knowledge: true report, sense perception, and reason (as in al-Nasafi's division in his creed texts). The tension remains over the apparent conflict between texts and science, which Ibn Rushd examined in his canon of interpretation. Contemporary philosophy of religion addresses the question with the tools of epistemology: is religious testimony an independent source of knowledge? And what does religious diversity imply for the truth of competing claims?

## Limits

It is difficult to verify a claim of revelation from outside faith, so researchers turn to studying the structure of the claim rather than its content. All the philosophical approaches above are tied to theological presuppositions: al-Farabi's conception is shaped by Neoplatonism and the theory of the Active Intellect, and it is not accepted by all theologians, who regard revelation as direct divine speech. Moreover, what the atlas offers here is a conceptual bridge between the study of religions and epistemology, not an evaluation of the truth of any revelation.
