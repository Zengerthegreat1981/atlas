# Statistical Analysis

## Definition

Statistical analysis is the use of quantitative methods to describe, summarise, and draw inferences from numerical data about social life. It turns the raw material of surveys, censuses, administrative records, and experiments into patterns that can be seen and tested: distributions, averages, rates, correlations, and the estimated effects of one variable on another. Descriptive statistics organise data — how income is distributed, how crime rates vary by region — while inferential statistics assess whether an observed pattern is likely to reflect a real feature of the population or merely chance. In sociology, statistical analysis is not an end in itself but a language for testing theories against evidence and for comparing groups, places, and periods on a common footing.

## Origins and Development

The statistical study of society began in the nineteenth century, when states began to count their populations systematically. Adolphe Quetelet, in *Sur l'homme et le développement de ses facultés* (1835; English translation *A Treatise on Man*, 1842), applied probability theory to human characteristics and introduced the idea of the "average man," showing that social phenomena such as crime and suicide display remarkable regularity in the aggregate. Emile Durkheim made this insight sociological in *Suicide* (1897), using official statistics to demonstrate that suicide rates vary systematically with levels of social integration and regulation — a pattern invisible at the level of the individual case, and explicable only by social causes.

The twentieth century brought the tools that made modern statistical sociology possible. Sampling theory allowed researchers to generalise from a survey of a few thousand to a population of millions. Paul Lazarsfeld, with Morris Rosenberg in the edited reader *The Language of Social Research* (1955), systematised the logic of variables, indicators, and multivariate analysis that still underpins quantitative sociology. Hubert Blalock's *Social Statistics* (1960) became a widely used textbook, introducing generations of sociologists to correlation, regression, and significance testing. Alan Agresti and Barbara Finlay's *Statistical Methods for the Social Sciences* (first published 1979; 2nd edition 1986) consolidated the field's core methods for a new generation, including the analysis of categorical data central to sociological research.

## Key Dimensions and Debates

Statistical analysis in sociology operates at several levels. Univariate analysis describes a single variable; bivariate analysis examines the relationship between two; multivariate analysis, especially multiple regression, estimates the effect of one variable while holding others constant, allowing researchers to disentangle the contributions of class, education, gender, and age to an outcome such as earnings. The choice of method depends on the measurement level of the data — nominal, ordinal, interval, or ratio — and on the design of the study, whether experimental, cross-sectional, or longitudinal.

The method has been the subject of long-standing debate. Critics argue that quantification can strip social life of meaning, reducing complex human action to numbers, and that statistical associations are easily mistaken for causes. The problem of spurious correlation — two variables moving together because both are driven by a third — is a permanent hazard, and the replication of findings across samples and settings is the discipline's main safeguard. Defenders reply that without statistical analysis, sociology cannot distinguish systematic patterns from anecdote, nor assess the scale of inequalities that theories claim to explain. The rise of large-scale data and computational methods has intensified both the power and the stakes of these debates.

## Contemporary Relevance

Statistical analysis remains the backbone of empirical sociology. It underpins research on income inequality, educational mobility, health disparities, voting behaviour, and crime, and it informs the evidence base of public policy, from the evaluation of welfare programmes to the monitoring of demographic change. The availability of administrative data and digital traces has expanded the scale of analysis enormously, while also raising new questions about privacy, bias in data collection, and the interpretation of results generated by automated methods. The central sociological skill has not changed: to use statistical tools with an understanding of what they can and cannot show, and to keep the numbers anchored in the social processes they are meant to illuminate. This matters most where statistics enter public debate: a reported rise in crime, a gap in earnings, or a change in life expectancy is never a bare fact but the product of definitions, classifications, and counting practices, and the competent reader of statistics asks how the numbers were made before asking what they mean.

## Comparisons across Schools

Schools assign statistics different roles. In the Durkheimian and later positivist tradition, aggregate regularities are central evidence about social causes. Weber used statistical regularities too, but held that sociological explanation also requires an interpretation of the meaningful action behind them. Symbolic interactionists, following Blumer, questioned whether variables capture the processes of interpretation, and ethnomethodologists turned counting into an object of study: Aaron Cicourel's *Method and Measurement in Sociology* (1964) and work on how official records are produced showed that categories depend on practical judgements. Marxist and critical researchers have both used statistics to document inequality and criticised official classifications that obscure it. Feminist scholars have likewise debated whether quantification objectifies respondents or makes patterns of disadvantage publicly visible. Alain Desrosières traced how statistical categories and state administration developed together. Many researchers now combine methods, though this does not settle the underlying disagreements about what counts as explanation.
