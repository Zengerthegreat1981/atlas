# Ethnicity: Shared Identity, Boundaries, and Belonging

**Ethnicity** is a form of collective identity based on a shared sense of common origin, culture, language, history, or descent — a sense that "we" are a people, distinct from other peoples. Ethnic identity can be a source of pride, solidarity, and belonging, and it can also be a basis for exclusion, discrimination, and conflict. It differs from race, which classifies people by supposed physical and biological traits, and from nationality, which is membership in a state: ethnicity rests on shared culture and a shared story about the past, whether or not that story is historically accurate. Anthropologists study ethnicity not as a fixed property of groups but as a relationship — something made and remade in the encounter between peoples, and in the politics of the states they live in.

## Definition and Development

The modern anthropological study of ethnicity begins with Fredrik Barth's *Ethnic Groups and Boundaries* (1969). Barth argued against the then-common view that ethnic groups were defined by a shared culture — a bundle of traits such as language, dress, and religion. What defines an ethnic group, he showed, is not its cultural content but its boundary: the social distinction between "us" and "them," maintained by the members themselves and by their neighbours. Cultural traits matter, but only those that are used to mark the boundary; two groups may be nearly identical in most respects yet insist on their difference, while groups that differ sharply may share an ethnic identity. Ethnicity is therefore a matter of social organisation, not of cultural inventory.

Abner Cohen, in *Custom and Politics in Urban Africa* (1969), added the political dimension. Studying Hausa migrants in the Yoruba city of Ibadan, Cohen showed that ethnicity is a political resource: Hausa identity was maintained and emphasised because it organised trade networks, provided mutual support, and secured a place in the city's politics. Ethnic groups, in this view, are interest groups as well as cultural communities, and ethnic identity is mobilised — strengthened, displayed, or played down — according to the advantages it brings. Anthony Smith, in *The Ethnic Origins of Nations* (1986), traced the long histories of ethnic communities, arguing that modern nations are built on older ethnic cores, on shared myths of origin, memories, and symbols that can survive for centuries.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Thomas Hylland Eriksen, in *Ethnicity and Nationalism* (1993), synthesised the field, distinguishing between the cultural content of ethnicity and its relational character. Ethnicity, Eriksen argued, is about the classification of persons and the social relationships that follow from it: it becomes important when groups come into contact, when they compete for resources, or when a state classifies its citizens. In isolated, homogeneous settings, ethnicity may be barely noticed; it is contact and competition that make it salient. Richard Jenkins, in *Rethinking Ethnicity* (1997), pushed the argument further: ethnic identity is not something people have but something they do, in interaction — claimed, ascribed, negotiated, and contested in everyday encounters.

These approaches share a rejection of primordialism, the view that ethnic ties are ancient, natural, and unchanging. Anthropologists now treat ethnicity as constructed and situational: its symbols and stories may be old, but their meanings change, and groups can form, dissolve, merge, and split. At the same time, scholars insist that construction does not mean invention from nothing: ethnic identities draw on real histories, languages, and experiences, and they are felt by their bearers as deep and authentic. The task is to understand both the making of ethnicity and the power of what is made.

## Ethnographic Examples

Ethnography shows ethnicity in many guises. In the Balkans, neighbours who share a language, a cuisine, and a history have maintained distinct Serb, Croat, and Bosniak identities through religion, script, and historical memory — and those distinctions have been mobilised, at terrible cost, in war. In Malaysia, the state's classification of its population into Malay, Chinese, and Indian has shaped politics, education, and economic policy, turning ethnic categories into the framework of national life. In the cities of West Africa, migrants maintain ethnic associations that provide housing, credit, and burial, while also adopting the languages and styles of their hosts.

In Latin America, indigenous ethnicity has become a basis for political mobilisation: Quechua, Aymara, and Maya movements have claimed recognition, land, and language rights, turning an identity once stigmatised into a source of political power.

## Contemporary Debates

Ethnicity is central to contemporary debates about nationalism, migration, and multiculturalism. One debate concerns the relationship between ethnicity and the state: should states recognise ethnic groups and grant them collective rights, or should they treat all citizens as individuals? Defenders of multicultural recognition argue that it protects vulnerable minorities; critics argue that it freezes identities and encourages division. A second debate concerns ethnic conflict: is it caused by ancient hatreds, by competition for resources, or by political entrepreneurs who mobilise ethnicity for their own ends? Most anthropologists favour the last two explanations, pointing to the many places where groups have lived together peacefully for generations until politics made their differences matter.

A third debate concerns the future of ethnicity in a globalised world. Migration, intermarriage, and digital media are producing new, hybrid identities that do not fit old categories — people who are at once Somali and Swedish, Korean and Brazilian, indigenous and urban.
