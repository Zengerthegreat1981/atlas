# Networked Religion (Heidi Campbell)

A concept developed by Heidi Campbell, building on the case studies and the religious-social shaping of technology framework in her book *When Religion Meets New Media* (2010) and formulated in her later writings (notably a 2012 article in the *Journal of the American Academy of Religion* and the introduction to *Digital Religion*, 2013), as an analytical framework for understanding how existing religious communities (not only new virtual communities) reshape their practices, authority and collective identities through engagement with digital and networked media. This engagement does not mean that traditional institutional religion is replaced or dissolved in the digital space.

## The Five Features

Campbell identified five recurring features of religious practice in a networked society, drawing on her comparative research on Jewish, Christian and Muslim communities negotiating the use of new media:

1. **Multisite Reality**: the religious person moves smoothly between the physical and digital spaces without a sharp separation between them.
2. **Convergence Practice**: traditional religious practices merge with new digital tools rather than being replaced by them.
3. **Networked Community**: bonds of belonging form across digital networks that cross the geographical boundaries of the traditional local community.
4. **Storied Identity**: religious identity is rebuilt through personal narratives published digitally.
5. **Shifting Authority**: traditional religious authorities (clergy and jurists) negotiate their credibility in a digital space that allows alternative voices to compete with them.

## Impact

The concept of networked religion has become a basic reference framework in the study of religion and digital media. It has replaced the simpler earlier binary classification ("religion on the internet" versus "virtual religion") with a more nuanced model that examines the continuous negotiation between religious institution and technology, instead of assuming a decisive replacement or resistance.

## Campbell's Comparative Method

Campbell built her framework on comparative case studies rather than on abstract speculative theory. In *When Religion Meets New Media* she examined how Jewish, Christian and Muslim communities negotiated the adoption of media technologies, showing that communities evaluate technologies through their own histories, priorities and authority structures (the religious-social shaping of technology). Comparison across traditions allowed her to identify recurring patterns in how religion is lived in a networked society, rather than describing a single religious case.

## An Illustrative Example: Online Islamic Fatwas

Online fatwas are often cited in this literature (for example by Gary Bunt) as an illustration of "shifting authority." While institutions such as al-Azhar and the Dar al-Ifta remain recognized sources, the internet allows preachers and self-styled muftis without institutional backing to compete with them for the attention of the same Muslim public. The result is a continuous negotiation rather than a decisive victory for either side.

## What each feature describes

In the 2012 *Journal of the American Academy of Religion* article, Campbell presents the five traits as research topics in the study of religion and the internet, and as signs of wider cultural change. In a 2013 interview she spelled out each trait for institutions. Networked community means that a congregation often functions as a loose social network rather than a tightly bounded hub: members may hold several affiliations and connect them through everyday media use. Storied identity treats religious identity as something people construct and perform, using blogs and social media to assemble a narrative and to find a group in which that self makes sense, rather than as a status fixed only by initiation. Convergent practice names the blending of practices and information drawn from more than one source as people move quickly across spaces. Multisite reality denies a sharp log-on and log-off divide: online and offline settings are carried together, and people try to live in both without treating them as conflicting selves. Shifting authority notes new interpreters online, and also the use of the same media by established leaders to rebuild or solidify their standing. The internet, on this account, challenges institutions and can empower them at the same time.
