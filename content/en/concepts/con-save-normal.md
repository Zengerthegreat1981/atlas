# Frances' Critique: Saving Normal

**"Saving Normal"** is the title of a book published in 2013 by the American psychiatrist **Allen Frances**. It is also the name given to the broader critique Frances led against the expansion of psychiatric diagnostic boundaries in contemporary American psychiatry, specifically against **DSM-5**. Notably, Frances himself chaired the task force that oversaw the drafting of **DSM-IV** (1994), which gives his critique particular weight as criticism from within, rather than from an outside opponent of psychiatric classification.

## Context and Central Argument

Frances wrote his book at the moment the American Psychiatric Association was preparing to publish **DSM-5** (2013). He warned that the expansion of diagnostic criteria and the new diagnostic thresholds would lead to **"diagnostic inflation"**: the classification of millions of people whom Frances calls the **"Worried Well"** as suffering from a psychiatric disorder that requires drug treatment. Instead, ordinary ups and downs of life (grief, shyness, normal anxiety about the future, tantrums in children) should be treated as part of the normal spectrum of human experience.

## Mechanisms of Diagnostic Expansion

Frances identifies several mechanisms of what he calls the **"medicalization of ordinary life"**:

- **Lowering diagnostic thresholds** in each new edition of the manual, so that milder symptoms of shorter duration, which were previously excluded, are included.
- **Adding new diagnoses** for borderline cases (such as disruptive mood dysregulation disorder) without a sufficient evidence base for treating them as independent disease entities.
- **The alliance of the pharmaceutical industry** ("Big Pharma") with diagnostic expansion. Each new or widened diagnosis opens a new drug market, which creates a structural incentive to expand, rather than narrow, the boundaries of illness.
- **Weak systematic oversight** of the diagnostic manual's drafting process itself. Frances argues that the DSM-5 committees worked with less transparency and weaker field testing than was the case for DSM-IV.

## Consequences He Warns Of

Frances argues that over-diagnosis harms not only those who are misdiagnosed (through unnecessary drug treatment, its side effects, and the stigma of classification), but also **the severely disturbed patients** themselves. Limited medical and social resources are spread across a wider base of mild cases, so less reaches those in greatest need.

## Impact

Frances's book provoked wide debate within and beyond the American Psychiatric Association, before and after the release of DSM-5, and became a frequently cited reference in discussions about the limits of psychiatric classification. It intersected with a similar critique from other quarters, such as the US National Institute of Mental Health, which in 2013 publicly stated that it would reorient research away from relying exclusively on DSM categories, through its Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) initiative.

## Limits

Frances's position is sometimes criticized for not offering a clear alternative classification, since it largely warns against expansion without providing criteria for drawing the line between "normal" and "pathological" more precisely than the manual he criticizes. Some defenders of DSM-5 also point out that many of the revisions Frances opposed were based on accumulated research evidence, and that his critique, though partly valid, may overstate the commercial motives of the pharmaceutical industry as a driver of diagnostic changes.
