# Drug Side-Effects Management

## Definition

Side-effect management in psychiatry is a set of practices for anticipating, monitoring, and minimizing the undesired effects of psychiatric medications without sacrificing effectiveness. It is a major reason patients abandon treatment. It rests on choosing a medication according to its side-effect profile, cautious initiation, periodic testing, joint education with the patient, and shared decision-making when benefit and harm conflict.

## Metabolic Effects of Antipsychotics

De Hert and colleagues reported in a review in *World Psychiatry* in 2011 that people with severe mental disorders have markedly reduced life expectancy (commonly cited as roughly 10 to 20 years), that cardiovascular and metabolic diseases account for a large share of this, and that some medications, such as olanzapine and clozapine, are associated with greater weight gain and adverse changes in blood sugar and lipids. A consensus statement by the American Diabetes Association, the American Psychiatric Association, and other bodies (*Diabetes Care*, 27, 2004) recommends measuring weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, blood sugar, and lipids at the start of treatment and afterward. Correll and colleagues found in *JAMA* in 2009 that children and adolescents gain weight more rapidly on second-generation antipsychotics. Strategies discussed in the literature include choosing a medication with a lower metabolic liability, adjunctive metformin, and lifestyle interventions, decided case by case by the treating clinician.

## Movement and Blood Effects

Potent antipsychotics cause extrapyramidal symptoms (tremor, stiffness, and restlessness), which clinicians may address by reviewing the dose or the choice of medication. Tardive dyskinesia may appear after prolonged use; it was described from the late 1950s onward, and VMAT2 inhibitors are now approved for it (valbenazine and deutetrabenazine, 2017, in the United States). Neuroleptic malignant syndrome is a medical emergency (fever, stiffness, and disturbed consciousness). Clozapine calls for regular white blood cell (neutrophil) counts because of the risk of agranulocytosis (in the United States the mandatory REMS reporting programme was removed in 2025, but the prescribing information still recommends monitoring), and Alvir and colleagues published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1993 estimates of the incidence and risk factors for this complication, which inform periodic blood monitoring.

## Antidepressants and Mood Medications

Serotonin reuptake inhibitors cause gastrointestinal upset, sexual dysfunction, and insomnia, and clinicians may adjust the dose or the choice of drug when these are troublesome. Fava and colleagues described, in *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics* in 2015, withdrawal symptoms after discontinuation including dizziness, nausea, and anxiety. Davies and Read reviewed the evidence in *Addictive Behaviors* in 2019 and concluded that the incidence, severity, and duration of withdrawal effects have been underestimated in some guidelines, which is one reason gradual, supervised dose reduction is generally advised (see the entry on serotonin reuptake inhibitors). Lithium requires monitoring of its blood level and of kidney and thyroid function (see the entry on mood stabilizers and lithium).

## The Patient and Context

Adherence to medication depends on understanding the patient and their beliefs about the drug and the illness. For Muslim patients, Ramadan raises practical questions about dosing schedules and the effect of fasting and dehydration on medication levels, which are best discussed with the treating clinician in advance. Fear of "addiction" or of "personality change" may lead to abandoning treatment, and stigma works in the same direction (see the entry on mental health stigma). This intersects with the clinician's practice of explaining and listening (see the entry on cultural competence).

## Limits

In practice it is difficult to distinguish a side effect from symptoms of the illness or from a placebo effect: many "withdrawal" effects and anxiety symptoms are mistakenly attributed to the drug, or the reverse. Long-term studies are scarce, monitoring guidelines vary across countries, and the aim of effectiveness sometimes conflicts with the patient's tolerance. This entry is educational and does not constitute clinical or prescribing advice; medication should not be started, stopped, or changed without consulting a clinician.
