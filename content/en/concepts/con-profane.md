# The Profane: The Ordinary and the Non-Sacred

The **profane is that which is not sacred—the ordinary, everyday, unrestricted realm of human activity not subject to ritual regulation or sacred prohibition**. While often defined negatively (as what the sacred is not), the profane is not merely absence or lacking. It is the domain where ordinary labor, social exchange, and economic activity occur; where normal rules apply; where casual contact does not require purification or ritual protection.

The sacred/profane distinction, though fundamental to religious systems, varies dramatically across cultures. Some cosmologies recognize clear boundaries; others blur boundaries extensively; still others do not organize reality through this binary. Understanding the profane requires understanding not only what is marked as sacred (and thus not profane) but also how particular communities distinguish ordinary from extraordinary, routine from ritual, everyday from ceremonial.

## Definition & Historical Development

Émile Durkheim's foundational work established the sacred/profane distinction as analytically fundamental. Durkheim defined the profane as the **ordinary realm of practical, everyday activity**—uninvested with sacred significance, not surrounded by prohibitions, accessible to ordinary handling. This distinction is not geographical (market vs. temple) but categorical—the same space can be profane at one time (market operating ordinarily) and sacred at another (market closed for festival, space temporarily ritually consecrated).

Early anthropology treated the profane realm as less theoretically interesting than the sacred, focusing attention on religion, ritual, and sacred systems while treating everyday life as background. This created an implicit hierarchy: the sacred is meaningful and worth studying; the profane is merely functional and background. Contemporary anthropology, however, recognizes that the profane is equally meaningful—how societies organize ordinary work, economic exchange, and daily routines reveals deep cultural logics.

The profane/sacred distinction has been subjected to postcolonial critique revealing that Western anthropology's theory of religion embedded Christian assumptions. Durkheim's framework, though proposed as universal, reflects specifically Christian and European assumptions about bounded religious domains. Indigenous systems often do not separate sacred and profane in equivalent ways—economic activity, kinship practice, and spiritual engagement may be inseparable rather than occupying distinct domains.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

**Émile Durkheim** (1858-1917) established the fundamental insight that the sacred's power derives partly from contrast with the profane. The sacred is marked, restricted, concentrated with force precisely *because* the profane is unmarked, unrestricted, ordinary. The distinction is relational—sacred and profane define each other. Durkheim showed that maintaining the boundary between sacred and profane (through prohibitions, purification rituals, temporal separation) is work societies must continuously perform.

**Mary Douglas** (1921-2007) analyzed how the profane represents **ambiguity and disorder** in relation to sacred order. In *Purity and Danger* (1966), Douglas showed that what is profane includes not only ordinary things but also anomalous things—things that do not fit classifications, threaten categorical order, are ambiguous or mixed. The profane is not simply non-sacred but includes the disordering potential of ambiguity. Thus, pigs are problematic in Jewish dietary law not because they are profane (ordinary/secular) but because they are *anomalous*—they possess some but not all characteristics of proper animals. The profane here represents categorical threat.

**Talal Asad** (1932-2024) critiqued how the sacred/profane distinction became a tool of colonial governance. Colonial administrators and missionaries distinguished "religious practice" (sacred, regulated, separate) from "secular governance," redefining indigenous cosmologies where spiritual and political were inseparable. This reorganization of the sacred/profane boundary itself was a form of colonial power. The "profane" secular domain became the space of colonial administrative control while indigenous sacred systems were quarantined and delegitimized. Asad's work shows that producing the profane as distinct from sacred was a colonial project.

**Pierre Bourdieu** (1930-2002) analyzed how the everyday (profane) is structured by habitus—internalized social structures shaping perception and practice. Bourdieu showed that the profane realm is not actually free or unregulated but structured by deep social logics manifesting as "second nature." What appears profane and ordinary—the way we hold our bodies, eat, speak, move—is deeply socially patterned. This challenged the idea that the profane is truly "unrestricted," revealing that profane practice is regulated, though by different mechanisms (socialization, habituation) than explicit sacred prohibition.

## Ethnographic Examples

**Ndembu Everyday Space and Time (Zambia)**: Victor Turner's ethnographic work documented how ordinary daily work (hunting, gathering, cooking, child care, marriage residence) contrasts with ritualized occasions. Yet Turner showed that the division is not absolute—profane labor contains implicit sacred dimensions; sacred rituals reference and enact profane relationships. The everyday work of maintaining matrilineal household structures (women cooking for their brothers, men residing with wives' kin) is profane activity; yet it expresses and maintains sacred kinship relationships simultaneously encoded in ritual. The profane and sacred are distinct categories but inseparable in practice.

**Islamic Daily Routine and Ritual Time (Middle East/Global)**: Ethnographic work on Islamic communities reveals how the five daily prayer times structure profane time. Work, commerce, and social interaction occur within a framework of ritual time—the profane is not separate from but punctuated by sacred temporality. Prayer times are announced publicly; markets close for prayer; social interaction is regulated by prayer schedule. This makes profane/sacred less a spatial distinction (separate spaces) than a temporal one (different times). The profane workday is structured around sacred moments rather than being wholly separate domain.

**Japanese Work and Ritual (Japan)**: Ruth Benedict's ethnographic observations and subsequent work document how Japanese society integrates profane work with sacred ritual. The profane workplace is structured by ritual-like forms (bowing, formal address, hierarchical ordering); simultaneously, sacred Shinto rituals are conducted in everyday settings (household shrine maintenance, seasonal observances). The boundary between profane work and sacred ritual is deliberately blurred. Profane productivity and sacred blessing are not separated but integrated—success in ordinary work is attributed partly to ritual blessing.

**Market Activity in Sacred Geographies (Various contexts)**: Ethnographic work across contexts (Hindu temples with adjacent market trading; pilgrimage sites with commercial activity; sacred waterfronts with fishing economics) reveals how profane economic activity occurs adjacent to and interpenetrating sacred sites. The coexistence is not always comfortable—communities debate whether profane commercial activity desacralizes sacred spaces, whether profit-seeking corrupts sacred practice. These debates reveal profane and sacred as actively negotiated boundary rather than ontologically fixed categories.

**Balinese Temple Festivals and Ordinary Time (Indonesia)**: Geertz's ethnographic documentation shows how Balinese communities move between ordinary profane time (market activity, labor, daily routine) and sacred ritual time (temple festivals following calendrical cycles). The temporal shift is dramatic—the same space transforms as profane becomes temporarily sacralized. The shift is not merely temporal but involves complete reorganization of activity, dress, comportment, and meaning. Yet profane time is not meaningless—ordinary market activity sustains the group economically, enabling ritual maintenance.

## Comparative Patterns

**Profane as Ordinary and Unmarked**: Across cultures, the profane is consistently characterized by ordinariness—unmarked, unrestricted, ordinary rules of causation and social conduct apply. The profane is the baseline against which the sacred stands out. This ordinariness is, paradoxically, deeply culturally patterned—"ordinary" in different societies looks radically different.

**Profane and Economic Activity**: The profane is consistently associated with economic exchange, productive labor, and material provisioning. Sacred systems, while requiring material support (temple maintenance, ritual food, priest sustenance), are conceived as transcending mere material utility. This creates characteristic tensions—how do communities maintain sacred space and ritual specialists while engaging in profane economic activity? How much commercial activity is compatible with sacred meaning?

**Profane as Threatening to Sacred**: While the profane is theoretically separate from sacred, in practice profane activity threatens sacred integrity—profane commercialism risks desacralizing sacred space; profane labor prevents ritual participation; profane language risks desecration through speech. This creates perpetual work communities must do to protect sacred from profane encroachment.

**Temporal Segregation**: Many societies structure the relationship through temporal separation—sacred time (festival, ritual calendar) contrasts with profane time (ordinary labor, work weeks). Yet these are not absolutely separate—profane time is preparation for sacred time (labor produces resources for ritual); sacred time enables profane time (blessing of crops enables productive season).

## Postcolonial & Contemporary Critique

Postcolonial scholars argue that colonialism involved **redefining the profane as the space of colonial administrative control**. By marking indigenous spiritual and political practices as "religious" (sacred, separate, manageable) or "superstitious" (profane, not serious), colonizers created new sacred/profane boundaries serving colonial governance. Indigenous systems where political authority, spiritual practice, economic exchange, and kinship were inseparable were fragmented into "religious" and "secular" domains, with the secular repositioned under colonial control.

**Indigenous scholars** emphasize that in many indigenous systems, the sacred/profane distinction is not organizing category. Rather, **all activity is embedded within relationships to land, ancestral presence, and ecological contingency**—distinctions more fundamental than sacred/profane. Profane/sacred was often *imposed* through colonialism rather than indigenous concept.

Contemporary scholarship recognizes that the profane itself is not static—what is marked as profane changes historically. Some contemporary movements **reclaim and resacralize previously profaned practices and spaces**, inverting colonial designation. Native land rights movements, for instance, reclaim desacralized lands as sacred, reasserting spiritual significance in contexts where colonialism had reduced them to profane resources for extraction.

## Performative and Social Dimensions

The profane is performatively enacted through ordinary behavior. What makes something profane is not its intrinsic property but how it is treated—casually, ordinarily, without restriction. This means the profane is vulnerable to **recategorization through changed performance**. Profane space becomes sacred through ritual performance; sacred objects become profaned through casual handling or desecration.

The profane also structures **access and labor**. Profane labor is often cheaper, less valued, associated with lower-status groups. Conversely, those managing sacred systems gain prestige and access to resources. This makes sacred/profane distinctions tied to social stratification—controlling what is marked as sacred is control over prestige and power.

## Methodological Approaches

**Ethnographic Attention to Ordinary Practice**: Understanding the profane requires careful observation of everyday activity—how people work, eat, move, interact ordinarily. Anthropological fieldwork that focuses only on ritual and sacred systems misses crucial dimensions of cultural meaning encoded in ordinary practice.

**Temporal Ethnography**: Systematic documentation of how communities structure time—what counts as profane time vs. sacred time, how transitions occur, how much time is devoted to each, how they relate. This reveals cultural logics of temporal organization.

**Boundary Analysis**: Studying where profane/sacred boundaries are drawn, what transgressions occur, how violations are managed. This reveals what matters most to communities and how they maintain meaningful categories.

**Historical Genealogy**: Tracing how categories shift—what was once sacred becomes profaned; what was profane becomes resacralized. Colonial genealogies reveal how imposed sacred/profane distinctions disrupted indigenous systems.

## Current Challenges & Future Questions

Contemporary profane studies grapple with: How do secular societies that do not organize reality through sacred/profane distinctions organize meaning and practice? What happens when profane commercial activity massively expands (global capitalism, commodification)—does the sacred become increasingly isolated and contested? How are marginalized communities creating new sacred/profane configurations as acts of resistance and reclamation?
