# The Ethics of Interest (Riba) Across Traditions

Economists explain **time preference** as a neutral basis for the existence of an interest rate: since individuals generally prefer present consumption to deferred consumption by an equal amount, whoever lends his money (deferring his consumption) deserves compensation, and this compensation is interest. This is a technical economic justification that seems self-evident from within modern theory, but ancient philosophical and jurisprudential traditions rejected it radically for centuries, starting from a philosophical examination of the nature of money itself rather than from a utilitarian calculation of time preferences.

## Aristotle: money is barren and usury is against nature

In *Politics* (Book I, chapters 9-10), Aristotle offered an influential philosophical argument later formulated in the concept of **chrematistics**. He distinguishes between the natural household economy (*oikonomia*), which aims to satisfy limited real needs, and the "art of acquiring wealth" (chrematistics), which seeks to accumulate money for its own sake without a natural limit. Aristotle holds that usury (generating money directly from money without real production or commodity exchange) is the most "unnatural" form of acquiring wealth, because the natural function of money is to be a medium of exchange only, not to "give birth" to more money. Money, from this perspective, is barren by nature, so generating money directly from money contradicts the purpose for which it exists. This is a philosophical argument about the natural teleology of things, not an arithmetical argument about allocative efficiency.

## Islamic jurisprudence: the prohibition of riba and its foundations

Islamic jurisprudence prohibits **riba** on the basis of Qur'anic verses (notably 2:275-279) and Prophetic traditions, and jurists distinguished among its forms, including *riba al-nasi'a* (a surcharge on deferred payment of a loan) and *riba al-fadl* (an excess in the exchange of certain commodities). The detailed scope of the prohibition, including whether and how it applies to modern bank interest, has been debated by modern scholars. Several justifications are offered for this prohibition in classical and modern literature; they partly intersect with Aristotle's argument without fully matching it, and there is no documented use of Aristotle's argument by Islamic jurists in this connection. One is that usury guarantees the lender a certain return without bearing a real risk in the outcome of the project he financed, unlike partnership and profit-sharing contracts (*mudaraba* and *musharaka*), in which both parties share profit and loss together. "Shared risk" thus becomes an ethical condition for legitimate gain, instead of a fixed guarantee imposed on one party. Another is a social dimension concerning the protection of the weak debtor from exploitation of his urgent need through the accumulation of compound interest that may drown him in an endless debt. This concern is closer to the argument from "unfairness" in contemporary philosophical debate about the limits of markets.

## Comparing the texts

The texts differ in form as much as in conclusion. Aristotle's case is brief: coin was introduced for exchange, interest makes it multiply out of itself, and the Greek word for interest, *tokos*, also means offspring, as if money bred money. The Qur'anic passage (2:275-279) instead commands: it rejects the claim that trade is just like *riba*, permits the one and forbids the other, contrasts *riba* with charity, and allows the creditor the principal so that neither party wrongs the other. Hadith reports then name six goods (gold, silver, wheat, barley, dates and salt) whose exchange is restricted, and jurists disputed the legal cause (*ʿilla*) by which the rule extends further: Hanafis looked to sale by weight or measure, while Shafi'is and Malikis distinguished monetary metals from certain foodstuffs, defined differently.

The closest parallels lie in reflection on money. Al-Ghazali, in the book on gratitude in the *Ihya*, argues that gold and silver coins exist to measure and mediate the value of other things, so that trading money for money at a gain turns a means into an end. Ibn Rushd, in *Bidayat al-Mujtahid*, relates the *riba* rules to preventing unfair exchange and notes the role of coin as a measure. Resemblance alone does not show borrowing from the *Politics*.

## The bridge: arguments with different roots, a close conclusion

What is philosophically striking about this intersection is that Aristotle and Islamic jurisprudence reach a closely similar practical position (condemnation of usury) from partly different argumentative foundations. Aristotle's argument is teleological, about the natural function of money (the Christian scholastics, notably Thomas Aquinas, later drew on it in their own condemnation of usury), whereas the arguments of Islamic jurisprudence mix the direct scriptural text with socio-economic purposes concerning the distribution of risk and protection from exploitation. This convergence across two independent philosophical traditions raises a deeper question: does it reflect a shared human moral intuition about the dangers of turning time and money themselves into a commodity open to direct pricing, or is it a situational similarity arising from comparable economic-historical contexts (agrarian societies with limited capital, in which usurious lending was feared as exploiting indebted peasants)?

## The importance of the bridge for the design of contemporary Islamic finance

Contemporary Islamic finance, in structuring alternative instruments (*sukuk*, *murabaha*, *mudaraba*), draws on classical jurisprudential categories, seeking to achieve the economic function of interest (compensating the lender for deferring his consumption and for risk) through forms that avoid fixed guaranteed interest and introduce an element of profit and loss sharing. The extent to which these instruments realize risk sharing is contested: critics such as Mahmoud El-Gamal argue that widely used products such as *murabaha* mimic conventional interest-bearing loans in economic substance. The Aristotelian link is a comparison of arguments and not documented influence on this industry.
