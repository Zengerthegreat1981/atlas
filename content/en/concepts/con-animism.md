# Animism

## Definition

Animism is the understanding that the world is populated by persons other than human beings: animals, plants, rivers, stones, winds, and ancestors may be regarded as sentient, wilful, and socially present, capable of entering into relationships with humans. The term was introduced into anthropology by Edward Burnett Tylor in *Primitive Culture* (1871), where he defined animism as the belief in spiritual beings and treated it as the earliest form of religion, born from dreams and the experience of death. For Tylor, animism was an intellectual error — primitive people mistaking the animating principle of life for spirits — that humanity would eventually outgrow. Contemporary anthropology has kept the term but reversed its valuation: animism is now studied as a sophisticated way of knowing and relating to the world, not as a stage to be surpassed.

## Origins and Development

Tylor's account dominated the subject for a century, but fieldwork steadily undermined it. Ethnographers found that peoples described as animist did not hold a doctrine of "spirits" in the abstract; they lived in ongoing, practical relationships with particular beings. The Berens River Ojibwa of Manitoba, studied by A. Irving Hallowell, treated thunder as a person — a powerful being with intentions — and addressed it accordingly. This was not a theory about the world, Hallowell argued in his classic essay "Ojibwa Ontology, Behavior, and World View" (1960); it was an ontology, a way of being in which personhood was not limited to humans.

The decisive rethinking came at the end of the twentieth century. Nurit Bird-David, in "'Animism' Revisited" (1999), argued that animism is best understood as a relational epistemology: a way of knowing the world through relationships rather than through detached observation. Among the Nayaka hunter-gatherers of South India, with whom she worked, people spoke of the forest as a giving parent, not because they believed trees had souls in a doctrinal sense, but because their practical engagement with the forest — gathering, sharing, waiting — was structured like a relationship with a person. Knowing, in this account, is something you do with others, not something you do about them.

Philippe Descola, in *Par-delà nature et culture* (2005; English translation *Beyond Nature and Culture*, 2013), placed animism within a broader comparative scheme. He argued that societies differ in how they attribute interiority (minds, intentions) and physicality (bodies) to beings. Animism attributes a shared interiority — humans and non-humans have similar kinds of minds — but different physicalities. A related Amazonian idea, analysed by Eduardo Viveiros de Castro as "perspectivism", holds that jaguars see themselves as people and see the blood they drink as manioc beer, while humans see them as jaguars. For Descola, animism is one of four great ontologies — alongside naturalism, totemism, and analogism — each a systematic way of organising the relations between humans and non-humans. Graham Harvey's *Animism: Respecting the Living World* (2005) offered a more accessible synthesis, defining animism as the recognition that the world is full of persons, only some of whom are human, and that respectful relationship with them is the proper stance of a person.

## Key Debates

The central debate concerns what animism is: a belief, an ontology, or a practice. Tylor treated it as belief — a set of propositions about spirits. The ontological turn associated with Descola treats it as a theory of what exists. Bird-David and others treat it as a practice — a way of relating. These are not mutually exclusive, but they lead research in different directions: toward catalogues of spirits, toward comparative models of being, or toward the ethnography of everyday encounters with animals, plants, and places.

A second debate concerns continuity with modernity. Tylor assumed animism would disappear with science; it has not. Animist ideas flourish in contemporary environmental movements, in the legal recognition of rivers as persons — the Whanganui River in New Zealand was granted legal personhood in 2017 — and in the everyday practices of people who talk to their cars or apologise to a plant. Anthropologists debate whether these are survivals, reinventions, or evidence that naturalism — the Western ontology that separates human subjects from a world of objects — was never as dominant as it claimed.

A third debate concerns ethics and politics. If animals, forests, and rivers are persons, what obligations do humans owe them? Animism has become a resource for environmental ethics and for Indigenous political claims, where the personhood of land and water underpins demands for protection. Critics warn against romanticising animism: relations with non-human persons can be hostile and dangerous, and animist worlds are as full of conflict as human ones.

## Contemporary Relevance

Animism matters today for three reasons. First, it has reshaped anthropological theory, contributing to the "ontological turn" that questions the universal validity of Western categories such as nature and culture, subject and object. Second, it has practical consequences in law and conservation: recognising the personhood of a river or a forest changes how it can be defended. Third, it speaks to the ecological crisis. In a time when the treatment of the non-human world as inert resource has produced catastrophic results, animism offers a worked-out alternative: a way of living in which the world is not a storehouse but a community of beings to whom humans are answerable.
