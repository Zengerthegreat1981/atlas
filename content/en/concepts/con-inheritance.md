# Inheritance

Inheritance is the transmission of valued things from one generation to the next: property and wealth, but also status, office, names, ritual knowledge, obligations, and debts. Every society must answer the same practical question — who gets what, and who becomes what, when a person dies, retires, or steps aside — and the answers societies give shape kinship, inequality, and the continuity of social life itself. Anthropologists study inheritance because it is the point where the abstract principles of kinship and property meet the concrete facts of death, ageing, and succession, and where the reproduction of a society from one generation to the next becomes visible. This entry treats inheritance as the general concept; the transmission of material property in particular is covered in the companion entry on property inheritance.

## Definition

Inheritance takes many forms, and the differences matter. Property inheritance concerns land, houses, livestock, money, and valuables. Status inheritance concerns rank, titles, and membership in a group: a child may inherit noble birth, caste position, or citizenship from a parent. Office inheritance concerns the right to hold a position — a chieftainship, a priesthood, a throne — and is often governed by rules quite different from those that govern property. People also inherit names, songs, ritual knowledge, and sacred objects, things whose value is symbolic rather than economic but which may be guarded more jealously than land. And inheritance is not only of assets: debts, feuds, ritual obligations, and the duty to care for elders also pass between generations. The rules that govern these transmissions vary enormously. In patrilineal systems, rights pass through the male line, typically from father to son; in matrilineal systems, they pass through women, often from a man to his sister's son. Primogeniture gives the estate or the office to the eldest child, preserving it intact; ultimogeniture favours the youngest; partible inheritance divides it among all heirs, which can fragment holdings over generations.

## Origins and Development

The anthropology of inheritance began with the study of kinship. Lewis Henry Morgan's work on systems of consanguinity in the nineteenth century already showed that who counts as a relative determines who can inherit. Friedrich Engels, in 1884, argued that the rise of private property transformed inheritance: once wealth could be accumulated and owned individually, controlling who inherited it — and therefore controlling marriage and descent — became central to the organisation of the family. Meyer Fortes, working among the Tallensi of Ghana in the 1940s and 1950s, showed that inheritance was not a single event but a process spread across the life cycle: sons gradually received land, houses, and ritual responsibilities as their fathers aged, so that the transfer of property was inseparable from the transfer of authority within the domestic group. Jack Goody's comparative work in the 1960s and after made inheritance a central theme of social anthropology. In *Death, Property and the Ancestors* (1962), Goody examined how property and funerary obligations were transmitted among the LoDagaa of West Africa, and in *Production and Reproduction* (1976) he contrasted African systems, in which property was often held by corporate kin groups and passed laterally among kin, with Eurasia, where intensive agriculture and private property predominated, inheritance was more often a direct transfer from parent to child, with a correspondingly greater emphasis on the conjugal family, marriage strategy, and the control of women's property.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns the relationship between inheritance rules and social structure. Does the pattern of inheritance simply reflect the economy, as Goody argued, or does it actively shape kinship itself, defining who counts as kin and what obligations they owe? A second debate concerns gender. Feminist anthropologists have shown that inheritance systems favouring men are a major source of women's economic disadvantage, and that women's struggles for equal inheritance rights — in South Asia, in Africa, and in Europe — are struggles over the material basis of equality. In many societies, state law now guarantees daughters equal shares while custom continues to favour sons. A third dimension is the tension between what is inherited and what is earned. In societies with strong inheritance rules, a person's life chances are largely fixed at birth; in societies that emphasise achievement, inheritance is viewed with suspicion, as an unearned privilege. Yet even in the most meritocratic societies, the inheritance of wealth, education, social connections, and cultural capital — what sociologists call the intergenerational transmission of advantage — remains powerful.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Inheritance is at the centre of contemporary debates about inequality. Thomas Piketty's work on capital showed that inherited wealth is a major driver of inequality in rich countries, and that its importance is growing as economic growth slows — a finding that has revived political debate about inheritance taxation. In the Global South, disputes over land inheritance drive conflict within families and communities, particularly where land is scarce, titles are insecure, and customary and statutory systems overlap. Colonialism disrupted indigenous systems, imposing European models of private property and written title, and the conflicts between customary and statutory inheritance that resulted persist in many postcolonial states. Critics of the classical anthropology of inheritance note that it focused on the transmission of land and lineage property, and paid less attention to the inheritance of debts, obligations, and care — the burdens as well as the assets that pass between generations. The enduring insight is that inheritance is never a merely private matter: it is the mechanism by which societies reproduce themselves, and by which inequality is carried from one generation to the next.
