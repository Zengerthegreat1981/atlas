# Agent & Patient in Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

The distinction between the moral agent and the moral patient is a basic distinction in moral philosophy. The agent is the being capable of discrimination and choice, and who can be held morally accountable for its actions. The patient is any being that can be benefited or harmed by an agent's act, whether or not it is itself capable of acting. An adult rational human is both agent and patient. An infant, an animal, or a patient in a coma is a patient but not an agent, since benefit and harm reach it without its being asked to choose.

## Jurisprudential and Philosophical Roots

In Islamic jurisprudence this distinction corresponds to the concept of legal accountability (*taklif*), which requires reason and maturity in the accountable person, the agent. Those whose blood and property are protected, such as the child and the insane, fall within a category whose care is obligatory, though they are not held accountable. This approximates the idea of the patient. Jurists built on this distinction the rules of guardianship (*wilaya*) and custody (*hadana*), and the principle that the pen is lifted from three: the child until he reaches maturity, the sleeper until he wakes, and the insane until he recovers.

In Western philosophy, Kant founded this distinction on the criterion of rationality. Rational beings are "ends in themselves" who bear duties, and Kant restricted direct duties to rational beings, treating duties regarding animals as indirect duties to humanity. Peter Singer extended moral consideration to animals on the criterion of sentience, the capacity to feel pain and pleasure, rather than rationality. Tom Regan, who used the terms moral agent and moral patient explicitly, argued that animals who are "subjects-of-a-life" are moral patients with rights. Both rejected limiting moral consideration to agents. This extension raises a problem: is the circle of patients determined by sentience, or by life in general, as deep ecology (associated with Arne Naess) holds?

## Examples and Applications

The distinction is clearly visible in discussions of the rights of embryos and of patients in permanent coma. There one asks whether the status of patient suffices to establish a right to life without agency. In contemporary debate on artificial intelligence, a new question arises: can a computational system be a responsible moral agent without being a patient who suffers genuine harm?

## The Problem of Grey Zones

A particular difficulty arises in borderline cases where it is hard to assign a being definitively to the category of agent or patient. Examples include an adolescent who gradually acquires the capacity to discern, or a patient with dementia who gradually loses agency without losing his status as a patient. Jurists addressed these cases through the theory of discernment (*tamyiz*). It grants the discerning child partial competence, such as the validity of some of his acts that are purely beneficial, rather than full competence. This gradation parallels the contemporary debate over "partial agency" in moral philosophy.
