# Brentano's Conscious Acts

## Context

In *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (*Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkte*, 1874), Franz Brentano argued that psychology should study mental *acts* (mental activities such as presenting, judging and loving or hating) and not, as in many associationist approaches of his time, merely the contents or elements of consciousness. He took what distinguishes mental from physical phenomena to be that mental phenomena are directed at an object, which he described by borrowing from Aristotelian and Scholastic usage the phrase "intentional (or mental) inexistence" of an object.

## Definition

A mental act, in this sense, involves:
- **The subject**: the one who perceives, remembers, loves or hates.
- **The object**: what the act is directed at, which need not exist outside the mind (one can think of or desire what does not exist).
- **The act**: the directedness itself, and its mode (presenting, judging, loving/hating).

## The Threefold Classification of Acts

Brentano classified mental phenomena into three basic classes, distinguished by the way they relate to their object.

### 1. Presentations (*Vorstellungen*)

The most basic class: merely having something before the mind, as in seeing, hearing, imagining or thinking of something. All other acts presuppose a presentation of their object.

### 2. Judgments (*Urteile*)

Acts of accepting (affirming) or rejecting (denying) what is presented. For Brentano, judgment is not the combination of concepts but the acknowledgment or denial of an object, which is the root of his theory of truth and of existential judgments.

### 3. Emotional or volitional phenomena ("phenomena of love and hate," *Gemütsbewegungen*)

Acts of taking an attitude of liking or disliking toward an object, including feeling, desire, preference, hope, fear and will. His later ethics (*The Origin of Our Knowledge of Right and Wrong*, 1889) rests on the idea that some such acts are experienced as correct.

## The Unconscious According to Brentano

Brentano rejected the existence of unconscious mental phenomena. In the 1874 work (Book II) he argued that every mental act is accompanied by a secondary, incidental awareness of itself (a view usually presented as avoiding an infinite regress of further acts). Alleged unconscious mental states are then to be understood either as physiological processes, which are not mental phenomena, or as conscious phenomena of which one is not explicitly aware.

## Descriptive Psychology

In lectures given in Vienna in the 1887-91 period, Brentano distinguished **descriptive psychology** (or "psychognosy"), which analyses the parts and kinds of mental phenomena on the basis of inner perception, from **genetic psychology**, which investigates their causal conditions. Inner perception (*innere Wahrnehmung*) is the direct awareness of one's present mental acts, and it is distinguished from inner *observation*, which he considered unreliable because attention alters the state observed.

## Philosophical and Psychological Influence

### On Husserl and phenomenology

Edmund Husserl studied with Brentano in Vienna in the mid-1880s. Husserl's *Logical Investigations* (1900-01) adopted and criticized Brentano's analysis of intentional acts, and his later phenomenology (the epoché, the phenomenological reduction and eidetic analysis) developed the study of intentionality into a distinct method.

### On analytic philosophy of mind

Roderick Chisholm, in *Perceiving* (1957), revived Brentano's thesis that intentionality is the mark of the mental, and the concept shaped later discussions of propositional attitudes. John Searle's *Intentionality* (1983) offers an independent account of intentional states and their directions of fit; it is not a development of Brentano's own classification.

### On Freud

Freud attended Brentano's lectures in Vienna in the mid-1870s. Whether and how far Brentano influenced his thought is debated; Freud's central claim of an unconscious mind stands in sharp contrast to Brentano's denial of unconscious mental acts.

## Clinical Illustrations

The following examples apply Brentano's distinction to later clinical cases; they are not his own. In a panic attack, a quickened heartbeat and altered breathing are physiological events: they are not about anything. Hearing or feeling them is a presentation, judging that they signal a heart attack is a judgment, and dreading that outcome is an emotional act. David Clark's cognitive model of panic (1986) turns on this difference, since it holds that attacks arise from catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily sensations, so that similar arousal can be read as exercise or as danger.

A hallucinated voice shows that an act can be directed at an object that does not exist outside the mind: the hearing is a mental phenomenon whether or not anyone speaks, while the accompanying neural activity belongs to physiology. The patient may also accept or reject the voice as real, a further act of judgment.

Blindsight, studied by Lawrence Weiskrantz, is a harder case. Some people with damage to the primary visual cortex respond above chance to stimuli they report not seeing. A strict Brentanian would have to count such processing as physiological, or else find an unnoticed conscious act in it; many cognitive scientists would call it unconscious perception.

## Contemporary Debate

### 1. Can there be unconscious mental acts?

- **The Brentanian position**: every mental act is conscious (and accompanied by awareness of itself).
- **The Freudian and cognitive-science position**: much mental processing, including intentional states, is not conscious.

### 2. Is every experience intentional?

The claim that intentionality marks all and only mental phenomena is contested. Pains, moods and bodily sensations have been proposed as non-intentional mental states, while some physical or representational systems are said to be intentional in a derivative sense. Representationalists, by contrast, try to treat all experience as intentional.

## The Place of the Concept in the Bridge

The concept of mental acts connects the descriptive psychology of the nineteenth century with phenomenology, analytic philosophy of mind and later discussions in psychology of how mental states relate to their objects. It is a point of contact, and not a claim of a single continuous tradition: the successive thinkers named here adopted, transformed and often rejected Brentano's own views.
