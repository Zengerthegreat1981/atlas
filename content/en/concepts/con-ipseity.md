# Ipseity: Identity as Selfhood Through Time

The term *ipseity* (French *ipséité*, from Latin *ipse*, "self") is used in two related but distinct senses in the Atlas's bridge between philosophy and psychiatry: in Paul Ricoeur's hermeneutic philosophy of selfhood, and in phenomenological psychiatry, where it denotes the basic, pre-reflective sense of being a subject of experience.

## Ricoeur's Ipseity

In *Oneself as Another* (*Soi-même comme un autre*, 1990), Ricoeur distinguishes two models of identity over time. **Sameness** (*mêmeté*, Latin *idem*) is identity as continued likeness or permanence: the same character, the same traits, the same fingerprint. **Selfhood** or **ipseity** (*ipséité*, Latin *ipse*) is the identity of the one who acts, speaks, narrates and is held responsible. Its characteristic form of permanence in time is not unchanging character but **self-constancy** (*maintien de soi*), most clearly exemplified by keeping a promise: I remain the one who promised even though my desires and circumstances change. Ricoeur holds that **narrative identity** mediates between the two poles, since a life story can combine permanence and change.

## Philosophical Meaning

Ricoeur uses the distinction to find a way out of the impasse of the modern debate on personal identity. Locke grounded personal identity in consciousness and memory, and Derek Parfit (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984) argued that what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness. Ricoeur's analysis (Study 5) holds that these accounts concentrate on sameness and cannot account for selfhood. The self is not first a thing to be discovered but a capacity to be attested: Ricoeur speaks of **attestation** (*attestation*) as the kind of certainty that belongs to the self's confidence in its power to speak, act, narrate and be accountable. In the final studies he links selfhood to an **ethical aim**: "the good life with and for others in just institutions."

## Psychological and Clinical Meaning

In phenomenological psychiatry, Louis Sass and Josef Parnas ("Schizophrenia, Consciousness, and the Self," *Schizophrenia Bulletin*, 2003) use "ipseity" for the minimal, pre-reflective self-awareness that accompanies all experience (the sense that my experience is mine, a notion indebted to Husserl and to later phenomenologists such as Michel Henry and Dan Zahavi). They propose that disturbance of this basic self-experience, involving **hyperreflexivity** and diminished self-affection, is a core feature of the schizophrenia spectrum. This is a phenomenological hypothesis that informs clinical interviewing (for example, the EASE interview of Parnas and colleagues) and research, and it is not a diagnostic criterion; it should not be read as Ricoeur's concept, since the two uses of the term differ. Narrative approaches in psychotherapy (for example, White and Epston, *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends*, 1990) also speak of self-narratives but do not derive from Ricoeur's analysis of ipseity.

## What Distinguishes It from Neighboring Concepts

Ricoeur's ipseity differs from **personal identity** as sameness (*idem*): ipseity concerns the self's constancy as agent and promise-keeper, not the persistence of traits. It differs from the **ego** in psychoanalysis, which Freud described as a part of the mental apparatus mediating between the id, the superego and reality, and from the **Self** in Jung's analytical psychology, the archetype of wholeness of the psyche. It also differs from **authenticity** in the existentialist sense (Heidegger, Sartre), a term Ricoeur does not use as his central concept; his theme is rather attestation and the ethical aim, though the two traditions can be compared in discussing responsibility for one's own life.
