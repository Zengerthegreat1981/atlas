# Attachment Styles

## Definition

**Attachment styles** are **emotional and behavioral patterns** that individuals develop in **childhood** through relationships with primary **caregivers** (often the mother, though other caregivers are equally significant). These styles shape internal working models of self and others, continuing to influence **romantic relationships, friendships, and stress regulation** throughout adulthood.

## The Classical Classification (Ainsworth)

**Mary Ainsworth** and colleagues identified three primary infant patterns using the standardized "Strange Situation" laboratory procedure (Ainsworth & Bell, 1970; Ainsworth et al., 1978). The percentages below represent approximate normative baselines derived largely from North American, non-clinical cohorts, which exhibit meaningful cross-cultural variation. The figures for the first three patterns reflect three-category coding; when the disorganized category is also coded, the normative distribution is about 62% secure, 15% avoidant, 9% resistant and 15% disorganized (van IJzendoorn et al., 1999):

### 1. Secure Attachment
- **Trust** in caregiver availability and responsiveness.
- **Exploration** of the surrounding environment using the caregiver as a **secure base**.
- **Distress** upon separation, followed by rapid soothing and **positive reunion** behaviors.
- **Prevalence**: roughly 60–65% of infants in normative community samples.

### 2. Anxious-Ambivalent (Insecure-Resistant) Attachment
- **Heightened anxiety** and apprehension prior to and during separation.
- **Reluctance** to explore unfamiliar surroundings independently.
- **Ambivalence** upon reunion: seeking contact while simultaneously displaying anger, petulance, or resistance to soothing.
- **Prevalence**: roughly 10–15% of infants in normative community samples.

### 3. Insecure-Avoidant Attachment
- **Apparent indifference** and minimization of affective display toward caregivers.
- **Avoidance** of proximity and contact during reunion episodes, focusing attention on toys.
- **Physiological arousal** (elevated heart rate and cortisol levels) despite lack of overt behavioral distress.
- **Prevalence**: roughly 20% of infants in normative community samples.

### 4. Disorganized/Disoriented Attachment

A fourth infant pattern was identified and validated by Mary Main and Judith Solomon (1986, 1990):

- **Absence of a coherent strategy**: contradictory behaviors (e.g., approaching with head averted, freezing, stereotypic movements).
- **Apparent fear** or apprehension directed at the caregiver, placing the child in a biological paradox where the source of fear is also the designated haven of safety.
- **Etiology**: strongly associated with caregiver fright, frightening behavior, severe neglect, maltreatment, or unresolved parental trauma.
- **Prevalence**: roughly 15% in low-risk cohorts, rising to roughly half in maltreated samples (about 48%), with smaller elevations in several other high-risk groups, such as low-income families (about 25%).

## Adult Romantic Attachment (Hazan & Shaver, 1987)

Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987) extended attachment theory from developmental infancy to **adult romantic relationships**, demonstrating that adult intimate bonds exhibit homologous emotional dynamics: seeking proximity, viewing the romantic partner as a haven of safety and secure base, and experiencing distress upon prolonged separation.

## Dimensional and Four-Category Models (Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991)

Kim Bartholomew and Leonard M. Horowitz (1991) systematized adult attachment into a four-category taxonomy based on the intersection of two orthogonal cognitive dimensions: the internal **model of self** (attachment anxiety, reflecting self-worth) and the internal **model of others** (attachment avoidance, reflecting expectations of others' availability):

1. **Secure** (Positive Self, Positive Other): Comfort with intimacy and autonomy; low anxiety, low avoidance.
2. **Preoccupied** (Negative Self, Positive Other): Chronic dread of abandonment and insatiable craving for closeness; high anxiety, low avoidance.
3. **Dismissing-Avoidant** (Positive Self, Negative Other): Defensive self-reliance, compulsive independence, and emotional suppression; low anxiety, high avoidance.
4. **Fearful-Avoidant** (Negative Self, Negative Other): Simultaneous desire for close relationships and intense fear of rejection, leading to self-protective withdrawal; high anxiety, high avoidance.

Subsequent psychometric research (Brennan, Clark, & Shaver, 1998; Fraley & Waller, 1998) established that adult attachment is most accurately represented as continuous scores along two underlying dimensions—**Attachment Anxiety** and **Attachment Avoidance**—measured reliably by instruments such as the Experiences in Close Relationships (ECR) inventory.

## Theoretical Foundations

### 1. Attachment Theory (Bowlby)
- Evolutionary and biological **foundations** of infant-caregiver attachment as a survival system.
- The concept of the **secure base** from which exploration proceeds and safe haven to which one returns.
- **Internal working models**: cognitive-affective representations of self and attachment figures that guide interpersonal expectations.

### 2. Behavioral and Developmental Research
- The **Strange Situation Protocol** as an empirically validated behavioral assessment.
- Caregiver **maternal sensitivity**, attunement, and prompt responsiveness as primary predictors of security.

### 3. Neurobiology and Physiological Correlates
- Neurobiological systems underlying social affiliation and stress regulation, including oxytocinergic, dopaminergic, and opioid circuits.
- Hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis reactivity: avoidant strategies typically involve hyper-regulation and suppressed behavioral signaling, whereas anxious strategies involve hyper-activating emotional strategies.

## Dynamics in Romantic Relationships

- **Secure dyads**: characteristically maintain higher relationship satisfaction, open communication, effective emotional vulnerability, and collaborative conflict resolution.
- **Preoccupied individuals**: prone to hyperactivating strategies, catastrophic interpretations of partner withdrawal, chronic reassurance seeking, and intrusive behaviors.
- **Dismissing-avoidant individuals**: deploy deactivating strategies, emotional detachment, stonewalling, and excessive self-sufficiency during interpersonal conflict.
- **Anxious-avoidant pairings**: frequently develop demand-withdraw interaction cycles that reinforce each partner's respective insecure working model.

## Stability and "Earned" Security

Attachment styles show moderate longitudinal stability across life transitions, but internal working models remain open to revision. Research in developmental and relational psychology identifies the phenomenon of **"earned security"** (a label used by Pearson and colleagues, 1994, building on Main and Goldwyn's Adult Attachment Interview coding system): individuals who experienced adverse or insecure caregiving in childhood achieve secure adult attachment working models through coherent cognitive processing, reparative adult relationships, or sustained psychological therapy.

## Clinical Applications

- **Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT)**: Developed by Sue Johnson, EFT explicitly uses attachment principles to identify distressed cycles in couples and reshape secure emotional bonds.
- **Attachment-Based Psychotherapy**: The therapist serves as a temporary "secure base" from which the client explores painful developmental memories and updates defensive internal working models.
- **Dyadic Developmental Psychotherapy (DDP)**: Applied in child and family therapy to restore relational attunement following complex developmental trauma.

## Legacy

- A foundational framework spanning developmental, social, cognitive, and clinical psychology.
- Standardized empirical assessment across life stages (Strange Situation, Adult Attachment Interview, ECR).
- Transformation of psychodynamic concepts into rigorous, testable hypotheses with profound clinical and therapeutic applications.
