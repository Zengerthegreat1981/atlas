# Attachment Styles

## Definition

**Attachment styles** are **emotional and behavioral patterns** that the individual learns in **childhood** through his relationship with his primary **caregivers** (often the mother, though other caregivers are equally relevant). These styles continue to influence **romantic relationships and friendships** in adulthood.

## The classical classification (Ainsworth)

**Mary Ainsworth** and colleagues identified three main infant patterns using the "Strange Situation" procedure (Ainsworth & Bell, 1970; Ainsworth et al., 1978). The percentages below are approximate figures from largely North American, low-risk samples and vary across populations:

### 1. Secure Attachment
- **Trust** in caregivers.
- **Exploration** of the world with a **secure base**.
- **Distress** at separation, **happiness** at reunion.
- **Rate** of roughly 60-65% of children in such samples.

### 2. Anxious-Ambivalent Attachment
- **Anxiety** about separation.
- **Difficulty** exploring.
- **Resistance** at reunion (anger + clinging).
- **Rate** of roughly 10-15% of children in such samples.

### 3. Avoidant Attachment
- **Avoidance** of caregivers.
- Early **independence**.
- **No** apparent distress at separation.
- **Rate** of roughly 20% of children in such samples.

### 4. Disorganized Attachment

A fourth infant pattern was described by Main and Solomon (1986):

- **Contradiction** in response.
- **Apparent fear** of caregivers.
- **Association** with maltreatment or unresolved caregiver trauma.
- **Rate** of roughly 15% in low-risk samples, and substantially higher in maltreated children.

## Adult romantic attachment (Hazan & Shaver, 1987)

**Hazan and Shaver** (1987) extended the three-pattern classification (secure, anxious-ambivalent, avoidant) to **adult romantic relationships**.

## The dimensional classification (Bartholomew & Horowitz, 1991)

**Bartholomew and Horowitz** proposed a four-category model of adult attachment based on two underlying models (of self and of others). Later work (for example Brennan, Clark & Shaver, 1998) describes adult attachment along two dimensions, usually labelled anxiety and avoidance. Research on adult attachment generally favors treating these as continuous dimensions rather than strict categories:

### Dimension 1: Anxiety
- **Anxiety** about rejection.
- **Doubt** about the other's love.
- **Excessive need** for acceptance.

### Dimension 2: Avoidance
- **Discomfort** with intimacy.
- **Excessive independence**.
- **Avoidance** of commitment.

### The four styles:
1. **Secure**: low anxiety + low avoidance.
2. **Preoccupied**: high anxiety + low avoidance.
3. **Fearful-Avoidant**: high anxiety + high avoidance.
4. **Dismissive-Avoidant**: low anxiety + high avoidance.

## Theoretical origins

### 1. Attachment theory (Bowlby)
- The biological **basis** of attachment.
- The **secure base** (Secure Base).
- **Internal working models** (Internal Working Models).

### 2. The Ainsworth model
- The **Strange Situation** as a research tool.
- **Three** basic patterns.

### 3. Neuroscience research
- Research on brain systems involved in threat and social bonding (for example the amygdala).
- Research on **oxytocin** in bonding, though calling it "the attachment hormone" is a simplification.

## Development in romantic relationships

### 1. Effects of each style

#### Secure
- Stable and satisfying **relationships**.
- Effective **communication**.
- Mutual **trust**.
- Constructive **conflict resolution**.

#### Preoccupied
- **Attraction** to unavailable partners.
- Excessive **jealousy**.
- Excessive **clinging**.
- **Fear** of abandonment.

#### Fearful-Avoidant
- **Desire** for intimacy + fear of it.
- Troubled **relationships**.
- **Oscillation** between closeness and distance.

#### Dismissive-Avoidant
- Excessive **independence**.
- **Avoidance** of commitment.
- Emotional **distance**.
- **Difficulty** expressing feelings.

## Influencing factors

### 1. Childhood
- The mother's **responsiveness** to the child's needs.
- **Sensitivity** (Sensitivity).
- **Consistency** (Consistency).
- Emotional **availability**.

### 2. Events
- Childhood **trauma**.
- **Loss** of a parent.
- **Maltreatment**.
- Emotional **neglect**.

### 3. Culture
- Cultural **differences** in practice.
- Social **expectations**.
- **Values** about the family.

## Change across the lifespan

### 1. The possibility of change
- Attachment styles show moderate stability, and a substantial minority of people change classification over time.
- Secure **relationships** modify the pattern.
- Psychological **therapy** is effective.

### 2. "Earned" security (Earned Security)
- Individuals who experienced **insecure attachment** but later developed **secure relationships**.
- Change is thought to remain possible across adulthood.

## Therapeutic applications

### 1. Attachment-Based Therapy
- **Analysis** of attachment patterns.
- **Building** a secure base with the therapist.
- **Modifying** internal models.

### 2. Family therapy
- **Improving** communication between generations.
- **Building** secure patterns.
- **Repairing** conflicts.

### 3. Couple therapy (Johnson's EFT)
- **Analysis** of interactional cycles.
- **Building** secure attachment between the partners.

## Legacy

- A **cornerstone** of developmental psychology.
- A **measure** for assessing relationships.
- A **foundation** for couple and family therapy.
- It has influenced **neuropsychology**.
- It has become a common **language** in relationships.
