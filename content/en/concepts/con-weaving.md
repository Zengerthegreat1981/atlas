# Weaving

Weaving is the interlacing of two sets of threads — warp and weft — to make cloth, and it is one of the most ancient, widespread, and socially charged technologies in human history. From the linen of pharaonic Egypt to Andean textiles, West African kente, and the handlooms of South Asia, woven cloth has clothed bodies, furnished houses, carried messages, stored wealth, and marked rank. Anthropologists study weaving as a technology, an art, and a social practice: the making of cloth is bound up with gender, skill, trade, and the transmission of knowledge.

## Definition

Technically, weaving is distinguished from other ways of making fabric, such as felting, knotting, and knitting, by the right-angled interlacing of warp threads, held under tension on a loom, with weft threads passed over and under them. Looms range from simple backstrap looms, where the weaver's own body provides the tension, to the great treadle looms of professional workshops. The structure of a fabric — plain weave, twill, satin, or tapestry — is determined by the order in which the weft passes, and pattern can be created by colour, by supplementary threads, or by the structure itself.

But a technical definition misses what matters most to anthropologists. Weaving is learned work, usually taught through close observation and imitation within a household or workshop. Irene Emery's classic analysis of fabric structures showed how much technical knowledge is embedded in cloth itself: a weaver's choices about yarn, tension, and pattern are decisions about materials, aesthetics, and social meaning, not merely mechanical steps.

## Origins and Development

The earliest evidence of weaving comes from the Palaeolithic: impressions of woven and twined fabrics in fired clay at Dolní Věstonice in Central Europe date to around 27,000 years ago. True loom weaving developed with the domestication of flax and wool in the Neolithic Near East, and by the Bronze Age, textile production was a major organised industry in Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Aegean, employing large numbers of workers and supplying cloth for trade, tribute, and ritual.

Different regions developed distinctive traditions. In the Andes, weavers achieved extraordinary complexity with the backstrap loom, producing textiles so valued that they were buried with the dead and offered to the gods. In West Africa, narrow-strip weaving produced the brilliant patterned cloths known as kente. In Europe and Asia, the treadle loom and later mechanisation transformed weaving from a household craft into a factory industry, a shift that reshaped whole economies and provoked resistance, most famously among the Luddites in early nineteenth-century England.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

Gender is central to the anthropology of weaving. In many societies weaving is women's work, and the loom is a source of female identity, income, and authority. Elizabeth Wayland Barber argued that women's textile work was one of the first great industries of the ancient world, and that the invention of the spindle and loom was as consequential as the invention of agriculture. Yet the gendering of weaving varies: in parts of West Africa and the Islamic world, professional male weavers dominate the market, and women weave for the household. These differences show that the association of weaving with women is a cultural arrangement, not a natural fact.

A second dimension is the relationship between cloth and communication. Woven patterns can encode identity: a motif may mark a clan, a village, a marital status, or a ritual role. In some societies, cloth functions as a visual language, readable by those who know the code. Cloth also stores wealth and value: in many parts of Africa and the Pacific, textiles serve as currency, bridewealth, and ceremonial gifts, and the accumulation of cloth is a measure of prestige.

A third debate concerns skill and knowledge. Weaving knowledge is often transmitted without writing, through the body — the hands, the eyes, the rhythm of the shuttle. Anthropologists therefore study weaving as a form of embodied knowledge, in which mastery is shown in the evenness of the fabric and the speed of the work rather than in explicit rules.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Weaving remains economically and politically significant. Handwoven textiles are sold in global markets as heritage crafts, and development projects promote weaving cooperatives as a route to women's income. Anthropologists working with these projects note the tensions: the demand for "authentic" traditional patterns can freeze innovation, while the pressure to produce for tourists can devalue the cloth's local meanings.

The history of industrialisation also gives weaving a critical edge. The mechanisation of weaving was one of the first great displacements of labour by machines, and the study of its social consequences — the loss of household production, the rise of factory discipline, the gendered division of textile work — remains relevant to debates about automation today. Weaving, in this view, is not a quaint survival but a technology through which people have negotiated skill, value, and power for thousands of years.
