# Big Push and Unbalanced Growth

These two opposing concepts refer to competing strategies in development economics for how to launch the process of industrialisation in an underdeveloped economy. The first is the **big push**, set out by Paul Rosenstein-Rodan in 1943 for the industrialisation of Eastern and South-Eastern Europe: a large investment undertaken in several linked sectors at the same time. The second is **unbalanced growth**, which Albert Hirschman proposed in 1958 in *The Strategy of Economic Development* as an alternative to it; Hirschman criticised the big push rather than originating it.

## The Logic of the Big Push

The logic of the big push rests on complementarities and pecuniary externalities between sectors, and on indivisibilities in production and demand, as Rosenstein-Rodan argued. Establishing a single factory in isolation may fail commercially because the small domestic market cannot absorb its output. But establishing several interlinked factories at the same time creates mutual demand among these very sectors (the workers of the first factory buy the products of the second, and vice versa). This makes each individual investment profitable within the whole group, even though it might have been unprofitable if carried out alone. The idea was close to Ragnar Nurkse's doctrine of balanced growth (1953), and was used to justify comprehensive economic planning programmes in several developing countries during the 1950s and 1960s. It was later formalised by Kevin Murphy, Andrei Shleifer and Robert Vishny (1989) as a model of multiple equilibria in which industrialisation can be profitable for every firm only if many firms industrialise together.

## Hirschman's Critique: The Unrealistic Administrative Assumption

Albert Hirschman criticised this model on a direct practical ground. Carrying out a simultaneous big push presumes a vast organisational and administrative capacity in the governments of developing countries. Such capacity is rarely present in newly independent economies that already suffer from a shortage of trained administrators and of institutions able to plan and execute several large projects at once, in a complex and synchronised way, without corruption or mismanagement that undermines the entire project.

## The Alternative: Deliberately Unbalanced Growth

Instead, Hirschman proposed the opposite strategy: focused pivotal investments concentrated in a single sector or project. Their success creates genuine bottlenecks and pressures (a shortage of particular inputs, or excess demand for supporting services). These force the rest of the economy to invest successively to relieve these bottlenecks gradually. The approach exploits the "forward and backward linkages" that each pivotal project generates in the rest of the economy, rather than attempting to plan everything at once with limited administrative resources.

## The Continuing Relevance in Contemporary Policy

The tension between these two logics remains present in contemporary development debates, especially in the debate over the effectiveness of foreign aid. Proponents of a large, coordinated increase in international aid invoke the logic of the big push to break the poverty trap in one stroke. Critics of large-scale aid, relying implicitly on a logic close to Hirschman's critique, prefer smaller, more manageable partial interventions that are amenable to precise empirical evaluation.

## Later assessment

The two arguments answer different constraints. A coordinated push is relevant where demand spillovers are large and a government can carry several projects at once. Hirschman's linkages were offered for settings where administrative capacity is scarce and one project's bottlenecks can induce the next investment. Historical cases do not line up neatly with either recipe. Coordinated industrial policy in parts of East Asia is not the same programme as Rosenstein-Rodan's 1943 proposal, and linkage stories are hard to separate from other causes of industrial growth. The dispute remains open in the literature on aid and industrial policy.
