# Socialization

## Definition

Socialization is the lifelong process through which individuals learn the norms, values, language, roles, and skills of their society and come to see themselves as members of it. It is how a biological human being becomes a social person: capable of communicating, cooperating, anticipating the expectations of others, and acting in ways a given group recognises as appropriate. Sociologists distinguish it from mere learning, because socialization also involves the internalisation of culture — taking external rules into the self so that they guide conduct even when no one is watching. It is not a one-way imposition. Individuals are shaped by their societies, but they also interpret, resist, and remake the norms they inherit, so socialization is best understood as an interactive accomplishment rather than a passive moulding.

## Origins and Development

The concept took shape in the early twentieth century, when sociology and social psychology began to ask how society gets inside the individual. Charles Horton Cooley argued in 1902 that the self is formed through the "looking-glass" — imagining how others see us and responding to that imagined judgement. George Herbert Mead developed this into a systematic theory in the lectures published as *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934): children learn to take the roles of particular others, then of the "generalized other," the organised attitudes of the whole community, and in doing so acquire a self. Sigmund Freud offered a rival account of the same problem, in which the child internalises parental authority as the superego. Jean Piaget, writing on moral development in 1932, showed that children's understanding of rules changes in stages, from obedience to authority toward mutual respect among peers.

Talcott Parsons later made socialization central to functionalist theory. In *The Social System* (1951) he treated it as the mechanism by which the value patterns of a society are reproduced in each new generation, with the family as the primary agency. Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann, in *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966), recast the idea in phenomenological terms: primary socialization in childhood creates the taken-for-granted world, while secondary socialization inducts the adult into the specialised roles of institutions, and rarely overwrites what came first completely.

## Key Dimensions and Debates

Sociologists commonly distinguish primary socialization, which takes place in infancy and childhood within the family, from secondary socialization, which continues through school, work, peer groups, and the media. Anticipatory socialization describes learning the norms of a group one hopes to join, while resocialization involves unlearning old roles and acquiring new ones — in a prison, a monastery, or a total institution, as Erving Goffman documented in *Asylums* (1961). Agents of socialization include the family, schools, peer groups, religious institutions, workplaces, and increasingly digital media.

A persistent debate concerns how much agency the individual retains. Functionalist accounts stress the reproduction of order; interactionist accounts stress negotiation and the creative self. Feminist and critical sociologists have shown that socialization is not neutral: children are socialised into gender, class, and racial positions, and what counts as normal behaviour reflects the power of dominant groups. Cross-cultural research has also challenged the assumption of a single developmental path, showing that the timing and content of socialization vary sharply across societies.

## Contemporary Relevance

Socialization remains central to explaining how societies persist and how they change. It frames research on schooling and inequality, on the transmission of language and religion, on political attitudes formed in youth, and on the effects of digital life, where children are now socialised partly by platforms and algorithms rather than only by face-to-face contact. Migration and multicultural societies raise the question of how people are socialised into more than one culture at once, and of how second-generation immigrants negotiate competing norms. The concept also underpins policy debates: early childhood programmes, civic education, and the rehabilitation of offenders all rest on the premise that socialization can be deliberately shaped — and on the recognition that it is never fully under anyone's control. Recent research on digital life has added a further complication: children are increasingly socialised by recommendation systems and peer networks that operate beyond the reach of parents and teachers, so that the agencies of socialization themselves are being reorganised. Understanding who socialises whom, and to what ends, has therefore become a question about the design of institutions and technologies as much as about the family and the school.
