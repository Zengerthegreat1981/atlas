# Magic

Magic is the performance of rites and the use of techniques believed to influence events, persons or natural forces through supernatural means. It is one of anthropology's oldest and most debated concepts, closely related to — but distinct from — religion, science, sorcery and witchcraft. Where religion typically involves prayer and supplication to spiritual beings, magic is usually understood as instrumental and coercive: the practitioner does not ask a god for help but performs the correct words, gestures and substances to compel a result. Where sorcery is a specific, usually harmful, learned technique, magic is the broader category, encompassing healing, protection, love, fertility, divination and the control of weather and crops, as well as harm.

## Origins and Development

The founding theory of magic was James Frazer's. In *The Golden Bough*, Frazer argued that magic rests on two principles of association: similarity, or homeopathic magic, in which like produces like — a wax image harmed in order to harm the person it resembles — and contagion, in which things once in contact continue to act on each other, so that a lock of hair or a piece of clothing can be used against its owner. Frazer saw magic as a mistaken science, a false theory of causation that humanity would outgrow. Bronisław Malinowski, working among the Trobriand Islanders, rejected this intellectualist view. For Malinowski, magic was not failed science but a practical response to uncertainty: Trobrianders used magic precisely where knowledge and skill ran out — in deep-sea fishing, in gardening against unpredictable weather, in love and in war — and it gave them confidence to act. Magic, he argued, is functional: it organises emotion, focuses effort and sustains morale.

Marcel Mauss, writing with Henri Hubert, offered a different, sociological account. Magic, for them, is a social fact: its power lies not in the rite itself but in the collective belief of the group and in the authority of the magician, whose status is conferred by society. A spell works because the community believes in it and in the magician who performs it, which makes magical efficacy a social achievement rather than a psychological illusion. E. E. Evans-Pritchard, among the Azande of Sudan, showed that magic and witchcraft were not irrational beliefs but parts of a coherent, rational system: the Azande knew that termites eat granary posts, but they asked why the granary collapsed on that particular man at that particular moment, and witchcraft and magic supplied the answer. Stanley Tambiah later argued that magical rites are not failed attempts at causation at all but performative acts, like promises or blessings, whose meaning lies in what they do socially rather than in what they cause physically.

## Key Debates

The central debate has been whether magic is a distinct category at all. Anthropologists have long noted that the distinction between magic and religion reflects Christian theology, in which proper worship is religion and improper ritual is magic — a judgement that missionaries and colonial administrators applied to other peoples' practices. Some scholars therefore argue that "magic" is a Western folk category with no cross-cultural validity, and that it should be abandoned. Others defend it as a useful comparative term for instrumental ritual, provided it is defined ethnographically rather than theologically. A second debate concerns efficacy: if magic does not work in the physical sense, why does it persist? Answers range from psychological reassurance, to social functions such as conflict resolution and the enforcement of norms, to placebo-like effects on health. A third debate concerns the magician's status: in some societies magicians are respected specialists, in others feared outsiders, and the line between healer and sorcerer is often a matter of social judgement rather than technique.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Magic has not vanished with modernity. Anthropologists study magical practices in urban Africa, in Latin American cities, in European neo-pagan movements, in alternative healing, and in the global trade in ritual services. The language of magic also shapes modern life: advertising, branding and political ritual all use symbolic techniques to produce effects, and scholars have drawn the comparison explicitly. The study of magic has also transformed anthropology's understanding of rationality: rather than asking whether other peoples are rational, ethnographers now ask how different societies reason about causation, evidence and uncertainty — and how their own society does the same. Magic, once treated as the mark of the primitive, is now a lens on the symbolic dimensions of all human action.
