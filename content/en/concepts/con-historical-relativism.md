# Historical Relativism

Historical relativism is an epistemological position within the philosophy of history asserting that all historical knowledge is inescapably shaped by the temporal, cultural, and ideological position of the historian. Rejecting the claim that history can produce an immutable, universally valid, or disinterested representation of the past "as it actually happened," historical relativism maintains that historical interpretation is always anchored in the concerns and conceptual frameworks of the present.

## Foundations in Historicism (*Historismus*)

The intellectual roots of historical relativism lie in nineteenth-century German historicism (*Historismus*), developed by thinkers such as Wilhelm von Humboldt, Johann Gustav Droysen, and Wilhelm Dilthey. Historicism insisted on the radical specificity and uniqueness (*Individualität*) of every historical epoch, arguing that human institutions must be judged according to their own contemporary norms rather than abstract universal standards.

However, as Ernst Troeltsch argued in *Der Historismus und seine Probleme* (1922), this historicist recognition generated a profound crisis: if every epoch is historically relative, then the historian's own values and interpretations must also be historically conditioned. In Italy, Benedetto Croce gave this insight a famous formulation in *Teoria e storia della storiografia* (1917), asserting that "all true history is contemporary history" (*ogni vera storia è storia contemporanea*), because historical inquiry is invariably prompted by living, present interests.

## Pragmatism and Mannheim's Relationism

In the 1920s and 1930s, the relativist challenge developed along two major theoretical paths:

- **Karl Mannheim and Relationism**: In his essay "Historismus" (1924) and his magnum opus *Ideologie und Utopie* (1929), the sociologist Karl Mannheim sought to rescue historical relativism from total skepticism. He developed the notion of **relationism** (*Relationismus*) to argue that recognizing knowledge as relationally bound to a social and historical location (*Standortgebundenheit*) does not render it arbitrary. Rather, truth emerges through the critical, comparative synthesis of multiple perspectives.
- **American Pragmatist Relativism**: Historians Carl Becker ("Everyman His Own Historian," 1931) and Charles A. Beard ("Written History as an Act of Faith," 1934) argued that because historians can never access the past directly in its entirety, every historical narrative is a selective reconstruction serving the practical and intellectual needs of contemporary society.

## Methodological Distinctions and Contemporary Defenses

In the late twentieth century, the rise of post-structuralism and Hayden White's narrative tropology renewed fears that historical relativism would slide into epistemic nihilism, erasing the distinction between evidence-based history and politically motivated propaganda or negationism.

In response, historians such as Richard J. Evans (*In Defence of History*, 1997) clarified crucial distinctions:
- **Epistemological Relativism versus Moral Relativism**: Acknowledging that historical interpretations are socially conditioned (epistemological relativism) does not compel historians to accept that all ethical frameworks or historical actions are morally equivalent.
- **Interpretive Multiplicity versus Factual Nihilism**: While multiple legitimate interpretations of an event may coexist contingent upon the historian's questions and theoretical models, those interpretations remain strictly bounded by verified documentary evidence. Relativism acknowledges the plurality of perspectives, but professional historical method provides rigorous, intersubjective criteria to expose and discard demonstrably false claims.
