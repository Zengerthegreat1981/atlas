# Historical Relativism

Historical relativism is an epistemological position in the philosophy of history which holds that all historical knowledge is conditioned by the temporal, cultural and ideological position of its writer, and that the claim to "objective" and "final" knowledge of the past as it actually happened is a methodological illusion. The origin of this orientation is attributed to the German historicist school (*Historismus*) of the nineteenth century, but it was critically crystallised in the twentieth century by thinkers such as Benedetto Croce, who declared that "all true history is contemporary history" (*Ogni vera storia è storia contemporanea*), meaning that the historian always writes from the horizon of his present and in response to the problems of his present.

## The Origin in Ranke and Its Transformations

Leopold von Ranke, the pioneer of scientific historical writing in the nineteenth century, is the starting point of the debate. Ranke called for history to be written "as it actually happened" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*), that is, with the utmost degree of objective detachment. However, historians of the following generation, above all Karl Mannheim in his essay "Historicism" (*Historismus*, 1924), held that this ideal was practically impossible: every historian belongs to a social and epistemic "position" that determines the angle of his vision. Through the concept of the "sociology of knowledge" (*Wissenssoziologie*), Mannheim attempted to develop a "pluralist" relativism that avoids falling into complete epistemic nihilism, by comparing multiple viewpoints instead of abandoning the idea of historical truth altogether.

## Collingwood and Croce: History as Intellectual Reconstruction

Influenced by Croce, R. G. Collingwood developed the theory of "re-enactment" (re-enactment of thought), according to which understanding the past is not the retrieval of inert material facts, but an intellectual reconstruction of what historical agents were thinking and why they did what they did. This reconstruction necessarily passes through the categories of the present historian. This position made Collingwood and Croce two foundational pillars of every later debate on the limits of historical objectivity.

## Criticism and Limits

Historical relativism has been subject to two main criticisms:
- **Criticism of the slide towards epistemic nihilism**: If every historical account is merely a reflection of its writer's position, how can one distinguish a well-documented account from a fabricated or propagandistic one? Historians such as Geoffrey Elton and Richard Evans (*In Defence of History*, 1997) sought to defend the possibility of relative objectivity through standards of evidence verification, against the more extreme postmodern tendencies.
- **Criticism of confusing it with moral relativism**: The methodological literature warns against confusing epistemological relativism (knowledge is conditioned by context) with moral relativism (there is no standard by which to judge the actions of the past). The former is a methodological position accepted to varying degrees by most contemporary historians, while the latter is the subject of sharp disagreement.

## Connection to the Debate on Postmodernism

In the 1980s and 1990s the debate on relativism intensified with the emergence of the "linguistic turn" and the contributions of Hayden White, who approached historical texts as narrative structures resembling literature. This led some to draw extreme relativist conclusions that deny the possibility of distinguishing one historical account from another in terms of their realistic "truthfulness". This trend was countered by empirical historians who stressed that the existence of multiple interpretations does not negate the existence of real facts that can be verified (such as the occurrence of an event at a particular time), even if interpretations of its meaning differ.

## Its Importance for the Atlas

This concept is a key reference point in the epistemology of history. It intersects with discussions of anachronism (the error of projecting the categories of the present onto the past) and historical consciousness (awareness of the distance separating the observer from the object of study), and it forms a necessary background for understanding the methodological disagreements between positivist, hermeneutic and postmodernist schools in writing history.
