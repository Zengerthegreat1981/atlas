# Urbanism as a Way of Life (Wirth)

A thesis formulated by Louis Wirth that defines the city as a distinct social-psychological entity with its own way of life, not merely a large population aggregate.

## Content

In his central essay *Urbanism as a Way of Life* (1938), Wirth identified three structural variables that distinguish the city from traditional rural society: (1) the large **size** of the population aggregate, which makes an individual's personal acquaintance with most of those around him impossible; (2) the high **density** of housing and activity, which imposes spatial proximity without necessarily any social proximity; and (3) the great social, cultural, ethnic and occupational **heterogeneity** among the city's inhabitants. Wirth argued that this threefold structural composition produces, regardless of any particular city, a distinct social-psychological way of life characterized by: superficial, transient and impersonal social relations (the individual deals with others in their partial functional roles, not their whole personalities); weakened traditional primary bonds (the extended family, close-knit neighborhood); a great increase in social and geographic mobility; and the spread of phenomena called "social disorganization" (such as higher rates of crime, suicide and mental disorder compared with rural society).

## Historical Context

Louis Wirth (1897-1952), a German-Jewish-born immigrant who joined the Chicago school of urban ecology, wrote this thesis in the context of his direct observation of the enormous transformation that the city of Chicago itself had undergone through waves of European and internal migration in the early twentieth century. He drew on Georg Simmel's analyses of the mental life of the metropolis and on Ferdinand Tönnies's distinction between "community" and "society," and sought to formulate a precise scientific sociological definition of the city that goes beyond the abstract demographic definition based on population size alone.

## Later Alternative Theories

Alongside empirical critiques such as Herbert Gans's (discussed below), the sociologist Claude Fischer later put forward the "Subcultural Theory of Urbanism," which reversed Wirth's logic: Fischer held that urban size, density and heterogeneity do not dissolve social bonds but, on the contrary, make possible the formation of diverse and specialized subcultural groups (artistic, occupational, ethnic, sexual) that could not form at the same critical mass in small, homogeneous rural society, thereby producing a rich social diversity and vitality in place of the supposed disintegration.

## Impact and Critique

This thesis became a basic reference in urban sociology throughout the mid-twentieth century, and laid the theoretical foundation for a large number of later field studies of American urban life. It was later subjected to an influential methodological critique, most prominently from the urban sociologist Herbert Gans, who showed through direct field studies (such as his study of an Italian-American neighborhood in Boston) that many urban groups in fact maintain strong, cohesive primary bonds (kinship and ethnic neighborhood networks) within the large city itself, which weakens Wirth's claim that size, density and heterogeneity inevitably produce general social disintegration. The relationship is more complex and more varied by group and context than the original generalized thesis assumed.
