# Maqasid al-Shari'ah according to al-Shatibi

**The objectives of Sharia** (*maqasid al-shari'a*) are the overarching ends for which **al-Shatibi** held that the detailed rulings of Sharia were enacted, not for their own sake. He set them out in detail in his book ***al-Muwafaqat*** as the framework through which every particular ruling is to be understood.

## The Five Necessities

The essence of the idea is that Sharia, in al-Shatibi's view, aims to preserve five matters necessary for human life. The list below follows the order familiar from al-Ghazali (al-Shatibi himself lists lineage and property before intellect, and the ordering varies among writers). The classification had been formulated before al-Shatibi, but he organized it within a complete usuli theory:

1. **Preservation of religion.**
2. **Preservation of life.**
3. **Preservation of intellect.**
4. **Preservation of lineage (progeny).**
5. **Preservation of property.**

Al-Shatibi, following earlier usulists, divided the levels of these needs into three: the **necessities** (*daruriyyat*, that without which religious and worldly life cannot subsist), the **needs** (*hajiyyat*, that remove hardship and difficulty without being vital for life), and the **embellishments** (*tahsiniyyat*, that beautify the rulings and complete noble morals).

## The Interpretive Function of the Objectives

The practical effect of the objectives in al-Shatibi's thought is that they become a **standard for understanding the particular text**. If the apparent meaning of a particular text conflicts with an overarching objective established by induction from the totality of Sharia, al-Shatibi preferred to understand the text in the light of the overarching objective. This is what makes his theory, from the perspective of general legal theory, an attempt to establish a **purposive coherence** for the whole religious legal system, rather than an interpretation of a text in isolation from the others. Al-Shatibi also distinguished the purposes of the Lawgiver from the purposes of the person under obligation.

Contemporary scholars compare this framework with the concept of the **legislative purpose** (*telos*) in modern purposive interpretation of law; this comparison is an analytical gloss by modern scholars, not al-Shatibi's own. The decisive difference, however, is that al-Shatibi derives the objectives from texts that he believed to be of divine origin, not from the intention of a human legislator.

## Historical Origins and Intellectual Antecedents

The idea of the objectives did not arise from nothing. It was preceded by theoretical groundwork in al-Juwayni's *al-Burhan*, which distinguished necessities, needs and embellishments. Al-Ghazali then elaborated them in *al-Mustasfa* by linking them to the concept of **public interest** (*maslaha*), holding that what preserves the five necessities is a legitimate interest in Sharia, and accepting an interest not attested by a specific text only where it is necessary, universal and certain. Al-Shatibi, in the eighth century AH, gave this accumulation a complete, systematic usuli formulation. He established the **inductive certainty** of the objectives (that they are derived by examining the totality of the texts, not from a single text) as a tool for answering the objection that these objectives are merely speculative inferences with no authority over the certain particular text.

## Practical Application and Examples

The effect of the theory of objectives appears in practice in juristic maxims derived from it, such as "necessities permit prohibited things" (an application of prioritizing the preservation of life), and "averting harms takes precedence over securing benefits." Some contemporary jurists have invoked the objectives in discussing questions such as medical necessity or hardship in financial dealings. These applications are debated, and such arguments are not themselves part of al-Shatibi's formulation. In the twentieth century the Tunisian scholar Muhammad al-Tahir Ibn Ashur (d. 1973) wrote a notable work on the objectives, *Maqasid al-Shari'a al-Islamiyya*.

## Contemporary Maqasid Scholarship

Modern writers have extended the theory well beyond its classical form. Ibn Ashur treated the study of objectives as deserving a method of its own, distinct in emphasis from conventional usul al-fiqh. He grounded it in the innate human disposition (*fitra*) and gave weight to social aims such as tolerance, equality, freedom and the orderly life of the community, alongside the protection of individuals.

Taha Jabir al-Alwani (1935-2016), an Iraqi scholar associated with the International Institute of Islamic Thought, argued that a small set of Qur'anic higher values, namely divine unity (*tawhid*), purification (*tazkiya*) and the building of civilization (*'umran*), should govern the reading of particular rulings.

Jasser Auda, in a work published in 2008, applied systems theory to the subject. He argued that Islamic legal reasoning should be understood as an open, multidimensional and purpose-oriented system, criticized the classical hierarchy for its narrow and individual focus, and proposed reading the old language of preservation in terms of human development and rights. Critics of expansive purposive readings caution that broadly worded objectives may be used to set aside specific texts.
