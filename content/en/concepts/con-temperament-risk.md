# Temperament & Risk Factors

## Definition

In developmental psychology, temperament refers to early individual differences that are relatively stable in emotional response, activity, and self-regulation. These differences have a biological basis and are shaped by experience. In psychiatry, temperament is studied as a risk or protective factor for later disorders, not as a disorder in itself. This entry addresses the link between childhood temperament and psychiatric disorder in adulthood.

## Temperament in Classical Arab Medicine

The word *mizaj* (temperament) carries an old medical meaning in Arabic. Greco-Arab medicine rested on the theory of the four humors (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile), and held that each person has a dominant temperament that determines their predisposition to disease and their character. Ibn Sina set out, at the beginning of *al-Qanun*, a section on the types of temperament and their relation to morals and actions. Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 873 CE), through his translations and summaries of Galen, was a principal transmitter of this view into Arabic (see the entries on Ibn Sina and Hunayn ibn Ishaq). In their tradition, the melancholic temperament was linked to a predisposition to melancholia. The ancient concept is not equivalent to modern temperament, since it was a qualitative bodily mixture. Nevertheless, it identified early on the idea of a stable predisposition that is influenced by environment, age, and diet.

## Modern Foundations

Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess began the New York Longitudinal Study in 1956. They presented their results in *Behavioral Individuality in Early Childhood* (New York University Press, 1963) and then in *Temperament and Development* (Brunner/Mazel, 1977). They distinguished the "easy," "difficult," and "slow-to-warm-up" child temperaments, and the idea of "goodness of fit" between the child and their environment. Kagan and colleagues described "behavioral inhibition" (*Science*, 1988). Hirshfeld and colleagues found in the *Journal of the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry* in 1992 that children who were stably inhibited on this trait were more prone to anxiety disorders (see the entry on childhood anxiety). Caspi and Silva showed in *Child Development* in 1995 that temperament at age three predicts traits in young adulthood. Caspi, Moffitt, and colleagues showed in *Archives of General Psychiatry* in 1996 that observations at age three predict psychiatric disorders at age twenty-one.

## Temperament Models in Psychiatry

In 1987, Cloninger, in *Archives of General Psychiatry*, set out a model that links temperament dimensions (novelty seeking, harm avoidance, and reward dependence; persistence was later treated as a fourth dimension) to proposed neurotransmitter systems and uses them to describe personality variants. These links are hypotheses and have been only partly supported. Hagop Akiskal and colleagues introduced the concept of "affective temperaments" (depressive, hyperthymic, cyclothymic, irritable, and anxious). They built on these categories a self-rated scale, TEMPS-A, whose validation studies appeared in the *Journal of Affective Disorders* in the early 2000s. Some of these patterns are linked to the bipolar spectrum (see the entry on the bipolar spectrum). This work extends the descriptions of temperament patterns by Kraepelin and Kretschmer.

## Temperament and Risk Factors

Temperament does not act alone. Its interaction with upbringing, trauma, and attachment determines the trajectory (see the entries on attachment theory and developmental pathways to psychiatric illness). Behavioral inhibition combined with excessive parental protection favors chronic avoidance, while impulsivity in a harsh environment favors conduct disorder. Temperament is discussed in prevention research, for example in parenting approaches adapted to a sensitive or emotionally intense child. This entry is educational, not clinical advice.

## Limits

Individual prediction is weak. Most inhibited children do not develop pathological anxiety, and part of temperament changes with age. Measures and labels vary across models, and it is difficult to separate temperament from early symptoms. Some scales were designed on Western samples, and the concept of the "difficult child" cannot easily be generalized across cultures that value calm or obedience differently.
