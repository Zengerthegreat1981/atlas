# Gharar (Uncertainty in Contracts)

**Gharar** is a jurisprudential term referring to the ignorance or essential uncertainty surrounding one of the pillars of a contract or its object, such that the outcome or consequence of the contract is unknown to one or both parties to a degree that leads to a probable dispute or excessive unfairness. The prohibition of gharar in financial contracts, together with the prohibition of *riba*, is among the most important general legal constraints governing contract theory in Islamic jurisprudence, and a primary source of many rules on the invalidity of contracts in the jurisprudence of transactions.

## Textual and Logical Basis for the Prohibition

The prohibition of gharar rests above all on a prophetic report, transmitted by Muslim, that forbade the sale of the pebble (*bay' al-hasat*) and the sale involving gharar (*bay' al-gharar*), together with reports forbidding pre-Islamic forms of sale such as *munabadha* and *mulamasa* (sales concluded by throwing or touching the goods, without inspection). Logically, it rests on the principle that financial contracts should be based on genuine mutual consent founded on sufficient knowledge of the object of the contract and its effects, so that the contract does not turn into a form of gambling or wagering that depends on pure chance rather than on fair economic exchange based on relatively equal information between the parties.

## Forms of Gharar in Classical Jurisprudence

Jurists discussed several forms of gharar, among them: ignorance of the existence of the object of the contract (such as selling what has not yet been created); ignorance of its essential attributes (such as selling something without inspecting it or describing it sufficiently); ignorance of the possibility of delivery (such as selling a bird in the air or fish in the water before catching it); and temporal gharar (such as contracts linked to an unknown term). Nevertheless, jurists distinguished between minor gharar that is customarily excused (since it cannot be entirely avoided in everyday transactions) and excessive gharar that invalidates the contract. Jurists commonly speak of excessive, moderate and minor gharar, and the schools differ in how they draw the lines. The Maliki school, for example, is generally regarded as more tolerant of a degree of uncertainty where needed by custom, while the Shafi'i school is generally stricter. Some later jurists, notably Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Qayyim, tied the prohibition closely to the danger of dispute and of gambling-like risk, which gives the contemporary jurist interpretive flexibility in applying this principle to newly arising transactions.

## Its Impact on Contemporary Financial Transactions

The principle of prohibiting gharar is one of the most important standards by which the compatibility of contemporary Islamic financing and insurance instruments with classical jurisprudence is evaluated. Conventional commercial insurance, for example, raises jurisprudential problems from the standpoint of gharar (the uncertainty of whether the insured party will receive compensation or not). Many contemporary jurists and Islamic finance bodies therefore favor alternatives such as cooperative insurance (*ta'awuni*, often called *takaful*), which attempts to avoid the problem by structuring the relationship as mutual contribution and donation rather than exchange. This position is discussed by scholars such as Wahba al-Zuhayli, and the wider field is surveyed in Vogel and Hayes's *Islamic Law and Finance*. Scholars still disagree about whether and how far commercial insurance is permissible.

## Comparison with Concepts of Positive Law

The principle of gharar is sometimes compared with modern legal concepts such as free and informed consent and the duty of disclosure in contracts. Both systems share a concern to ensure equal information between the parties to a contract, although the technical tools used to achieve this goal differ between classical Islamic jurisprudence (the nullity of the contract entirely in cases of excessive gharar) and modern positive law (such as the theory of defects of consent and partial contractual disclosure obligations).
