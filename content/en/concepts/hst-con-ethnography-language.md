# Ethnography of Language and Communication

In ancient historiography, language, dialect, translation, and cross-cultural communication formed crucial components of ethnographic description and diplomatic narrative. Classical Greek and Roman authors operated within environments shaped by profound linguistic chauvinism—most visibly reflected in the Greek designation *barbaros*, originally an onomatopoeic characterization of unintelligible foreign speech ("bar-bar"). Nevertheless, ancient historians frequently examined the origins of human language, the structural barriers to cross-cultural exchange, the institutional role of interpreters, and the cultural politics of bilingualism.

## Linguistic Inquiry in Herodotus

Despite the general Greek reluctance to master foreign languages, Herodotus displayed an extraordinary curiosity regarding linguistic diversity and the relationship between names, concepts, and cultural identity. In the prologue to his Egyptian *logos* (*Histories* 2.2), Herodotus recounted the experiment conducted by Pharaoh Psammetichus I to determine the world's original language. Psammetichus isolated two newborn infants in the care of a solitary shepherd, forbidding him from uttering a word. When the infants eventually approached the shepherd crying *bekos*, inquiry revealed this to be the Phrygian word for bread, leading the Egyptians to concede that the Phrygians were more ancient than themselves.

Throughout the *Histories*, Herodotus documented foreign vocabulary, etymologies, and the mechanisms of inter-ethnic communication. He explained Scythian compound words (such as *Arimaspoi*, from *arima* [one] and *spou* [eye]), noted that the Sauromatians spoke a corrupted form of Scythian because their Amazonian ancestors never mastered the language properly (4.117), and recorded non-verbal exchange. In Book 4 (ch. 196), Herodotus preserved the famous description of the "silent trade" practiced by Carthaginian merchants on the Atlantic coast of Africa, where goods and gold were deposited on the shore and exchanged without direct speech, the traders signalling their presence by smoke.

Furthermore, Herodotus recognized the institutional role of professional interpreters (*hermeneis*). In Book 2 (ch. 154), he documented how Psammetichus I established a hereditary caste of bilingual Egyptian interpreters by entrusting Greek mercenaries and merchants with Egyptian boys to be taught Greek, the ancestors of the class of Egyptian interpreters.

## Diplomacy, Interpreters, and Hellenistic Koine

In the classical and Hellenistic periods, historical narratives increasingly reflected the practical complexities of multilingual diplomacy. In the *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Thucydides recorded diplomatic encounters between Greek poleis and the Persian satraps of Asia Minor that necessitated official translators, notably when an Athenian squadron intercepted an envoy bearing a letter written in Aramaic (the administrative script of the Achaemenid Empire) from the Persian king to Sparta (4.50). In the *Anabasis*, Xenophon constantly relied on bilingual intermediaries to negotiate safe passage, gather intelligence, and secure provisions among the diverse tribes of Anatolia and the Persian plateau.

The conquests of Alexander the Great and the creation of Hellenistic successor kingdoms profoundly transformed the Mediterranean linguistic landscape. The Greek *koine* ("common tongue") became the trans-regional language of imperial bureaucracy, royal courts, commerce, and intellectual life from Egypt to Bactria. Hellenistic historians like Polybius recognized that political analysis required linguistic precision, particularly when translating Latin legal terminology and archaic Roman treaties into Greek conceptual categories.

## Roman Historiography and Elite Bilingualism

In Roman historical writing, language was closely bound to imperial identity and provincial administration. The Roman senatorial elite practiced a cultivated bilingualism, utilizing Latin for domestic legislation, judicial decrees, and official military commands, while using Greek for diplomatic correspondence and philosophical dialogue.

In *De Bello Gallico*, Julius Caesar highlighted the critical operational role of trusted interpreters during his campaigns. When Caesar held confidential conferences with Gaulish chieftains like Diviciacus of the Aedui, he dismissed ordinary military interpreters and relied on Gaius Valerius Procillus, a prominent bilingual Gaulish aristocrat endowed with Roman citizenship (1.19). Caesar also noted linguistic divisions across Gaul, recording in his famous opening chapter that the Belgae, Aquitani, and Celts differed from one another in language, customs, and laws.

In imperial historiography, Tacitus explored the ideological role of language in imperial assimilation. In the *Agricola* (ch. 21), Tacitus observed that his father-in-law encouraged the sons of British chieftains to master the Latin language and Roman rhetoric as part of an intentional civilizing policy, remarking that the unwary called this "civilization" (*humanitas*) when it was part of their servitude.
