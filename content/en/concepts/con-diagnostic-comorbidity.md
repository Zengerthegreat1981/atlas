# Diagnostic Comorbidity

## Definition

**Comorbidity** (also translated as "co-occurrence" or "diagnostic overlap") in psychiatry refers to a patient meeting the criteria for **more than one psychiatric diagnosis** at the same time, for example being diagnosed with both major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder. The term was introduced into general medicine by Alvan Feinstein in 1970, to describe an additional clinical entity coexisting with the index disease. Its application in psychiatry has raised deep methodological debate about its nature.

## Historical Context

Interest in comorbidity grew markedly after **DSM-III** (1980), which adopted a descriptive, criteria-based approach with sharply defined categories and a multiaxial system. Many "hierarchical exclusion rules" that had previously prevented the diagnosis of more than one disorder were reduced. Those rules barred diagnosing a second disorder when the first might explain its symptoms. This shift, along with explicit and separate diagnostic criteria for each disorder, led to a significant rise in reported rates of comorbidity in clinical and epidemiological studies from the 1980s onward.

## What Surveys Showed

Once hierarchical exclusions were relaxed, community surveys found that meeting criteria for more than one disorder was common, especially among mood, anxiety, and substance-use disorders. The Epidemiologic Catchment Area program (Boyd and colleagues, 1984) and later the National Comorbidity Survey and its replication (Kessler and colleagues) made that pattern visible. Recent rates for any named pair of disorders still depend on the interview, the time window (current, twelve-month, or lifetime), and whether subthreshold symptoms are counted. A single percentage therefore does not travel well from one study to another.

## Key Dimensions

- **The problem of being a methodological "artifact"**: Prominent critics (for example Mario Maj in the *British Journal of Psychiatry*, 2005) argue that much of what is called "comorbidity" is not a genuine coexistence of two separate illnesses. Rather, it is a by-product of the way the classification systems themselves (DSM and ICD) are built, dividing what may be a single pathological process into multiple overlapping diagnostic categories.
- **Overlap between depression and anxiety**: One of the most studied examples is the broad overlap between mood disorders and anxiety disorders, where many patients meet the criteria for both. This has led some researchers to propose shared underlying dimensions, such as negative affectivity (as in the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology, HiTOP, proposed by Kotov and colleagues in 2017), that explain this overlap better than assuming two independent illnesses.
- **The transdiagnostic approach**: In response to the comorbidity problem, a "transdiagnostic" research and treatment approach has developed over the past two decades. It focuses on mechanisms and dimensions shared across disorders, such as emotion regulation or repetitive negative thinking, instead of treating each DSM diagnosis as a completely separate disease unit.
- **Clinical impact**: In practice, comorbidity is associated with greater treatment complexity, more difficult diagnosis, and a worse prognosis. This makes it an important clinical criterion regardless of the theoretical debate about its nature.
