# Praxis: Reflective Action That Transforms the World

The Greek term *praxis* (action) was distinguished by Aristotle from *theoria* (contemplation) and *poiesis* (production): praxis is action whose end lies in the acting itself, especially ethical and political action (*Nicomachean Ethics* VI). In Marxist philosophy (Marx, *Theses on Feuerbach*, written 1845, first published 1888), praxis becomes "sensuous human activity": conscious, socially situated practice through which people change both their circumstances and themselves. The eleventh thesis states that philosophers have only interpreted the world while the point is to change it.

## The Philosophical Meaning

Marx used praxis to **criticise the idealist philosophers** (Hegel, Feuerbach) who were content with contemplation: philosophers regard human beings as "consciences" or "ideas," but in reality, as the sixth thesis on Feuerbach puts it, the human essence is "the ensemble of the social relations." In the third thesis Marx argues that circumstances are changed by people while the educators themselves must be educated, so that change of circumstances and of human activity coincide as "revolutionary practice." Marx developed these ideas further with Engels in *The German Ideology* (written 1845-46). Hannah Arendt, in *The Human Condition* (1958), distinguished **action** (the sphere of speech and deeds among equals, close to Aristotle's praxis) from **labour** (biological necessity) and **work** (fabrication of durable things), and criticized Marx for collapsing action into labour and production, so the two thinkers' uses of "praxis" differ substantially.

## The Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

In liberation psychology, as developed by Ignacio Martín-Baró (a Salvadoran Jesuit psychologist killed in 1989; see *Writings for a Liberation Psychology*, 1994) and others in Latin America, praxis is central: psychology should be oriented toward changing oppressive social conditions and not only describing individuals. This tradition intersects with the critical pedagogy of Paulo Freire in his book *Pedagogy of the Oppressed*, which makes "praxis" (action and reflection together) the condition for the liberation of consciousness from oppression. Antonio Gramsci, in the *Prison Notebooks* (written 1929-35), used "philosophy of praxis" as a name for Marxism and developed the concept of cultural hegemony, linking practice to the struggle against ideological domination. In community psychology, related ideas appear in **action research** (Kurt Lewin, 1940s) and in work on **empowerment** (Julian Rappaport, 1980s), though these developed separately from Marxism. Writers in decolonial and liberation psychology (for example Geraldine Moane) draw on praxis to argue that psychological work should address social conditions.

## The Distinction from Neighbouring Concepts

Praxis differs from **theoretical contemplation** (*theoria*), because it involves practical engagement and change. In Aristotle it differs from **production** (*poiesis*), which aims at an external product, whereas in Marx productive labour itself is a central form of praxis. It is also not identical with **activism** in general: in the Marxist and Freirean traditions it names the unity of reflection and action, in which theory is tested and revised by practice. Critics, including postmodern thinkers such as Jean-François Lyotard (*The Postmodern Condition*, 1979), are sceptical of the grand emancipatory narratives with which Marxist praxis is often linked.
