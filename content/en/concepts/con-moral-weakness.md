# Moral Weakness

## Meaning and Definition

Moral weakness, known philosophically as *akrasia* ("lack of self-control," from the Greek), is the state in which a person acts contrary to what he or she believes to be best or most right morally, despite knowing this and apparently being able to do the right act. The deep philosophical problem here is: how can a rational person knowingly do what is wrong? Does this not contradict the nature of reason, which is supposed to direct the will toward what it recognizes as good?

## Socrates and the Denial of Moral Weakness

As Plato reports in the dialogue *Protagoras*, Socrates denied the possibility of moral weakness altogether. He held that whoever does wrong does so out of genuine ignorance of its wrongness, or a mistaken assessment of the balance of pleasure and pain. No one does wrong while knowing with certainty that it is pure wrong. In other words, "no one errs willingly".

## Aristotle's Critique and His Affirmation of the Phenomenon

Aristotle disagreed with his teacher's teacher in the *Nicomachean Ethics* (Book VII), accepting the reality of the phenomenon, and distinguished between two types: "impetuous akrasia," in which a person acts out of an overwhelming appetite before completing his rational deliberation; and "weak akrasia," in which a person deliberates and then yields to appetite despite knowing better. Aristotle explained the phenomenon by saying that the person's knowledge at the moment of action may be "latent" knowledge that is not actually present in the mind, like the drunkard who recites lines of poetry without actually bringing them to mind.

## Islamic Philosophers: Between Reason and Appetite

Muslim philosophers addressed related questions through their psychology and ethics, largely in dialogue with Aristotle and Plato. Ibn Rushd, who wrote commentaries on the *De Anima* and on the *Nicomachean Ethics*, worked within a tradition in which the appetitive and irascible faculties can overcome the rational faculty when its authority weakens. Ibn Miskawayh, in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, traced this weakness to a failure in regulating the three psychological faculties: whoever has not had his appetitive faculty disciplined since childhood remains prone to yielding to it despite knowing what is right. Al-Ghazali linked the phenomenon to weakness in spiritual striving (*mujahada*), holding that knowledge alone is not enough unless it is joined with will and the practical training of the soul. This is sometimes compared to Aristotle's distinction between latent and exercised knowledge.

## Contemporary Significance

Contemporary philosophers, such as Donald Davidson in his well-known essay "How Is Weakness of the Will Possible?", take up this question, and the problem is often linked to phenomena such as addiction. The addict knows with certainty the harm of substance use but repeatedly submits to it, which keeps the discussion of akrasia highly relevant in clinical psychology and the philosophy of voluntary action.
