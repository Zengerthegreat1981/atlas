# Dhikr & Remembrance

In Sufi terminology, *dhikr* is the remembrance of God with the heart and the tongue. Its root is Qur'anic: "So remember Me; I will remember you" (2:152), "Verily, in the remembrance of God do hearts find rest" (13:28), and "And remember your Lord much" (3:41). On this root the Sufis built a practice that ranges from the repetition of the formula of divine unity and the single divine Name, to silent meditation.

## Dhikr Among the Early Authorities

Abu al-Qasim al-Qushayri (d. 465 AH/1072 CE), in the chapter on dhikr in *al-Risala al-Qushayriyya* (completed 437–438 AH / 1045–1046 CE), distinguishes remembrance by the tongue from remembrance by the heart. On his account the real remembrancer is the one in whose heart nothing remains alongside God. The saying that dhikr is the patent of sainthood (*manshur al-wilaya*) — whoever is given it is joined, and whoever is refused it is dismissed — appears in that chapter, where al-Qushayri reports it from his teacher Abu 'Ali al-Daqqaq. Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 751 AH/1350 CE) later wrote on dhikr in *al-Wabil al-sayyib*. Abu Nasr al-Sarraj (d. 378 AH/988 CE), in *Kitab al-Luma' fi al-tasawwuf*, records the states that early authorities attached to the practice. Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH/1111 CE) devoted Book 9 of the first quarter of *Ihya' 'ulum al-din*, "Invocations and Supplications" (*Kitab al-adhkar wa-l-da'awat*), to the etiquette of dhikr and of litanies. Kojiro Nakamura translated that book as *Invocations and Supplications* (1990).

## Muraqaba (Watchfulness)

*Muraqaba* in this context is the constant awareness of the servant that God is watching him. Al-Qushayri discusses it in the *Risala* as a station of the spiritual path, and it is commonly grounded in the hadith of Gabriel: "that you worship God as if you see Him, and if you do not see Him, He sees you." In the methods of self-examination of al-Harith al-Muhasibi (d. 243 AH/857 CE), *muraqaba* is combined with the examination of the soul (*muhasaba*).

## Dhikr in the Orders

The Sufi orders made dhikr a taught discipline. The Qadiriyya and the Shadhiliyya recite formulas including "There is no god but God" and the single Name "Allah". In the Shadhili line, Ibn 'Ata' Allah al-Iskandari (d. 709 AH/1309 CE) wrote *Miftah al-falah wa-misbah al-arwah* (The Key to Salvation and the Lamp of Spirits), which Mary Ann Koury Danner translated as *The Key to Salvation* (1996). He presents dhikr as a safeguard against heedlessness. Some orders practise collective dhikr in the *hadra*, with chanting and movement. The Mawlawiyya developed the *sama'* ceremony. The Naqshbandiyya are known for silent dhikr (*dhikr khafi*).

## The Jurisprudential Debate

Jurists treated dhikr as a basic act of worship and disagreed about its formulas and its public form. Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728 AH/1328 CE), in materials gathered in *Majmu' al-fatawa*, criticized gatherings that combined a prescribed formula with dancing and *sama'*. Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti (d. 911 AH/1505 CE) defended audible group dhikr in *Natijat al-fikr fi al-jahr bi-l-dhikr* (The Result of Reflection on Reciting Dhikr Aloud). Louis Gardet's article "Dhikr" in the second edition of the *Encyclopaedia of Islam* sorts the main types of the practice.

## Limits of Use

Dhikr cannot be confined to the Sufis, since it is a general act of worship in the transmitted invocations after prayer. Moreover, the relation of collective dhikr to the ecstatic state (*hal wajdi*) is a matter of debate. Sufi authors such as al-Qushayri warned against feigned ecstasy, and distinguished between *wajd* (ecstasy), *tawajjud* (feigned ecstasy) and *wujud* (finding).
