# Face-Threatening Acts & Mitigation Strategies

A systematic classification formulated by Brown and Levinson of five graduated strategies from which a speaker chooses to perform a speech act that threatens the addressee's face (such as a request, criticism or refusal), varying in the degree to which they mitigate the threat.

## The five-step gradient

The gradient begins with "bald on-record" (direct speech without any mitigation, such as saying "Close the door!" with no softening at all). Next comes "positive politeness" (displaying friendliness and closeness to lessen the threat, such as "My friend, would you close the door?"). Then comes "negative politeness" (respecting the addressee's independence through formal mitigating forms, such as "Sorry to bother you, could you possibly close the door, please?"). Then comes "off-record" performance (hinting without explicitly stating the request, such as "It's cold in here," implying that the door should be closed). Finally there is the complete "refraining" from performing the threatening act at all.

## Calculating the degree of threat

The model assumes that a speaker's choice of one of these strategies depends on a calculation (conscious or implicit) of three factors: the social distance between speaker and addressee, the difference in relative power or status between them, and the degree of "weight" of the act itself in the relevant culture (for example, asking for a cigarette is a lighter imposition than asking for a large financial loan). The higher the sum of these factors, the more the speaker tends toward strategies that are more mitigating on the scale of the gradient.

## Positive and negative face

The classification rests on the hypothesis of "face" borrowed from Erving Goffman. **Positive face** is the individual's desire to be liked, appreciated and to have their social belonging shared, while **negative face** is their desire to preserve their personal autonomy and not to have constraints imposed on them. Strategies of "positive politeness" aim to protect positive face (by displaying solidarity and friendliness), while strategies of "negative politeness" aim to protect negative face (by softening, apologizing and leaving room for refusal). Many speech acts (such as requests, criticisms, or even compliments) threaten one or both kinds of face to varying degrees, which makes politeness a compensatory mechanism that is permanently present in everyday social interaction.

## Applied examples from Arabic

Everyday Arabic discourse shows a rich use of mitigation strategies. These include plural honorific forms (*ḥaḍratukum* instead of *anta*, the singular "you"), apologetic introductory expressions (*law samaḥta*, "if you would permit"; *ma'dhiratan 'ala al-izʿaj*, "pardon the disturbance"), and softening techniques that use a question form instead of a direct command (*alā tawaddu an...?*, "would you not like to...?", instead of "do such-and-such"). All of these are examples of negative politeness. By contrast, using kinship terms of affection with strangers (such as *ya akhi*, "my brother," or *ya ammi*, "my uncle") serves as a device of positive politeness that brings the presumed social distance closer.

## Legacy

This classification provided a precise analytical tool, widely applied in the study of social interaction across languages and cultures, despite later criticism (such as Matsumoto's critique) of its claim to universality across cultures that do not adopt the individualistic concept of the self that the model implicitly assumes.
