# Gift

The gift is one of anthropology's founding concepts. A gift is a voluntary transfer of goods, services, or honours that creates and sustains social bonds — and, crucially, it is never free. Where a market transaction ends when payment is made, a gift opens an obligation: the recipient is expected to return something, later and in a different form, and the relationship endures in the interval between giving and returning. Anthropologists study the gift because it reveals how societies create solidarity, hierarchy, and obligation without money, contracts, or states. From the kula exchange of the Trobriand Islands to birthday presents, charitable donations, and the sharing of food among kin, the logic of the gift shapes much of everyday social life.

## Definition

The classic definition comes from Marcel Mauss, whose essay *The Gift* argued that in many societies, giving, receiving, and repaying are three inseparable obligations. To refuse a gift is an insult; to fail to repay it is a loss of honour; to give generously is to win prestige. Mauss proposed that the gift carries something of the giver — the Maori term *hau*, the spirit of the thing given, compels its return. Anthropologists distinguish the gift from other transfers: unlike a commodity, it is not sold for a price; unlike tribute or tax, it is not extracted by force; unlike sharing, which often responds to immediate need, it is a deliberate act that creates a debt. Marshall Sahlins placed these forms on a spectrum of reciprocity, from the generalised reciprocity of sharing among close kin, through the balanced reciprocity of the gift, to the negative reciprocity of theft and haggling with strangers.

## Origins and Development

Mauss built his theory on ethnographies of the early twentieth century, above all Bronisław Malinowski's account of the kula ring, in which men of the Trobriand Islands circulated shell necklaces and armbands across a vast network of islands, gaining fame rather than wealth. In the Pacific Northwest, the potlatch ceremonies of the Kwakwaka'wakw and other peoples involved the spectacular distribution — and sometimes destruction — of wealth, through which chiefs asserted rank and shamed rivals. Later anthropologists extended the analysis. Annette Weiner showed that some possessions are "inalienable": sacred objects, heirlooms, and ancestral relics that must be kept rather than given, and whose retention grounds identity and authority. Maurice Godelier argued that the gift always contains an element of the sacred and that the keeping of inalienable things is as socially important as their circulation. In modern societies, the gift has not disappeared: Christmas presents, wedding gifts, tips, and charitable donations all follow gift logic, and economists have noted with puzzlement that people spend considerable effort choosing gifts that recipients might value less than cash — evidence that the social message matters more than the material value.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

Several debates continue to shape the anthropology of the gift. First, is the gift truly voluntary? Mauss's own title in French, *Essai sur le don*, stresses that the gift is simultaneously free and obligatory — a "voluntary" act underwritten by social compulsion. Second, what motivates return? Some explanations invoke moral obligation and honour; others, following Pierre Bourdieu, treat gift exchange as a strategy of power, in which the timing of the return converts economic capital into symbolic capital. Third, feminist anthropology has challenged the male-centred focus of the classic accounts. Weiner showed that in the Trobriands, women's wealth — bundles of banana leaves and skirts exchanged at mortuary ceremonies — was as central to the social order as men's kula valuables, yet had been ignored by Malinowski. Fourth, anthropologists debate the boundary between gift and commodity. In practice the two are entangled: a gift can be sold, a commodity can be given, and in many societies the same object moves between regimes. Finally, there is the question of the "pure gift" — a gift with no expectation of return, such as an anonymous donation. Some argue this is a modern, often religious ideal; others argue that even anonymous giving brings a return in the form of moral standing or spiritual merit.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The gift remains a powerful lens on contemporary life. The global rise of charitable giving, crowdfunding, and open-source software — in which people contribute labour and knowledge without direct payment — shows gift logic operating at the heart of the digital economy. Anthropologists have used the gift to critique market ideology: where economics assumes self-interested exchange between strangers, the gift shows that exchange can be a way of making persons and relationships, not merely of allocating goods. The concept also illuminates welfare and care, where the unpaid labour of kinship — cooking, nursing, child-rearing — is a vast, uncounted gift economy, disproportionately borne by women. Critics caution against romanticising the gift: gift exchange can be coercive, creating debts that bind the poor to patrons, and ceremonial generosity can entrench hierarchy as surely as it builds solidarity. The enduring contribution of the anthropology of the gift is to show that no transfer is purely material, and that every act of giving is also an act of social making.
