# Leadership

Leadership is the exercise of influence that coordinates collective action — persuading, organising, deciding, and representing a group — and it is a universal feature of human social life. Yet leadership takes radically different forms across societies: the persuasive oratory of a Melanesian big-man, the hereditary authority of a chief, the charisma of a prophet, the bureaucratic command of a state official. Anthropologists study leadership not as a property of individuals but as a relationship: leaders lead only insofar as followers accept, support, and sometimes resist their direction.

## Definition

Anthropological approaches to leadership begin by separating leadership from domination. A leader may have no power to coerce: in many small-scale societies, a person who tries to command others is mocked, ignored, or deserted. Leadership in these settings rests on generosity, skill, eloquence, and the ability to build consensus. Marshall Sahlins's classic essay "Poor Man, Rich Man, Big-Man, Chief" (1963) distinguished two political types: the Melanesian big-man, whose influence is built up through personal achievement and the distribution of wealth, and the Polynesian chief, whose authority is tied to inherited office. The big-man must constantly prove himself; the chief inherits a position, but must still maintain it.

Max Weber's typology of legitimate domination — traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal authority — remains influential in anthropology. Traditional authority rests on custom and heredity; charismatic authority on the extraordinary qualities of an individual; rational-legal authority on rules and offices. Anthropologists note that these types are rarely pure: a chief may claim both ancestral legitimacy and personal charisma, and a modern politician may combine bureaucratic office with charismatic appeal.

## Origins and Development

The study of leadership in anthropology grew out of early ethnography of political organisation. In the nineteenth century, evolutionary schemes placed "primitive" societies at the bottom of a ladder and assumed they had little leadership at all. Fieldwork in the twentieth century overturned this picture, documenting elaborate systems of leadership without states: the segmentary lineages of the Nuer, the age-grade systems of East Africa, the big-men of Melanesia, and the headmen of Amazonian villages.

Pierre Clastres's *Society Against the State* (1974) offered a radical reinterpretation. Studying the Indigenous peoples of South America, Clastres argued that their societies were not failed states but societies that actively prevented the concentration of power. Chiefs were expected to be generous and eloquent, but they were forbidden to command, and a chief who tried to impose his will would be abandoned. Leadership, in this view, could be a mechanism for preventing domination rather than for exercising it.

The study of leadership expanded in the later twentieth century to include new domains: religious movements, anti-colonial struggles, trade unions, development projects, and corporations. Anthropologists showed that leadership is always culturally shaped — that what counts as a good leader, how leaders are chosen, and how they may be removed vary enormously.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One central debate concerns the relationship between leadership and inequality. Does leadership inevitably produce hierarchy, or can it exist without it? The big-man system suggests a paradox: the big-man gains influence by giving wealth away, but the more he gives, the more followers and obligations he accumulates, and the more his influence resembles domination. Sahlins argued that this dynamic limits the big-man's power: followers' demands for generosity can exhaust him, and the system rarely yields the durable inherited rank found in Polynesian chiefdoms.

A second debate concerns gender. Leadership in anthropology was long studied as a male domain, and women's leadership was overlooked or dismissed. More recent research has documented women's leadership in many forms: as queen mothers, as ritual specialists, as organisers of women's associations, and as leaders of social movements. This work has shown that the public, male face of leadership often depends on women's work behind the scenes, and that definitions of leadership need to be broadened to include influence exercised outside formal office.

A third dimension is the role of rhetoric and performance. Leaders persuade through speech, ceremony, and the management of symbols. A leader's authority may depend on the ability to tell a compelling story about the group's past and future, to mediate disputes with words rather than force, and to embody the group's values in public. The study of leadership is therefore also a study of language, ritual, and the politics of representation.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Leadership is a central concern of contemporary anthropology, from the study of populist politicians to the analysis of humanitarian organisations, social movements, and corporate cultures. Anthropologists bring a distinctive perspective: they ask not only what leaders do, but how leadership is understood, legitimated, and contested in different cultural settings. The charisma of a populist leader, the authority of a religious figure, and the influence of a community organiser all draw on culturally specific ideas about what a leader should be.

Critics note that the anthropology of leadership has often focused on small-scale societies, and that its concepts need rethinking for a world of states, corporations, and global institutions. Yet the core insight remains: leadership is a relationship, not a possession. It depends on followers, on shared meanings, and on the constant negotiation of authority. In a world where leaders promise certainty and followers demand accountability, that insight is more relevant than ever.
