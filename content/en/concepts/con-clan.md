# Clan

A clan is a unilineal descent group — a set of people who trace their descent from a common ancestor through one line only, either the father's line (patrilineal) or the mother's (matrilineal) — and who recognise one another as kin even though the founding ancestor is often mythical and the exact genealogical links cannot be traced. Unlike a lineage, whose members can demonstrate each step of descent from a known ancestor, a clan's unity rests on a shared name, a totem or emblem, exogamy rules, and a belief in common descent. Clans are typically corporate groups: they hold property, observe rituals together, regulate marriage, and act collectively in disputes and warfare. The clan is one of anthropology's oldest concepts, central to the study of kinship, politics and social organisation in societies without centralised states.

## Origins and Development

The concept was systematised in the nineteenth century by Lewis Henry Morgan, whose *Ancient Society* (1877) placed the clan — he called it the gens — at the centre of human social evolution. For Morgan, the gens was the fundamental unit of "savage" and "barbarian" society, organised by descent and governed by councils, and it was only with the rise of private property and the territorial state that kinship organisation gave way to political organisation. Morgan's evolutionary scheme is no longer accepted, but his insight that descent groups can perform political, economic and religious functions without a state endured.

Twentieth-century ethnography transformed the concept from an evolutionary stage into a functional institution. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's study of the Nuer of South Sudan, published as *The Nuer* (1940), related the segmentary structure of Nuer clans and lineages, in which small lineages nest inside larger ones, to a parallel structure of territorial political segments, whose level of joint action expands or contracts according to the scale of the conflict. Order, Evans-Pritchard argued, is produced not by chiefs or courts but by the balanced opposition of descent groups. Claude Lévi-Strauss, in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949), added that clans are bound together by the exchange of women in marriage: exogamy — the rule that one must marry outside one's own clan — creates alliances between groups and makes the clan a node in a wider network of reciprocity.

Meyer Fortes, in *Kinship and the Social Order* (1969), refined the analysis by distinguishing the clan's jural and political functions from the domestic functions of the family. The clan, in his account, is the domain of the politico-jural order: it defines who may inherit, who owes whom bloodwealth, who may speak in council, and who must avenge a killing.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns the distinction between clan and lineage. The classic position treats the lineage as a demonstrable descent group and the clan as a putative one, with the clan often subdivided into lineages. Critics note that the boundary is fluid: genealogies are shortened, ancestors invented and groups merged as political circumstances change, so that a lineage can become a clan and a clan can split into lineages. Genealogy, in this view, is less a record of biological fact than a charter for present social relations.

A second debate concerns the clan's corporate character. In many societies the clan owns land, controls ritual knowledge and exacts collective responsibility — a member's offence is the clan's offence, and compensation is paid by the group. This corporate logic shapes everything from marriage payments to feud. But anthropologists have also documented clans that are little more than named categories with a shared totem and a marriage prohibition, raising the question of how much institutional weight the term should carry.

A third debate concerns descent and gender. Matrilineal clans, documented among peoples such as the Trobrianders and the Akan, challenge the assumption that descent groups are inherently patriarchal: authority and property may pass through women even where men hold office. The study of clans thus became a window onto the variety of gendered power, not merely a catalogue of kinship rules.

## Contemporary Relevance

The clan has not disappeared with modernisation. In Somalia, clan affiliation structures politics, conflict and humanitarian access; in the Scottish Highlands, clan identity has been reinvented as heritage and tourism; in parts of the Middle East and Central Asia, clan networks mediate elections, business and dispute settlement where state institutions are weak or distrusted. Anthropologists now study clans less as survivals of a primitive past than as flexible idioms of solidarity that people activate, reinvent or downplay according to context. Diaspora communities sustain clan associations that organise funerals, remittances and marriages across continents. The concept also matters for policy: state-building projects that ignore clan organisation often fail, while those that work with it risk entrenching it. The clan thus remains a key tool for understanding how kinship, politics and identity intertwine in the contemporary world.
