# War: Organized Violence Between Political Communities

**War** is organized, large-scale violence between political communities — states, empires, or armed groups — pursued for political ends. It differs from murder, feud, and riot by its scale, its organization, and its purpose: war is waged by groups that claim political authority, it is planned and sustained over time, and it aims to impose a will on an opponent. For sociology and anthropology, war is not a breakdown of social order but a social institution with its own organization, meanings, and consequences for the societies that fight it.

## Definition and Development

The classic definition comes from Carl von Clausewitz, whose *On War* (1832), published after his death, defined war as "the continuation of policy by other means." Clausewitz insisted that war is never an end in itself; it is an instrument of politics, shaped by the aims of those who order it. He also saw war as a field of chance and friction, where plans collide with reality and where passion, chance, and reason interact in ways no commander can fully control. His account remains the starting point for the sociology of war because it refuses to treat war as either pure irrationality or pure calculation.

Anthropology long debated whether war is natural to humanity or an invention of particular societies. Lawrence Keeley, in *War Before Civilization* (1996), challenged the image of peaceful prehistoric peoples by marshalling archaeological and ethnographic evidence that violent conflict between groups was widespread long before states existed. Keeley argued that death rates from warfare in many non-state societies were high compared with those of modern states, and that the image of a peaceful primitive past was a myth. His book forced anthropologists to reckon with evidence they had long downplayed.

## Key Contributions: War and State-Making

Charles Tilly, in *Coercion, Capital, and European States, AD 990–1992* (1990), developed one of the most influential sociological arguments about war, summed up in his earlier formula of 1975 that "war made the state, and the state made war." European rulers who needed to fund armies built tax systems, bureaucracies, and eventually representative institutions to bargain with the people they taxed. War was therefore not only destructive; it was productive of the modern state itself. Tilly's argument explains why the map of Europe, with its competing states and its strong fiscal administrations, looks the way it does — and why regions where rulers could fund war without bargaining with their subjects developed different kinds of states.

Michael Mann, in the first volume of *The Sources of Social Power* (1986), placed military power alongside economic, political, and ideological power as one of the four sources through which human societies are organized. Military power is distinctive because it is organized around concentrated, lethal coercion. Mann showed that military organization has its own logic: it requires discipline, logistics, and hierarchy, and it tends to produce centralized command even in societies that are otherwise egalitarian. War therefore shapes not only relations between societies but the internal organization of the societies that wage it.

## Examples

The effects of war reach far beyond the battlefield. The two world wars of the twentieth century transformed the role of the state in everyday life: rationing, conscription, taxation, and state economic planning all expanded under the pressure of total war, and many of those expansions survived into peacetime. War also transforms gender relations, as women enter factories and armies and then face pressure to return to domestic roles when the fighting ends. And war produces its own cultures — of remembrance, of sacrifice, of veteran identity — that persist for generations after the guns fall silent.

In stateless societies, war takes different forms. Raiding, feuding, and ritual battle may be woven into the cycle of seasons and the obligations of kinship. Anthropologists have shown that such conflicts are often governed by strict rules about who may be attacked, when, and with what weapons — rules that limit killing even as they authorize it. War, in these settings, is a regulated social performance as much as it is an attempt to destroy an enemy.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Clausewitz's dictum that war serves policy has been challenged by wars that seem to serve no rational end — wars of revenge, wars that continue long after their political aims have become unreachable, wars driven by the interests of the military itself. Critics of Keeley argue that he overstated the frequency of prehistoric war by reading ambiguous archaeological evidence as proof of organized killing. And Tilly's state-making thesis, powerful for Europe, fits less well in regions where states were imposed by colonial powers rather than built through domestic warfare.

War remains a central sociological concern. Contemporary research studies drone warfare, in which killing is done remotely by operators who never see the battlefield; the privatization of war through mercenary companies; and the long aftermath of war in the bodies and minds of veterans and civilians. The questions Clausewitz posed — who decides to fight, for what ends, and at what cost — have not gone away; they have only become more urgent.
