# Ethics in Forensic Assessment

A branch of **professional medical ethics** that addresses the particular dilemmas faced by a psychiatrist or psychologist conducting a **forensic assessment** (on behalf of a court or legal body) instead of providing ordinary therapeutic care. The structure of this relationship differs fundamentally from the traditional therapeutic doctor–patient relationship.

## The Dual Agency Problem

The central dilemma is **dual agency**. In forensic assessment, the examiner does not act on behalf of a "patient" (the evaluee) in the traditional therapeutic sense. Instead, the examiner acts on behalf of a **third party** (the court, the prosecution, or the defense), while direct interaction with the examiner may lead that person, in good faith or because of prior expectations, to confuse the examiner's role with that of a therapist who seeks their therapeutic interest. The psychiatrist **Paul Appelbaum** argued (1997) that forensic psychiatry needs its own **ethical framework** and should not simply borrow the principles of traditional therapeutic medical ethics (beneficence and non-maleficence), which are built on a therapeutic doctor–patient relationship that is absent from the forensic context. He grounded forensic ethics instead in **truth-telling** and **respect for persons**, exercised in service of **justice**. Strasburger, Gutheil, and Brodsky (1997) analyzed the related problem of role conflict when one clinician acts as both treater and expert witness.

## Informed Consent Specific to the Forensic Context

The specialized literature, including the handbook by **Thomas Gutheil** and Paul Appelbaum, stresses the need for a **special kind of informed consent** before any forensic assessment begins. The evaluee must be explicitly told that **the examiner is not their treater**, that the information they provide **does not enjoy the usual confidentiality** that governs the therapeutic relationship, and that it may be used against them in the legal proceedings, whether by the prosecution or by the defense. This advance disclosure, sometimes described as a "non-patient warning" and likened to a Miranda warning, is necessary to distinguish forensic assessment ethically from the implicit deception of claiming a therapeutic relationship that does not actually exist.

## Additional Principles

The field adds other principles. **Objectivity** means committing to give a neutral professional opinion regardless of the party that retained the expert and pays the fees, and avoiding "advocacy bias" toward that party. **Honesty** means presenting the limits of scientific knowledge and not exceeding the certainty actually available when testifying in court. Avoiding **dual roles** is another principle, such as a physician who treats a person and is later called as an expert in that person's case; professional guidelines generally advise against this conflict. The discussion here reflects mainly the North American (especially United States) literature, and legal rules on forensic evaluation differ between jurisdictions.

## Importance

These principles provide a framework that protects the integrity of the judicial system, by ensuring neutral and unbiased expert testimony, and the rights of the evaluee, by ensuring that they know in advance the nature and limits of the relationship. Today they are taught within forensic psychiatry subspecialty programs as an essential part of professional training, and they are governed by specialized professional guidelines such as those issued by the American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law.
