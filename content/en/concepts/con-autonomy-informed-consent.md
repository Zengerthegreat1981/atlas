# Autonomy and Informed Consent

## Meaning and Definition

Autonomy (Autonomy) is an individual's capacity to determine their own fate in accordance with their own convictions, free from coercion or deception. Informed consent (Informed Consent) is the procedural mechanism that puts this autonomy into practice in the medical and research context: a person is given sufficient information about the nature of an intervention, its risks and its alternatives before freely agreeing to it. The corresponding concept in the jurisprudential heritage is "consent" (*al-rida*), which jurists made a condition for the validity of contracts, relying on the principle that no property of a Muslim is lawful except with his willing consent. This principle extended from financial transactions to the disposal of the body itself: jurists ruled that a physician may not treat a patient without the patient's permission, except in cases of unconsciousness or compelling necessity.

## Historical and Philosophical Context

In Western philosophy, John Stuart Mill, in *On Liberty* (1859), established a central principle, the "harm principle" (Harm Principle), which permits individuals to act in their own affairs so long as they do not harm others, thereby making autonomy a value protected in its own right and not merely a means. Kant developed a deeper conception linking autonomy to practical reason: a person is autonomous when they legislate the moral law for themselves, not when they submit to external desires. In the twentieth century, Beauchamp and Childress, in *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (1979), formulated autonomy as one of four central principles of medical ethics, alongside beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. Informed consent then became a mandatory procedural element in every medical intervention and every research study on human beings, following the Nuremberg Code (1947), the Tuskegee syphilis study, and the Belmont Report (1979), which named respect for persons as a basic principle of research ethics.

## Applications and Problems

Practical problems arise concerning the limits of consent. How is consent obtained from a minor or from a patient who lacks mental capacity? Is a formal signed consent sufficient without genuine understanding? A common example is the refusal by some followers of Jehovah's Witnesses to accept blood transfusions on the basis of their belief. This case tests the tension between respecting autonomy and the physician's duty to save life.

## Limits of Autonomy in Contemporary Jurisprudence

Contemporary Islamic jurisprudential councils have discussed how far a patient's right to refuse treatment extends, even if the refusal leads to their death. Views differ. A common position among contemporary jurists is that a competent adult may decline treatment whose benefit is uncertain or which carries serious risk, while there is more disagreement about refusing treatment that would certainly save life at little harm. This distinguishes between the inviolability of the soul, which must be preserved, and lawful freedom of action. It is a delicate balance between autonomy and the obligation to preserve life, which is one of the overarching objectives of Islamic law.
