# Indigenous Knowledge

Indigenous knowledge — also called local knowledge or traditional ecological knowledge — is the body of knowledge, practice, and belief that indigenous and local peoples have developed through generations of living in a particular place. It includes knowledge of plants, animals, soils, weather, and water; techniques of farming, fishing, hunting, and healing; and the cosmologies, stories, and values that give that knowledge its meaning. Anthropologists study indigenous knowledge not as a quaint survival but as a living, dynamic system, comparable in sophistication to scientific knowledge and often more attuned to local conditions. The study of these knowledge systems raises fundamental questions about what counts as knowledge, who is authorised to speak it, and how different ways of knowing can coexist.

## Definition

Indigenous knowledge is typically characterised as place-based, transmitted orally and through practice, and embedded in social relations and spiritual life, rather than written down, abstracted, and separated from its context. This embeddedness was long treated by outsiders as a defect — evidence that indigenous knowledge was mere superstition — but anthropologists have shown that it is also a strength: knowledge tied to practice and to place is continually tested against experience and adapted to change. It is also important to distinguish indigenous knowledge from "tradition" in the sense of something fixed and unchanging. Indigenous knowledge systems are dynamic: they absorb new information, experiment, and innovate. The term itself is contested. Some scholars prefer "local knowledge" or "traditional ecological knowledge," while others note that "indigenous" carries political weight, marking the knowledge of peoples who have been colonised and marginalised, and whose knowledge claims are also claims to rights, land, and recognition.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of indigenous knowledge has a long history. Early ethnographers recorded detailed accounts of plant use, navigation, and weather lore, often treating them as data to be collected rather than as knowledge in their own right. The ethnoscience movement of the 1960s began to study indigenous classification systems — of plants, animals, colours, and kin — on their own terms, showing that they were systematic and often highly detailed. In the 1980s and 1990s, the field was transformed by the environmental movement and by the political mobilisation of indigenous peoples. Development agencies, confronted with the failure of top-down projects, began to value local knowledge; conservationists recognised that indigenous peoples often manage biodiversity more effectively than state agencies; and indigenous activists asserted their knowledge as part of their cultural and political identity. Fikret Berkes's *Sacred Ecology* showed how the ecological knowledge of Cree hunters and other peoples was integrated with ethics and cosmology, and how it sustained resource management over centuries. At the same time, scholars such as Arun Agrawal warned against romanticising indigenous knowledge or treating it as a single, homogeneous category, and argued that the divide between "indigenous" and "scientific" knowledge is itself a political construction.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One central debate concerns the relationship between indigenous and scientific knowledge. Some argue that the two are fundamentally different ways of knowing, incommensurable and irreducible to each other; others argue that they overlap and complement each other, and that the best environmental management combines them. A second debate concerns ownership and appropriation. Pharmaceutical companies have patented medicines based on indigenous plant knowledge, and seed companies have claimed rights over crop varieties developed by farmers over centuries — a process critics call biopiracy. International agreements now recognise the rights of indigenous peoples over their knowledge, but enforcement remains weak. A third dimension is gender: women often hold distinct knowledge — of seeds, of medicinal plants, of food processing — that male researchers and development projects have overlooked. A fourth debate concerns the politics of recognition. To call something "indigenous knowledge" is to make a political claim, and states, NGOs, and corporations all have interests in how that label is applied. Finally, there is the question of transmission: as young people migrate to cities and formal schooling displaces oral learning, indigenous knowledge is being lost, and communities are developing new strategies — schools, recordings, gardens, and legal protections — to preserve it.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Indigenous knowledge is now central to global debates about climate change, biodiversity, and development. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change and the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services both recognise indigenous knowledge as an essential source of evidence and of solutions, particularly in adaptation to climate change. At the same time, the recognition of indigenous knowledge raises difficult questions. When states and corporations adopt indigenous practices, do they empower indigenous peoples or extract value from them? When knowledge is documented and archived, who controls the archive? Anthropologists argue that recognising indigenous knowledge must go together with recognising indigenous rights — to land, to self-determination, and to the benefits of their knowledge. The critique of the field also continues: scholars warn against treating indigenous knowledge as a storehouse of useful facts to be mined by science, rather than as a complete way of being in the world. The enduring lesson is that there is more than one way of knowing, and that the survival of the planet may depend on taking them all seriously.
