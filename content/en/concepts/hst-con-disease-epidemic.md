# Disease and Epidemics in Historical Events

In classical historiography, epidemics and pestilence (*loimos* in Greek, *pestilentia* or *pestis* in Latin) occupied a significant place as catalysts of demographic crisis, social upheaval, and political transformation. Rather than treating widespread disease merely as divine retribution or random misfortune, ancient historians increasingly integrated epidemics into their causal frameworks, examining how biological catastrophes exposed the fragility of social norms, subverted military campaigns, and reshaped geopolitical balances.

## Thucydides and the Clinical Model

The foundational text for the historical analysis of epidemics is Thucydides' account of the Plague of Athens (430 BCE) in Book 2 of his *History of the Peloponnesian War* (2.47–54). Having contracted and survived the disease himself, Thucydides adopted an empirical and diagnostic methodology comparable in spirit to contemporary Hippocratic medical writing. Rejecting supernatural explanations, he provided a meticulous clinical record of somatic symptoms—severe fever, inflammation of the eyes, internal ulceration, gangrene, and memory loss—so that future observers might recognize the disease should it ever recur.

Beyond pathology, Thucydides' primary historiographical contribution was his acute analysis of the psychological and civic consequences of mass mortality. The suddenness of death and the failure of religious supplication produced an unprecedented breakdown of social order (*anomia*). Citizens abandoned conventional morality, squandered fortunes on immediate pleasures, and neglected sacred funeral rites, leaving bodies piled unburied in temples and streets. The epidemic dealt a severe blow to Athenian military manpower and hastened the death of Pericles, thereby destabilizing Athenian political leadership and altering the course of the war.

## Roman Historiography: Livy and the Siege of Syracuse

In Roman historiography, pestilence frequently intersected with military operations and religious crises. In *Ab Urbe Condita*, Livy repeatedly recorded epidemics affecting Rome and its armies, often interpreting them through the lens of traditional Roman religion as disruptions of the *pax deorum* (peace with the gods) that required expiatory rituals such as the *lectisternium* or the drive of the sacred nail (*clavum figere*).

However, Livy also recognized the direct military impact of disease. In his narrative of the Roman siege of Syracuse (212 BCE) during the Second Punic War, Livy described how an autumn epidemic decimated both the Carthaginian and Roman besieging forces. The disease struck the Carthaginian camp camped on marshy ground with particular ferocity, killing the Carthaginian commander Himilco and the Syracusan leader Hippocrates and crippling the Carthaginian relieving force, which helped pave the way for Marcus Claudius Marcellus to capture Syracuse.

## Late Antique Pandemics: The Antonine Plague and Procopius

The broader geopolitical consequences of epidemics became central in late antique and early Byzantine historiography. The Antonine Plague (c. 165–180 CE), likely smallpox, swept through the Roman Empire under Marcus Aurelius and Lucius Verus, causing heavy mortality across the empire. Our evidence includes the physician Galen, a contemporary, and Cassius Dio, who records a later severe outbreak at Rome under Commodus; the plague's demographic and strategic effects remain debated among modern historians.

In the sixth century CE, Procopius of Caesarea described the Justinianic Plague (541–542 CE) in Constantinople in Book 2 of his *History of the Wars* (2.22–23). Explicitly modeling his prose on Thucydides, Procopius detailed the emergence of bubonic swellings, delirium, and sudden death. He described how the epidemic disrupted the city's administration and food supply and affected Emperor Justinian's military campaigns to reconquer the Western Roman territories.
