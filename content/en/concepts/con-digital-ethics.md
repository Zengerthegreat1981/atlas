# Digital Ethics

Digital ethics is a branch of applied moral philosophy that examines the standards that should govern the conduct of individuals, institutions, and computing systems in the digital space: from the collection and processing of personal data, to the design of algorithms and artificial intelligence systems, to forms of communication and identity online. It differs from traditional ethics in that its subject matter consists of technologies that change more rapidly than stable social and legal norms can take shape.

Because of its modernity, the classical Islamic or philosophical ethical tradition did not address this subject directly. Its general principles, however, of trust (*amana*), the preservation of rights, and harm ("there shall be no harm nor reciprocating harm"), are invoked today with force in contemporary jurisprudential reasoning on data privacy and digital transactions. Some contemporary scholars treat the violation of digital privacy as analogous to the prohibition of spying mentioned in the Qur'an (49:12).

The field has roots in the writings of Norbert Wiener, the founder of cybernetics, notably *The Human Use of Human Beings* (1950), who anticipated the impact of computing on human values. It was then developed by James Moor ("What Is Computer Ethics?", 1985), who described the "policy vacuum" that new technology creates before clear rules are drawn up for it. With the spread of the internet, the school of "information ethics" emerged in the work of Luciano Floridi, who proposed expanding the circle of moral consideration to include "informational entities," not persons alone. Debates on algorithmic fairness also arose with Cathy O'Neil, who warned of "weapons of math destruction," that is, models that reproduce human bias behind a false appearance of objectivity.

The most prominent issues revolve around: data privacy and the individual's right to control his or her information (as in the European GDPR regulation); the responsibility of social media platforms for offensive or misleading content; the ethics of artificial intelligence in terms of transparency, accountability, and avoidance of bias; and the question of the "digital divide," which keeps some social groups from benefiting from technology. A major objection holds that the speed of technical development means that any ethical codification always lags behind reality, and that large companies use the discourse of "digital ethics" as a marketing tool (*ethics washing*) to avoid strict legal regulation.

An example: the use of recommendation algorithms on social media platforms to lengthen the time users stay on the platform at the expense of their mental health is a classic digital ethical dilemma, since the logic of commercial profit conflicts with the individual's real interest.

The field is also linked to the question of "digital agency," meaning the extent to which an individual can remain the free author of his or her decisions in an environment designed specifically to influence him through the techniques of "behavioral economics" and choice architecture. Questions of memory also arise in dealing with the digital archive and digital forgetting. Personal content remains preserved forever, unlike the natural forgetting mechanism that historically protected individuals from their past mistakes. Some legal frameworks, such as the European Union's GDPR (Article 17) and the 2014 *Google Spain* judgment of the Court of Justice of the EU, recognize a "right to be forgotten" in response to this challenge, which reflects an attempt by technology and legislation to catch up with ethical questions that practice had already raised. The need remains to develop flexible normative frameworks capable of keeping pace with innovation without sacrificing human dignity and the individual's right to determine his or her informational destiny.
