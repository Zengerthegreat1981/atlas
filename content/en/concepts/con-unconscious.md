# The Unconscious (Psychoanalysis)

**Sigmund Freud** formulated the concept of the unconscious as the pillar of the **school of psychoanalysis** that he founded in Vienna in the late nineteenth century. The concept refers to an enormous reservoir of repressed desires, painful memories and instinctual drives, hidden from immediate conscious awareness, which exert a continuous, dynamic and driving influence on a person's behavior, dreams, neurotic symptoms and slips of the tongue.

## Clinical Manifestations of Unconscious Activity

Unconscious content emerges through dreams, which Freud described in his book **The Interpretation of Dreams** (Die Traumdeutung, published in late 1899 and dated 1900) as "the royal road to the unconscious," and through slips of the tongue and psychosomatic conversion symptoms.

## The Relation of the Unconscious to Repression

The unconscious is closely tied to the mechanism of **repression**: repressed content (painful desires and memories) is what forms the core of the content of the unconscious for Freud, and repression is the mechanism that keeps it hidden from awareness.

## Contemporary Cognitive and Neural Developments

Modern neuroscience confirms the existence of "implicit unconscious cognitive processing," without literally matching the Freudian theory of instincts.

## Selected Quotations

"The interpretation of dreams is the royal road to a knowledge of the unconscious activities of the mind." — Sigmund Freud, *The Interpretation of Dreams* (Die Traumdeutung, 1899/1900).

## The Topographic and Structural Models

Freud first put forward the "topographic model," which divides the psychic apparatus into three layers: the conscious (what the individual actually perceives at a given moment), the preconscious (latent content that can be recalled with relative ease), and the unconscious (repressed content that resists direct recall and appears only in disguised form). Freud then reformulated his theory in 1923 in his book "The Ego and the Id" with a tripartite structural model (id, ego, superego), in which the unconscious was no longer synonymous with a single layer but a quality that parts of each of these three structures can have, including the ego itself, which contains unconscious defense mechanisms.

## Free Association as the Tool for Reaching the Unconscious

Freud developed the technique of "free association" as a replacement for the hypnosis that he used in his early days with Josef Breuer, asking the patient to say everything that comes to mind without censorship or selection, on the assumption that the chain of associations would inevitably lead to the repressed unconscious content through the slips, pauses and resistances that appear during speech itself.

## Contemporary Reception and the Limits of the Scientific Claim

Many cognitive neuroscientists today accept the existence of unconscious information processing in a limited sense (such as unconscious priming, or the processing of information without conscious attention), but they hold back from literal acceptance of the theory of the Freudian "dynamic unconscious" in all its instinctual details (such as the theory of the libido and the Oedipal conflict), considering that current neural evidence supports the idea of general implicit processing without necessarily proving the mechanisms of repression and psychological defense in the detail Freud described.
