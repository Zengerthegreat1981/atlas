# Qawa'id Fiqhiyya (Islamic Legal Maxims)

**Qawa'id fiqhiyya** (legal maxims) are general principles, concisely worded, that summarize a ruling shared by a large number of scattered jurisprudential questions across different areas of law. They are usually phrased in compact, eloquent formulas, such as "Actions are judged by their intentions," "Harm must be removed," "Hardship brings facilitation," "Custom is authoritative," and "Certainty is not removed by doubt." These maxims differ from usul al-fiqh in function. Whereas usul al-fiqh is concerned with the rules for deriving a ruling from its direct evidence (such as the rules governing the meaning of words), the qawa'id fiqhiyya aim to summarize what is common to subsidiary rulings that were originally derived by varied methods. They are therefore closer to a later inductive generalization than to a primary tool of derivation.

## Origins and Development

The seeds of this field appeared early among Hanafi jurists (such as al-Karkhi in his brief treatise on principles and maxims), but its systematic foundation is attributed to **Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam** in his book *Qawa'id al-Ahkam fi Masalih al-Anam*, which built a complete system around the principle of securing benefits and averting harms. His student **Shihab al-Din al-Qarafi** developed a parallel field, the science of distinctions, concerned with separating cases that appear similar rather than with gathering their commonalities. Further important works followed, most notably *al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir* by Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti (Shafi'i) and by Zayn al-Din ibn Nujaym (Hanafi). This development reached its practical peak in the Ottoman *Majalla al-Ahkam al-Adliyya* in the 1870s, which set out ninety-nine legal maxims in its legislative introduction, most of them drawn from the Hanafi tradition.

## The Major Maxims

Most writers in this field agree on a set of "major maxims" (al-qawa'id al-kubra) under which numerous subsidiary maxims fall. The best known include: "Actions are judged by their intentions" (the consideration of intention in qualifying transactions); "Certainty is not removed by doubt" (the basis of istishab, the presumption of continuity); "Hardship brings facilitation" (the basis of concessions in cases of necessity and need); "Harm must be removed" (the basis of removing harm and preventing its occurrence); and "Custom is authoritative" (the consideration of custom as governing the interpretation of words and transactions).

## How many "major" maxims, and where the exceptions sit

The familiar set of five is a later stabilization, not a count fixed in every book. Al-Suyuti reports that Qadi Husayn (d. 462/1069) reduced the Shafi'i school to four maxims: certainty is not removed by doubt, hardship brings ease, harm is to be removed, and custom governs. Other scholars added a fifth, that matters follow their purposes, and compared the five to the five pillars of Islam. Taj al-Din al-Subki's list of five includes that fifth maxim and phrases the custom rule as a return to customary practice. Ibn Nujaym, writing as a Hanafi, placed an extra maxim first: there is no reward except with intention. Wolfhart Heinrichs records the same five as the maxims said to be acknowledged across the schools, with small changes of wording, and notes Ibn Nujaym's extra maxim in front of them. The bare five are the shared core, not a complete comparative code. Each school's books attach branch maxims and lists of exceptions, and those lists are madhhab-specific. Ibn Nujaym himself compiled a separate Hanafi collection of controls and their exceptions. The exception in a given school has to be read in that school's *ashbah* or *fawa'id*, not from the five formulas alone.

## Maxims and Controls

Later writers distinguish the **maxim** (*qa'ida*) from the **control** (*dabit*, plural *dawabit*) chiefly by reach. A maxim gathers subsidiary cases drawn from several chapters of the law; a control organizes cases within a single chapter. Ibn Nujaym states the distinction in this form in his *al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir*, and Taj al-Din al-Subki, in his work of the same title, likewise separates the general maxim from the narrower rule confined to one subject.

Thus the principle that certainty is not removed by doubt operates in ritual purity, worship, family law and transactions alike, and counts as a maxim. By contrast, the rule, based on a Prophetic report, that tanning purifies a hide belongs to the law of purity alone and serves as a control, although the schools disagree about which hides it covers. A control is often tied to the doctrine of a particular school, and may state a condition or classification as much as an underlying legal rationale.

The boundary was not observed uniformly. Several authors used the two terms interchangeably or included narrow controls in collections of maxims, so the classification of a given formula can depend on the writer.

## Practical and Theoretical Value

Legal maxims offer a highly practical tool for the contemporary jurist and the modern legislator alike. They allow a general juristic principle to be applied to newly arising circumstances for which no specific text exists, without rebuilding the full usuli argument from scratch for each case. For this reason Mustafa al-Zarqa drew attention to them in the modern period, comparing them to the "General Principles of Law" found in modern legal systems.

## Impact and Critique

Some scholars argue that the qawa'id fiqhiyya represent, alongside usul al-fiqh, a second layer of Islamic legal theorizing that is closer to direct practical application. Others caution that stating these maxims in concise form may encourage mechanical use that ignores the many exceptions the jurists themselves specified in detail. Their use therefore always requires reference to the detailed commentaries, not reliance on the bare, condensed formulations alone.
