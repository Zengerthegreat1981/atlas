# Atman, Brahman, and the Unity of Cosmic Consciousness (Atman-Brahman)

The deepest metaphysical and spiritual concept in Indian philosophy and in non-dualist Vedānta. It holds that the 'Atman' (a person's inner self and pure consciousness) is not separate in its true essence from 'Brahman' (the supreme, absolute cosmic consciousness and being), and that ignorance of this unity, an illusory separation produced by māyā, is the source of suffering and rebirth.

## Overcoming Subject-Object Duality: Witness-Consciousness

In Advaita epistemology and psychology, the apparent division between the experiencing subject and the external objective world is an artifact of superimposition (*adhyāsa*). The empirical ego (*ahaṃkāra*) misidentifies pure consciousness with the mind-body apparatus (*upādhis*), projecting an external world of separate, multiplicity-laden objects.

To dissolve this illusion, classical Advaita analyzes experience across three states of consciousness: waking (*jāgrat*), dreaming (*svapna*), and dreamless deep sleep (*suṣupti*). In Advaita readings of the *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad*, developed from Gauḍapāda's commentary onward, while the contents of awareness and bodily sensations appear and disappear across these states, consciousness itself persists uninterrupted as the transcendental witness (*sākṣin*). In the fourth state (*turīya*), consciousness is realized in its pristine, non-dual nature—unconditioned by mental fluctuations (*vṛttis*) or the dichotomy of knower, known, and act of knowing.

## The Root in the Upanishads

The equation occurs in the Upanishads in statements called the "great sayings," the most famous of which is "Tat tvam asi" (You are That) in the Chandogya Upanishad, where the father Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu that the subtle essence in everything is the truth and is his very self, and "Aham brahmasmi" (I am Brahman) in the Brihadaranyaka. Shankara (traditionally dated 788-820 CE, though modern scholarship generally places him in the eighth century, around 700-750 CE) gathered these texts into the doctrine of Advaita (non-duality): Brahman alone is reality, the manifold world is an appearance arising from ignorance (avidyā) or māyā, and a person is liberated by knowledge (jñāna) that he is Brahman, not by acquiring anything new. The doctrine likens the matter to a rope mistaken for a snake in the dark: when light is shed, the illusion vanishes without the rope having changed. The Atman is the witness that cannot itself be witnessed, for everything we know of thoughts, organs, and feelings is an object for it.

## Comparison with Pantheism in Sufism

This idea occupied early Muslims. Al-Biruni, in his eleventh-century book on India, reported Indian beliefs and in places compared them with Sufi and Greek ideas. The Mughal prince Dara Shikoh then wrote his book *Majma' al-Bahrayn* (1655), comparing the terminologies of Sufism and Vedānta, and oversaw the translation of fifty Upanishads into Persian (*Sirr-i Akbar*, 1657). Comparisons are drawn with the doctrine of waḥdat al-wujūd (the unity of being), associated with Ibn Arabi's school (the phrase itself is mainly used by later commentators), and with the saying attributed to al-Hallaj, "I am the Truth," but the difference is essential: the Sufi affirms servanthood and the distinction between servant and Lord even if traces are annihilated, whereas Advaita makes unity a reality without distinction, and many Muslim scholars rejected any statement of union. For that reason researchers warn against equating the two terminologies.

## Application in Transpersonal Psychology

Transpersonal psychologists, among them Ken Wilber in "The Atman Project," took from this heritage the idea that psychological development does not stop at the ego but extends to stages in which consciousness expands until it includes the whole. He explains peak experiences and ecstasy as moments in which what lies beyond the ego is disclosed. However, transferring a metaphysical concept to a model of psychological development raises questions about empirical evidence.

## Limits and Criticism

Buddhist philosophers objected that the doctrine of a permanent Atman does not agree with what experience reveals of flux and impermanence, which is the basis of the concept of anatta. Vedāntins of other schools (Ramanuja in Vishishtadvaita and Madhva in Dvaita) criticized the unity of Advaita because it abolishes the difference between worshipper and worshipped and makes the world an illusion. Other critics hold that explaining ignorance by māyā runs in a circle: if Brahman is the only truth, who is it that is ignorant? Advaitins responded that avidyā cannot be described as either real or unreal (*anirvacanīya*), a formulation developed especially by later Advaita thinkers.
