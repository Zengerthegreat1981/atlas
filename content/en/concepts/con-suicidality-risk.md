# Suicidality and Risk Assessment

## Definition

The term "suicidality" covers a spectrum of interrelated clinical phenomena: suicidal ideation, planning, incomplete suicide attempts, and completed suicide. "Risk assessment" refers to the clinical tools and approaches used to estimate the likelihood that a person will engage in suicidal behavior within a given time frame, in order to guide therapeutic decisions (such as the need for hospital admission or intensive follow-up).

## Theoretical Framework

Aaron Beck and colleagues developed early assessment tools in the 1970s, such as the **Beck Hopelessness Scale** (1974) and the **Scale for Suicide Ideation** (1979), and reported that hopelessness, more than depression severity alone, was a strong cognitive correlate and predictor of later suicide in psychiatric patients. Later, Thomas Joiner proposed the **interpersonal theory of suicide** (2005), which holds that the actual desire for suicide arises from the intersection of two feelings: "thwarted belongingness" and "perceived burdensomeness." Moving from desire to action, however, requires the **acquired capability** to harm oneself, which is built through repeated exposure to painful or frightening experiences that reduce the innate fear of death. This theory provided an explanatory framework rather than merely recording isolated statistical risk factors. Later "ideation-to-action" frameworks (for example, Klonsky and May's three-step theory) likewise distinguish between those who think about suicide and those who move on to an attempt.

## Applications

- The **Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale** (C-SSRS), a standardized instrument used worldwide to screen the severity of suicidal ideation and prior suicidal behavior in clinics, emergency departments and clinical research.
- The **SAFE-T protocol** adopted by the US Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), which integrates the assessment of risk and protective factors within a unified clinical safety plan.
- Collaborative **safety planning** between therapist and patient, as an alternative to "no-suicide contracts," which have little supporting evidence.

## Challenges

Scientific evidence shows that traditional risk factors (such as severe depression, previous attempts and social isolation) have weak statistical predictive power at the level of the individual, although they are meaningful at the group level. This makes clinical assessment an ongoing probabilistic process that requires repeated reassessment rather than a single final decision.
