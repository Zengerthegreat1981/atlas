# Hyperactive Agency Detection Device (HADD)

A cognitive-evolutionary concept introduced by Justin Barrett (c. 2000) within the cognitive science of religion. It describes an innate perceptual mechanism that makes the human mind inclined to interpret movement, sound or an ambiguous pattern as the product of an **intentional agent** (a living being with intentions), even when no agent is in fact present.

## Evolutionary Logic

The concept rests on an account of the cost of error: assuming a predator is present in the undergrowth when none is (a false positive) costs little, a needless startle; but failing to detect a real predator (a false negative) may cost one's life. It is therefore assumed that natural selection favoured, over many generations, a detection mechanism that is "hypersensitive", triggering many false alarms rather than missing a single real threat.

## From Predator Detection to Religious Belief

Barrett argues that this mechanism, continuing to operate even when there is no direct animal danger (such as a mysterious sound in the house, thunder, or a sudden illness), creates a natural cognitive predisposition to explain obscure phenomena by invisible agents: spirits, gods, ancestors. This does not mean that HADD "produces" religion directly; rather, it lays a cognitive groundwork that makes belief in invisible agents easier to accept than alternative assumptions.

## Relation to an Earlier Concept: Guthrie's Anthropomorphism

Stewart Guthrie (`thk-stewart-guthrie`) developed a closely related thesis, first in a 1980 article and then in *Faces in the Clouds* (1993): religion is a form of anthropomorphism, rooted in a perceptual strategy of treating ambiguous stimuli as humanlike because wrongly assuming an agent costs less than missing one. Barrett later gave the mechanism the name HADD and linked it to theory of mind.

## Critique

It is noted that the hypothesis explains why belief in invisible agents is cognitively **easy**, but not why this belief **becomes** an organised religion with doctrine, ritual and institution. Strands of the cognitive science of religion concerned with ritual and institutions, such as Whitehouse's modes of religiosity (`con-modes-of-religiosity-theory-whitehouse`), address this gap. The empirical evidence for a dedicated, hyperactive mechanism is also debated, and some scholars prefer to speak of a general, broadly tuned tendency to detect agents.

## Illustrative Examples

Examples frequently cited in this literature: a child who hears the rustling of trees at night automatically assumes "someone" is there before rationally checking that it is just the wind; or the famous experiment of Fritz Heider and Marianne Simmel (1944), which showed that viewers of a short film of simple geometric shapes moving (triangles and circles) tend automatically to describe their movement in the language of intentions ("chases", "fears"), despite the absence of any real agent in the scene, which is presented as early experimental evidence for the strength of this hyperactive perceptual tendency.
