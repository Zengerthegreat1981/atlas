# The Actantial Model of Narrative

A model formulated by Algirdas Julien Greimas in *Sémantique structurale* (1966), drawing on Lucien Tesnière's notion of "actants" and Étienne Souriau's dramatic functions. It abstracts the thirty-one narrative "functions" that Vladimir Propp identified in folk tales into six more abstract structural "actants." These are applicable to any narrative, regardless of its literary or cultural type.

## The Six Actants

The model distributes narrative roles across three pairs of opposites. The "Subject" strives to achieve a goal, against the desired "Object." The "Sender" motivates the subject to strive, against the "Receiver," who benefits from the achievement of the goal. The "Helper" assists the subject, against the "Opponent," who obstructs it.

## Abstraction from the Individual Character

The essential difference between an "actant" and a narrative "character" is that a single actant may be embodied by several characters in the same story. Conversely, a single character may occupy several actants at once or across different stages of the narrative. The model therefore describes the deep functional structure of the plot, not the surface distribution of the actual characters.

## Its Relation to Propp's Model

Greimas drew on Propp's classification of seven "spheres of action" in the Russian folk tale, but reformulated them as a system of binary oppositions (subject/object, sender/receiver, helper/opponent). This replaces Propp's linear sequence of thirty-one functions. The result is a more abstract structure suited to analyzing simultaneous narrative, not only successive narrative.

## The Semiotic Square and Analytic Application

The actantial model is often paired with Greimas's other tool, the "semiotic square" (Carré Sémiotique), which analyzes the logical relations between opposed and contradictory values within a text. The two models are applied together in analyzing folk tales, epics and modern novels alike. For instance, an Arab folk tale can be analyzed by assigning the roles of "hero" (subject), "treasure" or "marriage" (object), "king" (sender), "ogre" or "sorcerer" (opponent), and "helpful jinni" (helper). The same structuralist methods have been applied to the narrative structure of the *Thousand and One Nights*, among other corpora.

## Impact

The model has been widely used to analyze literary, cinematic, advertising and mythological narrative, and it has been influential in comparative literary criticism and media studies. Analysts have also applied it to news and political narrative, and to film and drama plots.

Greimas later set the six actants inside a canonical narrative schema with successive phases often labeled contract, competence (or qualification), performance, and sanction. The subject is mandated to act, acquires the means to act, carries out the action, and is recognized or judged. The schema is a descriptive hypothesis about a recurrent plot pattern, not a claim that every narrative follows it.

## Critique of the Model

The model has been criticized as so abstract that it sometimes loses the ability to distinguish between narrative patterns that genuinely differ in cultural and stylistic detail. It also implicitly assumes a purposive, goal-directed structure (a subject striving toward a specific object). This may not suit modern narrative patterns that deliberately break the linear, goal-directed plot, such as experimental, purposeless fiction. This has limited its applicability to all forms of narrative without exception.
