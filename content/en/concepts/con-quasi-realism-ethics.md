# Quasi-Realism

"Quasi-realism" is a modern meta-ethical position that attempts to reconcile emotivist non-realism (the view that moral judgments are originally expressions of attitudes and desires rather than descriptions of objective facts) with the retention, in everyday moral language, of all the features of realist discourse. We say that a moral proposition is "true" or "false," we speak of moral "knowledge" and moral "facts," and we use conditional and inferential logic in moral argument, as if we were fully realists. The quasi-realist claims that it is possible to "earn" all these linguistic features of realism without being philosophically committed to the existence of moral facts independent of our attitudes.

## An Approach from Islamic Thought

This reconciliatory position stands at the opposite extreme from the prevailing Islamic theological position, whether among the Mu'tazilites, who held that good and evil are real qualities of actions perceptible by reason, or among the Ash'arites, who tied good and evil to the ruling of the Sharia. Both camps hold that a true moral judgment answers to something beyond human attitudes (rational or divinely revealed), so they have no need to "earn" the language of realism.

## Simon Blackburn and Allan Gibbard

The British philosopher Simon Blackburn is the best-known defender of quasi-realism, especially in *Spreading the Word* (1984). He adopts a basically "expressivist" position, on which a moral judgment expresses an "attitude of approval or disapproval" rather than describing an external fact. However, he argues that these attitudes can acquire a complex logical structure (such as conditionals: "If torturing children is wrong, then inciting others to do it is also wrong") by a "projectivist" account on which we project our attitudes onto the world, so that moral discourse behaves linguistically as if it were realist; he describes the aim as earning the right to speak of moral truth and fact. Allan Gibbard offered a related formulation under the name "norm-expressivism," focusing on the idea that a moral judgment expresses the acceptance of a system of norms rather than a mere passing emotion.

## The Logical Problem and Objections

Quasi-realism faces what is known as the "Frege-Geach problem," which asks: how can the expression of an emotional attitude (rather than a proposition) appear within a compound conditional sentence while retaining its stable logical meaning? When I say "If lying is wrong, then inciting others to lie is wrong," I am not expressing disapproval of lying here (since the conditional does not assume that any lying occurs), so what exactly am I "expressing" in this case? Many critics hold that Blackburn's attempts to answer this question, despite their technical sophistication, remain highly contested.

## Illustrative Example

When a speaker says, "Enslaving human beings may have been socially accepted in the past, but it was nonetheless a real wrong," quasi-realism seeks to explain how this statement can be "true" in a strong sense resembling objective truth, even though its philosophical root is nothing more than a general attitude of disapproval shared by most people today.
