# Primary and Secondary Poverty Lines in Rowntree

A methodological concept formulated by Seebohm Rowntree to measure poverty quantitatively instead of judging it impressionistically, by fixing a physiological minimum necessary for subsistence and distinguishing two kinds of falling below it.

## The Content

In *Poverty: A Study of Town Life* (1901), Rowntree calculated a precise quantitative "poverty line" based on the minimum monetary cost needed to cover the necessary physiological minimum of calories and protein (drawing on nutritional research contemporary with him), in addition to the minimum costs of housing, clothing and fuel. He then distinguished between two kinds of falling below this line. "Primary poverty" is where the household's total income is insufficient to cover this minimum even with perfectly optimal spending and no waste. "Secondary poverty" is where the income is in principle sufficient to cover the minimum, but part of it is actually spent on non-necessities (such as alcohol or gambling, according to his classification), so that the household falls below the actual poverty line despite the theoretical sufficiency of its income.

## Impact and Critique

This quantitative standard became a reference model that influenced British social welfare policy throughout the twentieth century and the development of later, more precise poverty measures. It later faced a fundamental methodological critique: the "physiological minimum" standard is a narrow biological one that ignores the social and relative character of deprivation. What counts as "necessary" for full participation in social life differs with historical and cultural context, and cannot be reduced to a purely physiological need fixed across time and place. This critique later paved the way for the development of "relative poverty" measures in the twentieth century, calculated as a proportion of a society's average income and not as an absolute physiological minimum. Peter Townsend's *Poverty in the United Kingdom* (1979) was the most influential statement of this relative approach, and the European Union's at-risk-of-poverty indicator is conventionally set at 60 per cent of national median equivalised income.

## The Field Method and Comparison with Charles Booth

Rowntree conducted his survey in the English city of York in 1899 with a precise field methodology that included collecting house-to-house information on nearly every working-class household in the city, including earnings (partly estimated) and rents. He was influenced by Charles Booth's earlier survey of London poverty, but he distinguished himself from Booth by formulating an explicit, costed standard of need, based on his own house-to-house inquiries, in place of the classifications that Booth had drawn largely from the reports of school-board visitors. Rowntree's results surprised British public opinion at the time, showing that about 28% of York's population (roughly 10% in primary and 18% in secondary poverty) were living in poverty at the time of the survey. This challenged prevailing notions that poverty was confined to a small marginal "lower class."

## The Cycle of Poverty over the Life Course

One of Rowntree's most important additional discoveries was his observation that working-class households pass through successive stages of poverty and relative prosperity over their life cycle: poverty in early childhood (when the number of dependent children is relatively high compared with the father's sole income), then relative prosperity as the children grow up, begin working and contribute to the household income, then poverty again in old age when the breadwinner retires or becomes unable to work. This finding on the "cycle of poverty" fed into later thinking about social insurance and family support, including the context of the Beveridge Report of 1942.
