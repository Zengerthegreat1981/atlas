# Mental Health and Human Rights

## Definition

This concept addresses the tension between protecting people with a psychiatric disorder and guaranteeing their rights. Psychiatry holds the power of involuntary detention and treatment, and this power needs controls. The subject covers legal capacity, informed consent, detention, restraint and seclusion, conditions in institutions, and discrimination in employment, housing and education.

## Legal Capacity in Islamic Jurisprudence

Islamic jurisprudence treated madness as a condition affecting legal capacity. The tradition transmitted from the Prophet about the pen being lifted from three persons, including the insane person until he recovers, which Abu Dawud and others narrated, established that the insane person is not held responsible or accountable for his actions. Jurists established guardianship and interdiction to protect his property and interests. Dols, in *Majnun* (Clarendon Press, 1992), discussed how jurists and physicians dealt with madness in medieval Islamic society, including its effect on legal responsibility. The model differs from modern rights in that it rests on protection and guardianship rather than independence. The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities today challenges this model (see the entry on human rights in Islam).

## Modern Reform and Critique of the Institution

It is said that Philippe Pinel unchained the inmates at Bicêtre in Paris in 1793, a story that became heavily mythologized (the hospital superintendent Jean-Baptiste Pussin had already begun to reduce the use of chains). Michel Foucault re-read the history in *Histoire de la folie* (Plon, 1961) as a history of "great confinement" of deviants, a reading that has been debated and corrected historically. Thomas Szasz, in *The Myth of Mental Illness* (Hoeber-Harper, 1961), argued that "mental illness" is a metaphor that justifies coercion. Erving Goffman analyzed "total institutions" in *Asylums* (1961). Rosenhan's report in *Science* in 1973 described healthy volunteers who feigned auditory hallucinations, were admitted to psychiatric hospitals, and were not identified as healthy by staff. The report influenced public debate about institutional practice. Its methods were criticized at the time, and later historical investigation, including Susannah Cahalan's *The Great Pretender* (2019), has raised serious doubts about how the study was conducted and reported. It is a weak basis for empirical claims.

## International Standards

In 1991 the UN General Assembly adopted the "Principles for the Protection of Persons with Mental Illness and the Improvement of Mental Health Care" (Resolution 46/119). In 2006 it adopted the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, which entered into force in 2008. Article 12 of the Convention provides that persons with disabilities have legal capacity on an equal basis with others, and Article 14 prohibits the deprivation of liberty on the basis of disability alone. These provisions caused debate: is involuntary treatment ever permissible? The Convention challenged the guardianship system in favor of "supported decision-making", and the Committee on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, in its General Comment No. 1 (2014), interpreted Article 12 as requiring the abolition of substitute decision-making. Some states and many clinicians dispute this reading. Drew and colleagues, in *The Lancet* in 2011, documented widespread abuses against people with mental illness around the world, from chaining in some communities to long-term confinement without review. The World Health Organization launched its QualityRights initiative in 2012 to change services (see the entry on international human rights).

## The Arab Context

The subject intersects with shrines, sheikhs and healers (*ruqat*), where physical restraint is sometimes practiced, and with modern laws in several Arab countries that regulate involuntary admission. It requires balancing respect for traditions with protecting the patient from harm (see the entry on cultural healing traditions).

## Limits and Debate

The risk of applying the principle of autonomy excessively is discussed, when a patient lacks insight during acute psychosis, so that treatment is delayed and their condition deteriorates. Others argue that the absence of good community options is what makes compulsion common (see the entry on community mental health).
