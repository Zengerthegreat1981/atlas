# Majaz and Isti'ara (Metaphor in Language)

In the Arabic rhetorical heritage, **majaz** (figurative usage) is the use of a word in a sense other than its original meaning, on the basis of a relation between the two meanings together with an indicator (*qarina*) that prevents the literal meaning from being intended. **Isti'ara** (metaphor) falls under majaz as its most refined form, resting on an implicit comparison whose particle and point of comparison are omitted. This pair formed the core of the science of **bayan** (eloquent expression), one of the three branches of Arabic rhetoric (alongside *ma'ani*, meaning, and *badi'*, ornamentation).

## Theoretical Foundation: Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani

**Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani** (d. 471 AH / 1078 CE) is the foundational theorist of a precise rhetorical account of majaz and metaphor, in his two reference works *Asrar al-Balagha* (*The Secrets of Rhetoric*) and *Dala'il al-I'jaz* (*The Proofs of Inimitability*). Al-Jurjani turned a scattered heritage of grammatical, philosophical and critical observations into a coherent theory built around the concept of **nazm** (the arrangement of words according to the requirements of grammatical meaning). In *Dala'il al-I'jaz* he held that eloquence lies not in single words taken in isolation but in the **syntactic relations** among words within the sentence. Among his key concepts is **tamthil** (representation), an elaborate form of figurative comparison in which the point of resemblance is drawn from a composite situation or image rather than from a single, direct attribute.

## Distinguishing Literal and Figurative Meaning

Classical theory of majaz rested on a basic distinction between **haqiqa** (literal meaning: the original sense for which the word was set) and **majaz** (its use in another sense through a relation with it, such as metaphor based on resemblance, or metonymic majaz based on other relations such as causality or contiguity). This distinction generated a long linguistic and jurisprudential debate about whether every word necessarily has a first "literal" meaning from which it later shifts figuratively, or whether much linguistic usage is figurative from the start without any earlier literal meaning historically.

## Use of Majaz in the Sciences of the Qur'an and Jurisprudence

The study of majaz acquired special importance in **the principles of jurisprudence** (*usul al-fiqh*) and in Qur'anic exegesis. Commentators and jurists needed to distinguish literal from figurative words in the sacred text in order to control legal deduction. Some Zahiris and Hanbalis (such as Ibn Taymiyya) rejected the very existence of majaz in language or in the Qur'an, regarding this division as a later terminological innovation, arising after the first three centuries of Islam, with no basis in the speech of the Arabs.

## Influence and Afterlife

Al-Jurjani's theory of majaz and metaphor influenced the development of the whole Arabic science of rhetoric after him. Al-Sakkaki, in *Miftah al-Ulum* (*The Key to the Sciences*), systematised the three rhetorical sciences in a methodical way. In comparative studies today, his theory is compared with contemporary Western theories of metaphor, such as Lakoff and Johnson's theory of "conceptual metaphor", and researchers have noted a similarity in focusing on the conceptual structure of metaphor rather than on mere verbal ornament.

## Limitations

The dispute over the limits of majaz in language and in the religious text remains a live debate between the schools of jurisprudence. Modern functional linguists, among them Ahmad al-Mutawakkil, have reread parts of the classical rhetorical and grammatical heritage as a theory of communicative function. That rereading does not, by itself, decide the older legal dispute over majaz in the Qur'an.
