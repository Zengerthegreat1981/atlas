# Criminalization Theory

**Criminalization theory** is a field of the philosophy of criminal law that examines the central normative question: when may the state use the coercive criminal tool, with all the social stigma and deprivation of liberty it carries, to criminalize a particular conduct? And what are the moral and constitutional limits of this power?

## From Packer's criteria to Husak's constraints

Much of the contemporary debate traces to Herbert Packer's *The Limits of the Criminal Sanction* (1968), which proposed criteria for when criminal sanctions are appropriate, including that the conduct be socially threatening and not widely condoned, that enforcement be even-handed, and that no reasonable non-criminal alternative exist. Douglas Husak, in *Overcriminalization* (2008), proposed a set of internal constraints (such as nontrivial harm or evil, wrongfulness and desert) and external constraints (such as a substantial state interest, direct advancement of that interest, and necessity) on criminalization. He warned against **overcriminalization**, the expansion of criminal law that he saw in contemporary legal systems, especially in the United States.

## Feinberg's harm principle

Joel Feinberg, in his expansive project *The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law* (four volumes, 1984–1988), offered a detailed philosophical analysis of the **harm principle** associated with John Stuart Mill. The four volumes are *Harm to Others*, *Offense to Others*, *Harm to Self* and *Harmless Wrongdoing*. Feinberg defended the harm principle as a legitimate ground for criminalization and, with strict qualifications, the offense principle for serious offensiveness, while rejecting legal moralism (punishing conduct merely because it is immoral) and treating legal paternalism with great caution.

## The communicative and collective dimension in Duff

Antony Duff, together with Lindsay Farmer, Sandra Marshall, Massimo Renzo and Victor Tadros, developed in *Criminalization: The Political Morality of the Criminal Law* (2014) an approach that links the justification of criminalization to the idea of public wrongs and to membership in a shared political community. It builds on Duff's communicative theory of punishment in *Answering for Crime* (2007). This adds a communicative dimension to the debate that goes beyond the abstract calculation of harm and benefit, toward a deeper question about the legitimacy of holding an individual accountable before the community that the criminal law addresses.

## A comparison with the principle of benefit and harm in usul al-fiqh

This debate meets the principle of benefit (maslaha) and harm (mafsada) in Islamic usul al-fiqh. In Islamic criminal law, discretionary punishment (ta'zir), unlike the fixed hudud penalties, is commonly justified by reference to public interest (maslaha). The maxim that averting harms takes precedence over bringing benefits (*dar' al-mafasid muqaddam 'ala jalb al-masalih*) is often cited as counselling caution against unjustified punitive expansion. The comparison is one of structure, not of identical doctrine.

## Legislative inflation and preventive criminalization

Contemporary scholars in this field are also concerned with the phenomenon of **legislative inflation** in criminal law. Legislatures create new offenses at a high rate, often in response to immediate political and media pressures, without systematic review of their consistency with the general principles of criminal law. Preventive or preparatory offenses, such as early terrorism offenses that criminalize acts remote in time from any actual realized harm, are also expanding.

## Impact and critique

Criminalization theory has become an independent and influential research field in debates on contemporary criminal policy reform. Debate continues, however, over the difficulty of applying its criteria in borderline cases where it is hard to determine precisely whether a clear real harm exists.
