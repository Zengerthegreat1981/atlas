# Positive & Negative Symptoms

A central classification in psychiatry of the symptoms of schizophrenia and psychotic disorders. It distinguishes between two fundamentally different types of dysfunction: **positive symptoms**, which represent an excess or distortion of normal mental functions, and **negative symptoms**, which represent a loss or absence of functions that normally exist in a healthy person.

## Definition

**Positive symptoms** are abnormal additions to ordinary mental experience: hallucinations (especially auditory ones) and delusions (firmly held beliefs that resist contrary evidence). Disorganized speech and behavior are often grouped with them, although later factor analyses treat them as a separate dimension. They are called "positive" because they add something that was not present, not because they are positive in a value sense.

**Negative symptoms** reflect an absence or decline in normal functions: affective flattening, lack of energy and withdrawal from activity (anergia), poverty of speech (alogia), social withdrawal, and loss of the capacity to feel pleasure (anhedonia).

## Theoretical Framework

The positive/negative terminology has roots in the neurologist John Hughlings Jackson's nineteenth-century ideas about dissolution of brain function. In 1980, in the *British Medical Journal*, Timothy Crow proposed a type I syndrome (positive symptoms, more responsive to antipsychotics) and a type II syndrome (negative symptoms, more closely linked to intellectual impairment). Nancy Andreasen's work in the early 1980s was highly influential: she published a definition of negative symptoms in 1982 and developed two standard rating instruments, the **Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms** (SANS, 1983) and the **Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms** (SAPS, 1984). Later research showed that the symptom structure of schizophrenia is more complex than a simple dichotomy. Liddle's 1987 factor analysis of chronic schizophrenia described three syndromes: **reality distortion** (hallucinations and delusions), **disorganization** (disorganized speech, behavior, and inappropriate affect), and **psychomotor poverty** (negative symptoms). Andreasen and colleagues and others reported similar three-dimensional structures, and this model is why disorganization is often discussed separately from the classical positive-negative binary. The NIMH-MATRICS consensus (2006) identified five negative-symptom domains: blunted affect, alogia, asociality, anhedonia, and avolition.

## Clinical Importance

This distinction has direct therapeutic implications. Positive symptoms usually respond better to antipsychotics, which block dopamine D2 receptors. Negative symptoms respond less well to current drug treatment, and are more closely associated with long-term functional impairment (work, relationships, independence) and with the overall prognosis.

## Applications

- Guiding the choice of drug treatment, and assessing response to it, according to the dominant symptom dimension in each patient.
- Diagnostic distinction between primary negative symptoms (an essential part of the illness) and secondary ones (resulting from accompanying depression, drug side effects, or environmental deprivation in institutions).
- Designing psychosocial rehabilitation programs that specifically target negative symptoms and daily functioning.

## Limits

Distinguishing "true" negative symptoms from similar symptoms that result from accompanying depression or drug side effects (extrapyramidal, parkinsonism-like effects) remains a practical diagnostic challenge that has not been fully resolved.
