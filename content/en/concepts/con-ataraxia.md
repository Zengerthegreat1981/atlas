# Ataraxia and Peace of Mind

ἀταραξία: the absence of disturbance. The word is composed of a negating prefix and "ταραχή", meaning turmoil and anxiety — so it is negative in formulation: it is not defined by what it attains but by what it removes. This linguistic composition is the key to the whole concept: the goal for the Hellenistic thinkers is not the attainment of a positive state but the disappearance of what disturbs.

The schools of the Hellenistic age all shared in taking peace of the soul as a goal, and differed decisively over the path to it — and this difference in path is what distinguishes one school from another, not the goal itself.

## The Epicurean Path: To Be Treated by Knowledge

For Epicurus (341–270 BCE) ataraxia is a goal reached through physics. His logic is that the source of human anxiety is a false belief, and that refuting it by science removes it. Epicurus identifies two chief fears, and each has a theoretical remedy:

- Fear of the gods: removed by atomic physics — that the world is a composition of atoms and void, and that the gods, if they exist, live in untroubled blessedness and neither manage our affairs nor punish. So there is no place for fear of divine wrath.
- Fear of death: removed by a famous logical argument: death is the dissolution of the composite, so there is no sensation after it. What has no sensation has no evil, so "death is nothing to us". The one who fears death fears a state in which he will not be present.

Epicurus adds a division of pleasures into natural and necessary (such as food), natural but unnecessary (such as luxurious food), and neither natural nor necessary (such as wealth and fame) — and that restricting desire to the first produces self-sufficiency (αὐτάρκεια), from which peace comes. Epicureanism is thus a moderate and practical discipline, not license, contrary to what became common about it. For Epicurus the highest pleasure is the absence of pain in the body (aponia) and of disturbance in the soul (ataraxia).

What best sums this up is the "fourfold remedy" (tetrapharmakos), a formula preserved in a Herculaneum papyrus of Philodemus and attributed to the school: that the divine is not to be feared, that death is not to be felt, that the good is easy to obtain, and that evil is endurable.

## The Pyrrhonian Path: To Be Treated by Abstention

For Pyrrho (c. 360-270 BCE) and the later Pyrrhonian skeptics, as reported chiefly by Sextus Empiricus, ataraxia is the result of epoché — the suspension of judgment. The logic is exactly the opposite of the Epicurean:

Anxiety for the skeptics does not disappear through correct knowledge but by ceasing to seek knowledge. As long as every argument has an equal argument against it (isostheneia), the seeker of truth is in perpetual torment. And whoever concedes that nothing tips the balance stops judging, and tranquility comes to him by accident, not by pursuit — on the model of the painter Apelles, who could not paint the foam of the horse and threw the sponge in anger, and the foam appeared.

The difference is thus fundamental: the Epicurean reassures with the answer, and the Pyrrhonist reassures by abandoning the question. This makes the Epicurean, in the skeptics' view, a partisan of a doctrine (dogmatikos), that is, he did not rid himself of the source of anxiety but replaced it with another doctrine.

## In Contrast with Stoicism

The Stoics have a goal that approaches it in description and differs in origin, and their more precise term is apatheia (ἀπάθεια): not the absence of feeling but the absence of false passions — that is, the disappearance of emotion built on a mistaken judgment about the estimation of what is good and evil.

| | Epicureanism | Pyrrhonism | Stoicism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Goal | Ataraxia | Ataraxia | Apatheia and a life in accord with nature |
| Path | Correct knowledge that dispels fear | Suspension of judgment | Correcting judgment about what is up to me and what is not |
| Stance toward the world | Withdrawal to the Garden | No stance | Participation in social duty |
| The decisive datum | Atomic physics | Equipollence of arguments | The division between what is in my power and what is not |

## Its Lasting Influence

- In cognitive therapy: the Hellenistic structure — that emotion is built on a judgment, and that correcting the judgment changes the emotion — is the structure of cognitive therapy; Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck both cited Stoic philosophers as precursors.
- In cognitive defusion: as for the Pyrrhonian path — ceasing to judge rather than correcting — it is widely compared with what the "third wave" of cognitive therapies does: acceptance and commitment therapy does not aim to dispute or correct the thought but to change one's relation to it, which resembles epoché in clinical conceptualization, though the comparison is an analogy and not a direct historical lineage.
- In the conception of mental health: defining health by the absence of disturbance rather than the presence of a positive state is a conception that long shaped psychiatry until positive psychology objected to it — that is, the dispute lies in the very name: ataraxia (negation) versus flourishing (affirmation).

## Contemporary Clinical Applications: Epoché and Anxiety Reduction

In contemporary cognitive and behavioral therapies, the Pyrrhonian practice of epoché (suspension of judgment) finds a functional counterpart in third-wave interventions, particularly Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT). While classical Beckian cognitive therapy aims to identify, dispute, and correct maladaptive judgments (mirroring the Stoic and Epicurean approach of evaluating beliefs), mindfulness and cognitive defusion techniques invite individuals to suspend epistemic and evaluative judgments regarding their distressing thoughts. Rather than determining whether an anxious, catastrophizing thought is true or false, the individual practices "defusion"—stepping back to view the thought as merely a transient psychological event rather than a literal fact requiring resolution. This descriptive stance interrupts obsessive rumination and the perpetual search for epistemic certainty, yielding an unforced reduction in anxiety analogous to the skeptic's accidental tranquility (*ataraxia* following *epoché*).

## Comparative Perspectives: Islamic and Eastern Traditions

### Ataraxia and Islamic Tawakkul
While Hellenistic ataraxia and the Islamic theological and spiritual concept of *tawakkul* (reliant trust in God) both seek liberation from anxiety (*qalaq*) and internal turmoil, they rest on distinct ontological grounds. In Epicureanism and Pyrrhonism, tranquility is attained through immanent, human-centered means—either rational demystification of nature or radical epistemic detachment. In contrast, *tawakkul*, as elaborated by classical thinkers such as Abu Hamid al-Ghazali in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* (Book of Monotheism and Reliance on God), achieves peace of mind through a conscious surrender to transcendent divine providence (*qadar*). Both traditions recognize that agonizing over circumstances beyond human control is the chief cause of misery, but where the Hellenistic sage seeks self-sufficiency (*autarkeia*) or suspension of conviction, the believer in *tawakkul* finds peace through profound relational trust in divine wisdom and benevolence.

### Ataraxia, Nirvana, and Upekkha in Buddhism
Comparative philosophers have noted suggestive parallels between Hellenistic tranquility and ancient Indian thought, likely facilitated by Pyrrho's journey to India with Alexander the Great's expedition. Within Buddhist psychology, ataraxia corresponds most closely to *upekkhā* (equanimity), the fourth of the sublime abodes (*brahmavihāras*). *Upekkhā* is an unshakeable, non-reactive balance of mind that neither grasps at pleasure nor recoils from pain, mirroring the affective neutrality of the Pyrrhonian skeptic. At a deeper soteriological level, *nirvāṇa* represents the total cessation of mental disturbance and suffering (*dukkha*), brought about by extinguishing the fires of craving (*taṇhā*) and ignorance. However, while ataraxia is an ethical and psychological poise maintained within mundane worldly life, *nirvāṇa* entails an ultimate release from cyclic existence (*saṃsāra*), grounded in the radical realization of impermanence (*anicca*) and non-self (*anattā*).

## What It Is Criticized For

- From Stoicism: that withdrawal to the Garden is abandonment of civic duty, and that a tranquility bought with isolation is not a virtue.
- From within the logic: that the Epicurean argument on death removes fear of the state of death but does not remove grief over the loss of life and what is in it — an objection that stands to this day in the philosophy of death.
- Against Pyrrhonism: that abstaining from every judgment is not livable, and that the skeptic in fact judges in his daily life even if he denies it.
- The class limit: that a tranquility based on restricting desires is easy for one who has his minimum; and the concept does not address one who lacks sustenance.

## Sources

- *Epistula ad Menoeceum* (Letter to Menoeceus) and *Kyriai Doxai* (Principal Doctrines), Epicurus — transmitted in Diogenes Laertius's *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, Book X; they are the most important of what survives of Epicurus in his own words, and contain the argument on death and the division of pleasures.
- *De rerum natura*, Lucretius, 1st century BCE — the complete verse exposition of Epicurean physics and its effect in removing fear, the fullest surviving source of the doctrine.
- *Πυρρώνειοι ὑποτυπώσεις* (*Outlines of Pyrrhonism*), Sextus Empiricus, 2nd century CE — Book I: the sequence of isostheneia, then epoché, then ataraxia, and the example of Apelles.
- *Diatribai* (Discourses) and *Encheiridion*, Epictetus — the opposing Stoic side: apatheia by correcting judgment, not by withholding it.
- A. A. Long and D. N. Sedley, *The Hellenistic Philosophers*, Cambridge University Press, 1987.
- Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* [The Revival of the Religious Sciences], Book 35: *Kitab al-Tawhid wa-l-Tawakkul* (English: *Faith in Divine Unity and Trust in Divine Providence*, trans. David B. Burrell, Islamic Texts Society, 2001).
