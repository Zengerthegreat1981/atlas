# Moral Internalism

Moral internalism is a position in the philosophy of moral psychology holding that there is a conceptual link (not merely an accidental causal connection) between sincerely making a moral judgment and having some motive, even a weak one, to act in accordance with that judgment. If someone sincerely says "helping the needy is a duty" but feels no inclination whatsoever to do so in any circumstance, internalists doubt that the judgment is sincere in the first place. Alternatively, they regard such a case as an exceptional anomaly that falls outside the normal use of moral language.

This position finds an echo in the Islamic moral tradition through the concept of "faith" (*iman*) itself. Many theologians and Sufis held that genuine inner assent is never separated from practical effect. They argued that mere theoretical knowledge of a ruling, without any effect on conduct, indicates weakness of faith or hypocrisy, not a principled separation of knowledge from motivation. Warnings in the tradition against knowledge that is not acted upon reflect an early awareness of the tension between abstract knowledge and action, although they were not formulated in a technical philosophical vocabulary.

In Western philosophy, R. M. Hare formulated an extreme internalist position called "universal prescriptivism" (developed in *The Language of Morals*, 1952, and *Freedom and Reason*, 1963). It holds that a moral judgment is prescriptive by nature, so that it is logically impossible for someone to make it without being bound, at least by default, to act accordingly. Michael Smith developed a weaker, rationalist version: a person who judges an action right is, on his account, disposed to be motivated to do it insofar as she is practically rational, since moral judgments concern what we would desire if fully rational. This leaves room for cases of weakness of will (*akrasia*), which do not invalidate internalism because they represent a departure from full rationality rather than a genuine logical exception. David Brink criticized internalism by appeal to the possibility of the amoralist, who makes moral judgments without being motivated, arguing that such a person is conceivable.

Internalists face a central challenge in explaining the Aristotelian phenomenon of weakness of will, in which the agent recognizes that an action is wrong but performs it anyway. This is a common psychological phenomenon, and it poses a difficult test for any theory that binds judgment to motivation closely. Internalists respond with a range of explanations that preserve the conceptual link while acknowledging that its practical force is weakened in cases of psychological deviation.

*Example:* a person who sincerely judges that they ought to help a struggling relative but keeps putting it off is a borderline case. Internalists treat this as weakness of will or a temporary failure of practical rationality rather than as a counterexample; externalists treat it as evidence that the connection is not necessary.

This debate is closely connected to the practice of moral education. If internalism is correct, teaching a child the meaning of right and wrong is, in principle, sufficient to generate a motive to act rightly. If externalism is correct, attention must be paid in parallel to cultivating the appropriate desires and emotions, not merely to explanation. Some contemporary philosophers, such as Shaun Nichols, have brought findings from empirical psychology, including studies of psychopathy, to bear on this debate; the interpretation of those findings is contested. Some are led to a middle position that recognizes a strong normal connection between moral judgment and motivation without treating it as a conceptual necessity.
