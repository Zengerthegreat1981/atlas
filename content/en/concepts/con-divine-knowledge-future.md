# Divine Knowledge and the Future

Every monotheistic theology faces the question: if God knows everything that will be, how can a human being be free? And if His knowledge of changing particulars entails change in His essence, how can He remain transcendent? The answer in the Islamic tradition is distributed between theology (*kalam*) and philosophy, and is intertwined with the question of divine decree (*qadar*).

## Decree in early theology

In the late first and early second centuries AH, Ma'bad al-Juhani and Ghaylan al-Dimashqi denied that God had decreed the acts of His servants. Traditional accounts attribute to the earliest Qadariyya the claim that "the matter is newly begun" (*amr unuf*), meaning that God does not know an act before it occurs. This was one of the gates of the debate between knowledge and freedom. The Mu'tazila offered a middle position that denies that God creates the acts of servants, and they placed justice among their fundamental principles, so that God does not punish a person for what he cannot do. Among the Ash'aris, God's knowledge was explained as eternal, encompassing what has been and what will be, and they said that the human being "acquires" (*iktisab*) the act that God creates. This formulation has a long history of dispute. W. Montgomery Watt studied this question in *Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam* (1948).

## Ibn Sina: knowledge in universals

Ibn Sina (d. 428 AH / 1037 CE) held that God knows particulars "in a universal manner," that is, through their causes and attributes rather than as things changing in time, in order to preserve the unity and immutability of the divine essence. Michael Marmura studied this view in an article in the *Journal of the American Oriental Society* (1962), and showed that it does not mean God is ignorant of particulars, but that He knows them in a manner that does not enter time.

## Al-Ghazali and Ibn Rushd

Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH / 1111 CE) counted this question among three issues for which he declared the philosophers to be unbelievers in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahafut al-falasifa*): the eternity of the world, God's knowledge of particulars, and the denial of the resurrection of bodies. Ibn Rushd replied in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* (*Tahafut al-tahafut*) that God's knowledge is neither universal nor particular, but a knowledge of another kind that cannot be measured against ours, because our knowledge is caused by existing things, whereas His knowledge is the cause of them.

## The best possible world

When al-Ghazali said that "nothing in possibility is more wondrous than what is," he opened a wide debate between those who accused him of fatalism and those who defended him. Eric Ormsby devoted a book to this question, *Theodicy in Islamic Thought* (1984).

## The limits of the question

The debate was never settled. The Ash'ari formulation may be accused of negating human choice, the Mu'tazili formulation of limiting God's power, and the Avicennan formulation of reducing the connection between God and the world. All of them start from the Qur'an, which admits both directions: it says "And you do not will except that God wills," and it also says "Whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve."
