# Divine Knowledge and the Future

Every monotheistic theology faces the question: if God knows everything that will be, how can a human being be free? And if His knowledge of changing particulars entails change in His essence, how can He remain transcendent? The answer in the Islamic tradition is distributed between theology (*kalam*) and philosophy, and is intertwined with the question of divine decree (*qadar*).

## Decree in early theology

In the late first and early second centuries AH, Ma'bad al-Juhani and Ghaylan al-Dimashqi denied that God had decreed the acts of His servants. Traditional accounts attribute to the earliest Qadariyya the claim that "the matter is newly begun" (*amr unuf*), meaning that God does not know an act before it occurs. This opened one line of the debate between knowledge and freedom. The Mu'tazila offered a middle position that denies that God creates the acts of servants, and they placed justice among their fundamental principles, so that God does not punish a person for what he cannot do. Among the Ash'aris, God's knowledge was explained as eternal, encompassing what has been and what will be, and they said that the human being "acquires" (*iktisab*) the act that God creates. This formulation has a long history of dispute. W. Montgomery Watt studied this question in *Free Will and Predestination in Early Islam* (1948).

## Ibn Sina: knowledge in universals

Ibn Sina (d. 428 AH / 1037 CE) held that God knows particulars "in a universal manner," that is, through their causes and attributes rather than as things changing in time, in order to preserve the unity and immutability of the divine essence. Michael Marmura studied this view in an article in the *Journal of the American Oriental Society* (1962), and argued that this does not mean God is ignorant of particulars, but that He knows them in a manner that does not enter time.

## Al-Ghazali and Ibn Rushd

Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH / 1111 CE) counted this question among three issues on which he charged the philosophers with unbelief in *Tahafut al-falasifa* (The Incoherence of the Philosophers): the eternity of the world, God's knowledge of particulars, and the denial of bodily resurrection. Ibn Rushd replied in *Tahafut al-tahafut* (The Incoherence of the Incoherence) that God's knowledge is not universal or particular in the human sense. Our knowledge is caused by existing things. His knowledge, Ibn Rushd argues, is their cause, and so it is not measured by our distinction between universal and particular.

## The best possible world

When al-Ghazali said that "there is nothing in possibility more wondrous than what is" (*laysa fi al-imkan abda' mimma kan*), he opened a long dispute. Some readers took the formula as fatalism. Others defended it as a claim about divine wisdom. Eric Ormsby followed that dispute through later commentators, well past the thirteenth century, in *Theodicy in Islamic Thought* (1984). The question of foreknowledge and human acts did not close with Ibn Rushd.

## The limits of the question

The debate was never settled. The Ash'ari formulation may be accused of negating human choice, the Mu'tazili formulation of limiting God's power, and the Avicennan formulation of reducing the connection between God and the world. All of them start from the Qur'an, which admits both directions: it says "And you do not will except that God wills," and it also says "Whoever wills, let him believe, and whoever wills, let him disbelieve."
