# Colonial Psychology

Colonial psychology is the study of the psychological life of colonialism: how colonial domination shapes the minds, identities, and emotions of both the colonised and the coloniser, and how psychology itself has served colonial power. It asks what it does to a person to be told, day after day, that their language, skin, and culture mark them as inferior — and what it does to the coloniser to live inside a system that requires them to deny the humanity of those they rule. The field spans psychiatry, psychoanalysis, social psychology, and anthropology, and it treats the colonial encounter not as a backdrop to psychological life but as one of its formative conditions.

## Origins and Development

The field's founding text is Frantz Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952), a study of the psychic injuries of racism in the French colonial world. Fanon, a psychiatrist from Martinique working in Algeria, argued that the Black person is made to experience themselves through the white gaze — to wear a "white mask" over a Black skin — and that this produces a divided self, haunted by inferiority and by the desire for recognition from those who withhold it. In *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961), written during the Algerian war of independence, he extended the analysis to collective psychology, arguing that decolonisation required not only political independence but a psychological unmaking of the colonial self, and that violence — a claim that remains fiercely debated — could be part of that unmaking.

Albert Memmi's *The Colonizer and the Colonized* (1957) offered a complementary portrait of the colonial relationship as a system in which both parties are deformed: the colonised is denied their history and language, while the coloniser, even the well-intentioned one, is trapped in a role that requires domination. Ashis Nandy's *The Intimate Enemy* (1983) deepened the analysis by showing how colonialism worked inside the minds of Indians and Britons alike, and how resistance could take the form of refusing the coloniser's categories rather than merely reversing them. Frantz Fanon's legacy was systematised by Hussein Abdilahi Bulhan in *Frantz Fanon and the Psychology of Oppression* (1985), which developed Fanon's insights into a framework for understanding oppression across contexts. Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), though a work of literary and cultural criticism, supplied a crucial companion argument: that Western knowledge about the East — including psychological knowledge — constructed the colonised as an object to be studied and managed.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One central debate concerns psychology's own complicity. Colonial administrations used psychological testing to classify colonised peoples as intellectually inferior, and psychiatry in the colonies often pathologised resistance as madness. Scholars in the field therefore ask whether psychology can be a tool of liberation or whether its categories are themselves colonial products — a question that has fuelled the development of liberation psychology, associated with Ignacio Martín-Baró's work in El Salvador in the 1980s, which sought a psychology written from the standpoint of the oppressed rather than the observer.

A second dimension is the psychology of the coloniser. Memmi and Nandy both insisted that colonialism damages the coloniser as well, producing bad faith, anxiety, and a dependence on the very people it subjugates. A third dimension concerns transmission: how colonial wounds pass across generations, through family silences, educational systems, and the internalised hierarchies of skin colour and language that persist after formal independence. Contemporary research on intergenerational trauma and on the mental health of postcolonial and diaspora communities extends this inquiry, while debates continue about how far psychological categories — trauma, self-esteem, identity — developed in Western contexts can capture experiences shaped by colonial histories.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Colonial psychology remains directly relevant to debates about mental health, education, and identity in postcolonial societies and in multicultural states. It informs critiques of diagnostic systems that pathologise culturally specific forms of distress, of school curricula that erase colonial histories, and of development and aid programmes that reproduce colonial relations of expertise. It also shapes the growing movement to decolonise psychology itself — to question whose knowledge counts, whose languages are used in therapy and research, and whose suffering is recognised as real.

Critics raise several cautions. Some argue that the field risks reducing complex political and economic structures to individual psychology, as if colonialism's harms were mainly matters of self-esteem. Others warn against treating "the colonised mind" as a single condition, when colonial experiences differed enormously across places, classes, and genders. And there is a methodological challenge: how to study inner life without imposing the very categories under criticism. The field's enduring contribution is its refusal to separate mind from history — its insistence that psychology is not a universal science of an abstract human nature, but a discipline whose objects, including the self, are made and remade in relations of power.
