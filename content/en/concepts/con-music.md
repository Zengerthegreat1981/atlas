# Music

Music is organised sound that people make, hear, and give meaning to — song, instrumental performance, rhythm, and dance are among its most widespread forms, yet no single definition captures what counts as music in every society. Anthropologists treat music not as a universal language but as a human behaviour shaped by culture: what is heard as beautiful, sacred, noisy, or not music at all depends on learned ideas about sound, the body, and social life. The anthropology of music, closely tied to the discipline of ethnomusicology, asks how musical practices organise relationships, express identity, mark ritual occasions, and carry memory across generations.

## Definition

Early anthropological definitions of music struggled with the fact that many languages have no single word equivalent to the English term "music." In some societies singing, drumming, and dancing are named as one activity; in others, sound-making is inseparable from speech, prayer, or healing. Alan P. Merriam argued that music should be studied as human behaviour in three connected layers: the sounds themselves, the behaviour of the people who make and hear them, and the concepts that guide that behaviour. This framework moved the field away from collecting tunes and toward understanding music as part of social action.

Anthropologists distinguish between music as a product — songs, instruments, and repertoires — and music as a process: performance, listening, learning, and judging. Instruments themselves carry social meaning. A drum may signal chiefly authority, a flute may be restricted to initiated men, and a particular song may belong to a lineage the way land or a name does. Music also crosses the boundary between the human and the non-human: in many traditions songs are received from spirits, ancestors, or the landscape, and singing is a way of communicating with them.

## Origins and Development

The study of music within anthropology developed alongside the recording technologies that made it possible. In the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, collectors used wax cylinders to document songs, often treating them as survivals of an earlier stage of culture. Franz Boas and his students pushed back against this evolutionary framing, insisting that each musical tradition be understood on its own terms. By the mid-twentieth century, ethnomusicology had emerged as a distinct field, combining musical analysis with ethnographic fieldwork.

John Blacking's research among the Venda of southern Africa, begun in the 1950s, and his book *How Musical Is Man?* (1973) were a turning point. Blacking argued that musical ability is a general human capacity, not a rare talent, and that the social value of music lies in what it allows people to feel and do together. His famous claim that music is "humanly organised sound" widened the field to include sounds that Western listeners might dismiss as noise. Around the same time, Steven Feld's work with the Kaluli of Papua New Guinea showed how song, weeping, and the sounds of the rainforest form a single expressive system, in which the cry of a bird could become the model for a lament.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One long-running debate concerns whether music is universal. On one side, the near-universal presence of song, rhythm, and dance suggests a shared human foundation, possibly rooted in the body, in infant-caregiver vocal interaction, and in the demands of coordinated group action. On the other side, anthropologists stress that the very categories of "music," "song," and "noise" are cultural. A performance that one group experiences as sacred may strike outsiders as tuneless, and the boundary between speech and song is drawn differently everywhere.

A second debate concerns meaning. Does music communicate like a language, with something like grammar and reference, or does it work through feeling, association, and social context? Feld's Kaluli research supported a view of music as deeply tied to place and emotion, while other scholars emphasised formal structure. Most anthropologists now accept that musical meaning is neither purely internal to the sounds nor purely imposed from outside: it is made in performance, in the shared history of listeners and performers.

A third dimension is power. Music can uphold authority — royal anthems, chiefly drumming, and state ceremonies — but it can also resist it. Work songs, protest songs, and satirical performances allow people to criticise rulers and employers in a form that is hard to punish. Gender is equally central: in many societies women and men have separate repertoires, instruments, or performance contexts, and the control of musical knowledge can be a source of status.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Music remains a key site where identity is made and contested. Diaspora communities use song to maintain ties to a homeland; national movements adopt folk repertoires as symbols of authenticity; and global music industries repackage local styles for world markets, raising questions about ownership and appropriation. The recording and streaming era has intensified these debates, as music travels faster than the social contexts that gave it meaning.

Contemporary anthropology also questions the field's own history. Early collections of non-Western music were often made under colonial conditions, with little credit to performers and little attention to their own theories of sound. Scholars now emphasise collaboration with musicians as co-theorists and the repatriation of archival recordings. The anthropology of music today therefore spans the laboratory and the village ceremony, united by the question of how organised sound becomes meaningful human action.
