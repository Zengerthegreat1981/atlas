# Commodification

Commodification is the process by which things, relationships, or activities that were not previously bought and sold become commodities — objects with a price, available for exchange on a market. Land that was held in common is fenced and sold; water that was drawn freely from a spring is bottled and priced; care for the sick, once given by kin, is purchased from an agency; a sacred ritual is performed for tourists who pay to watch. Commodification is not a single event but an ongoing, contested process: things move into the market, and sometimes move out again, as societies argue over what should be for sale and what should not. Anthropologists study commodification because it reveals what a society values, what it protects from the market, and how market logic transforms the things it touches.

## Definition

A commodity is something produced for exchange, with a value expressed in money. Commodification is the process of making something into a commodity. The concept, as developed in anthropology, rests on a key insight: whether something is a commodity is not a fixed property of the thing but a phase in its social life. Arjun Appadurai, in the volume *The Social Life of Things* (1986), argued that anthropologists should follow things through their careers — their production, exchange, use, and disposal — rather than classifying them once and for all as commodities or non-commodities. Within that volume, Igor Kopytoff's essay "The Cultural Biography of Things" made the central argument: commoditisation is a process, and a thing can move into and out of commodity status over time. A ring bought in a shop is a commodity; when it becomes a family heirloom, passed from grandmother to granddaughter and never sold, it has been singularised — removed from the market and given a unique, incommensurable value. If it is later sold at auction, it becomes a commodity again. Every society draws lines between what may be commodified and what must remain outside the market, and those lines vary: some societies forbid the sale of land, others of sacred objects, others of human organs. Commodification is therefore always a cultural and moral question, not only an economic one.

## Origins and Development

The idea has its roots in Karl Marx, who in *Capital* (1867) analysed the commodity as the elementary form of capitalist wealth, and showed how the commodity form conceals the social relations of labour that produced it — a process he called commodity fetishism. But the anthropological study of commodification received a major impetus from Appadurai's 1986 volume, which brought together studies of how things circulate in different societies and proposed the commodity as a phase rather than a type. Kopytoff's distinction between commoditisation and singularisation became the field's central vocabulary. Margaret Jane Radin, in *Contested Commodities* (1996), developed the legal and philosophical implications: she argued that some things — bodily integrity, sexuality, reproductive capacity, personal identity — are incompletely commodified, and that treating them as ordinary commodities damages personhood. Michael Sandel, in *What Money Can't Buy* (2012), extended the argument to contemporary life, cataloguing the expansion of markets into areas once governed by other norms — paying children to read books, selling the right to pollute, buying a place in a queue — and arguing that markets do not merely allocate goods but express and promote attitudes toward them.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns whether commodification corrupts or empowers. Critics argue that turning something into a commodity changes its meaning: care given for money is not the same as care given from love; a ritual performed for tourists is not the same as a ritual performed for the gods. Others argue that this view is nostalgic, and that commodification can be a source of income, recognition, and autonomy for marginalised people — artisans who sell their crafts, communities that sell their heritage to tourists, women who earn money from surrogacy. A second debate concerns the limits of commodification: is there anything that should never be for sale? Most societies agree that people themselves should not be commodities, yet slavery, human trafficking, and the sale of organs show that the line is contested in practice. A third debate concerns the process itself: commodification is rarely complete or irreversible. Things move in and out of commodity status, and people resist commodification through gifts, heirlooms, sacred objects, and the refusal to sell. Anthropologists study these resistances as closely as the commodification itself, because they show where a society draws its moral boundaries.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Commodification is one of the most visible processes of contemporary life. Healthcare and education have been increasingly organised as markets; personal data has become a commodity traded by technology companies; the human body is commodified through the sale of blood, sperm, eggs, and organs, and through surrogacy markets that span the globe. Cultural heritage is commodified through tourism, as rituals, crafts, and festivals are packaged for visitors, raising questions about authenticity and about who profits. The environment is commodified through carbon trading, in which the right to pollute is bought and sold. Each of these cases provokes the same argument: does the market extend human freedom, or does it erode the values that make life meaningful? The enduring insight is Kopytoff's: commodification is a process, not a state, and the biography of a thing — its movements into and out of the market — tells us how a society negotiates the boundary between what can be priced and what cannot.
