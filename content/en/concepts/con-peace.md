# Peace

Peace is commonly understood as the absence of war and violence, but anthropologists and peace researchers argue that this definition is too narrow. Peace also involves the presence of justice, the resolution of grievances, and the social conditions that make violence unnecessary. Societies maintain peace through diplomacy, mediation, ritual reconciliation, trade, intermarriage, and shared norms — and through the deliberate cultivation of nonviolent ways of settling disputes. The study of peace asks a question that is often overlooked: not why people fight, but how they manage not to fight, and what kinds of social order make sustained peace possible.

## Definition

Peace researchers distinguish between negative peace and positive peace, a distinction introduced by Johan Galtung in 1969. Negative peace is the absence of direct violence: no war, no killing, no physical assault. Positive peace is the absence of structural violence — the social arrangements, such as extreme inequality, discrimination, and exclusion, that harm people without any single person raising a hand. A society can be at negative peace while still suffering deep injustice. Anthropologists add a further dimension: peace as a lived, everyday practice. In many societies, peace is not a state that is achieved once and for all but a relationship that must be continually maintained through greetings, gift-giving, feasting, and the careful avoidance of insult. Peace can also be understood as a value: some societies, such as the Semai of Malaysia, place a strong cultural emphasis on nonviolence, restraint, and the avoidance of conflict, while others valorise martial honour.

## Origins and Development

Reflection on peace is ancient. Religious traditions — Buddhism, Christianity, Islam, and many Indigenous cosmologies — have all articulated visions of a peaceful order, whether as divine command, spiritual liberation, or cosmic harmony. The modern study of peace began in the aftermath of the Second World War, when scholars sought to understand the causes of war in order to prevent it. Raymond Aron, writing in 1962, analysed peace as a condition of international relations, shaped by the balance of power and the fear of war. Margaret Mead argued in a famous 1940 essay that warfare is a cultural invention, not a biological necessity — and that what is invented can be uninvented. Galtung's 1969 essay became a landmark of the developing field of peace research, distinguishing kinds of violence and kinds of peace. In the late twentieth century, anthropologists turned to the ethnography of peace: Douglas Fry's cross-cultural research, published in 2007, documented societies that rarely or never wage war, and the social mechanisms — cross-cutting ties, mediation, and conflict avoidance — that keep them peaceful.

## Key Debates

A central debate concerns human nature: are humans naturally violent, with peace requiring the restraint of the state, or naturally cooperative, with war arising from particular social conditions? The ethnographic record supports the second view more than is often assumed: many societies have lived for generations without organised warfare. A second debate concerns the relationship between peace and justice. Can there be genuine peace without justice — or is a peace built on oppression merely a pause between wars? This question shapes debates over peace agreements that grant amnesties to perpetrators of atrocities. A third debate concerns how peace is made: through top-down diplomacy and treaties, or through bottom-up reconciliation, truth-telling, and the rebuilding of everyday relationships? Finally, scholars debate whether peace is best understood as the work of states and institutions or as an everyday achievement of ordinary people — neighbours, traders, and families who choose, day after day, not to escalate a dispute.

## Contemporary Relevance

Peace is under pressure in the contemporary world. Interstate wars, civil conflicts, and communal violence persist, while new forms of violence — cyberattacks, economic coercion, and environmental destruction — challenge old definitions. At the same time, peacebuilding has become a major international enterprise, involving the United Nations, regional organisations, and local civil society groups in post-conflict reconstruction, disarmament, and reconciliation. Anthropologists contribute by showing how peace agreements made in capital cities succeed or fail in villages, and by documenting the everyday practices through which people rebuild trust after violence. The study of peace also speaks to domestic life: in societies marked by inequality and polarisation, the question of how people live together without violence is as urgent within states as it is between them.
