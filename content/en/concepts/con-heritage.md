# Heritage: The Past in the Present

**Heritage** is the past as it is selected, valued, and used in the present: the monuments, objects, practices, languages, and landscapes that a society treats as its inheritance and seeks to preserve for the future. Heritage is not simply what survives from the past; it is what the present chooses to keep, and that choice is always political. A cathedral may be preserved while a workers' housing estate is demolished; a folk song may be celebrated while a minority language is allowed to die. Anthropology studies heritage as a social process — a way of making identity, claiming territory, and negotiating power through the material and immaterial traces of the past.

## Definition and Development

The modern heritage regime was created in the twentieth century. The UNESCO Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage (1972) established the World Heritage List, giving international recognition to sites of "outstanding universal value" — from the pyramids of Giza to the historic centre of Rome. The Convention Concerning the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage (2003) extended protection to practices, expressions, and knowledge: oral traditions, performing arts, rituals, and traditional craftsmanship. Together the two conventions define the official framework within which states, experts, and international bodies decide what counts as heritage.

David Lowenthal, in *The Past Is a Foreign Country* (1985), showed that heritage is not the past itself but a present-day attitude toward it. Every generation remakes the past in its own image, preserving what serves its needs and forgetting what does not. Nostalgia, Lowenthal argued, is a modern condition: as societies change rapidly, the past becomes a source of comfort, identity, and authenticity that the present seems to lack.

## Key Contributions: Invention and Authorised Discourse

Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, in *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), demonstrated that many traditions presented as ancient are in fact recent creations. Scottish Highland dress, the British monarchy's ceremonial, and various national folk customs were invented or standardised in the nineteenth century to serve modern political purposes. Their argument does not mean that such traditions are fake; it means that tradition is always a creative act, and that claims of antiquity are themselves a political strategy.

Laurajane Smith, in *Uses of Heritage* (2006), gave the field its most influential critical concept: the **authorised heritage discourse**. Smith argued that heritage, as defined by experts and state institutions, privileges grand, material, ancient monuments — the castle, the cathedral, the archaeological site — and the authority of professionals to judge their value. This discourse marginalises the intangible, the everyday, and the subaltern: the heritage of working-class communities, indigenous peoples, and migrants, whose pasts do not take monumental form. "There is, really, no such thing as heritage," Smith wrote; there is a discourse about heritage that constitutes what we think, talk, and write about the past. Heritage, in her account, is not a thing but a cultural process of remembering, performing, and negotiating identity in the present.

## Examples

The old city of Jerusalem shows how heritage concentrates conflict: the same stones are claimed as the heritage of Jews, Christians, and Muslims, and every archaeological dig or restoration project becomes a political act. In Egypt, the temples of Abu Simbel were moved stone by stone in the 1960s to save them from the Aswan dam — a feat that also showed how heritage can be detached from its landscape and remade as a monument. In Morocco, the medina of Fez is listed as World Heritage, but its preservation raises questions about who benefits: tourists and the state, or the residents whose homes are regulated by conservation rules? Intangible heritage offers different examples: the Arabic coffee ceremony, inscribed by UNESCO, or the storytelling traditions of the *hakawati*, show that heritage can live in performance rather than in stone.

## Critique and Contemporary Questions

Heritage has been criticised as a form of exclusion. When a state defines its heritage, it also defines who belongs to the nation and whose past matters; minorities and former colonies often find their heritage ignored or appropriated. The restitution debate — whether objects taken during colonial rule, such as the Benin Bronzes or the Parthenon Marbles, should be returned — has made heritage a central issue of postcolonial justice.

Economics also shapes heritage. Tourism turns heritage into a resource, generating income but also freezing living places into museum displays and displacing residents. Climate change now threatens coastal sites and historic cities, forcing a choice between preserving fabric and adapting to new realities. Contemporary heritage studies therefore ask not only what should be preserved but for whom, by whom, and at what cost — and whether the very idea of heritage, born in European nationalism, can be remade to serve more inclusive and plural forms of belonging.
