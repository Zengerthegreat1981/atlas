# Interview: The Conversation as a Method of Knowing

An **interview** is a purposeful conversation in which a researcher asks questions in order to learn how people understand their lives, their work, and their world. It is the most widely used method in qualitative social research, and one of the oldest: the life-history interview, the ethnographic conversation, and the survey interview all share the same basic form — one person speaking, another listening and asking — but they differ profoundly in what they assume about knowledge, truth, and the relationship between researcher and researched. The interview is not simply a way of collecting facts; it is a social encounter in which meaning is made together.

## Definition and Development

The interview became a central research method in the twentieth century, as sociology and anthropology moved from the study of documents and statistics toward the study of lived experience. The Chicago School of sociology in the 1920s and 1930s used life-history interviews to study hobos, gangs, and immigrants, letting people tell their own stories in their own words. Anthropologists, meanwhile, made the open-ended conversation a core part of fieldwork, learning that the most revealing material often came not from formal questions but from the talk that surrounded them.

The method was professionalized in the second half of the century. Survey research developed the structured interview, with identical questions asked in identical order, so that answers could be compared and counted. Qualitative research developed the semi-structured and unstructured interview, in which the researcher follows the respondent's lead, probing for detail and meaning. The two forms rest on different assumptions: the structured interview treats the respondent as a source of information, while the qualitative interview treats the respondent as an interpreter of their own experience.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Steinar Kvale, in *InterViews: An Introduction to Qualitative Research Interviewing* (1996), gave the method its most influential theoretical account. Kvale distinguished two metaphors for the interviewer: the miner, who digs out nuggets of knowledge that are already there, waiting to be extracted; and the traveller, who journeys with the respondent and comes home with a story co-authored in conversation. The traveller metaphor, Kvale argued, better captures what actually happens: knowledge is not collected but constructed in the interaction between interviewer and interviewee, and the quality of the interview depends on the quality of that relationship.

James Spradley, in *The Ethnographic Interview* (1979), showed how the interview works in anthropology. The ethnographic interview is a series of friendly conversations into which the researcher gradually introduces ethnographic questions — descriptive questions about what people do, structural questions about how they organize their experience, and contrast questions about how they distinguish one thing from another. Spradley's point was that the interview is a skill: the researcher must learn to ask questions that make sense in the respondent's world, not only in the researcher's.

Irving Seidman, in *Interviewing as Qualitative Research* (1991), emphasized the interview as a way of understanding experience over time. His three-interview structure — life history, the details of present experience, and reflection on meaning — gives respondents time to reconstruct their experience and to think about what it means. Herbert Rubin and Irene Rubin, in *Qualitative Interviewing: The Art of Hearing Data* (1995), stressed listening: the interviewer's main task is not to ask clever questions but to hear what the respondent is actually saying, including what is left unsaid.

## Examples

Interviews are used across the social sciences. In sociology, they reveal how people experience unemployment, illness, migration, and work — experiences that statistics can count but cannot describe. In anthropology, they reveal the categories through which people understand kinship, illness, and the sacred. In oral history, they preserve the memories of people whose lives would otherwise leave no record. In evaluation research, they show how a policy is experienced by those it affects, often in ways its designers never anticipated.

The interview also has limits that researchers must acknowledge. People forget, exaggerate, and present themselves in the best light. They answer the question they think is being asked, not the one the researcher intended. And the interview itself changes what it studies: a person who has never thought about their experience in these terms may form an opinion in the course of answering. Good interviewing does not pretend these problems away; it treats them as data, asking why a person tells their story in this way, at this time, to this interviewer.

Interviewing across languages raises further problems. When researcher and respondent do not share a language, interpreters and translators become part of the encounter, and choices about translation can shape what is heard and reported; researchers are therefore encouraged to document how translation was handled. Differences in power between interviewer and respondent, whether of class, gender, nationality, or institutional position, likewise affect what people are willing to say.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics have argued that interviews privilege talk over action, and that what people say they do is a poor guide to what they actually do. Ethnographers answer that talk is itself a form of action, and that the interview, combined with observation, is stronger than either alone. Feminist researchers have argued that the traditional interview reproduces a hierarchy between the knowing researcher and the passive respondent, and have called for more reciprocal, conversational forms. Others have warned that the interview can exploit: respondents give their time and their stories, and researchers take the credit.

The method remains central. Contemporary research applies it in new settings: video interviews with people across the world, interviews conducted by text message and social media, and interviews with elites who are skilled at managing their image. The questions have not changed: who is speaking, to whom, under what conditions, and what does their account reveal about the world they live in? The interview remains the most direct way social science has of learning how the world looks from the inside.
