# Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT)

## Definition

**Mentalization-Based Treatment** (MBT) is a structured psychotherapy approach developed in the United Kingdom by the psychiatrist **Anthony Bateman** and the psychoanalytic psychologist **Peter Fonagy**. It is aimed primarily at treating **borderline personality disorder**. It is based on strengthening the patient's capacity for **"mentalizing,"** meaning the ability to understand one's own behavior and that of others as a reflection of underlying mental states (intentions, feelings, beliefs, desires), rather than as direct, surface-level actions.

## Theoretical Basis

The treatment rests on the hypothesis that **mentalizing** is a psychological capacity that develops gradually in early childhood within a **secure attachment** relationship, when the child's caregiver reflects the child's inner states accurately enough to help the child build an internal model of his or her own mind and the minds of others. Bateman and Fonagy propose that **failure to acquire this capacity in a stable way**, as a result of disturbed early attachment or trauma, is the **core underlying problem** behind borderline personality disorder. The patient loses the ability to mentalize specifically at moments of high emotional arousal (such as a conflict between people). Their understanding of the intentions of others, and of their own intentions, then collapses, and they resort to emotionally charged and inaccurate interpretations (such as assuming that the other person intends deliberate harm). These interpretations feed the mood instability, impulsivity, and relational difficulties that characterize the disorder.

## Key Features

- **Immediate therapeutic goal**: not to change the content of particular thoughts or behaviors (as in cognitive behavioral therapy), but to **restore the general mental capacity** for mentalizing in the moment, especially during emotional intensity.
- **Stance of the therapist**: in MBT the therapist adopts a curious **"not-knowing" stance**. They explore the patient's mental state with genuine curiosity rather than offering ready-made explanations, thereby modeling the process of mentalizing for the patient.
- **Therapeutic structure**: it is usually delivered within a combined program of weekly individual and group sessions. In the original partial hospitalization program treatment lasted up to eighteen months; later outpatient adaptations are typically delivered over about a year.
- **Empirical basis**: the first randomized controlled trial of MBT's effectiveness was published in 1999 (within a partial hospitalization program). The trial compared the program with standard psychiatric care and found greater improvement in the MBT group; follow-up reports (including a 2003 analysis of health service costs) suggested the approach was not more costly than general psychiatric care.

## Impact

MBT is regarded as one of the **evidence-supported psychotherapies** for borderline personality disorder, alongside dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) and transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP). Its application has since expanded to other conditions (adolescent self-harm, eating disorders, antisocial personality disorder), with a smaller evidence base in these areas. The concept of "mentalizing" itself has had a wide influence on contemporary attachment theory and developmental psychology.

## Limits

The treatment requires specialized training and an institutional structure (a treatment team, regular supervision) that may not be easily available outside specialized centers. The volume of rigorous randomized evidence, although positive, remains smaller than that for dialectical behavior therapy, which is the more widely used and researched treatment worldwide.
