# Trauma (Existential Perspective)

Trauma, in existential terms, is the rupture of the assumptive world: the largely unspoken narrative by which a person understood themselves and the world. A traumatic event reveals that the world is not safe, that control is limited and that meaning cannot be guaranteed. The existential approach asks what realities of human existence the event has laid bare.

## Definition

Clinically, trauma refers to exposure to events involving actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, and to the lasting reactions that may follow. Posttraumatic stress disorder entered the diagnostic manuals in 1980 (DSM-III). The existential perspective does not replace this account. It adds a reading of what trauma means for a person's relation to the world, to others and to their own mortality.

## Origins

The idea of an "assumptive world" comes from Colin Murray Parkes's work on bereavement. Ronnie Janoff-Bulman's *Shattered Assumptions* (1992) proposed that trauma shatters basic beliefs, such as that the world is benevolent and meaningful and that the self is worthy, and that recovery involves rebuilding a more complex set of beliefs. Existential writers such as Yalom and van Deurzen link such shattering to the "givens" of existence: death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness, which trauma can force into awareness. Viktor Frankl, writing out of his experience of the camps, emphasized the human capacity to take a stance toward even extreme suffering. Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) gave a widely influential account of the stages of recovery: safety, remembrance and mourning, and reconnection.

## Trauma and Loss of Groundedness

Where cognitive approaches treat trauma as memory to be processed, and psychodynamic approaches as a wound to be healed, existential therapy asks: what existential realities has trauma exposed, and what assumption has been shattered? This does not minimize the neurobiological and psychological reality of trauma or the value of trauma-focused treatments. It acknowledges that part of healing may involve integrating existential knowledge: that vulnerability is constitutive of human life, that randomness and cruelty are real, and that safety is always provisional.

Analytical psychology offers a related reading. Donald Kalsched's *The Inner World of Trauma* (1996) describes how, after overwhelming early trauma, the psyche may split, with a protective inner system that guards the vulnerable core of the person yet can also attack hope and closeness. Such accounts are interpretive models used by some clinicians rather than established findings.

## Integration vs. Recovery

The existential framing suggests the goal is not "recovery," a return to the pre-trauma state, but integration: developing a realistic and more complex understanding of self, others and world. A traumatized person has acquired a kind of knowledge, and healing involves carrying it rather than erasing it. Tedeschi and Calhoun's work on posttraumatic growth describes how some people report changed priorities and deeper relationships after trauma, though growth is not universal and does not require suffering.

## In Practice

Existential therapy is generally used alongside, not instead of, evidence-based trauma treatment. Established trauma-focused therapies, including trauma-focused cognitive-behavioral therapy, prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy and EMDR, have research support, and clinical guidelines generally recommend them. An existential perspective can help a person make sense of what the event has meant and how to live afterward. Establishing safety and stability comes first, and approaches are adapted to the person's readiness.

## Criticisms and Limits

Critics note that existential interpretations of trauma may over-philosophize an experience that is also bodily and neurobiological, and may suggest that trauma carries a lesson, which can feel invalidating when harm was inflicted by another person. Talk of growth can pressure survivors to find meaning they do not feel. Some popular claims about trauma and the brain, as in van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014), have also been questioned by researchers. Anyone struggling with trauma symptoms should be encouraged to seek qualified professional help.
