# Polytheism

Polytheism is the belief in and worship of many gods. Where monotheism centres on a single, exclusive deity, polytheism distributes divine power across a pantheon: gods of sky and sea, of war and harvest, of the household and the city, each with a character, a domain, and a following. The gods of ancient Greece, Rome, Egypt, India, and Mesopotamia are the classic examples, but polytheistic systems also appear in Africa, Oceania, and the Americas. Polytheism is not a single religion but a way of organising the sacred, and anthropologists study it to understand how societies imagine power, order, and the cosmos.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of polytheism began with the sociology of religion. Emile Durkheim argued in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) that religion is society worshipping itself: the gods are collective representations of the social order, and ritual renews the group's solidarity. His main case was Australian totemism rather than a developed pantheon, but later scholars drew on his approach to relate plural divinities to plural social groups and functions. It treated religion of every kind as a social fact rather than a product of ignorance, and it shaped a century of debate.

Walter Burkert's *Greek Religion* (1985) gave an influential account of a polytheistic system in practice. Greek religion, he showed, was not a theology but a set of practices: sacrifices, festivals, oracles, and the tending of sanctuaries, organised around local gods whose identities shifted from city to city. The Greek pantheon was not a fixed hierarchy but a flexible network. Burkert stressed that polytheism worked precisely because it was open: new gods could be added, old ones reinterpreted, and local traditions absorbed without breaking the system.

Jan Assmann's essay "Monotheism and Polytheism" (2004), developing his "Mosaic distinction" from *Moses the Egyptian* (1997), reframed the contrast between the two. He argued that the crucial difference is not the number of gods but the claim to exclusive truth: monotheism insists that only one god is true and all others false, while polytheism tolerates translation between gods, recognising the gods of others as real under different names. This tolerance, Assmann suggested, made the rise of exclusive monotheism a profound break in human history.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns whether polytheism is a stage or a type. Nineteenth-century evolutionists, notably Edward Tylor, arranged religions in a sequence from animism through polytheism to monotheism, treating each as a step toward rationality. Modern anthropologists reject that ladder: polytheism is not a primitive version of monotheism but a distinct organisation of the sacred, with its own logic and durability. Hinduism, with its many deities held within a single tradition, shows that polytheism and a unifying theology can coexist, while the persistence of spirit cults in Christian and Muslim societies shows that monotheism rarely eliminates plurality in practice.

A second dimension is the structure of the pantheon. H. S. Versnel argued in *Inconsistencies in Greek and Roman Religion* (1990) that polytheistic systems are inherently inconsistent: gods have overlapping domains, contradictory myths, and local variants, and worshippers live comfortably with these contradictions. The pantheon is not a coherent system but a repertoire, and its power lies in its flexibility. Different gods serve different needs, and the same worshipper may appeal to one god for healing, another for victory, and a third for the safety of a voyage, without demanding that the gods agree with one another.

A third dimension is the political role of the gods. In Mesopotamia and Egypt, the gods legitimated kingship: the king was the god's steward, and the temple was the centre of the economy. In Greece, the gods belonged to cities, and civic festivals bound citizens together. In Rome, the state adopted foreign gods and emperors were themselves deified, using polytheism's openness as an instrument of empire. Polytheism thus raises the question of how divine plurality relates to political plurality: whether many gods suit a world of many cities and peoples, and whether one god suits a world ruled by one empire.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Polytheism has not disappeared. Hinduism, which includes polytheistic as well as monotheistic and monist understandings of its many deities, remains one of the world's largest religions, and indigenous religions across Africa, Asia, and the Americas continue to honour many spirits and deities. In the West, modern pagan movements, from Wicca to the revival of Norse and Greek worship, have reclaimed polytheism as a living practice, often linking it to environmentalism and feminism. In practice, the lived religion of most monotheists is more plural than their theology admits.

The study of polytheism matters because it challenges the assumption that religion must be exclusive to be serious. Polytheistic systems show that people can hold multiple sacred allegiances, negotiate between gods, and tolerate religious difference without insisting on a single truth. In a world where religious conflict is often framed as a clash of absolutes, the polytheistic past offers a different model: one in which the sacred is many, the divine is negotiable, and the gods, like the societies that worship them, are diverse.
