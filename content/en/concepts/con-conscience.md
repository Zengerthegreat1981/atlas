# Conscience

## Meaning and Definition

Conscience is the inner faculty that a person experiences when they act or refrain from acting. It holds them accountable for whether their act conforms to or departs from the moral standards they believe in, producing a sense of satisfaction or remorse (the "reproach of conscience") depending on the case. Three main currents dispute its nature. Is it a transcendent divine voice implanted in human nature? Is it an independent rational faculty that perceives good and bad? Or is it merely the product of accumulated upbringing and social conditioning, as Freud held with his concept of the "superego"?

## Conscience in the Islamic Tradition

The Holy Qur'an expressed this meaning through the term *al-nafs al-lawwama* (the self-reproaching soul), which God swore by in the verse "I swear by the self-reproaching soul." It is the soul that blames its owner for shortcomings even after repentance, in contrast to *al-nafs al-ammara bi-l-su'* (the soul that commands evil) and *al-nafs al-mutma'inna* (the tranquil soul). Commentators explained this self-reproaching soul as the origin of what is called "conscience" today.

Theologians disagreed about the source of this faculty. The Mu'tazila held that natural reason perceives the goodness and badness of actions independently, and that the self's reproach is simply the effect of this rational perception. The Ash'aris, by contrast, held that this inner sense does not make revelation unnecessary, since conscience may err if it is not refined by the Shari'a. Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, discussed "self-accounting" (*muhasaba*) and "self-monitoring" (*muraqaba*) as voluntary training that strengthens the voice of the self-reproaching soul and makes it a faithful guardian of the believer's conduct. He held that neglecting this daily self-accounting gradually kills conscience, until its owner becomes accustomed to sin without reproach.

## Conscience in Western Philosophy

In medieval Christianity, Thomas Aquinas distinguished *synderesis*, the innate habit of grasping the first principles of practical reason, from conscience (*conscientia*), the application of those principles to particular acts. In modern philosophy, Joseph Butler described conscience as the "supreme faculty" that governs and directs the other psychological motives toward the good. Freud, by contrast, offered a reductive psychological explanation. He saw conscience as the product of the internalization of paternal and social authority during childhood, forming a psychic structure called the "superego" that monitors the "ego" and punishes it with feelings of guilt. This explanation strips conscience of any objective moral authority and makes it a purely psychological effect amenable to causal explanation.

## Objections and Examples

It is objected that conscience is not a reliable standard, since it varies with cultural upbringing. A fanatic may feel at ease in conscience while committing a manifest injustice. Defenders of conscience's authority reply that this does not negate its existence. It calls, rather, for refining it through education and the Shari'a, not for abolishing it.

*Example:* an employee is asked to falsify a financial report and refuses despite suffering a material loss, moved by an inner voice that tells them the act is wrong. This is a model example of conscience's restraining function.
