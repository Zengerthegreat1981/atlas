# Mood Stabilizers & Lithium

## Definition

Mood stabilizers are medications used to treat manic episodes and to prevent the fluctuations of bipolar disorder. The most important is lithium; others include anticonvulsants such as valproate, carbamazepine, and lamotrigine, as well as some antipsychotics. There is no precise definition of the class: it is a clinical designation for a drug that treats the manic or depressive pole and prevents relapse without switching the patient to the opposite pole.

## Discovery of Lithium

The Australian physician John Cade worked at the Bundoora Repatriation Mental Hospital in Melbourne. He injected guinea pigs with urine from patients with mania and then tested lithium urate, a soluble form of uric acid, and observed that the animals became placid; he concluded, correctly in practice, that lithium itself was the active agent. He tried it on patients with mania and published his results in the *Medical Journal of Australia* in 1949 ("Lithium salts in the treatment of psychotic excitement"). Mogens Schou and colleagues in Denmark conducted an early controlled clinical trial in 1954, published in the *Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery and Psychiatry*. Baastrup and Schou then reported the preventive effect of lithium in 1967, and further studies followed. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved lithium for the treatment of mania in 1970, later than several other countries, with concerns about toxicity and the limited commercial incentive for an unpatentable drug often cited as reasons for the delay.

## Evidence

Lithium is a cornerstone of long-term prevention in bipolar disorder. Geddes and colleagues reviewed lithium trials in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* in 2004 and found a substantial reduction in the risk of relapse. Cipriani and colleagues showed in the *BMJ* in 2013 that lithium is associated with a lower risk of suicide in mood disorders. In the BALANCE trial (*Lancet*, 2010), lithium alone and lithium combined with valproate were superior to valproate alone for relapse prevention. Goodwin and Jamison produced a comprehensive reference for the field in *Manic-Depressive Illness* (Oxford University Press, 1990; second edition 2007) (see the entry on the bipolar spectrum).

## Monitoring and Risks

Lithium has a narrow therapeutic window, so its blood level is monitored, as are kidney and thyroid function. Its side effects include tremor, thirst, frequent urination, and nausea, and toxicity is aggravated by dehydration, the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and some diuretics. Exposure during pregnancy may be associated with an increased risk of heart defects. Valproate carries a substantial risk of fetal malformations and impaired neurodevelopment, and regulatory guidance in several countries restricts its use in women of childbearing potential. Lamotrigine prevents depression more than mania and requires slow titration to avoid serious rash. Fasting during Ramadan raises dehydration and blood-level monitoring concerns for patients taking lithium, which clinicians address individually (see the entry on drug side-effects management).

## Historical Background

These medications emerged from twentieth-century laboratories and clinics rather than from the Arabic medical heritage. However, classical Arabic medicine described mania and melancholia as two opposite conditions and treated them with diet, water, and music (see the entry on major depressive disorder). The alternation between high and low episodes remains a shared clinical key between the older and the modern descriptions.

## Limits

Responses vary: a large proportion of patients do not improve with lithium alone, and some reject it because of its severe effects. Abrupt discontinuation of lithium has been associated with an increased risk of relapse and suicidal behavior.
