# Social Capital

Social capital names the resources people draw from relationships: trust, obligations, information, and the standing that comes from being known. Unlike money or tools, it is not stored in a bank or a workshop. It exists in ties between persons and in the norms that make those ties usable. A household with kin who lend money, a trader with partners who extend credit, or a neighbourhood where people watch one another's children is rich in social capital even when cash is scarce. Anthropologists met the idea in the study of kinship, gift exchange, and patronage long before the phrase became a policy slogan.

## Three lines of definition

Pierre Bourdieu, in "The Forms of Capital" (1986), treated social capital as the sum of actual or potential resources linked to a durable network of mutual acquaintance and recognition. Membership in a family, a club, or an elite school is a credential. The volume of social capital depends on the size of the network and on the economic and cultural capital each member can mobilise. For Bourdieu the concept belongs to a theory of class: networks reproduce advantage, and the labour of keeping them up — visits, gifts, introductions — is real work.

James Coleman, in "Social Capital in the Creation of Human Capital" (1988), defined the term by its function. Aspects of social structure that make action easier count as social capital: obligations and expectations, information channels, and norms backed by sanctions. Closure, the fact that people in a network know one another, lets them enforce those norms. Coleman stressed a public-good quality. Parents who know one another help one another's children, including families who did little to build the network.

Robert Putnam, in *Making Democracy Work* (1993) and *Bowling Alone* (2000), scaled the idea up to regions and countries. Civic associations, generalised trust, and norms of reciprocity, he argued, make government more effective and everyday cooperation cheaper. He distinguished bonding ties, which join people who are alike, from bridging ties, which cross lines of class, ethnicity, or belief.

## Anthropological uses

Fieldworkers have long described the same facts without always using the label. Gift cycles create debt and credit. Marriage alliances join groups. Patron-client ties move jobs, protection, and votes. What the concept adds is a way to compare those arrangements with schooling, firms, and voluntary associations, and to ask when a tie is an asset for a person and when it is a constraint.

Nan Lin's *Social Capital* (2001) kept the focus on resources embedded in networks that actors can access for purposive action. That formulation sits easily with ethnographic attention to who can ask whom for what, and at what cost in honour or future obligation. It also keeps social capital distinct from the trust or the income it sometimes produces.

## Limits

Alejandro Portes, in a 1998 review, warned that the term was being stretched until it explained everything and therefore little. Sources, the relationships themselves, were being confused with effects such as school success or lower crime. Dense networks also have a dark side: they exclude outsiders, demand conformity, and can underwrite corruption or organised crime. A closed kin group may help members find work and at the same time block marriages, dissent, or trade with strangers. Bonding capital can deepen a boundary that bridging capital would cross.

Level of analysis remains unsettled. Bourdieu wrote mainly of persons and families investing in connections. Putnam wrote of communities and nations that "have" more or less social capital. Coleman tried to link the two. Policy that treats volunteering as a cure for poverty can mistake a symptom of weak public institutions for a cause, and can ask people to network their way out of conditions that networks did not create.

The word is still useful when it is kept narrow: access to resources through social ties, unevenly distributed, costly to maintain, and capable of harm as well as help.
