# State Formation

State formation is the process by which centralised political authority emerges: the creation of institutions that govern a territory and its people, collect taxes, make law, and hold a monopoly on legitimate force. Anthropologists distinguish the state from other political forms, bands, tribes, and chiefdoms, by its scale, its bureaucracy, and its capacity to reach into everyday life. The earliest states appeared independently in Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley, China, Mesoamerica, and the Andes. To study state formation is to ask how societies cross the threshold into centralised rule, and what that crossing costs and creates.

## Origins and Development

The modern theory of state formation was shaped by Morton Fried's *The Evolution of Political Society* (1967), which distinguished egalitarian, ranked, and stratified societies and argued that the state arises with social stratification: once a society divides into classes with unequal access to resources, a state is needed to maintain that order. Elman Service offered a different account in *Origins of the State and Civilization* (1975), proposing a sequence from band to tribe to chiefdom to state, in which each stage integrated larger populations through more complex organisation. For Service, the state emerged to coordinate irrigation, trade, defence, and redistribution.

Robert Carneiro proposed the most influential single theory in "A Theory of the Origin of the State" (1970). He argued that states arise where circumscribed environments, such as river valleys bounded by desert or mountains, prevent defeated peoples from fleeing, so that warfare leads to conquest and the subordination of one group by another, and then to the institutions needed to govern the conquered. Environmental circumscription, population pressure, and warfare were, in his account, the engine of state formation, and his theory explained why states appeared first in fertile valleys rather than in open plains. Henri Claessen and Peter Skalnik's edited volume *The Early State* (1978) gathered comparative studies that tested these theories against cases from Africa, Asia, and the Americas, showing that no single cause fit every case and that ideology, trade, and religion mattered alongside warfare and ecology.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The central debate concerns the causes of state formation: conflict or integration. Conflict theories, following Carneiro and Fried, see the state as an instrument of domination, created by conquerors to extract surplus and enforce inequality. Integration theories, following Service, see it as a manager of common problems, built to coordinate irrigation, redistribute food, and settle disputes. Most anthropologists now accept that both processes operate together: the state coordinates and coerces, and its history is the history of that tension. Charles Tilly captured the coercive side in "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime" (1985), arguing that European states were built by rulers who extracted resources through war and then offered protection in return.

A second debate concerns the definition of the state itself. Max Weber's definition, a monopoly on the legitimate use of force within a territory, remains influential, but anthropologists note that many early states lacked clear borders, standing armies, or written law, and that power was often personal, ritual, or fragmented rather than bureaucratic. The early states of Africa and Southeast Asia, with their fluid boundaries and overlapping sovereignties, challenge the European model of the state as a bounded container. Anthropologists therefore distinguish between the state as an idea, the state as an institution, and the state as an everyday encounter with officials, taxes, and documents.

A third dimension is the experience of being governed. James Scott's work on societies that evaded the state, notably *The Art of Not Being Governed* (2009), showed that many peoples in the uplands of Southeast Asia actively resisted incorporation, choosing mobility, dispersed settlement, and oral traditions to remain outside state control. State formation, in this view, is a project that some peoples embraced and others fled. The state's expansion brought literacy, infrastructure, and security, but also taxation, conscription, and the loss of autonomy, and its history must be written from below as well as from above.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

State formation continues today. New states are born from secession and decolonisation, fragile states struggle to govern their territories, and supranational institutions like the European Union raise the question of whether the state is being transcended or transformed. Anthropologists study the state ethnographically, following bureaucrats, police, and border officials to see how state power is actually exercised. They also study the afterlives of state formation: the borders drawn by colonial powers, the nationalisms built on them, and the conflicts that erupt where the state's authority is contested.

The concept's value lies in its insistence that the state is made, not given. It emerged at particular times and places, through violence and negotiation, and it can weaken, fragment, or be remade. Understanding state formation means understanding the most powerful political institution in human history, not as a timeless necessity but as a historical achievement, one that continues to shape who governs and who is governed.
