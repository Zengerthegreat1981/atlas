# Pepper's World Hypotheses

## Context

In *World Hypotheses: A Study in Evidence* (1942), the American philosopher Stephen C. Pepper (1891-1972) argued that metaphysical systems are best understood as elaborations of a small number of **root metaphors**, each drawn from a common-sense area of experience and then extended to interpret everything. A "world hypothesis" is a system of categories developed from one such root metaphor and tested against evidence by its **scope** (how much it can interpret) and **precision** (how exactly it does so). Pepper's practical conclusion was that no single hypothesis is adequate to everything and that each should be judged on its own terms, which makes the scheme a form of structured pluralism.

## The Four Relatively Adequate Hypotheses

Pepper identified four hypotheses he regarded as relatively adequate:

1. **Formism**: root metaphor of similarity, form, and kind. The world consists of forms or universals instantiated in particulars. Associated with Plato and Aristotle.
2. **Mechanism**: root metaphor of the machine. The world is a system of parts in lawful interaction. Associated with Democritus, Galileo, Descartes, Hobbes, Locke, and Newtonian science.
3. **Contextualism**: root metaphor of the historic event, the act in its setting. Reality is a network of ongoing, changing events understood through their context. Associated with pragmatists such as Peirce, James, Dewey, and Mead.
4. **Organicism**: root metaphor of the integrated, developing organism. The world is a unified whole realised progressively. Associated with Schelling, Hegel, Bradley, and Royce.

Pepper also discussed **animism** and **mysticism** as less adequate hypotheses, treating them as comparatively poor in precision or scope. Placements of individual thinkers under each heading are Pepper's own and have been disputed; many philosophers combine metaphors.

## Uses in Psychology

Pepper's scheme has been applied to the philosophical assumptions behind psychological theories:

- **Mechanism** is commonly used to characterise stimulus-response and other machine-like models of behaviour.
- **Organicism** and **mechanism** have been contrasted as models of human development, as in Reese and Overton's paper "Models of Development and Theories of Development" (1970).
- **Contextualism** has been developed explicitly in contextual behaviour science. Hayes, Hayes, and Reese (1988) argued that functional contextualism provides the philosophical core of behaviour analysis, and Hayes's work on Acceptance and Commitment Therapy draws on this lineage.
- Rosnow and Georgoudi (eds.), *Contextualism and Understanding in Behavioral Science* (1986), collected contextualist treatments across the field.

## The Place of the Concept in the Bridge

The concept links philosophy of science to the practice of psychological research: it offers a way to make explicit the metaphysical assumptions that different schools of psychology carry, and to see why their disputes sometimes concern what counts as an adequate explanation rather than a matter of fact. Classifying a psychological school under a single hypothesis is an interpretive judgment, and one school may draw on more than one.

## Limits

Pepper's four-fold scheme is one classification among others and was criticised as too neat; his own appraisal of root metaphors involves philosophical choices. It should not be treated as an empirical test of theories.
