# Autonomy in Kantian Ethics

A founding concept in Kant's philosophy, set out in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) and reformulated by contemporary Kantian philosophers such as Christine Korsgaard, Onora O'Neill and Barbara Herman. Autonomy is the capacity of the self to assign the moral law to itself through practical reason, rather than submitting to desires or external authorities.

## Definition in Kant

- "Autonomy" (Autonomie): the property of the will of being the source of its own law. Opposed to "heteronomy" (Heteronomie): the will that receives its law from outside.
- The moral law is not imposed by God, nature or society; reason itself imposes it on itself.
- "Act only on that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law" (the first formula).

## The Contemporary Formulation (Korsgaard, O'Neill)

- **Autonomy as practical rationality**: the moral act is an act in which the agent chooses laws that can be rationally universalized.
- **"Moral agency"**: the moral act expresses a coherent "agency", not an empirical "self".
- **"Autonomy as a human principle" (the Humanity formulation)**: "Act so that you treat humanity, in your own person and in the person of another, as an end and never merely as a means."
- **Animals**: in *Fellow Creatures* (2018) Korsgaard argues, from Kantian premises, that sentient animals should be treated as ends in themselves.

## The Contemporary Debate

- **O'Neill** distinguishes Kant's "principled autonomy", that is, the agent's commitment to principles that every rational being can share, from "individual autonomy", which means freedom of personal choice.
- **Barbara Herman** (*The Practice of Moral Judgment*, 1993) develops a Kantian account of moral judgment and respect for persons.

## Kantian Autonomy and Liberal Autonomy

Kantian autonomy is easily confused with the contemporary liberal notion of autonomy as freedom to pursue one's own preferences. For Kant the autonomous will legislates only the moral law, which is universal, and a choice made on inclination is heteronomous even if it is freely made.

## Kantian Autonomy and Existentialist Freedom

Existentialist freedom, as in Sartre, is the groundless freedom of a being that must choose itself without any given essence or universal law. Kantian autonomy is also self-legislation, but what reason legislates is constrained by the requirement of universalisability and by respect for humanity as an end. The two share the idea that the human being is not simply determined by nature, but they differ over whether freedom has a rational structure that guides it.

## From Self-Legislation to Medical Ethics

In the second half of the 20th century the term moved from Kant's philosophy to medical ethics, and "patient autonomy" became a first principle in informed consent. But the concept of the autonomous patient who chooses what he wants differs from Kant's autonomy, which does not mean doing what I want, but submission to a law that reason lays down for itself. Onora O'Neill, in her book on autonomy and trust in bioethics, drew attention to this difference, and held that turning autonomy into a mere right of choice weakens mutual trust between patient and physician. An example is that consent to a treatment after signing a long form does not necessarily mean that the patient understood the options and used his practical reason.

## Reception and Criticism

Hegel objected in the *Philosophy of Right* that the Kantian moral law is formal and empty, and does not guide action with specific content. Critics from the feminist school, among them those influenced by the ethics of care, said that the picture of the autonomous agent stripped of relationships ignores the human being's dependence on others. Skeptics denied that self-legislation is possible without a metaphysical foundation. Korsgaard defended autonomy by tying it to the practical identity of the agent: a human being constitutes himself when he adopts principles he cannot give up without losing himself. Schneewind, in his book on the invention of autonomy, treated how the concept changed historically from a discussion about obedience and moral authority to the idea of self-legislation.

## Comparison with the Islamic Tradition

What the Mu'tazila called rational moral assessment of good and bad (al-tahsin wa al-taqbih al-aqliyyan) comes close to an independent meaning of morality, since they held that reason perceives the good and the bad before revelation arrives, while the Ash'arites said that the good is what the law made good. George Hourani studied this topic in the work of Qadi Abd al-Jabbar. The Ash'arite position can be compared with what Kant calls "heteronomy", although the analogy is loose, because Kant does not make reason contradictory to faith but a source of independent morality, while the Mu'tazila made reason an instrument of disclosure, not a legislator.
