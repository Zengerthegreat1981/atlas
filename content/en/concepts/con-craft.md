# Craft Specialization

## Definition

Craft specialization is the concentration of the production of particular goods — pottery, textiles, metalwork, stone tools, basketry — in the hands of some members of a society rather than all of them. In its simplest form, every household makes what it needs; in a specialised system, some people make pots for others, some weave for others, and the products move through exchange. Anthropologists treat craft specialization as a key index of social complexity: where it appears, it signals surplus, exchange, and a division of labour, and its organisation reveals how a society distributes skill, wealth, and power.

## Origins and Development

V. Gordon Childe, in *Man Makes Himself* (1936), made craft specialization central to his account of the "urban revolution". Once agriculture produced a surplus, he argued, some people could be supported in full-time craft work: the smith, the potter, and the weaver freed from farming, sustained by the grain of others and in turn supplying the tools, vessels, and ornaments that farming societies needed. The specialist, dependent on patrons and on exchange, was for Childe one of the defining figures of civilisation — and also one of its first signs of inequality, since those who controlled the specialists' output controlled wealth.

Later research replaced Childe's single trajectory with a typology of forms. Elizabeth Brumfiel and Timothy Earle, in "Specialization, Exchange, and Complex Societies" (1987), distinguished between independent specialists, who produce for a general market and are motivated by efficiency, and attached specialists, who work for elites and produce prestige goods — fine pottery, gold ornaments, carved jade — whose value lies in their exclusivity. The distinction matters because the two kinds of specialization have different causes and consequences: independent specialization follows from demand and comparative advantage, while attached specialization follows from elite competition and the politics of display.

John Clark and William Parry, in "Craft Specialization and Cultural Complexity" (1990), added a further refinement, distinguishing part-time from full-time specialization and household production from workshop and factory production. Part-time specialization — a farmer who also makes pots in the dry season — is found in many societies that lack full-time specialists, and it complicates any simple equation of specialization with complexity. Cathy Costin, in "Craft Specialization: Issues in Defining, Documenting, and Explaining the Organization of Production" (1991), provided the field's most widely used framework, setting out four parameters along which production can vary: the context of production (including the degree of elite control), the concentration of producers, the scale of the production unit, and the intensity of effort. Costin's point was that specialization is not a single variable but a configuration, and that archaeologists and ethnographers must describe the configuration before explaining it.

## Key Debates

The first debate concerns causes. Why does specialization arise? One answer stresses efficiency: specialists produce better goods faster, and exchange benefits everyone. Another stresses power: elites sponsor specialists to produce the prestige goods that mark rank, and specialization is a political strategy rather than an economic one. A third stresses risk: in uncertain environments, specialising in a craft and exchanging for food spreads risk across households. The evidence suggests all three operate, in different proportions in different cases.

A second debate concerns gender. In many societies, particular crafts are strongly gendered — women weave and make pots, men smith and carve — and specialization can reinforce or reshape gender relations. When a craft becomes commercialised, control over it often shifts: in several documented cases, women's household crafts taken over by men once they became profitable, or vice versa. Craft specialization is therefore also a question about who controls skill and income.

A third debate concerns knowledge. Craft skill is embodied knowledge, learned through apprenticeship over years of practice, and often guarded within families or guilds. Anthropologists study how such knowledge is transmitted — through imitation, correction, and the discipline of the body — and how it is lost when apprenticeship systems break down. The loss of a craft is not only an economic event; it is the disappearance of a way of knowing.

## Contemporary Relevance

Craft specialization remains a live topic for three reasons. First, archaeologists use the organisation of production to reconstruct ancient economies and political systems, reading the distribution of workshops, raw materials, and finished goods as evidence of how societies were organised. Second, development policy has rediscovered craft: programmes promoting "heritage crafts" and fair-trade artisanal production seek to sustain livelihoods in a globalised economy, raising questions about authenticity, marketing, and who benefits. Third, the study of craft has fed into a broader anthropological interest in skill, materiality, and making — in how people think through their hands — which has influenced the study of everything from surgery to software.

In the end, craft specialization matters because it is where economy, skill, and social relations meet. A pot is never only a pot: it is evidence of who knew how to make it, who had the time, who controlled the clay, and who could afford to buy it.
