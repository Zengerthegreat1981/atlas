# Ritual Dream Incubation in Comparative Religious Practice

"Dream incubation" (from the Greek *enkoimesis*) is a ritual practice based on deliberately sleeping in a sacred place in order to summon an instructive, healing or prophetic dream. It is not the waiting for a spontaneous dream, but its institutional pursuit through a specific ritual procedure.

## The Greek Model: The Temples of Asclepius

The best-documented example is the tradition of *enkoimesis* in the temples of the healing god Asclepius in ancient Greece (most notably at Epidaurus). There, patients slept in a designated hall (the *abaton*) in the hope that the god would appear in their dream and offer a diagnosis or a remedy. This tradition was studied in encyclopaedic detail by Gil H. Renberg in *Where Dreams May Come: Incubation Sanctuaries in the Greco-Roman World* (2017). His work documents a wide network of incubation sites across the Greco-Roman world, not limited to the cult of Asclepius alone.

## Comparative Examples

Kelly Bulkeley (see `thk-kelly-bulkeley`), in his comparative survey (`wrk-dreaming-in-the-worlds-religions-bulkeley`), examines dreaming and its ritual uses across many religious traditions. A parallel often drawn is the Islamic prayer of istikhara (seeking guidance), in which a person asks for divine guidance through a specific prayer before sleep, in hope of a dream that directs a particular decision. It can be compared with Greek dream incubation as a ritual structure, although its theological context differs radically.

## The Islamic Tradition: Istikhara According to Edgar

Iain R. Edgar (see `thk-iain-edgar`), in *The Dream in Islam* (2011, `wrk-dream-in-islam-edgar-2011`), discussed the istikhara prayer, a ritual of two units of prayer followed by a supplication for guidance, as an Islamic form of dream incubation, tracing its roots in hadith literature and its contemporary practice. In practice, many Muslims do not expect the answer to come as a dream, so the dream element varies by context. With David Henig, he compared istikhara practices ethnographically across different Islamic contexts (2010).

## Related Practices Compared

Tibetan dream yoga (`con-tibetan-dream-yoga-milam`) trains awareness within the dream rather than seeking a message from outside, so it is structurally different from Greek incubation and istikhara. The Native American vision quest (`con-vision-quest-native-american`) is a related but distinct form of ritually sought guidance.
