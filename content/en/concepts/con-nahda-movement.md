# Nahda Awakening Movement

*Nahda* is the name of the movement of cultural and intellectual renewal that the Arab world has experienced since the beginning of the nineteenth century. In Arabic the word means both "awakening" and "renaissance." Albert Hourani dates its beginning to Napoleon's expedition to Egypt in 1798, in his book *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age 1798–1939* (1962). Many Arab researchers, however, see its antecedents as older: in Ottoman reform, and in changes in printing, education and trade.

## The First Centres: Egypt and Syria

In Egypt, Muhammad Ali (r. 1805–1848) built a modern state that needed engineers, doctors and officers. He founded schools, sent missions abroad, established the Bulaq Press around 1820 and the official gazette *al-Waqa'i' al-Misriyya* in 1828. Rifa'a al-Tahtawi (1801–1873) was imam of the Paris mission in 1826 and wrote *Takhlis al-Ibriz fi Talkhis Bariz*. He later led the School of Languages (1835) and the Translation Bureau. In Syria, Butrus al-Bustani (1819–1883) founded the National School (1863) and produced the *Da'irat al-Ma'arif* (Encyclopaedia). Nasif al-Yaziji and Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq combined the revival of the language with the renewal of literature.

## The Press and Periodicals

Journalism was the platform of the Nahda: *al-Muqtataf* (1876) by Ya'qub Sarruf and Faris Nimr, *al-Hilal* (1892) by Jurji Zaydan, *al-Manar* (1898) by Rashid Rida, and *al-'Urwa al-Wuthqa* (1884), which Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh issued in Paris. Through these periodicals a reading public formed that debated science, religion and the state.

## Diverse Currents

The Nahda was not a single trend. There was Islamic reform in the work of al-Afghani and Abduh; a secular modernizing current, as in Shibli Shumayyil and Farah Antun; an emerging nationalist movement; and an Egyptian liberal trend, as in Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid and Taha Husayn. Ahmad Amin, in *Zu'ama' al-Islah fi al-'Asr al-Hadith* (Leaders of Reform in the Modern Age, 1948), traces the lives of some leading figures of religious reform, while George Antonius wrote *The Arab Awakening* (1938) from the perspective of nationalism.

## Critique of the Concept

The concept of "Nahda" is criticized for implying that what preceded it was death, whereas in the eighteenth century there was scientific and literary activity in al-Azhar, Aleppo and Mosul. Adonis, in *al-Thabit wa-l-Mutahawwil: Sadmat al-Hadatha* (Constancy and Change: The Shock of Modernity, 1978), argued that the nineteenth-century Nahda remained captive to the old model in poetry. Marwa Elshakry, in *Reading Darwin in Arabic* (2013), traced the Nahda through the reception of modern science, showing that science was an arena of debate rather than of surrender. The traditional picture is also limited to Egypt and Lebanon and overlooks Tunisia, Algeria and Iraq.
