# Fusion of Horizons (Horizontverschmelzung): Understanding the Self Through the Other

In Gadamer's philosophy (*Wahrheit und Methode*, *Truth and Method*, 1960), understanding takes place when the **horizon** of the interpreter (the range of vision determined by history, language and tradition) meets the horizon of the text, person or tradition being interpreted. Gadamer calls the result a "fusion of horizons" (*Horizontverschmelzung*): not the interpreter's horizon alone, nor the straightforward adoption of the other's, but the formation of a broader horizon in which the difference between them is brought into relation. The concept is closely tied to his claim that understanding is always historically situated and that this situatedness is a condition of understanding and not merely an obstacle to it.

## The Philosophical Meaning

The concept rejects the ideal of a purely objective, presuppositionless understanding: every interpreter understands from a situation already shaped by tradition and "prejudices" (*Vorurteile*, pre-judgments), which Gadamer rehabilitates against the Enlightenment's blanket suspicion of them. He calls the awareness of being so shaped "effective-historical consciousness" (*wirkungsgeschichtliches Bewusstsein*). Yet he does not endorse relativism: the encounter with the other can correct one's pre-judgments, and the meaning of a text is not exhausted by the interpreter's own views. Understanding is modelled on **dialogue** (conversation, question and answer) rather than on the mastery of an object. Gadamer's notion of horizon draws on Husserl and Nietzsche as well as Heidegger, whose analysis of Dasein as always understanding from within a historical situation, and of the hermeneutic circle, is the main root of Gadamer's project. Horizons are not closed and fixed: they shift as we move, and the interpreter's own horizon is changed in the encounter.

## The Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

Some writers have used Gadamer's notion to describe psychotherapy and cross-cultural counselling: the therapist's professional framework and the client's cultural and personal framework are seen as horizons that come into contact, with understanding emerging in dialogue and not by applying a fixed theory to the client. Work on hermeneutics in psychology (for example, Messer, Sass and Woolfolk, eds., *Hermeneutics and Psychological Theory*, 1988) develops this line of thought. The extension is an analogy and a way of describing practice, and it is not itself part of Gadamer's text; multicultural counselling frameworks (such as those of Derald Wing Sue and Paul Pedersen) were developed independently of Gadamer.

## Its Place within Philosophical Hermeneutics

The concept is central to **philosophical hermeneutics**, as Gadamer developed it from Heidegger, and it is bound up with the **hermeneutic circle**: understanding moves back and forth between part and whole, and between the interpreter's pre-understanding and the matter itself, and not as a single act of grasping. **Paul Ricœur** developed a related hermeneutics of text and narrative; in debate with Gadamer (and with critical theory), Ricœur argued for combining a "hermeneutics of faith" (trust in the meaning of tradition) with a "hermeneutics of suspicion" (as in Freud, Marx and Nietzsche), as he explored in *Freud and Philosophy* (1965).

## The Difference from Neighbouring Concepts

The fusion of horizons differs from **perspective taking** in cognitive and social psychology, which is a measurable process of inferring another's viewpoint, whereas the fusion of horizons is a philosophical description of the structure of understanding. It also differs from **multiculturalism** as a political stance: Gadamer is concerned with the conditions of understanding, not with identity politics.
