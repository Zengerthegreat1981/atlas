# Utilitarianism

## Meaning and Definition

Utilitarianism is a consequentialist ethical theory holding that the rightness or wrongness of an act is measured solely by the amount of benefit (happiness or good) it produces and the harm it causes, not by the agent's intention or the intrinsic nature of the act. Its classical formulation requires the agent to choose the act that maximizes "the greatest happiness for the greatest number" among those affected by it. This is what links it fundamentally to the school of consequentialism, of which it is the most famous and most influential form.

## Precursors in Islamic Thought

No comprehensive utilitarian theory was formulated in classical Islamic thought. However, the theory of *maslaha* (public interest), developed by al-Ghazali in *al-Mustasfa* and later crowned by al-Shatibi in *al-Muwafaqat*, carries a consequentialist resonance. For both, legal rulings are grounded in overarching objectives that consist in securing benefits and warding off harms. Juristic maxims such as "harm is to be removed" and "warding off harms takes precedence over securing benefits" involve a weighing of good against evil, though scholars debate how far this amounts to consequentialism. The essential difference, however, is that for al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi *maslaha* is tied to the objectives of the Lawgiver (religion, life, intellect, progeny, and property) and is not separated from the textual sources of the Shari'a. Western utilitarianism, by contrast, takes pleasure, welfare or preference satisfaction as the standard independent of any textual reference.

## Development in Western Philosophy

Jeremy Bentham gave this school its systematic classical form in *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), with an explicitly quantitative approach, the "felicific calculus," that weighs pleasure and pain by dimensions such as intensity, duration, certainty, and proximity (nearness in time). John Stuart Mill refined this formulation in *Utilitarianism* (serialized 1861, book form 1863) by distinguishing between "higher" pleasures (intellectual and aesthetic) and "lower" pleasures (sensory), affirming that "it is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied." Henry Sidgwick gave it a systematic treatment in *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), and Peter Singer later developed a prominent contemporary form. Singer extended the circle of moral consideration to sentient beings in general, including animals, on the criterion of the capacity to feel pleasure and pain. Alongside act utilitarianism, which assesses each act directly, rule utilitarianism holds that it is best to follow general rules that maximize benefit over the long term, rather than calculating each act separately.

## Principal Objections

A well-known objection concerns the treatment of individuals: in the often-cited transplant case, if five patients could be saved by killing a healthy person and distributing their organs, pure utilitarian calculation would in theory permit this act despite its apparent moral wrongness. Critics of utilitarianism (above all Kantians and rights theorists) use this to show that it disregards the rights and dignity of the individual in favor of the aggregate. It is also practically difficult to measure happiness and compare it across different individuals (the problem of interpersonal comparison of utility). Utilitarians have replied in various ways, for instance by appeal to rule utilitarianism or to the unreliability of intuitions about extreme cases.

## Illustrative Example

In the "trolley problem" (introduced by Philippa Foot in 1967 and developed by Judith Jarvis Thomson), a runaway tram can be diverted so as to kill one person instead of five. Utilitarianism favors diverting it, because it maximizes net benefit. Other theories hesitate, owing to the moral distinction between killing and letting die, or doing and allowing.

## Further Primary Texts

Before Bentham, Francis Hutcheson's *Inquiry* of 1725 judged the best action to be the one that secures the greatest happiness for the greatest numbers, and William Paley's *Principles of Moral and Political Philosophy* (1785) offered a theological version in which general happiness indicates God's will. William Godwin's *Enquiry Concerning Political Justice* (1793) pressed impartiality to demanding conclusions about whom one should help.

G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903) kept a consequence-based account of right action while rejecting hedonism about the good. R. M. Hare's *Moral Thinking* (1981) separated an intuitive level of everyday rules from a critical level at which they are assessed. Peter Singer's article Famine, Affluence, and Morality (1972) argued that distance does not reduce the duty to prevent serious suffering. Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) explored problems of total and average welfare across populations. Among critics, Bernard Williams, in *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (1973, with J. J. C. Smart, who defended the theory), argued that it threatens personal integrity, and Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971) objected that it does not take seriously the distinction between persons.
