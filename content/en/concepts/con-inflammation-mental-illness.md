# Inflammation and Mental Illness

## Definition

This concept, which forms the core of the field of **psychoneuroimmunology**, examines the causal and associative relationship between **chronic low-grade systemic inflammation** (elevated inflammatory markers such as the cytokines IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, and C-reactive protein) and the onset and worsening of psychiatric disorders, especially **major depressive disorder** and schizophrenia.

## Mechanism

The immune system communicates with the brain through multidirectional pathways. Peripheral inflammatory cytokines, released into the blood, can reach the brain through neural pathways (such as the vagus nerve) and humoral pathways (through leaky regions of the blood-brain barrier and active transport), thereby activating an inflammatory response within the brain itself (neuroinflammation) through **microglia** cells. This brain inflammatory response in turn affects several systems related to mood: **monoamine** metabolism (diverting tryptophan away from the serotonin pathway and toward the kynurenine pathway), **glutamate** activity, and secretion of **brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)**, which is essential for neural plasticity.

## Key Features

- **Depression:** a subgroup (though not all) of patients with major depression show elevated inflammatory markers. Therapeutic interferon-alpha, given for example to patients with hepatitis C or melanoma, induces depressive symptoms in a substantial minority of patients. This is strong evidence that immune activation can induce depression, rather than merely being associated with it, although it does not show that inflammation causes most depression.
- **Schizophrenia:** cross-sectional studies show elevated IL-6 and TNF-α in patients with schizophrenia. These markers are sometimes linked to symptom severity (as measured by PANSS scales), and inflammatory-neurodevelopmental hypotheses (such as the effect of maternal infection during pregnancy) are proposed among the causes of schizophrenia.
- **Adaptive versus pathological response:** some "depressive" behaviors (withdrawal, loss of energy, sleep disturbance) during acute exposure to infection are interpreted as an adaptive response that conserves energy to fight the disease ("sickness behavior"). **Chronic** inflammatory activation, by contrast, becomes a pathogenic factor when it persists after the original cause has disappeared.

## Impact

This framework opened a new research horizon, exploring **anti-inflammatory drugs** (such as cyclooxygenase inhibitors and TNF inhibitors) as adjunctive treatment for depression. A meta-analysis (Köhler et al., 2014) found some benefit of anti-inflammatory add-on treatment, and a trial of the TNF antagonist infliximab (Raison et al., 2013) found no overall benefit over placebo, although an interaction analysis favoured infliximab in patients with baseline C-reactive protein above 5 mg/L. These approaches remain experimental and are not established treatments. It also helped shift the explanatory model of mental illness from a narrow "monoamine" model to a broader **systemic-immune-neural** model that links bodily health to mental health.

## Limits

The inflammatory elevation does not appear in all patients with depression or schizophrenia, but only in a proportion of them (a meta-analysis reported roughly a quarter of depressed patients with C-reactive protein above 3 mg/L; Osimo et al., 2019). Associations are also confounded by factors such as obesity, smoking, and medication, and the direction of causation is not settled. This suggests that inflammation may be a **subtype pathway** affecting a specific group of patients, rather than a comprehensive pathological mechanism that explains all cases of depression or schizophrenia.
