# Liminality

Liminality is the condition of being in between: no longer what one was, and not yet what one will become. The word comes from the Latin *limen*, a threshold — the doorway between one room and another. In anthropology, it describes the middle phase of a rite of passage, when a person has been separated from their old status but has not yet been incorporated into a new one: the initiate in seclusion, the bride between families, the mourner between the death and the funeral's end. In that threshold state, ordinary rules are suspended, normal identity is stripped away, and something new becomes possible. Liminality began as a technical term for one phase of ritual, but it has become one of anthropology's most widely used concepts, applied to migration, adolescence, illness, political crisis, and any experience of being betwixt and between. It is closely related to rites of passage, which provide the ritual frame in which liminality occurs; this entry focuses on the threshold state itself and its later extensions.

## Origins and Development

The concept was first formulated by the French anthropologist Arnold van Gennep in *The Rites of Passage* (1909). Van Gennep divided every rite of passage into three phases — separation, transition, and incorporation — and identified the middle phase, the *marge* or threshold, as the heart of the ritual process. His book was little read for decades, but it was taken up and transformed by the British anthropologist Victor Turner. In *The Forest of Symbols* (1967), based on fieldwork among the Ndembu of Zambia, Turner described the liminal period as "betwixt and between": liminal persons are stripped of rank, property, and the ordinary marks of social identity, often represented symbolically by images of ambiguity — nakedness, darkness, bisexuality, temporary death. In *The Ritual Process* (1969), Turner developed the idea into a general theory. Liminality, he argued, is the realm of anti-structure: where structure is hierarchy, status, and rule, liminality is equality, spontaneity, and possibility. From this experience arises what Turner called communitas — an intense, egalitarian bond among those who pass through the threshold together, in contrast to the structured relationships of ordinary social life. Turner argued that the tension between structure and anti-structure is a permanent motor of social life: societies need liminal moments to renew themselves, to generate new symbols, and to allow change.

## The Liminal and the Liminoid

Turner later extended the concept beyond ritual. In an essay of 1974 and in *From Ritual to Theatre* (1982), he distinguished the liminal from the liminoid. Liminal phenomena belong to traditional, small-scale societies, where rites of passage are obligatory and central to social life. Liminoid phenomena belong to modern, complex societies, where similar experiences — festivals, theatre, holidays, pilgrimages, sporting events — are optional, leisure activities, entered into voluntarily and standing at the margins of the working world. The distinction has been widely discussed and criticised: critics argue that it draws too sharp a line between traditional and modern societies, and that modern rites of passage — military training, medical residency, immigration procedures — can be as obligatory and consequential as any initiation.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns whether liminality is a universal feature of ritual or a concept shaped by Turner's particular fieldwork. Critics note that Ndembu initiation is only one kind of ritual, and that in many societies the transitional phase is not characterised by equality and ambiguity but by instruction, discipline, and the assertion of authority. A second debate concerns communitas: is the egalitarian bond of the threshold a genuine experience, or a romantic idealisation? Ethnographers have shown that hierarchy often persists inside the liminal phase — senior initiates dominate junior ones, and ritual specialists retain authority — and that communitas may be more aspiration than fact. A third debate concerns the extension of the concept. As liminality has been applied to migration, gender transition, chronic illness, unemployment, and the experience of refugees, some scholars argue that it has been stretched too thin, becoming a vague synonym for any kind of in-betweenness. Bjørn Thomassen, in *Liminality and the Modern* (2014), has argued for a more careful use, distinguishing the liminal as a specific social process from the merely transitional.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Liminality has travelled far beyond anthropology, into sociology, political science, literary studies, architecture, and management. It is used to analyse the experience of migrants caught between two countries, of patients in hospitals, of workers in precarious employment, and of societies in political transition. The concept's appeal lies in its ability to name an experience that is common but hard to articulate: the feeling of being neither one thing nor another. Critics warn that this very popularity is a weakness: a concept that explains everything explains nothing, and the metaphor of the threshold can obscure the specific mechanisms — legal, economic, bodily — that keep people in between. The enduring insight is Turner's: social life is not only structure — roles, ranks, and rules — but also the moments when structure is suspended, and it is in those moments, on the threshold, that new forms of social life are born.
