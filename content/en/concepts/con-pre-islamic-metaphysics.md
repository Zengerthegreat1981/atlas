# Pre-Islamic Metaphysics

The Arabs of the peninsula before Islam left no written philosophical system. What we know of their conception of existence and destiny is inferred from pre-Islamic poetry, from the Qur'an, which recounts some of their opinions and refutes them, and from the books of the reports on the idols. The picture must therefore be handled with caution, since many of its sources were written in the Abbasid period, two centuries after Islam.

## Time and Death

The most prominent notion attributed to the pre-Islamic Arabs is that of **time** (*al-dahr*) as a dominant and unavoidable fate. The Qur'an reports their saying: "And they say, 'There is nothing but our worldly life; we die and live, and nothing destroys us except time'" (45:24). In poetry, the "fates" (*al-manaya*), the "death-doom" (*al-himam*) and "the uncertainty of fate" (*rayb al-manun*) appear as forces that strike a person. Helmer Ringgren gathered this material in *Studies in Arabian Fatalism* (1955), and argued that in poetry *dahr* is not a god but a determined time. Toshihiko Izutsu, in *God and Man in the Qur'an* (1964), saw it as part of a view of existence that denies resurrection and grounds ethics in manliness (*muruwwa*) in the face of mortality.

## God and the Idols

The Qur'an indicates that the Meccan polytheists recognized "God" as creator: "If you asked them who created the heavens and the earth, they would surely say, 'God'" (31:25). But they took intermediaries, namely al-Lat, al-'Uzza, Manat and Hubal, as Ibn al-Kalbi (d. c. 204 AH/819 CE) related in *Kitab al-Asnam*. Jawad Ali studied these religions in *al-Mufassal fi tarikh al-'arab qabl al-Islam* (The Detailed History of the Arabs before Islam, 1968–1973), and Julius Wellhausen examined the remnants of Arab paganism in *Reste arabischen Heidentums* (1887). Gerald Hawting, in *The Idea of Idolatry and the Emergence of Islam* (1999), re-examined the traditional picture, arguing that the description of the Jahiliyya as explicit idolatry was formulated in a later Islamic discourse.

## The Hanifs and Monotheism

A monotheistic tendency associated with the *hanifs* existed in the peninsula, such as Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt and Waraqa ibn Nawfal. In pre-Islamic poetry there are glimpses of a divine conception: for example Labid's line "Surely everything except God is vain", and Zuhayr's verse in his Mu'allaqa: "Do not conceal from God what is in your souls, that it may be hidden; whatever is concealed, God knows it." It contains a divine knowledge of inner secrets and a book for reckoning.

## The Jinn and the Unseen World

The Arabs believed in the jinn, the ghouls and the omens, and established between them and humans a relationship of mediation. The soothsayer was in contact with a companion from among the jinn, and a poet might be ascribed to a devil who inspired him. For this reason the claim of the Quraysh that the Prophet was a poet or a soothsayer was made. This shows that poetry itself had an unseen dimension.

## Limits of Inference

It cannot be said that "metaphysics" here means an organized philosophy. The material is scattered, and historians are divided. Some see pre-Islamic belief as a continuation of certain creeds that persisted into Islam, while others emphasize rupture. Knowledge remains constrained by late sources. Taha Husayn, in *Fi al-shi'r al-jahili* (On Pre-Islamic Poetry, 1926), raised doubts about the attribution of much poetry to the Jahiliyya, a doubt that touches the primary source of this picture.
