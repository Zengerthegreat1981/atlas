# Pre-Islamic Metaphysics

Pre-Islamic metaphysics designates the conceptions of ultimate reality, time, destiny, and the unseen that can be reconstructed for the Arabian Peninsula in the generations before the Hijra of 622 CE. Revelation is traditionally dated from about 610. The active dates here mark the late pre-Hijra period, not a philosophical school with a founding text. The pre-Islamic Arabs produced no systematic philosophical treatises. Modern reconstructions of their metaphysical assumptions rely primarily on surviving pre-Islamic poetry (*al-shi'r al-jahili*), Qur'anic polemics that quote and refute polytheist doctrines, and early Abbasid mythographic compendia such as Hisham ibn Muhammad ibn al-Kalbi's (d. 204 AH / 819 CE) *Kitab al-Asnam* (*The Book of Idols*). These varied sources outline a worldview centered on impersonal time, subordinate cultic beings, and spirits inhabiting the landscape. They do not amount to a philosophical system.

## Time, Fate, and Mortality: The Reign of Dahr

The pre-eminent metaphysical concept recorded in pre-Islamic poetry is **al-dahr**—impersonal, blind, and relentless time as the supreme governing agency of mortal existence. In *Studies in Arabian Fatalism* (1955), Swedish scholar Helmer Ringgren argued that *dahr* was not personified as an anthropomorphic deity, but experienced as the inexorable passage of time that wears down mountains, scatters tribal communities, and brings sudden demise.
- **The Vicissitudes of Destiny**: Arabic verse deployed a rich fatalistic vocabulary, presenting death as inescapable doom (*hamam*), the unpredictable blows of fate (*rayb al-manun*), and the fates (*manaya*, sing. *maniyya*) that strike indiscriminately.
- **Denial of the Afterlife**: Much of the poetry, and the opponents whom the Qur'an quotes, deny bodily resurrection (*al-ba'th*). Surah al-Jathiya 45:24 reports their claim: there is only this life, in which people die and live, and only time (*al-dahr*) destroys them. The quotation is a polemic, not a census of Arabian belief. Jews, Christians, and the *hunafa'* are remembered as holding otherwise. Where resurrection was denied, poetry placed weight on courage, generosity, and a name that outlives the person (*dhikr*).

## The Divine Hierarchy: The Supreme God and Cultic Intermediaries

Classical Arabic sources, above all Ibn al-Kalbi, describe a cult with a high god and lesser beings, not a flat list of fetishes. Historians disagree on how far that picture matches Hijazi practice.
- **Allah as High God**: The Qur'an portrays the people it addresses as acknowledging **Allah** as creator of the heavens and the earth and as sender of rain (Surah Luqman 31:25; al-'Ankabut 29:61), and it charges them with associating other beings with him. Julius Wellhausen reconstructed this as a high-god cult. The verses are evidence for the Qur'an's polemic. They are not, by themselves, an ethnography.
- **Intercessors**: Lesser regional beings are presented as intercessors (*shufa'a'*), not as creators. Surah al-Najm (53:19–20) names **al-Lat**, **al-'Uzza**, and **Manat**. Early Muslim antiquarians place al-Lat at Ta'if, al-'Uzza at Nakhlah, and Manat at Qudayd. Ibn al-Kalbi describes **Hubal** as a red carnelian figure in the Ka'ba, before which divining arrows (*azlam*) were cast. Gerald R. Hawting, in *The Idea of Idolatry and the Emergence of Islam* (1999), argues that this polemic against *shirk* is not a transparent report of Meccan idol worship and may address intermediary beings among people who already affirmed a high god.
- **The Hanifs and Monotheistic Ferment**: Peninsular society contained monotheistic currents known as the *hunafa'* (sing. *hanif*), who rejected idolatry in search of the pure religion of Abraham (*millat Ibrahim*). Figures such as Waraqa ibn Nawfal, Zayd ibn 'Amr ibn Nufayl, and the poet Umayya ibn Abi al-Salt reflected this monotheistic yearning. Verses attributed to poets like Labid ibn Rabi'a ("Verily, everything save God is vanity") and Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma in his *Mu'allaqa* similarly invoked divine moral scrutiny and an omniscient reckoning.

## The Unseen World: Jinn, Divination, and Poetic Inspiration

The pre-Islamic cosmos was densely populated by supernatural entities bridging the physical and unseen worlds:
- **Jinn and Spirits**: Arabs believed that desert wastes and nocturnal spaces were inhabited by the *jinn*, *shayatīn*, and shape-shifting ghouls (*ghul*). These spirits possessed superior physical power and knowledge of hidden occurrences.
- **Soothsayers (*Kuhhan*) and Mantic Discourse**: Diviners (*kahin*, pl. *kuhhan*) claimed communion with spirit familiars (*ra'i* or *qarin*), delivering cryptic prophecies in rhythmic, rhymed cadence (*saj' al-kuhhan*).
- **The Metaphysics of Poetic Inspiration**: Poetic genius was attributed to demonic or spiritual visitation, traditionally localized in the mythical valley of 'Abqar. This cultural link between metaphysical inspiration and poetic utterance explains why Qurayshite opponents initially accused the Prophet Muhammad of being a soothsayer (*kahin*) or a mad poet (*sha'ir majnun*), charges which the Qur'an vigorously repudiated.
