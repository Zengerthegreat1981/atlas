# Brentano's Intentionality

## Definition

Franz Brentano, in *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (*Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkte*, 1874), proposed intentionality as the mark that distinguishes mental from physical phenomena. In a much-discussed passage he says that every mental phenomenon is characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the "intentional (or mental) inexistence" of an object: every mental act is directed toward something, whether in presenting, judging, loving or hating it. Physical phenomena, he argued, show nothing like this. (The passage is quoted and translated differently across editions, and the exact wording is best consulted in the translation by Rancurello, Terrell and McAlister.)

## Linguistic and Historical Root

The word *intentionality* derives from the Latin *intentio* and the verb *intendere* ("to stretch toward, to aim at"). Brentano took the idea explicitly from Aristotelian and Scholastic philosophy. Historians commonly trace medieval Latin *intentio* to the Arabic *ma'na* in the works of Avicenna and Averroes, as transmitted through Latin translations, although the details of this development are debated. Brentano's own reading of the Scholastic notion, and in particular what he meant by the "inexistence" (in-existence, existence *in* the mind) of the object, has itself been disputed by his interpreters and by Brentano himself, who later revised his view.

## Intentionality Is Not Linguistic Reference

For Brentano, intentionality is not a feature of verbal signs but of mental acts themselves. When I imagine a winged horse, my act of imagining is directed toward a winged horse, even though no such animal exists. This is what he calls the **intentional inexistence** of the object (also called its "immanent objectivity"). A central puzzle is how an act can be directed at something that does not exist, and whether the "object" is then something in the mind or whether there is merely an act with a content but no object; later philosophers (including Brentano's own pupils Twardowski and Meinong) offered competing answers.

## Kinds of Intentional Act

Brentano distinguished three classes of mental phenomena, each characterized by a different way of being directed at an object:

1. **Presentations (*Vorstellungen*)**: merely having an object before the mind, as in seeing, hearing or imagining.
2. **Judgments (*Urteile*)**: accepting or rejecting what is presented.
3. **Phenomena of love and hate (*Gemütsbewegungen*)**: taking an attitude of favor or disfavor toward an object, including feeling, desire and will.

## Intentionality in Later Philosophy

1. **Phenomenology**: Edmund Husserl, a pupil of Brentano, adopted and transformed the doctrine in the *Logical Investigations* (1900-01) and *Ideas I* (1913), making the analysis of intentional structure (the act, its content or "sense," and its object) the central task of phenomenology. Husserl, Scheler, Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty each developed the idea differently.
2. **Existential phenomenology**: Sartre's short essay "Intentionality: A Fundamental Idea of Husserl's Phenomenology" (1939) celebrates the idea that consciousness is always consciousness *of* something, and it is central to the analysis of the for-itself in *Being and Nothingness* (1943).
3. **Analytic philosophy of mind**: Roderick Chisholm (*Perceiving*, 1957) and others revived Brentano's thesis that intentionality marks the mental, in debate with Wilfrid Sellars. John Searle's *Intentionality* (1983) offers a theory of intentional states, and Daniel Dennett's "intentional stance" (*The Intentional Stance*, 1987) treats attributing beliefs and desires as a predictive strategy that need not presuppose an intrinsic mental property.
4. **Cognitive science**: theories of mental representation (for example, Fodor's) treat intentionality as the problem of explaining how internal states can be about things; this is a descendant of Brentano's problem, not of his own doctrines.

## The Contemporary Debate: Are All Mental Phenomena Intentional?

- **Brentano's thesis**: all and only mental phenomena are intentional.
- **Challenges**: pains, moods and bodily sensations have been cited as mental states that are not (or not clearly) directed at objects. Some philosophers (representationalists) respond that these too have intentional content; others accept non-intentional mental states.
- **Emotions**: many contemporary theorists treat emotions as intentional, as appraisals of situations, though others emphasize their bodily aspects.

## Intentionality and Qualia

The concept of intentionality raises a parallel debate with the question of qualia: is every experiential quality (such as the sensation of pain or the sight of red) structured intentionally toward an object, or are there raw qualities that refer to nothing outside themselves? Representationalists such as Michael Tye and Fred Dretske hold the former; defenders of irreducible qualia hold that phenomenal character is not exhausted by intentional content.

## Position in the Bridge

The concept connects philosophy and psychology: it is a philosophical thesis about the nature of the mental, a feature of psychological phenomena studied in attention, memory and judgment, and a methodological starting point for describing conscious experience. This is a conceptual connection, not a claim that the later traditions share Brentano's views.
