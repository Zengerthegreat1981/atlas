# The Labour Theory of Value

The **labour theory of value** is the economic thesis that the exchange value of a commodity is determined primarily by the quantity of socially necessary labour required to produce it, rather than by its subjective utility or its scarcity alone. This theory formed a common thread linking classical political economy to Marx's critique of it, before it gave way to the marginal utility theory of neoclassical economics.

## From Smith to Ricardo: a gradual emphasis

Adam Smith held that labour is the real measure of the exchangeable value of commodities in the "early and rude state of society" that precedes capital accumulation and land appropriation, but in his analysis of more complex societies he partly retreated toward a composite theory that combines wages, rent and profit as components of the natural price. David Ricardo developed this conception with greater rigour, attempting to hold labour as the main determinant of relative values even in advanced economies, although he faced difficulties, because commodities with different proportions of labour and capital respond differently to changes in wages, and he never found an "invariable measure of value".

## Marx: from measuring prices to explaining exploitation

Karl Marx took the Ricardian labour theory of value and used it as a radical critique rather than only as a tool for explaining prices. If commodities exchange according to their labour values, where does capitalist profit come from at all? The answer Marx developed is that **labour power** itself is a commodity sold at its value (the subsistence wage), but in a single working day it produces a value greater than its own value. The difference between what the worker produces and what he is paid as wages is **surplus value**, which the capitalist appropriates.

## An earlier Khaldunian parallel

Some comparative scholars note a similarity between this theory and passages in Ibn Khaldun's *Muqaddimah* (late fourteenth century), where earnings and gains (*kasb*, *ribh*) are treated as the value realized from human labour, and acquired wealth is traced to labour. This anticipation is usually cited as an independent parallel rather than a documented historical influence.

## Decline before marginal utility, and Marxist persistence

By the 1870s neoclassical economics (Jevons, Menger, and Walras) had replaced the labour theory of value with the **marginal utility** theory, shifting the focus of analysis from the labour expended to the preferences of the individual consumer. Despite this decline in mainstream academic economics, the labour theory of value remained alive within the Marxist tradition. It continued to face a persistent logical challenge known as the "transformation problem," the passage from labour values to actual production prices (see the separate debate entry on the transformation problem). Later economists, among them Piero Sraffa (*Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities*, 1960) and John Roemer, approached this problem in very different ways.
