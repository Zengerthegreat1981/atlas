# Disenchantment of the World (Weber)

## Definition

Max Weber's pivotal concept, put forward in its most famous formulation in his lecture "Science as a Vocation" (*Wissenschaft als Beruf*), delivered in Munich in 1917 and published in 1919, to describe the long-term historical course of the rationalization of Western thought and the retreat of magical and religious explanations of the world.

## Historical Context

Weber developed this concept within his broader project of understanding the distinctiveness of Western historical development in comparison with other civilizations, a project he also took up in his comparative studies on the sociology of the world religions (China, India and Protestantism). The German term *Entzauberung* (literally "de-magicking") is Weber's own usage, though it has been linked to Friedrich Schiller's phrase about the "de-divinization" of nature. Weber made it a sociological and historical concept describing a long-term course whose roots, for Weber, begin with the ancient Jewish prophets who rejected magic and divination, which passed through Christian theology, and then accelerated decisively with the Protestant Reformation and the modern scientific revolution.

## Main Ideas or Method

Weber argued that modern Western history moved in the direction of a gradual and inevitable "disenchantment" of man's understanding of his world. Instead of explaining phenomena (illness, natural disasters, luck) by magical forces or direct divine wills that can be entreated through rituals and prayers, the modern world came to be understood through rational causal laws that can be calculated, predicted and technically controlled (in Weber's words, one can "in principle master all things by calculation"). This process, although it freed man from fear of mysterious forces that cannot be controlled, produced in return a vacuum in the comprehensive existential meaning that magical-religious frameworks traditionally provided, leaving modern individuals alone before questions of value and ultimate meaning with no possible "scientific" answer to them.

## Impact and Critique

This concept became one of Weber's most influential and enduring diagnoses for understanding Western modernity, closely tied to his analysis of rationalization and bureaucracy (the "iron cage"), and it influenced many later analyses of the relation of modernity to religion and existential meaning, including contemporary "post-secular" debates. Some researchers criticized Weber for the linear and deterministic character that his supposed historical course toward complete disenchantment appears to have, considering that the return of new spiritual movements and forms of non-institutional religiosity in contemporary Western societies casts doubt on the inevitability of this course. It was also objected that Weber over-generalized this course to non-Western civilizations without sufficient regard for the particularity of the path of rationalization in each civilization. Nevertheless the concept remains a basic analytical tool for understanding the relation of modern science to the decline of traditional religious certainty in Western thought.
