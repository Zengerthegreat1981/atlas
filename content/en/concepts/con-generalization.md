# Generalization

## Definition

Generalization is the move from observations of particular cases to claims about a wider population, setting, or class of phenomena. It is the step that turns a study of one factory, one village, or one survey sample into knowledge about factories, villages, or populations in general. Without generalization, research would produce only disconnected descriptions; with it, sociology can build theory, inform policy, and accumulate knowledge across studies. The concept therefore sits at the heart of sociological method, and disagreements about how far a finding can be generalized are often disagreements about what kind of knowledge sociology can produce. Statistical generalization infers population characteristics from a representative sample, using probability theory to estimate the margin of error. Analytical or theoretical generalization, by contrast, infers from a case to a theory, arguing that the mechanisms identified in one setting should operate wherever similar conditions hold.

## Origins and Development

The problem of generalization is as old as the discipline. Émile Durkheim's *Rules of Sociological Method* insisted that sociology should seek general laws by comparing social facts across societies, treating particular cases as instances of broader types. John Stuart Mill's methods of agreement and difference, set out in *A System of Logic*, supplied the logical machinery for this comparative project, showing how to isolate causes by comparing cases that agree or differ in only one respect. In the twentieth century, Karl Popper recast the problem in terms of falsification: universal statements can never be verified by any number of observations, but a single counter-example can refute them, so scientific progress consists in proposing bold generalizations and subjecting them to severe tests. Survey research institutionalised statistical generalization, with sampling theory providing a rigorous basis for inferring from a few thousand respondents to a national population. Meanwhile, qualitative researchers developed a different logic. Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss's grounded theory argued that generalization in field research proceeds by building concepts from data and then refining them through theoretical sampling, comparing new cases until the categories are saturated rather than counting frequencies.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The most persistent debate opposes those who treat statistical representativeness as the gold standard and those who argue that case studies can generalize in a different but equally valid way. Robert Yin argued that case studies generalize to theoretical propositions, not to populations, and that multiple cases function like multiple experiments, replicating a finding under varied conditions. Bent Flyvbjerg went further, arguing that the social sciences have overvalued formal generalization and that context-dependent knowledge, gained from close study of particular cases, is often more valuable than the search for universal laws. A second debate concerns the scope of generalization: a finding about American factory workers in the 1950s may not travel to platform workers in Southeast Asia today, because the mechanisms operate in a different institutional context. This raises the question of scope conditions, the explicit statement of where a theory is expected to hold. A third debate concerns generalization across levels of analysis. A regularity observed among individuals, such as a correlation between education and income, does not automatically hold for groups or nations, and inferring group properties from individual data is the ecological fallacy. Conversely, assuming that what holds for a society holds for its members is the individualistic fallacy. Careful generalization must specify the level at which the claim is made.

## Empirical Illustrations

The failure of the *Literary Digest* poll in the 1936 US presidential election is a standard example of faulty statistical generalization: despite more than two million returned ballots, its sample, drawn largely from lists of car and telephone owners, over-represented wealthier voters and wrongly predicted a Landon victory, while George Gallup's smaller quota samples came closer. W. S. Robinson's 1950 article on ecological correlations showed that state-level correlations between literacy and ethnicity could differ sharply from individual-level ones, illustrating the ecological fallacy. At the other pole, William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943), a study of one Boston neighbourhood, is widely cited for theoretical insight although critics question how far its findings extend to other communities.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Generalization has become more contested as sociology has diversified its methods and its objects. The replication crisis in psychology and related fields has shown that many widely cited findings do not reproduce in new samples, prompting demands for preregistration, larger samples, and more careful attention to context. Big data and machine learning introduce new forms of generalization: algorithms trained on one population are deployed on others, sometimes with harmful results when the training data does not represent the target group. At the same time, feminist and postcolonial scholars have questioned the universalizing ambitions of classical sociology, arguing that theories built from European cases were presented as general laws while ignoring the experiences of colonised peoples and women. The response has not been to abandon generalization but to make it more modest and more explicit: to state scope conditions, to test theories in diverse settings, and to treat generalization as a claim to be argued and tested rather than assumed. In this form, generalization remains the bridge between the particular and the general, and therefore between empirical research and sociological theory.
