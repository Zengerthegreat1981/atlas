# Adoption

Adoption is the social and legal transfer of a child from one set of parents to another, creating kinship where no biological tie exists — or, in some societies, strengthening ties that already exist. Anthropologists treat adoption as a window onto kinship itself: because it separates the social facts of parenthood from the biological facts of birth, it reveals what each society considers essential to being a parent, a child, and a family. Across cultures, adoption takes many forms, from the informal circulation of children among relatives to formal, state-regulated, and transnational adoption.

## Definition

In Euro-American law, adoption is a formal legal act that severs a child's ties to birth parents and creates a new, exclusive parent-child relationship, usually modelled on the nuclear family. Anthropologists note that this model is historically specific. In many societies adoption does not sever earlier ties but adds new ones: a child may have several mothers and fathers, each with different rights and obligations. In parts of Oceania and West Africa, children routinely circulate among kin households for reasons of labour, education, affection, and alliance, and the distinction between "adopted" and "biological" children is less sharp than in Western law.

Anthropologists therefore distinguish between adoption as a legal institution and the broader practice of child circulation, fostering, and the social construction of parenthood. Judith Modell's study of adoption in the United States showed that even within a legal system that insists on exclusive parenthood, adoptive families develop their own practices of openness, storytelling, and negotiation with birth families. Kinship, in this view, is something people do, not simply something they are born into.

## Origins and Development

Adoption is ancient. Roman law recognised adoption as a way for childless men to secure heirs, and the emperor Augustus himself was adopted. In many pre-modern societies adoption served political purposes: chiefs adopted allies, lineages adopted outsiders to replenish their numbers, and childless couples adopted heirs to keep property within the family. In Japan, the adoption of adult sons-in-law into merchant and samurai households was a recognised strategy for securing succession.

Modern adoption took shape in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, when states began to regulate it. The first modern adoption statutes appeared in the United States in the mid-nineteenth century, and by the twentieth century adoption had become a professionalised system involving social workers, courts, and agencies. After the Second World War and the Korean War, intercountry adoption grew rapidly, as children from Asia, Latin America, and later Eastern Europe and Africa were adopted by families in North America and Europe. This history is inseparable from war, poverty, and inequality: adoption has often moved children from poorer countries and communities to wealthier ones.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One central debate concerns the interests of the child versus the interests of adults. Adoption law in most countries now declares the child's best interests paramount, but anthropologists ask whose interests are actually served when adoption systems respond to the demand of prospective parents rather than the needs of children. Critics of intercountry adoption argue that it can function as a market in children, in which poverty is treated as a reason to remove children from their families rather than as a problem to be addressed.

A second debate concerns identity and belonging. Transnational adoptees have written and spoken powerfully about growing up between cultures, losing a first language, and searching for birth families. Barbara Yngvesson's ethnography of Swedish families who adopted children from abroad shows how adoptive parents work to make a child "belong" — through naming, storytelling, and the selective incorporation of the child's country of origin — while the child's own sense of belonging may remain unsettled. Kath Weston's earlier work on lesbian and gay families in the United States showed that adoption could also be a creative act, as people built kinship outside the heterosexual nuclear family.

A third dimension is the relationship between adoption and race. In the United States, the adoption of Black and Native American children by white families has been intensely controversial. The Indian Child Welfare Act of 1978 was passed after evidence that large numbers of Native children were being removed from their communities. These debates show that adoption is never only a private family matter: it is shaped by histories of race, colonialism, and state power.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Today adoption is changing again. Open adoption, in which birth and adoptive families maintain contact, has become common in many countries. DNA testing and social media have made it easier for adoptees to find birth relatives, sometimes overturning decades of secrecy. At the same time, international adoption has declined sharply, as sending countries have tightened regulation and as critics have questioned the ethics of the practice.

Anthropology's contribution is to insist that adoption be understood in its social and historical context. The question is not only whether a particular adoption is legal, but what it means for the child, the birth family, and the adoptive family, and what it reveals about how a society defines kinship, responsibility, and belonging. Adoption shows that family is made as much as it is born.
