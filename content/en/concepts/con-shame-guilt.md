# Shame and Guilt

Shame and guilt are the emotions of self-evaluation. They arise when a person measures themselves — or their actions — against a standard and finds the result wanting. They are so often experienced together, and so often confused with one another, that they are treated here as a single paired entry; yet the distinction between them is one of the most consequential in the psychology of emotion. In brief: guilt condemns what a person did, while shame condemns who a person is.

## The Core Distinction: Self versus Act

The classic formulation comes from Helen Block Lewis, whose *Shame and Guilt in Neurosis* (1971) first drew the line with clinical precision. In guilt, Lewis argued, the self judges a specific behaviour: "I did a bad thing." The self remains intact and capable of repair. In shame, the self judges itself: "I am a bad person." The whole self is experienced as defective, exposed, and unworthy, and the impulse is not to repair but to hide, to shrink, to disappear from the gaze of others.

Richard Lazarus gave the same distinction a precise appraisal form in *Emotion and Adaptation* (1991). Guilt, he wrote, follows the transgression of a moral imperative — a negative evaluation of a particular act. Shame follows the failure to live up to an ego-ideal — a negative evaluation of the self as a whole. Guilt motivates confession, apology, and reparation: because the fault lies in the act, the act can be undone or compensated. Shame motivates concealment and withdrawal: because the fault lies in the self, there is nothing to fix, only something to hide.

## Shame and Guilt in the Emotion Taxonomy

In the prototype taxonomy of emotion developed by Shaver, Schwartz, Kirson, and O'Connor (1987), shame and guilt sit together inside the large sadness family, in a shame subcategory that also includes regret and remorse. This placement is instructive: shame and guilt are kin to sadness because they share its core theme of loss — the loss of standing, of approval, of the self one hoped to be.

Both emotions belong to the class psychologists call self-conscious emotions, because they require capacities that simpler emotions do not: a sense of self, an internalised set of standards, and the ability to imagine how one appears in the eyes of others.

## Empirical Research: Tangney and Dearing

The distinction between shame and guilt was put to empirical test by June Price Tangney and Ronda Dearing, whose programme of research culminated in *Shame and Guilt* (2002). Developing the Test of Self-Conscious Affect (TOSCA) and related measures, they asked large samples of adults and adolescents to describe how they would respond to everyday failures and transgressions, and tracked the correlates of shame-proneness and guilt-proneness over time.

Their findings gave the theoretical distinction real weight. People prone to shame were more likely to report symptoms of depression and anxiety, to externalise blame, and to respond to failure with anger directed at others. People prone to guilt showed weaker associations with psychological symptoms and were more likely to apologise and make amends. Guilt, in their data, looked like the adaptive member of the pair: painful but reparative. Shame looked like the risk factor: painful and paralysing.

## The Self in Culture

Whether shame or guilt dominates a person's moral life depends in part on culture. Hazel Rose Markus and Shinobu Kitayama, in "Culture and the Self" (1991), contrasted the independent self of many Western societies with the interdependent self of many collectivist societies. Where the self is defined by its connections, shame is readily tied to the reputation of the family or group, not only to the individual.

Anthropologists of the Mediterranean world, including Lila Abu-Lughod in *Veiled Sentiments* (1986), her study of Bedouin poetry and honour, documented elaborate codes in which honour and modesty govern social life and shame is a collective affair. In the Arab-Islamic context, several neighbouring concepts must be kept distinct: *haya'* (modesty), a praised virtue; *'ayb* (shamefulness), a social judgement on appearance and reputation; and *haram* (the forbidden), a religious and moral category. Ruth Benedict's contrast, in *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946), between a Japanese "shame culture" and a Western "guilt culture" is now regarded as an over-rigid generalisation, but it usefully posed the question that later research refined.

## Shame and Guilt in the Classical Arabic-Islamic Tradition

In classical Arabic-Islamic ethics (*ilm al-akhlaq*) and psychological philosophy, the interplay between internal moral evaluation and social exposure was analyzed with considerable depth. Miskawayh (d. 1030), in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (*The Refinement of Character*), identified *haya'* (shame, modesty, or moral reticence) as the earliest observable indicator of a developing intellect and moral capacity in childhood. Crucially, Miskawayh distinguished between commendable shame (*al-haya' al-mahmud*), an internal cognitive discernment that restrains the person from base or unjust actions, and defective or excessive shame (*al-khajal* or *al-jaban*), a social timidity and passivity that paralyzes agency and prevents one from pursuing truth or justice.

In spiritual psychology, Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (d. 1111) in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* (*The Revival of the Religious Sciences*, particularly the books on repentance and on fear and hope) provided an extensive analysis of moral guilt and remorse (*al-nadam*). Operating from the foundational prophetic maxim "Remorse is repentance" (*al-nadamu tawbah*), al-Ghazali framed authentic guilt as a focused, action-oriented affective state: the painful realization of transgression motivates concrete restitution (*radd al-mazalim*) and spiritual reformation rather than passive self-condemnation. Ibn Hazm (d. 1064) in *Al-Akhlaq wa al-Siyar* (*Morals and Conduct*) likewise differentiated between moral vigilance grounded in the fear of actual ethical disgrace and superficial conformity driven merely by public opinion (*'ayb*).

## Healthy and Toxic Shame

Shame is not always destructive. Some researchers distinguish moral shame — the brief, sharp recognition that one has fallen short of one's own standards, which can prompt self-correction — from toxic or pathological shame, a chronic sense of the self as fundamentally defective, which is associated with depression, addiction, and trauma. A similar distinction applies to guilt, which can be constructive when it tracks real wrongdoing and destructive when it becomes free-floating and detached from any act.

## Shame, Guilt, and Repair

The practical difference between shame and guilt is most visible in what happens next. Guilt points outward toward the person harmed and asks: what can I do to make this right? Shame points inward toward the self and asks: how can I avoid being seen? Some therapeutic approaches therefore aim less at eliminating these emotions than at helping people move from global self-condemnation toward a focus on the specific act, where repair becomes possible. Understood this way, shame and guilt are not merely pains to be endured but instruments of moral life, flawed and dangerous, yet indispensable to how human beings hold themselves and one another to account.
