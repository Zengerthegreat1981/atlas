# Professional Dominance in Medicine

The idea that physicians possess an exceptional professional autonomy that protects them from external accountability, since they monopolize the authority to define "illness" and "recovery" socially and not only scientifically.

## Definition

The concept of "Professional Dominance," as formulated by the American sociologist Eliot Freidson (1923–2005), refers to the unique position that modern medicine reached in twentieth-century societies: its ability to be the sole legally and socially recognized authority for defining what is "illness" and what is "recovery," and at the same time to be responsible for evaluating the performance of its own members with little effective external oversight.

## Historical Context

This analysis appeared in the context of a wave of critical studies in the Sociology of Professions in the 1960s and 1970s, when researchers such as Freidson and Magali Sarfatti Larson began to ask how some professions acquire an exceptional social authority that is not necessarily proportional to their absolute scientific superiority. Freidson's work came at the moment of the expansion of the American welfare state (the Medicare and Medicaid programs were enacted in 1965), when the regulation of the medical profession became a direct political issue, and it coincided with the rise of patients' rights movements and the social critique of the medical establishment, represented also by Ivan Illich in his book *Medical Nemesis* (1975) from a different and more radical angle.

## Main Ideas

In *Profession of Medicine: A Study of the Sociology of Applied Knowledge* (1970), Freidson put forward a detailed analysis of "professional dominance": unlike many occupations, physicians possess an exceptional professional autonomy. They themselves set the standards of good practice and evaluate one another's performance through "Self-Regulation" mechanisms such as licensing boards and peer-review committees, and they monopolize the authority to define "illness" and "recovery" socially, so that their diagnosis becomes a social act with legal and moral authority (such as certifying inability to work, a certificate of need for treatment, or determining eligibility for insurance compensation) rather than a purely scientific opinion. Freidson distinguished control over the technical content of work (including the right to judge the quality of medical work) from control over its social and economic terms, and treated the former as the core of professional autonomy. Mary Ann Elston (1991) later separated economic, political and clinical autonomy. He argued that this unique professional authority is a historical product of medicine's success in monopolizing the scientific and legal legitimacy to define health and illness through its alliance with the state since the late nineteenth century (as Paul Starr later documented in *The Social Transformation of American Medicine*, 1982), not an inevitable result of its absolute scientific superiority alone. In his book *Professional Dominance: The Social Structure of Medical Care* (1970) he added that this dominance extends to include physicians' control over the division of labor within the entire health system, as the other health professions (nursing, physical therapy, pharmacy) are classified as "Paramedical Professions," legislatively and clinically subordinate to the physician's final authority.

## Major Works or Representatives

In addition to Freidson himself, the sociologist Paul Starr developed this line in *The Social Transformation of American Medicine* (1982, winner of the Pulitzer Prize), which chronicled the rise of this professional dominance and the beginning of its later decline in the face of pressure from insurance companies and bureaucratic management. Magali Sarfatti Larson in *The Rise of Professionalism: A Sociological Analysis* (1977) also addressed the broader mechanisms of professional market control, with medicine as a central case.

## Impact and Critique

This analysis became a basic reference in the sociology of the medical professions, and deeply influenced later debates on physician accountability and the reform of health-care systems. However, later scholars, and Freidson himself in his late work (including *Professionalism: The Third Logic*, 2001), discussed pressures on this dominance from the 1980s onward, notably: the rise of "Managed Care," which transferred clinical decision-making authority partly to insurance companies and administrators; the growth of the "Evidence-Based Medicine" movement, which codified clinical decisions in standard protocols that limit individual discretion; and the growth of patients' rights movements and of patients' access to medical information, including online. Researchers from the school of Michel Foucault also directed a different critique that sees medical dominance as a form of the broader "Biopower" rather than an isolated professional privilege.
