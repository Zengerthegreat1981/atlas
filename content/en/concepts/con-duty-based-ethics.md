# Duty-Based Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Duty-based ethics (deontology) is a moral system that makes the concept of "duty" (duty) or "obligation" the source of moral rightness. An act is judged right or wrong according to whether it conforms to a rule or commitment that is binding in itself, not according to its consequences. Unlike consequentialist ethics, which asks "what produces the greater good?", duty-based ethics asks "what must I do regardless of the outcome?" On this view, keeping a promise, telling the truth, or acting justly are duties in their own right, even when fulfilling them sometimes produces less beneficial results.

## Foundations in Islamic Thought

The concept of obligation (*wujub*) occupies a central place in Islamic jurisprudence and the principles of jurisprudence (*usul al-fiqh*). An act is classified among the five categories of legal rulings (obligatory, recommended, permissible, disliked, and prohibited) according to the address of the Lawgiver, not according to its expected consequences. Theologians, particularly the Mu'tazila, examined whether obligation can be known by pure reason before revelation or whether it is established only by transmitted textual evidence. The Mu'tazila held that reason can perceive the wrongness of injustice and the obligation of justice, while the Ash'ari school held that obligation is established only by the command of the Lawgiver. Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, connected the performance of religious and moral duties with the purification of the soul, though his framework is a religious ethics of the soul rather than a deontology in the Kantian sense.

## The Western Context and Principal Positions

Immanuel Kant is the central figure of duty-based ethics in modern Western philosophy. In *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), he distinguished between actions performed "in conformity with duty" merely and those performed "from duty" itself. He held that genuine moral worth is realized only in the second case, when the motive is respect for the moral law rather than emotional inclination or calculation of benefit. Kant formulated the "Categorical Imperative" test for determining duties: act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law. In the early twentieth century, the British philosopher W. D. Ross developed a more flexible theory of "prima facie duties" (prima facie duties), such as fidelity, justice, and beneficence, which may conflict with one another and must be weighed through practical moral judgment, in contrast to Kant's absolute strictness.

## Objections and Criticism

Kant's strictness is objected to on the grounds that it may lead to results that shock moral intuition. In his well-known essay "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy," he maintained that lying is impermissible even to save the life of an innocent person from a murderer who asks for their whereabouts. Ross's theory, despite its flexibility, is also objected to for lacking a clear criterion for weighing one duty against another in cases of conflict, leaving moral judgment dependent on individual intuition that cannot be justified systematically.

## Illustrative Example

Fulfilling a promise made to a dying friend, to deliver their property to a specific person, remains a binding duty within duty-based ethics, even if it later becomes clear that distributing the money to a charitable cause would have produced greater benefit.
