# Women Scholars in the Islamic Period

A sustained scholarly presence of women across the history of Islamic civilization, concentrated historically above all in the science of **hadith transmission**. This discipline allowed women a relatively broader and documented public participation than other fields of knowledge, from the early Islamic period to the modern era.

## Aisha bint Abi Bakr: the early exemplar

**Aisha bint Abi Bakr** (c. 614–58 AH/678 CE), wife of the Prophet Muhammad, is the most prominent woman scholar of the first generation. Classical enumerations credit her with about 2,210 narrations. That figure counts separate chains, not 2,210 distinct texts. On that reckoning she is the most prolific woman among the Companions and one of the most prolific Companions overall. She also corrected reports transmitted by other Companions when she judged the wording or the legal inference to be wrong. Later authors collected these corrections, among them Badr al-Din al-Zarkashi (d. 794 AH/1392 CE) in *al-Ijaba li-Irad ma Istadrakathu 'A'isha 'ala al-Sahaba*.

## Women hadith scholars across the centuries

The pattern after Aisha was not a single unbroken line. **Amra bint Abd al-Rahman** (d. c. 98–106 AH/716–724 CE), a student of Aisha and a Medinan jurist, was a leading authority on Aisha's hadith; Umar ibn Abd al-Aziz is reported to have ordered that her reports be written down. **Hafsa bint Sirin** (d. c. 101 AH/719–720 CE) was esteemed for Qur'an recitation, devotion and transmission. Asma Sayeed has argued that women's recorded share in hadith then fell sharply from the second/eighth to the fourth/tenth century, and revived from the fifth/eleventh century in another form: elite women transmitted prestigious books and held certificates (*ijazat*), often without the professional legal posts of male jurists. Karima al-Marwaziyya (d. 463 AH/1070 CE) transmitted *Sahih al-Bukhari* in Mecca. Shuhda al-Katiba (Shuhda bint Ahmad al-Ibari, d. 574 AH/1178 CE) was a well-known transmitter in Baghdad. Al-Sakhawi's *al-Daw' al-Lami' li-Ahl al-Qarn al-Tasi'* (Cairo, 1353–1355 AH/1934–1936), whose last volume is devoted to women, records many women of the ninth/fifteenth century who held such authorizations, including in Damascus.

## The modern example: Aisha Abd al-Rahman (Bint al-Shati)

In the modern era, the Egyptian researcher **Aisha Abd al-Rahman**, known as **"Bint al-Shati"** (1913–1998), developed a literary approach to Qur'anic exegesis, drawing on the method of her teacher Amin al-Khuli. She wrote many books on exegesis, literature and the Prophet's biography, and was one of the prominent women in academic Islamic studies in the twentieth century.

## Structure and historical limits

Despite this notable presence in hadith science, women's participation in other fields (such as formal jurisprudence, the judiciary and institutional public legal opinion) remained more limited and varied by era and region. Recognition of women's competence to transmit and critique hadith did not necessarily translate into parallel recognition of their competence to hold judicial or official juristic posts in most historical schools of jurisprudence.

## Contemporary impact

Contemporary scholars, including Asma Sayeed and Mohammad Akram Nadwi (in *al-Muhaddithat: The Women Scholars in Islam*), have restored attention to this record. Classical biographical collections had already entered many of the names; later general surveys often passed over them. Sayeed and Nadwi use the record in contemporary debates about women's religious and scholarly authority.

## Caveats

The documentation of many of these women's lives remains brief in classical sources compared with their male counterparts. Writing about them today therefore sometimes relies on scattered inference from books of men's biography and generational classes (*tabaqat*), rather than on extensive independent biographies.
