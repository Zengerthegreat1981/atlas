# Moral Patiency

## Definition

"Moral patiency" is a concept that stands in contrast to "moral agency". Whereas a moral agent is one who can be questioned and held accountable for their actions by virtue of possessing reason and will, a "moral patient" is any being that deserves to be treated morally and taken into account in judging right and wrong, whether or not it is itself capable of moral action. An infant, a person with severe mental disability and perhaps an animal are all "moral patients" who deserve care, even though they are not "moral agents" who can be held accountable for their actions. The central question is: what condition makes a being deserving of this status? Is it reason, the capacity to feel pain and pleasure, membership in the human species, or life itself?

## Historical Context and Islamic Thought

For centuries, Islamic jurisprudence has contained principles concerning those who are not morally accountable agents but who nonetheless deserve care. A child who lacks discernment and a person who is insane are exempt from the pen (they are not held accountable), yet they remain subjects of full legal rights to maintenance, custody and inheritance. Islam also established detailed rights for animals that go beyond merely not torturing them, such as the obligation to sharpen the blade and to relieve the slaughtered animal, the report of a woman punished on account of a cat she confined, and the saying "for every moist liver there is a reward." These establish for animals a recognized moral standing, even though they are not responsible agents. Al-Ghazali, in *The Revival of the Religious Sciences*, elaborated on the etiquette of dealing with animals, discussing the proper treatment of animals and treating cruelty to them as a fault of character in the agent.

## Contemporary Importance and Philosophical Debate

This distinction has recently been applied in animal ethics. Tom Regan distinguished between "moral agents" and "moral patients," affirming that an animal, even if it is not a moral agent (cannot be held accountable), is a "subject-of-a-life" with its own interests that deserve respect. He built on this his argument against the exploitation of animals in experimentation and food. Peter Singer took a similar direction, but on the basis of the criterion of "the capacity to suffer" alone (rather than possessing a complex individual life), regarding the exclusion of animals from moral consideration merely on grounds of species membership as a form of "speciesism", analogous to racism and sexism. This extension raises sharp questions about the boundaries of the moral circle. Should consideration extend to plants and ecological systems? Do degrees of moral standing vary with the complexity of the nervous system?

## Objections and Example

Some philosophers object that the criterion of "suffering" alone is too broad and difficult to apply in practice (where, for instance, do we place insects?). Others argue that restricting moral consideration to rational humans alone excludes infants and people with severe dementia from the circle of entitlement, which is a morally unacceptable result. An illustrative example: the fetus in its mother's womb is not a moral agent in any sense, yet the entire abortion debate revolves around whether it is a "moral patient" that deserves protection, which demonstrates the major practical impact of this distinction.
