# Herodotean Inquiry Method

Herodotus of Halicarnassus (c. 484–c. 425 BCE) developed a new form of prose inquiry (*historia*)—an active, systematic inquiry into human affairs, ethnography, and causation.

## Definition and Core Epistemology

In classical Greek, *historia* did not initially denote a literary genre or the past itself, but the process of inquiry, research, and arbitration conducted by an investigator (*histor*). Herodotus announced this program in the opening sentence of the *Histories*: "Herodotus of Halicarnassus here displays his inquiry (*histories apodexis*), so that human achievements may not become forgotten in time, and great and marvelous deeds—some displayed by Greeks, some by barbarians—may not lose their renown; and especially the reason why they waged war against each other."

Scholars commonly analyze Herodotus' method under three heads:
1. *Autopsia* (eyewitness observation): the primary criterion of certainty, involving direct inspection of monuments, battle sites, geography, and religious artifacts.
2. *Akoe* (oral report or hearsay): the gathering of oral traditions, testimonies of local guides, priests (such as in Memphis and Thebes), and participants.
3. *Gnome* (critical judgment and inference): the analytical faculty used to weigh competing accounts against physical evidence and rational plausibility (*to eikos*).

## Methodological Principles and Source Evaluation

A foundational tenet of the Herodotean method is the explicit preservation of variant accounts. As Herodotus famously formulated his editorial standard in Book VII (7.152): "My duty is to report what is said, but I am not obliged to believe it all—and let this saying hold good for my entire history." Rather than imposing a single authoritative voice, Herodotus frequently cited competing versions of an event—such as Persian, Phoenician, and Greek explanations for the origins of hostilities—allowing readers to assess the balance of evidence.

Herodotus combined political narrative with extensive ethnographic and geographical digressions (*logoi*). He recognized that political actions could only be comprehended within the cultural traditions (*nomoi*) and environmental realities of the peoples involved, famously affirming Pindar's dictum that "custom is king of all."

## Cross-Cultural Parallels and Critical Reception

Herodotus's practice of preserving multiple contradictory transmissions finds a significant parallel in early Arabic-Islamic historiography. Chroniclers such as Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari (224–310 AH / 839–923 CE) in his *Ta'rikh al-Rusul wa-l-Muluk* similarly recorded multiple distinct oral reports (*akhbar*) regarding the same event, presenting their complete chains of transmission (*asānīd*) to let the reader evaluate conflicting claims.

In antiquity, Herodotus faced sharp criticism. Thucydides (1.20–22), without naming him, criticized predecessors for uncritical and entertaining accounts (*to mythodes*), which readers often take to include Herodotus, while Plutarch penned a scathing treatise, *De malignitate Herodoti* ("On the Malice of Herodotus"), accusing him of barbarian sympathies (*philobarbaros*) for depicting Persian and Egyptian cultures with empathy. In the Renaissance and modern eras, however, scholars such as Arnaldo Momigliano and François Hartog reassessed Herodotus as a pioneer of ethnography and of the use of oral tradition.
