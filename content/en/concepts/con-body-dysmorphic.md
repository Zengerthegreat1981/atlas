# Body Dysmorphic Disorder

## Definition

**Body dysmorphic disorder** (BDD) is a psychiatric disorder marked by excessive preoccupation with a perceived defect or defects in physical appearance that are in fact nonexistent or very slight and not noticeable to others. In the **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision** (DSM-5-TR), it is classified within the category **"Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders,"** alongside obsessive-compulsive disorder itself, trichotillomania, hoarding disorder, and excoriation disorder. This reflects the phenomenological similarity between these disorders in terms of intrusive thoughts and repetitive behaviors.

## Diagnostic Criteria

The preoccupation was described in the nineteenth century as "dysmorphophobia" (Enrico Morselli, 1891). BDD entered DSM-III-R in 1987 under the somatoform disorders, and DSM-5 moved it to the obsessive-compulsive and related disorders chapter.

DSM-5-TR requires: (1) preoccupation with one or more perceived defects in appearance that are not noticeable or appear slight to others; (2) that at some point during the course of the disorder the individual has performed repetitive behaviors in response to this preoccupation (such as repeatedly checking the mirror, excessive grooming, or seeking reassurance) or mental acts (such as mentally comparing their appearance with that of others); and (3) that the preoccupation causes clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning; and (4) that it is not better explained by concerns about body fat or weight in an individual meeting criteria for an eating disorder. DSM-5 (2013) added a new criterion requiring the presence of these repetitive behaviors, which was not mandatory in DSM-IV. It also added the **muscle dysmorphia specifier** (preoccupation with the body being too small or insufficiently muscular) and a **specifier for degree of insight** (from good insight to absent insight or delusional beliefs). Under DSM-5, a delusional degree of conviction is diagnosed as BDD with absent insight rather than as a second diagnosis of delusional disorder, which DSM-IV had allowed.

## Key Dimensions

- **The difference from ordinary appearance concern**: what distinguishes BDD clinically is the intensity of the preoccupation, the distress or impairment it causes, and the repetitive checking, grooming, camouflaging, or comparing that follows it. Research interviews often treat preoccupation for about an hour or more a day as a marker of clinical severity. That hour is not a DSM-5-TR duration criterion.
- **Relation to cosmetic surgery**: many people with BDD seek repeated cosmetic or dermatological procedures, and these procedures often do not relieve the preoccupation and may even make it worse. This makes psychological screening before cosmetic surgery an important preventive tool.
- **Variable insight**: the degree to which the patient recognizes that their beliefs are unrealistic ranges from good insight to beliefs that are almost delusional in their certainty. This sometimes determines the clinical distinction between BDD and a typical delusional disorder of the somatic type.
- **Treatment**: cognitive-behavioral therapy and serotonin reuptake inhibitor medication are the best-studied approaches; this entry is descriptive and not a treatment guide.
- **Classification in ICD-11**: the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases (World Health Organization) also places BDD within the group "obsessive-compulsive disorders and related conditions," which is consistent with the logic of DSM-5-TR.

*This entry is educational and descriptive; it is not clinical advice.*
