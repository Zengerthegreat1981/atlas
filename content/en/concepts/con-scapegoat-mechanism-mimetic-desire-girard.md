# The Scapegoat Mechanism and Mimetic Desire

A central concept in René Girard's theory of the origin of religion, describing a social mechanism in two successive stages: a mimetic crisis that ends in a collective sacrificial violence.

## Stage One: Mimetic Desire and the Crisis

According to Girard, an individual does not desire something by virtue of its intrinsic properties, but because another "model" (an intermediary) desires or possesses it: a triadic structure (subject/model/object), not a dyadic one. When the model comes close to the subject within the same social space, it changes from an exemplar into a rival competing for the same object, and the "mimetic crisis" escalates into a generalised competitive violence in which the antagonists lose their individual differentiation (what Girard calls the "crisis of non-differentiation").

## Stage Two: Concentration of Violence on a Single Victim

The crisis is resolved, automatically and without collective awareness, by concentrating the violence of the whole community on a single individual or minority (the victim or scapegoat). This victim is falsely blamed for the crisis on the basis of incidental signs that make them "accusable" (a visible difference, social weakness). Killing or expelling this victim suddenly restores social peace, and the community, unaware of the mechanism of its own projection, interprets this as a superhuman power lodged in the victim, who is then deified after death. From this deification arise ritual (the symbolic and controlled re-enactment of the founding killing) and prohibitions (preventing the repetition of the conditions that led to the original crisis).

## The Difference from Hubert and Mauss's Theory of Sacrifice

While the theory of Hubert and Mauss describes the ritual function of sacrifice (mediation between the sacred and the profane through an intermediary victim) without assuming a specific historical violent origin, Girard goes further: he posits an actual collective act of violence (not merely symbolic) as the historical-anthropological origin of every later ritual and prohibition. This is a theory more ambitious in scope and more contested in terms of its testability by ethnographic evidence.

## Signs of Accusation and the Victims of Crisis

In his later works (especially *Le Bouc émissaire*, 1982), Girard details the kinds of "signs" that make an individual or minority "accusable" in times of major social crisis (epidemics, famines, disturbances): a visible bodily difference, membership of a minority that is socially and politically weak, or a marginal position that leaves the individual exposed to accusation without any tribal protection. He cites recurring historical examples, such as the accusation that Jews spread the Black Death in fourteenth-century Europe, as a structural repetition of the scapegoat mechanism itself throughout human history, rather than an isolated incident.

## Application to the Religious Text: Exposing the Mechanism Rather than Concealing It

Girard holds that most ancient myths reproduce the scapegoat mechanism from the perspective of the persecuting community (so the victim appears genuinely guilty), while certain Biblical and Gospel texts (such as the story of Joseph, and more clearly the Gospel accounts of the Passion of Christ) expose the real structure of the mechanism from the perspective of the innocent victim. Girard regards this as a distinctive feature of the Judeo-Christian scriptures, which in his view dismantle the myth of the alleged guilt rather than consecrating it. This claim is theological as well as anthropological and is among the most contested parts of his work.
