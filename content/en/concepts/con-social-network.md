# Social Networks

A social network is a set of actors, such as people, groups, or organisations, connected by ties of kinship, friendship, communication, exchange, or influence. Social network analysis studies the pattern of these ties rather than the attributes of individual actors: who is connected to whom, how dense the connections are, and where the gaps and bridges lie. A person's position in a network shapes what information they receive, whom they can influence, and what opportunities reach them. Networks are therefore a basic structure of social life, as fundamental as groups, roles, or institutions, and they can be found in villages, corporations, scientific communities, and online platforms.

## Definition

A network is represented by nodes and ties. Nodes are the actors; ties are the relationships between them. Ties can be strong or weak, direct or indirect, reciprocal or one-sided. A network's structure can be described by its density, the proportion of possible ties that actually exist; its centrality, the extent to which some nodes are more connected than others; and its clustering, the tendency for friends of friends to be friends with one another. Two people with the same job, income, and education can have very different lives if one is embedded in a dense, supportive network and the other is isolated.

Social network analysis differs from other approaches by treating relationships as the primary data. Rather than asking what kind of person someone is, it asks where they sit in a web of relationships. This shift has proved powerful: many outcomes, from finding a job to adopting a new practice, are better predicted by network position than by individual traits.

## Origins and Development

The study of networks has several roots. Jacob Moreno, in *Who Shall Survive?* (1934), invented the sociogram, a diagram of who chose whom as a friend or work partner, and founded sociometry, the measurement of social relationships. Anthropologists such as John Barnes, in "Class and Committees in a Norwegian Island Parish" (1954), used the term "social network" to describe the web of ties that cut across formal groups in a small community. In Manchester, the anthropologists around Max Gluckman used network ideas to study urban life in Africa, where formal groups were less important than shifting personal ties.

The field was transformed by Mark Granovetter's "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973), which argued that acquaintances, or weak ties, are crucial for the diffusion of information, because they bridge otherwise separate groups. Ronald Burt, in *Structural Holes* (1992), developed this into a theory of brokerage: people who span structural holes, the gaps between disconnected groups, gain access to diverse information and control over its flow, and are therefore more likely to succeed. Stanley Milgram's small-world experiments, reported in 1967, suggested that people in a large population are connected by surprisingly short chains of acquaintances, a finding later formalised by Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz in "Collective Dynamics of 'Small-World' Networks" (1998).

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the relative importance of structure and content. Network analysis is sometimes criticised for describing the shape of ties without attending to what flows through them: trust, affection, obligation, or money. A tie between two people can mean very different things, and the same structure can support cooperation or conflict. Researchers have responded by combining network analysis with ethnography and interviews, studying both the pattern and the meaning of ties.

A second dimension is the distinction between strong and weak ties. Strong ties provide emotional support, but they tend to connect people who already know the same things. Weak ties provide novel information and access to new worlds, but they offer less support. Burt's work on structural holes adds a further point: advantage comes not only from having many ties, but from bridging otherwise unconnected groups.

A third debate concerns agency and structure. Do people choose their networks, or do networks choose them? People do select friends and partners, but their choices are constrained by geography, institutions, and social categories. Network research shows that homophily, the tendency to associate with similar people, is pervasive, and that it reproduces inequality by keeping resources within advantaged groups.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Social network analysis is now used across the social sciences and beyond. Epidemiologists trace the spread of disease through contact networks; criminologists map gangs and trafficking; organisational researchers study how information flows through firms; and political scientists examine how protest spreads through social media. Online platforms have made networks visible at unprecedented scale, generating vast data on friendship, following, and interaction, and enabling new research on influence, polarisation, and the diffusion of misinformation.

Critics argue that network analysis can become a purely technical exercise, producing elegant diagrams that say little about power, meaning, or history. Digital trace data has intensified this concern, as researchers study what platforms make measurable rather than what matters most. Others note that network concepts, such as centrality, can be misread as individual traits, obscuring the institutional forces that create network positions.
