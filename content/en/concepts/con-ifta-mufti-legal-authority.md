# Ifta' (The Institution of the Mufti and Legal Opinion)

**Ifta'** is the issuing of a jurisprudential opinion (a *fatwa*) on a sharia question, at the request of an inquirer (*mustafti*), by a qualified scholar (the **mufti**). It is one of the oldest functional institutions in Islamic jurisprudence and one of the most continuous across history, operating in parallel with the institution of judgeship but in a fundamentally different nature.

## The Usuli Distinction between Fatwa and Judgment

Many usuliyyun, notably Shihab al-Din al-Qarafi (d. 684/1285) in his book *al-Ihkam fi Tamyiz al-Fatawa 'an al-Ahkam*, drew a precise distinction between fatwa and judicial ruling. The fatwa is an **announcement** of God's ruling on the matter presented. It has no coercive force, although many jurists held that a layperson who lacks the ability to reason independently should follow a qualified mufti. The judicial ruling, by contrast, is an actual **binding** act in a specific dispute between two parties, in which the judge has the authority to enforce it by compulsion. This distinction makes the mufti's authority essentially a religious-epistemic authority rather than an executive one, unlike the authority of the judge.

## The Source of the Mufti's Authority

Unlike the judge, who derives his authority from appointment by the ruler (the *wilaya al-qada'*), the mufti's authority derives primarily from his **scholarly qualification** (*ahliyyat al-ifta'*), recognized socially among his fellow scholars, not necessarily from formal appointment. Although Islamic history later saw this function gradually absorbed into official institutions (such as the office of Shaykh al-Islam in the Ottoman state, whose holders, including Ebussuud Efendi (in office 1545-1574), issued fatwas that helped align imperial law with the sharia, and modern national dar al-ifta), this created a tension between the conception of the mufti as an "independent scholar" and his conception as a "government employee" subject to institutional rules that may restrict his individual interpretive independence.

## Fatwa Literature as a Historical Source

Collected fatwa compilations (such as the *al-Mi'yar al-Mu'rib* of the Maliki jurist Ahmad al-Wansharisi, d. 914/1508) are among the most important sources for the social and legal history of Islamic societies. They reveal the details of real everyday life presented to muftis over the centuries, unlike the abstract theoretical jurisprudential treatises, which assume general hypothetical cases rather than particular actual facts.

## Ifta' in the Modern Era

With the emergence of the modern nation-state, the function of ifta' in many Islamic countries was transformed into central official institutions (such as the national dar al-ifta). Egypt's Dar al-Ifta, established in 1895, is a well-known example. These institutions issue fatwas that sometimes carry a quasi-official character. Collective fatwa bodies, such as the International Islamic Fiqh Academy (founded under the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation in 1981) and the European Council for Fatwa and Research (founded in 1997), issue opinions through deliberation among several scholars. This has revived the old debate about the relationship between the scholarly independence of the mufti and the authority of the state that funds or appoints him. This tension roughly parallels the tension over the independence of the judiciary from the executive authority in modern separation-of-powers theory, although the conceptual frameworks of the two contexts differ fundamentally.
