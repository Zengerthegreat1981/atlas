# Material Agency

A concept in the anthropology of material culture that reconsiders the traditional assumption that "agency" (the capacity to act and to affect the social world) belongs to humans alone. It proposes that objects, tools and works of art can themselves exercise a kind of social action, not as conscious beings in their own right, but as active elements within a network of social relations that shapes and directs human behavior.

## Origins: Alfred Gell

The British anthropologist Alfred Gell provided the most influential theoretical framework for this concept in his book published after his death, *Art and Agency: An Anthropological Theory* (1998), drawing on an earlier work entitled "The Technology of Enchantment and the Enchantment of Technology" (1992). Gell argues that works of art, ritual objects and technical artifacts are treated in many cultural contexts (idols, amulets, magical works, and even contemporary Western art) as something closer to persons or subjects than to inert objects: sources and targets of social agency, capable of "captivating" the viewer or directly influencing their behavior.

## Theoretical Mechanism

This does not mean that Gell grants objects genuine independent consciousness. He proposes instead that their agency is "derived" (secondary agency) from the social relations in which they are embedded (he distinguishes "primary" agents, who act intentionally, from "secondary" agents, such as artefacts, through which that agency is distributed): who made them, for whom they were used, and what meaning and human intention has accumulated upon them. When a person fears a ritual statue or is awed before a work of art, this actual effect is socially real, even though its source is ultimately the network of human relations that produced the object and gave it this symbolic power.

## Relation to Actor-Network Theory

This framework intersects with "Actor-Network Theory," developed in parallel by the French sociologist Bruno Latour, which also treats non-human objects (technologies, tools, even microbes) as "actants" that participate in shaping socio-technical networks, rather than as passive intermediaries used only by human agents.

## Applications

- **Anthropology of art**: re-analysis of how works of art and ritual statues work socially in diverse cultural contexts, moving beyond the traditional aesthetic question ("is this beautiful?") to a functional question ("what does this object do socially?").
- **Archaeology**: modern archaeological approaches have adopted the framework of material agency to understand how ancient tools, buildings and artifacts shaped the behavior of the societies that produced them, rather than simply reflecting a prior culture passively.
- **Technology studies**: analysis of how technical designs (the structure of a building, the interface of a digital application) actually direct the behavior of their users, in a way resembling an "agency" embedded in the design itself.

## Limitations

This framework remains the subject of sharp philosophical debate over the limits of using the term "agency" itself for unconscious objects. Some researchers criticize it as an overstated metaphor that may blur the essential difference between conscious human intentionality and the purely causal material influence of objects.
