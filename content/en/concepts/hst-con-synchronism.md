# Synchronism and Coordination of Chronologies

Synchronism (*synchronismos*) was the method developed by ancient historians and chronographers to coordinate divergent, localized calendrical systems into a shared temporal sequence. In the absence of a universal calendar or an agreed common era, establishing that an event in one society occurred at the same time as an event in another was essential for writing comparative, supra-regional, and universal history.

## Definition and Core Concept

In classical antiquity, almost every city-state (*polis*), kingdom, and regional culture operated according to its own calendar. The Athenians dated events by their annual eponymous archon, the Spartans by their ephors, the Romans by consular pairs, and Near Eastern empires by the regnal years of their monarchs. Synchronism refers to the deliberate intellectual and mathematical effort to align these separate temporal streams by identifying points of intersection—such as shared astronomical phenomena (lunar and solar eclipses), major diplomatic treaties, inter-state battles, or pan-Hellenic athletic festivals.

## Historical Context and Development

Early Greek logographers and historians wrestled continuously with the fragmentation of local time. Hellanicus of Lesbos (fifth century BCE) made an early attempt at systematic chronology by compiling the sequence of the priestesses of Hera at Argos (*Hiereiai tes Heras en Argei*), using their sacerdotal tenures as a benchmark across regional Greek narratives. Herodotus frequently used rough generational estimates (calculating three generations to roughly a century) and synchronisms with Egyptian pharaonic and Persian royal lineages to locate historical figures within a broader temporal continuum.

A major methodological breakthrough occurred in the Hellenistic era with the historian Timaeus of Tauromenium (c. 356–c. 260 BCE), who popularized the four-year Olympic festival cycle (inaugurated traditionally in 776 BCE) as a pan-Hellenic chronological standard. By cross-referencing Olympiads with Athenian archon lists, Spartan ephors, and Western Greek civic records, Timaeus provided a unified chronological matrix. This system was subsequently refined by Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276–c. 194 BCE) in his *Chronographiae*, which established a scientific chronological baseline from the traditional date of the fall of Troy down to Alexander the Great, and by Apollodorus of Athens (second century BCE) in his verse *Chronicle*.

Roman historians similarly integrated Greek synchronisms into their annalistic traditions. Cornelius Nepos, Marcus Terentius Varro, and Titus Pomponius Atticus (*Liber Annalis*) harmonized the Roman consular fasti with the Olympic calendar, ultimately fixing the canonical foundation of Rome to the third year of the sixth Olympiad (753 BCE). Later, Christian chronographers such as Sextus Julius Africanus and Eusebius of Caesarea developed synchronistic tables in parallel columns, coordinating biblical genealogies with Greek, Roman, Babylonian, and Persian dynastic sequences.

## Literary and Rhetorical Synchronisms

In addition to technical chronological calculations, ancient historians frequently deployed dramatic or rhetorical synchronisms. These were symbolic alignments designed to underscore moral, philosophical, or geopolitical patterns. Herodotus famously recorded the tradition that the Greek victory over the Persians at Salamis occurred on the exact same day that the Sicilian Greeks defeated the Carthaginians at the Battle of Himera in 480 BCE (7.166), a coincidence later writers found significant. Similarly, traditions aligned the birth of Alexander the Great with the burning of the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus in 356 BCE.

## Historiographical Limitations and Modern Reassessment

Ancient synchronisms faced substantial limitations. Calendrical variations—such as lunar intercalations, shifting civic new years, and scribal corruptions in king lists—frequently led to cumulative errors. Furthermore, ancient chronographers often forced synchronisms to validate ideological agendas, such as demonstrating the priority and superior antiquity of one civilization over another. Modern historical criticism relies on epigraphic fasti, astronomical calculations of eclipses, and archaeological stratigraphy to identify and correct the artificial synchronisms embedded in classical literary sources.
