# Age Grades

Age grades are formally recognised groups of people of roughly the same age who move together through a series of named grades or stages over the course of their lives, acquiring new rights, duties and identities at each stage. The system is best known from East Africa, but age grades and age sets occur in many societies, and the principle — that age organises social life — is universal. In an age-grade system, a person's social position depends less on who their parents are than on which grade they occupy: children, initiates, warriors, married elders and retired seniors each have their own roles, their own gatherings and their own authority. Age grades are therefore a form of social organisation in their own right, comparable to kinship, and they cross-cut descent groups, binding together people from different families who share a stage of life.

## Origins and Development

The classic accounts of age grades come from East Africa. Among the Maasai, boys are circumcised together and enter the warrior grade, the *morani*, spending years living apart, herding, protecting the community and proving their courage, before graduating to junior elderhood, marrying and taking on political authority. Paul Spencer's study of the Maasai of Matapato showed that the system is both a ladder and a cycle: each new age set climbs the same rungs, and the grades are linked in a repeating sequence, so that the elders of today were the warriors of yesterday and the warriors of today will be the elders of tomorrow. Among the Samburu, closely related to the Maasai, age sets are formed every fourteen years or so, and the long intervals between sets create distinct generations with strong internal solidarity. Philip Gulliver's study of the Turkana described a different variant: age sets that are less rigidly graded, but that still organise labour, warfare and ritual, and that give young men a corporate identity beyond their families.

Arnold van Gennep's theory of rites of passage provides the framework for understanding how age grades work. Movement from one grade to the next is marked by ritual — separation from the old status, a liminal period of transition, and incorporation into the new grade — and the ritual does not merely recognise the change but creates it. Victor Turner developed this insight, showing that the liminal phase of initiation generates intense solidarity, or *communitas*, among those passing through it together. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, writing on the Andaman Islanders and on African societies, emphasised the structural function of age grades: they distribute authority by age, prevent the concentration of power in any one generation, and provide a mechanism for the orderly transfer of roles as people grow old and die.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns the relationship between age grades and generation. An age set is a group formed at one time that moves together through life, while an age grade is the stage itself; in some systems the two coincide, in others they do not, and the distinction matters for understanding how authority passes between generations. A second debate asks whether age-grade systems are egalitarian or hierarchical: within a grade, members are formally equal, but the grades themselves are ranked, with elders commanding deference and warriors commanding respect, so the system combines equality among peers with hierarchy between grades. A third debate concerns gender: age-grade systems are usually described through men, and women's grades — if they exist — have often been overlooked by male ethnographers. Recent research has documented women's age organisations in East Africa and elsewhere, showing that women too are graded, initiated and organised by age, and that their systems are not mere reflections of men's. A further debate asks how age grades survive change: schooling, wage labour, urban migration and Christianity have disrupted the timing and the rituals of grading, yet age-set identities often persist, reorganised around new institutions such as schools, churches and political parties.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Age grades remain socially powerful. Among the Maasai and Samburu, age-set membership still shapes political mobilisation, marriage and conflict, and politicians appeal to age-set loyalties in elections. More broadly, the anthropology of age grades has shaped how scholars think about the life course everywhere: the idea that societies divide life into stages — childhood, adolescence, adulthood, old age — and mark the transitions with ritual is now a standard tool for studying schools, the military, retirement and the professions. The concept also carries a critique of Western assumptions: where Euro-American societies treat age as a biological fact and youth as a problem, age-grade systems treat age as a social achievement, and old age as the summit of authority rather than its decline. Studying age grades is therefore not only a study of other societies but a mirror held up to our own ways of growing up and growing old.
