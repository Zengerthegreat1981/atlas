# Kinship Universals

A classic anthropological question: are there features or rules in the organization of kinship and marriage that are shared by all known human societies, regardless of differences in their languages, terminologies, and social structures? This question intersects with the broader debate between cultural relativist anthropology and the evolutionary and structural approaches that seek universal patterns.

## Leading Candidates for Universality

- **The incest taboo**: this is almost the most broadly agreed upon candidate for universality among "kinship universals." No human society has been recorded that permits marriage or sexual intercourse between parent and child or between full siblings as a general institutional practice (with very limited exceptions among ruling elites, such as sibling marriage in some royal families of ancient Egypt and the Inca, and the notable non-royal case of brother-sister marriages recorded in census returns from Roman Egypt). However, the boundaries of the taboo, meaning who counts as "forbidden" beyond the nuclear family (such as parallel versus cross cousins), vary widely between cultures. This is why the "taboo" is universal in principle but not in its details.

- **The nuclear family as a basic unit**: George Peter Murdock argued that the nuclear family (a couple and their children) is the primary unit around which broader kinship systems are formed in nearly all societies, even when it is incorporated into larger formations (polygynous or extended families). This claim sparked broad debate, since later studies offered examples of societies (such as the Nayar of South India historically) whose family structure does not conform to a strict nuclear model.

- **Exogamy rules within the kin group**: most kinship systems, whether patrilineal or matrilineal, require marriage outside the immediate kin group. This creates alliances between groups through the exchange of marriages. Claude Lévi-Strauss built his structuralist theory of the "elementary structures of kinship" (1949) on this idea: the incest taboo is not merely a prohibition, but in its positive essence an obligation to social exchange between groups through marriage.

- **Kinship terminologies classified into limited patterns**: Murdock and later researchers showed that human languages, despite their diversity, classify kinship terms (such as the names for siblings and cousins) into a relatively limited number of patterns that recur worldwide (such as the Eskimo, Hawaiian, or Sudanese systems). However, contemporary researchers (Passmore and Jordan, 2020, "No universals in the cultural evolution of kinship terminology") tested proposed links between kinship terminology and social practices across language families and found no universal pattern of co-evolution, so recurring types should not be read as the product of strict universal rules.

## Theoretical Debate

The explanation of these recurring patterns falls into three main positions. A **biological-evolutionary** explanation holds that there is an instinctive aversion to marriage between those who grew up together from childhood, as proposed by Edvard Westermarck and later supported by ethnographic studies of the "Westermarck effect" on Israeli kibbutzim and the Taiwanese *sim-pua* marriages. A **functional-social** explanation holds that the incest taboo protects family cohesion from internal sexual competition, as argued by Malinowski. A **structural-exchange** explanation (Lévi-Strauss) holds that the taboo is a tool for enforcing social exchange between groups. The weighing among these explanations remains open, and the contemporary trend tends toward accepting that they combine in varying degrees.

## Limits

The most important methodological warning is that "universality" as a general principle (the existence of an incest taboo) does not imply universality in applied details (exactly who is covered by the taboo). The conflation of these two levels occurred often in early literature. Moreover, some candidates for universality (such as the nuclear family) have faced enough ethnographic counterexamples to weaken a strict universalist claim and turn it into a "high statistical majority" rather than an absolute rule.
