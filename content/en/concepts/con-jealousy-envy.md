# Jealousy and Envy

Jealousy and envy are frequently conflated in everyday discourse, yet psychological research reveals a structural distinction in the social geometry of these emotions: the number of parties involved. Envy is fundamentally a dyadic emotion involving two entities: an individual desires a quality, possession, or achievement held by another. Jealousy is a triadic emotion involving three entities: an individual perceives a threat from a rival to an existing, valued relationship with a significant other. This distinction, drawn in appraisal-based accounts such as Lazarus's (1991), helps explain why the two emotions generate distinct subjective experiences, behavioral reactions, and moral evaluations.

## Definition and Core Concepts

Envy, in cognitive appraisal theory, arises from an unfavorable upward social comparison that induces feelings of inferiority, resentment, and longing. The core appraisal is one of subjective lack: another person possesses something that the envier values but does not have. Jealousy, by contrast, centers on the threat of relational loss or displacement. The core appraisal is one of existential insecurity: a third party threatens to sever or diminish the affection, fidelity, or attention of a partner or attachment figure.

Although jealousy and envy can co-occur—such as when an individual envies a rival's charm while experiencing jealousy over their partner's attention to that rival—their emotional profiles differ significantly. In empirical studies by Parrott and Smith (1993), envy was characterized by feelings of inferiority, longing, resentment, and disapproval of the emotion. Jealousy was characterized by fear of loss, distrust, anxiety, and anger.

## Empirical Classifications and Evolutionary Perspectives

In prototype studies of emotion concepts, Phillip Shaver and colleagues (1987) discovered that both jealousy and envy cluster within the anger family rather than the love or sadness families. This placement highlights the hostile, antagonistic properties inherent in both emotional experiences, distinguishing them from pure grief or depressive withdrawal.

From an evolutionary psychological perspective, David Buss and colleagues (1992) investigated sex differences in jealousy triggers. They hypothesized that men, confronting paternity uncertainty, would report greater distress over a partner's sexual infidelity, whereas women, confronting the potential diversion of paternal investment and resources, would report greater distress over emotional infidelity. While subsequent cross-cultural and methodological studies debate whether this divergence stems from evolved cognitive modules or domain-general sociocultural socialization, the evolutionary paradigm established jealousy as an adaptive mechanism designed to defend valued partnerships.

Regarding envy, Niels van de Ven, Marcel Zeelenberg, and Rik Pieters (2009) established an important functional distinction between benign and malicious envy:
- **Benign envy**: generates admiration and self-motivating drive to elevate one's own standing through legitimate effort (*leveling up*).
- **Malicious envy**: generates hostility and schadenfreude, motivating destructive actions aimed at pulling the advantaged rival down (*leveling down*).

## Jealousy and Envy in the Arabic-Islamic Tradition

The classical Arabic-Islamic tradition analyzed envy (*hasad*) and protective jealousy (*ghira*) with philosophical and moral rigor across ethical, medical, and psychological treatises:
- **Al-Ghazali (1058–1111 CE)** devoted substantial chapters of *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* (The Revival of the Religious Sciences) to the anatomy of *hasad*, identifying it as a fatal affliction of the heart (*amrad al-qulub*). He carefully distinguished between destructive envy (*hasad*), which wills the removal of another's blessing (*zawal al-ni'ma*), and emulation (*ghibta*), which aspires to attain similar virtue without ill will toward the other—directly presaging the modern distinction between malicious and benign envy.
- **Al-Jahiz (776–868/869 CE)** examined envy in his treatise *Risalat al-Hasid wa-l-Mahsud* (Epistle on the Envier and the Envied), presenting an acute psychological analysis of the envier's inner misery, noting that envy is a unique vice because its torment is experienced primarily by the envier before it ever harms the envied.
- **Abu Bakr al-Razi (Rhazes, 865–925 CE)**, in his rationalist moral-medical work *Kitab al-Tibb al-Ruhani* (The Spiritual Physic), classified envy as an irrational passion that consumes vital physical and mental energy. He prescribed cognitive-behavioral strategies to restore psychic equilibrium by reminding the envier of the illusory nature of worldly status.
- **Ibn Hazm (994–1064 CE)**, in *Mudawat al-Nufus* (The Healing of Souls) and *Tawq al-Hamama* (The Ring of the Dove), analyzed jealousy (*ghira*) as an inherent accompaniment to genuine love, describing how noble jealousy guards relational boundaries, whereas excessive, groundless suspicion (*al-shakk*) devolves into a destructive pathology.

## Contemporary Relevance and Clinical Considerations

In contemporary society, digital connectivity and social media algorithms continually stimulate upward social comparison, intensifying both benign and malicious envy. Passively scrolling curated depictions of peers' wealth, beauty, and success frequently precipitates distress and depressive symptoms. In clinical practice, chronic jealousy and envy typically reflect underlying narcissistic vulnerability, insecure attachment (notably anxious or fearful styles), and acute fears of abandonment. Therapy focuses on helping individuals distinguish real relational boundary violations from internal projective anxiety.
