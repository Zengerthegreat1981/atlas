# Jealousy and Envy

Jealousy and envy are among the most frequently confused emotions in ordinary language, in English and in Arabic alike, yet they differ in a structural feature that changes everything about how they are experienced: the number of people involved. Envy is a two-person emotion — I want what you have. Jealousy is a three-person emotion — I resent a third party because I am losing, or fear losing, someone's affection. This distinction, formulated by Richard Lazarus, is the key to understanding both emotions, and it explains why they feel so different, why they motivate such different behaviour, and why they have been classified, studied, and morally judged in such different ways.

## Definition and Core Concept

Envy, in Lazarus's formulation, is the desire to possess what another person possesses — a quality, an achievement, a position, a good. It is a dyadic relation, involving the envier and the envied, and its core appraisal is one of inferiority: the other has something I lack, and I want it. Jealousy, by contrast, is the resentment of a third party for the loss, or the threatened loss, of another person's affection. It is a triadic relation, involving the jealous person, the beloved, and the rival, and its core appraisal is one of threat: someone is coming between me and the person whose love I need. The two emotions can coexist — a person may envy a rival's attractiveness and be jealous of the attention that rival receives — but they are not the same experience. Research by Parrott and Smith (1993) showed that envy is dominated by inferiority, resentment, and longing, while jealousy is dominated by fear, anxiety about loss, and anger toward the rival.

## The Place in Shaver's Classification

One of the more surprising findings in the psychology of emotion is the place that jealousy and envy occupy in the classification developed by Phillip Shaver and his colleagues in 1987. Shaver's team asked ordinary people to sort emotion words into families, and found that both jealousy and envy clustered with the anger family, not with a separate family of their own, and not with love or desire, where intuition might have placed them. This placement frames both emotions as hostile, antagonistic states rather than as forms of love or longing, and it distinguishes them structurally from sadness and from shame and guilt, which Shaver's classification places in the sadness family. The classification should not be accepted uncritically: it reflects how ordinary speakers group emotion words, and ordinary language may conflate what a more careful analysis would separate. Whether the anger-family placement has sufficient empirical support remains an open question, recorded here as such.

## Empirical Research

The empirical study of jealousy and envy has produced some of the most debated findings in emotion research. David Buss and his colleagues, in a landmark study published in 1992, found a sex difference in jealousy: men, on average, reported greater distress over a partner's sexual infidelity, while women reported greater distress over a partner's emotional infidelity. Buss interpreted this difference in evolutionary terms, arguing that men faced the adaptive problem of paternity uncertainty, while women faced the problem of losing a partner's investment. The finding has been replicated in some studies and challenged in others, and it remains the subject of methodological debate, but it established jealousy as a central topic in evolutionary psychology. Research on envy has taken a different direction: Richard Smith and Sung Hee Kim, in a review published in 2007, distinguished malicious envy, which aims at harming the envied person, from benign envy, which motivates self-improvement — a distinction that has reshaped the moral understanding of envy.

## Jealousy and Envy in the Arabic-Islamic Tradition

The Arabic-Islamic tradition treats envy, hasad, with a seriousness that has few parallels in the modern psychological literature. The Quran condemns the envier in the chapter al-Falaq, "from the evil of the envier when he envies," and a widely transmitted hadith warns, "Beware of envy, for envy consumes good deeds as fire consumes wood." The jurists and moralists distinguished sharply between hasad, the blameworthy desire for another's blessing to be removed, and ghibta, the praiseworthy desire to attain a similar blessing without wishing its removal — a distinction that anticipates the modern contrast between malicious and benign envy. Al-Ghazali devoted extended passages of his Ihya ulum al-din to the condemnation and treatment of envy, counting it among the destructive traits of the soul. Jealousy, ghira, is treated with a more complex moral valuation: praised when it concerns the protection of family honour, condemned when it becomes excessive suspicion — close to the modern distinction between reactive and pathological jealousy.

## Contemporary Relevance and Open Questions

Jealousy and envy are more visible today than ever before, because the conditions that produce them have intensified. Social media exposes people to a constant stream of others' achievements, possessions, and relationships, and research on social comparison suggests that this exposure increases envy and its attendant distress. Romantic jealousy has acquired new triggers — the partner's online interactions, the ambiguity of digital communication — and clinicians report that both emotions are among the most common presenting problems in couples therapy, and among the most difficult to treat, because they are rooted in genuine vulnerabilities: the fear of being unlovable, the sense of being less than others, the terror of abandonment. The open questions are therefore practical as well as theoretical: how far are these emotions universal, and how far shaped by culture? What distinguishes the jealousy that protects a relationship from the jealousy that destroys it? And how should the Arabic-Islamic vocabulary of hasad, ghibta, and ghira be brought into conversation with the psychological literature?
