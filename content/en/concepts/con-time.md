# Time

A concept central to the phenomenological wing of existential psychotherapy.

## Defining the Concept

Time here is not clock time but **lived time**: how the future, the past, and the present appear to this person. And mental illness is read as a disturbance in this structure before it is a list of symptoms.

## The Core Disagreements Within the Concept

There is no great dispute here, but a variation in emphasis: the European phenomenological line (Minkowski, Straus, Binswanger) developed it in detail, while many later practical approaches treat time mainly as a tool or a theme, for example in work on mortality and priorities.

## How It Appears in the Session

Direct, practical questions: How does next week look in your mind? Do you see yourself five years from now? And what is your sense of time passing — slow or accelerating? The answers reveal a structure that mood questions do not.

## The Most Common Misunderstanding

Confusing lived time with time management. The first is a structure of experience, the second a skill of organisation — and there is no relation between them.

## Example and Counter-Example

**Example:** A client complains that time "passes without meaning" despite his constant busyness. Asking about his present, past, and future reveals that he lives entirely in a deferred future ("I will be happy when I succeed") with no actually lived present. The work here is on lived time (how time is experienced from within), not on his schedule.

**Counter-example:** A therapist advises a client who complains of feeling that time is "accelerating out of control" to use time-management techniques (scheduling apps, task lists) as an existential solution. But the problem here is structural-experiential, not organisational, and time-management tools may hide the symptom without touching the structure of temporal experience itself that Minkowski and Straus described.

## What Cannot Be Inferred from This Concept

It **does not mean** that every complaint of "not enough time" or procrastination is a deep existential crisis in the structure of lived time calling for philosophical analysis — many are an organisational matter or simple habits solved with practical tools.

Nor does it mean that the past has "ended" and has no value in therapeutic work — Frankl insists on the opposite: the accomplished past is preserved, in his image of the granaries of the past, and cannot be erased, and this is a therapeutic resource, not a temporal waste to be moved past, going only forward.

## The Arab and Cultural Context

Analyses of lived time (Minkowski, Straus, Gebsattel) were formulated within a modern Western linear conception of time — a fixed past, a present, a future open to individual planning. Traditional Arab cultures, particularly rural or religious ones, may carry temporal conceptions closer to the circular or tied to ritual, seasonal, and religious cycles (Ramadan, Hajj, agricultural seasons) rather than the accelerating individual linear time that much contemporary Western existential clinical practice assumes.

This means that a disturbance in the "relation to time" in an Arab client may in truth be a tension between two coexisting cultural rhythms (modern accelerating time in work and the city, and traditional circular time in family and religion), not a simple individual defect in the "structure of lived time" as classical European phenomenology describes it — a dimension the original literature never addressed, because it was not designed for contexts with a dual temporal rhythm.

## Comparative Table

| Thinker | Key Textual Source | His exact formulation | What follows in the room | His disagreement with others |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heidegger | *Being and Time* (1927), §65 ("Temporality as the ontological meaning of care") and §68 | Temporality is the structure of Dasein itself. The human being always runs ahead of themselves toward their possibilities, and is burdened with what has been. | Directs attention to the person's relation to their future, not only to their history. | Ontological, and the origin on which Minkowski and Binswanger built. |
| Minkowski | *Lived Time* (1933/1970) | In depression the future closes and the past grows heavy, so the present becomes a wall. And in some forms of schizophrenia the vital contact with reality breaks. | Describing the condition in the language of time gives a precision that symptom lists do not, and the patient understands it about themselves at once. | The most precise phenomenological application in the history of the school. |
| Binswanger | "The Case of Ellen West" (1944); *Being-in-the-World* (1963) | Each world has its own time: some worlds are accelerated, some frozen, some have no future. | The patient's world is described as a whole rather than broken apart. | Draws on Heidegger, though Heidegger himself objected to Binswanger's adaptation of his ideas. |
| Frankl | *Man's Search for Meaning* (first English edition 1959 as *From Death-Camp to Existentialism*), Part II; *The Doctor and the Soul* (1955) | The past is not lost but preserved: what has been achieved and lived cannot be taken away. In his image, the granaries of the past are full, not empty. | Work with the elderly and with patients at the end of life: turning the gaze from what remains to what has been accomplished. | The most consoling formulation in the school, and the most liable to turn into motivation. |
| Yalom | *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980), the chapters on death | Awareness of mortality rearranges time: one who has faced their end loses the luxury of postponing what matters. | Using the approach of the end, or a moment of awakening, to rearrange priorities. | Uses time as a tool, not a subject. |
| Van Deurzen | *Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy in Practice* (2012) | Her four dimensions of existence (physical, social, personal, spiritual) can each carry a different relation to time. | Revealing conflicts between these: for example, that the body is at one stage of life and the personal project at another. | A practical elaboration not found in the others. |
