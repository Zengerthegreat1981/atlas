# Comparative Religious Legal Systems

When one sets Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*), Jewish *halakha*, canon law and Hindu *dharma* side by side in a single comparison, a question arises that precedes the comparison itself: are these systems of the same kind, such that they may be called "religious law"? The answer commonly given in the studies is that each combines ritual, ethics, rights and penalties; that its declared source is revelation or sacred authority; and that its interpretive apparatus is a class of scholars.

## The Roots of Comparison in the Arab Heritage

The Islamic world anticipated the comparison between schools within a single tradition: comparative jurisprudence (the science of disagreement) produced major works such as Ibn Rushd al-Hafid's *The Distinguished Jurist's Primer* (*Bidayat al-mujtahid*, late twelfth century), which presents the questions of jurisprudence along with the evidence of the schools and then traces the disagreement to its causes. Al-Biruni went further in *Verifying What Is Said of India* (c. 1030), describing the rules of marriage, inheritance and penalties among the Indians in comparison with those of the Muslims and others, and it is an early example of descriptive comparison across religious and legal traditions. Al-Shahrastani, in *The Book of Religions and Sects* (*al-Milal wa-l-nihal*, c. 1127), surveyed Islamic sects and other religions and philosophies by their doctrines, a heresiographical rather than strictly legal comparison.

## The Modern Beginning

Comparative law in its modern sense began at the International Congress of Comparative Law in Paris in 1900, on the initiative of Édouard Lambert and Raymond Saleilles, and had been preceded by the work of Henry Maine, who drew on Roman, Indian and early Irish law in *Ancient Law* (1861). Then came Joseph Schacht, with his *An Introduction to Islamic Law* (1964), which offered a history of the development of Islamic jurisprudence and provoked a wide and continuing debate. In recent decades, H. Patrick Glenn, in *Legal Traditions of the World* (2000, 4th edition 2010), attempted a classification placing religious traditions alongside civil and customary ones, and Wael Hallaq, in *Sharia: Theory, Practice, Transformations* (2009), stressed that the Sharia was a moral-legal system whose structure differed fundamentally from that of the modern state.

## Aspects of Comparison

- **The source**: the Qur'an, the Sunna, consensus (*ijma'*) and analogy (*qiyas*), in contrast to the Torah and oral tradition in Jewish thought, the Bible, church councils and papal decrees among Catholics, and the *Shruti* and *Smriti* in Hindu dharma.
- **The interpreter of the text**: the *mujtahid*, the *mufti* and the judge; the rabbi and the *beth din*; the ecclesiastical courts; and the pandit. Menachem Elon, in *Jewish Law* (1994), explains that *halakha* developed through the Mishnah, the Talmud and rabbinic responsa.
- **The relationship with the state**: it varied between integration and separation. In the Church, an independent law arose from Gratian's *Decretum* (c. 1140), as James Brundage shows in *Medieval Canon Law* (1995).
- **The variable and the constant**: the opening or closing of the gate of *ijtihad*, and the distinction between acts of worship (*'ibadat*) and transactions (*mu'amalat*).

## Limits of the Comparison

A central criticism is that the Western concept of "law" versus "religion" is projected onto traditions that do not separate the two. In India, Donald Davis, in *The Spirit of Hindu Law* (2010), argues that Hindu law has an indigenous history as a tradition of *dharmashastra* learning and practice, while the colonial administration, beginning with projects such as the *Code of Gentoo Laws* (1776), recast those texts as a judicial code and so shaped the modern concept of "Hindu law." Comparison also overlooks contexts: Islamic jurisprudence was for much of its history the work of jurists largely independent of the state, whereas canon law was developed within a hierarchical church institution. Finally, the comparison should not be understood as a matter of similarity: in many cases it is a structural difference calling for caution.
