# Counter-Majoritarian Difficulty

The **counter-majoritarian difficulty** is a term coined by **Alexander Bickel** in his book *The Least Dangerous Branch* (1962) to describe a structural paradox that accompanies the exercise of **judicial review of legislation** in any constitutional democracy: how can the authority of a body of unelected judges to strike down a decision made by an elected majority through its legislative representatives be justified?

## Formulation

Bickel observes that, in abstract structure, this practice is in tension with the basic democratic principle of majority rule. American federal judges are appointed rather than elected, and hold office during good behavior (in effect, for life), yet they are empowered to invalidate laws enacted by Congress or by state legislatures, bodies directly subject to periodic electoral accountability. In Bickel's view this paradox cannot be fully eliminated. Bickel's own response was to seek a principled account of when courts should intervene and when they should hold back, including what he called the "passive virtues" (techniques such as standing, ripeness and the avoidance of constitutional questions, developed in *The Least Dangerous Branch*).

## Its Significance in the History of American Constitutional Thought

This concept became the near-obligatory starting point for every subsequent theory of the legitimacy of judicial review in American constitutional law, whether those theories seek to justify judicial review despite the difficulty (such as **John Hart Ely**'s attempt to confine it to reinforcing the representative process itself, in *Democracy and Distrust*, 1980) or to deepen skepticism about it (such as **Jeremy Waldron**, who in "The Core of the Case Against Judicial Review" (2006) argued that strong judicial review is hard to justify in societies with working democratic institutions and a reasonable commitment to rights). **Ronald Dworkin** answered from the other side, arguing in his "moral reading" of the constitution that democracy properly understood includes substantive constitutional rights, so that enforcing them by courts is not undemocratic.

## Further Dimensions of the Difficulty

Later scholars noted that the difficulty, as Bickel formulated it, implicitly assumes that democratic legislation faithfully reflects the will of the majority. That assumption faces further complications in political systems marred by representational defects, such as malapportioned or gerrymandered districting, restrictions on voting, or the influence of money and lobbying. This opens the way to a counter-argument: that judicial review may sometimes correct defects in democratic representation itself, rather than merely contradict it. This is the argument Ely exploited in his theory.
