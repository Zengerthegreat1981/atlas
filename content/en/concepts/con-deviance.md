# Deviance: Breaking the Rules, and Who Gets to Say So

**Deviance** is behaviour, belief, or appearance that violates the norms of a group or society and is met with a negative response — disapproval, stigma, punishment, or exclusion. Deviance is not a property of an act itself: the same act can be deviant in one society and normal in another, praised in one era and punished in the next. Sociology therefore studies deviance not as a catalogue of strange behaviours but as a social process: who defines the rules, who is labelled a deviant, and what happens to those who are labelled.

## Definition and Development

Robert K. Merton, in "Social Structure and Anomie" (1938), gave the first influential sociological account of why deviance occurs. When a society's approved goals — above all, material success — are emphasized while the legitimate means of reaching them are unequally distributed, people adapt. Merton distinguished five adaptations: conformity; innovation, pursuing the goals by illegitimate means; ritualism, abiding by the means while abandoning the goals; retreatism, withdrawing from both goals and means; and rebellion, seeking to replace the goals and means altogether. Merton's point was that deviance is not a failure of socialization but a normal product of the social structure itself: a society that promises success to all while denying the means to many will produce deviance as a matter of course.

Edwin Lemert, in *Social Pathology* (1951), drew the distinction that organized the field for decades. Primary deviance is the initial rule-breaking, often trivial and unnoticed. Secondary deviance begins when the person is caught, labelled, and begins to reorganize their identity around the label — the teenager caught shoplifting who comes to see themselves as a thief, and then steals again because that is who they now are. Lemert's argument was that the reaction to deviance matters more than the act itself: it is the labelling that creates the career of the deviant.

## Key Contributions: Labelling and Moral Panics

Howard Becker, in *Outsiders: Studies in the Sociology of Deviance* (1963), turned Lemert's insight into a research programme. Deviance, Becker argued, is behaviour that people so label. His studies of marijuana users and dance musicians showed that people learn deviance in groups: they learn how to use drugs, how to enjoy their effects, and how to manage the stigma attached to them. Becker also showed that the rules are not made by society as a whole but by moral entrepreneurs — reformers and officials who campaign to criminalize behaviours they disapprove of — and enforced by rule enforcers whose careers depend on finding rule-breakers.

Stanley Cohen, in *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (1972), showed how deviance is amplified by the media and the state. Studying the clashes between Mods and Rockers in 1960s Britain, Cohen traced how a minor disturbance was reported as a threat to the nation, how the public demanded action, and how the police and courts responded with new powers — which in turn produced more of the behaviour they were meant to suppress. A moral panic, Cohen argued, is a cycle in which labelling creates the very deviance it claims to control.

## Examples

The history of drug use illustrates the labelling process. Alcohol is a legal drug whose harms are widely known; cannabis was criminalized in many countries in the twentieth century and is now being legalized in others — without the drug itself changing. What changed was the label, the law, and the interests behind them. Similarly, homosexuality was classified as a mental disorder until 1973, when the American Psychiatric Association removed it from its diagnostic manual. The behaviour did not change; the definition of deviance did.

Deviance also operates in everyday life. A student who cheats, a worker who steals time, a neighbour who plays loud music at night — each is judged against norms that are enforced unevenly. Studies consistently show that the same act is more likely to be labelled deviant when committed by the poor, the young, or members of stigmatized minorities than when committed by the powerful.

## Medicalization

Peter Conrad and Joseph Schneider's *Deviance and Medicalization* (1980) described the process by which behaviors once treated as moral failings or crimes come to be defined and managed as medical conditions. They argued that this changes who has authority over the behavior (physicians rather than courts or clergy) and how responsibility is assigned, and they showed that medicalization is shaped by social movements and professional interests as well as by science.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Labelling theory has been criticized for a paradox: it explains how deviance is defined but says little about why people break rules in the first place, before any label is applied. Critics also note that it works best for minor, victimless deviance and less well for serious harm — few sociologists would argue that murder is only deviant because it is labelled so. And the theory has been charged with romanticizing the deviant, treating rule-breakers as victims of labelling rather than as agents with their own motives.

Deviance remains central to sociology and criminology. It now frames research on digital life, where new forms of deviance — trolling, doxxing, online fraud — are defined and policed by platforms as much as by states; on the medicalization of deviance, where behaviours once punished are now treated; and on the politics of labelling, where the power to call something deviant is itself a form of social power. The study of deviance is, in the end, the study of how societies draw their boundaries — and who gets to draw them.
