# Age Sets

An age set is a group of people of roughly the same age who are formally initiated together and who pass through the stages of life together as a recognised corporate group. Unlike an age grade — a rank or stage, such as "youth" or "elder," that individuals move through one by one — an age set is a permanent cohort: its members are bound to one another for life, share a name, and advance collectively from one grade to the next. Age-set systems are best known from East Africa, particularly among the Maasai, Samburu, and related pastoralist peoples, but they also occur in other parts of Africa and beyond. They organise labour, warfare, marriage, and political authority, and they give the passage of time a social form.

## Origins and Development

The classic ethnography of age sets is E. E. Evans-Pritchard's study of the Nuer, whose age-sets he described in a 1936 article: Nuer boys initiated together formed a set with its own name and identity, and the set moved as a body through the grades of youth, manhood, and elderhood. Philip Gulliver's *The Family Herds* (1955), a study of the Jie and Turkana of Uganda and Kenya, showed how age sets coordinated the herding economy: young men guarded the cattle, older men managed the herds, and elders controlled ritual and dispute settlement. The division of labour followed the division of age.

Paul Spencer's *The Maasai of Matapato* (1988) gave the fullest account of a living age-set system. Among the Maasai, boys are circumcised in groups, becoming *morani* — warriors — who live apart, guard the herds, and defend the community. After roughly fifteen years, the whole set is promoted together to junior elderhood, and later to senior elderhood, while a new set of warriors takes its place. Promotion is a collective ritual, and the rivalry between successive sets — each eager to prove itself braver than the last — is a motor of social life.

The comparative literature consolidated these findings in the 1970s and 1980s. P. T. W. Baxter and Uri Almagor's edited volume *Age, Generation and Time* (1978) compared age organisation across Africa, and Bernardo Bernardi's *Age Class Systems* (1985) offered a systematic typology, distinguishing age sets (cohorts) from age grades (stages) and from generation sets, in which membership is determined by descent rather than by age.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns the difference between age sets and age grades. The distinction matters because the two principles organise society differently. An age-grade system, such as that of the Nyakyusa of Tanzania, sorts individuals into ranks by life stage, and the ranks are the stable structure. An age-set system makes the cohort the stable structure: the set endures while its members age. In practice the two often combine — sets pass through grades — but keeping them analytically separate clarifies how a society handles succession, authority, and conflict between generations.

A second dimension is power. Age-set systems are gerontocratic in tendency: elders control marriage, ritual, and the allocation of resources, and young men must wait — sometimes for decades — before they can marry and become full adults. The system thus produces a structural tension between generations, and much of the ethnography documents how young men negotiate, evade, or challenge elder authority, through raiding, migration, or the adoption of new livelihoods.

A third dimension is gender. Age-set systems are most fully developed for men, and anthropologists have asked whether women have parallel institutions. Among the Maasai and Samburu, women are grouped by the age sets of their husbands, and their status changes as their husbands are promoted; but women's own age-based groupings, circumcision cohorts, and networks of mutual support have received less attention, and recent ethnography has begun to correct that imbalance.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Age-set systems have not disappeared. Among the Maasai, the warrior phase remains a powerful source of identity even as young men attend school, work in towns, and join the army — and the tension between the demands of the set and the demands of formal education is a recurring theme in contemporary ethnography. The system's calendar has also been affected by state intervention: governments have discouraged the ceremonies of promotion, and missionaries and development agencies have criticised the practice of delaying marriage.

Critics of the classic literature argue that it treated age-set systems as timeless structures, underplaying their flexibility and their entanglement with colonial and postcolonial history. Age sets were reshaped by taxation, military recruitment, and land enclosure, and they continue to adapt — for instance, when a set's promotion ceremony becomes an occasion for political mobilisation or for asserting cultural identity in the face of the state. The strongest recent work therefore treats age sets not as relics of a traditional order but as living institutions through which people organise time, authority, and belonging in a changing world.
