# Working Memory in Sentence Parsing

Working memory in sentence parsing refers to the role of limited-capacity cognitive resources in storing and integrating linguistic representations during online sentence comprehension. As a reader or listener encounters a sentence word by word, the human parser must construct syntactic hierarchies incrementally. Incoming lexical items must be held active in memory, syntactic predictions must be maintained across intervening words, and structural dependencies—such as connecting a moved object pronoun to its subcategorizing verb—must be resolved in real time. Psycholinguistic research investigates how memory limitations constrain this incremental parsing process and explain processing difficulty.

## Capacity and Locality Models: Dependency Locality Theory

Early cognitive models attributed processing difficulty primarily to limitations in working memory storage capacity (Just & Carpenter, 1992). This framework culminated in Edward Gibson's **Dependency Locality Theory (DLT)** (1998, 2000), which quantifies the computational resource demands of sentence comprehension in terms of two distinct cost components:

1. **Storage Cost**: The memory resources required to maintain unfulfilled syntactic predictions (such as a subject awaiting an obligatory verb) active in memory. Each unsatisfied structural head imposes a continuous cognitive load until resolved.
2. **Integration Cost**: The cognitive effort required to connect an incoming word to an earlier dependent element across intervening discourse referents. According to DLT, integration difficulty increases as a direct function of the number of new discourse entities introduced between the dependent elements.

A classic demonstration of locality effects occurs in center-embedded relative clauses. While a single subject-extracted relative clause (e.g., "The reporter who attacked the senator admitted the error") is processed with relative ease, center-embedded object-extracted structures (e.g., "The reporter who the senator that the intern praised attacked admitted the error") impose severe processing breakdowns. Although entirely grammatical, multiple unclosed structural dependencies simultaneously overload working memory capacity.

## Cue-Based Retrieval and Similarity-Based Interference

While resource-capacity models emphasize decay and limited storage slots, contemporary psycholinguistic theories formulate sentence parsing within a content-addressable, **cue-based retrieval architecture** (Lewis & Vasishth, 2005; McElree, 2000; Van Dyke & Lewis, 2003).

In cue-based models, linguistic items are not actively held in a specialized short-term buffer; instead, they are encoded into working memory as bundles of syntactic, semantic, and morphological features. When a downstream head (such as a verb) requires a dependent (such as an agreement-marked subject), it initiates a direct, cue-based retrieval from memory:
- **Retrieval Interference**: Processing difficulty arises when non-target words (distractors) share features with the retrieval cues. For instance, if an incoming verb requires an animate, singular subject, the presence of multiple intervening animate, singular nouns generates similarity-based interference (both retroactive and proactive).
- **Fast Content-Addressable Access**: Psycholinguistic reaction-time experiments show that retrieval times do not scale linearly with memory load in the manner predicted by serial search, supporting direct content-addressable retrieval mechanisms vulnerable to feature overlap rather than pure temporal decay.

## Individual Differences and Clinical Neuropsychology

The interaction between working memory and sentence parsing provides critical insight into individual differences across populations:
- **Working Memory Span**: Performance on complex working memory assessments, such as the Reading Span task (Daneman & Carpenter, 1980), reliably predicts individual comprehension accuracy for syntactically ambiguous or complex sentences (e.g., garden-path constructions and long-distance wh-dependencies).
- **Cognitive Aging and Aphasia**: Older adults and individuals with agrammatic Broca's aphasia often show selective deficits when parsing long-distance dependencies or non-canonical word orders (e.g., passive or object-relative structures), even when core lexical knowledge remains intact.
- **Methodological Paradigms**: These effects are empirically substantiated using millisecond-accurate behavioral measures—such as eye-tracking during reading (first-pass fixations and regressions) and self-paced reading—as well as event-related potentials (ERPs), where syntactic processing bottlenecks frequently elicit the Left Anterior Negativity (LAN) and P600 components.
