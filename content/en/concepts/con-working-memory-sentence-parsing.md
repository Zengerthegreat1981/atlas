# Working Memory in Sentence Parsing

This concept describes the role played by working memory, that is, the limited mental capacity to hold and process information at the same moment, in understanding grammatically complex sentences. Models of language processing assume that every grammatical element not yet closed (such as a subject awaiting its verb, or a relative pronoun awaiting the verb from which it was displaced) stays "suspended" in working memory until it is linked to a later element, and that this suspension consumes limited cognitive resources whose difficulty increases the longer the distance between the two linked elements.

## The Effect of Distance and Complexity

This principle is known as the "locality effect" or Dependency Locality Theory (Gibson, 1998), and it is classically tested by comparing sentences that contain central relative clauses (Center-Embedding), such as a sentence whose subject contains a relative clause that in turn contains another relative clause, as in "The mouse that the cat that the neighbor raised chased fled." Such sentences become very hard to understand once there are two or more levels of embedding, despite their complete grammaticality, because working memory is unable to hold several suspended subjects at once while awaiting their associated verbs.

## The Activation-Based Memory Model

Recent models, such as the "cue-based retrieval" model developed by Richard Lewis and Shravan Vasishth, propose that the difficulty of grammatical linking arises not only from the consumption of storage capacity but from interference among retrieval cues when the elements of the sentence competing for memory are similar (such as the presence of several nouns each of which could be the subject), producing confusion or slowing in identifying the correct element linked to the verb, similar to the phenomenon of "retrieval interference" known in the general memory literature.

## Its Importance in the Study of Individual Differences

This framework is used to explain differences between individuals in understanding complex sentences, since performance on tasks that measure working memory capacity (such as the "Reading Span" task of Daneman and Carpenter, 1980) is related to the ability to understand sentences with central embedding or with long-distance syntactic movement, as in long interrogative questions. This principle is also employed in explaining linguistic difficulties among older adults or among some patients with agrammatic aphasia, whose ability declines specifically in processing sentences with long syntactic dependencies, despite the soundness of their mental lexicon.
