# The Moral Economy and Subsistence Politics

Traditional political economy treats prices and wages as the product of a balance between supply and demand, changing freely in response to shocks in production or income. The concept of the **subsidy state and the Arab social contract** documents how governments subsidize basic commodities such as bread in order to maintain social stability. But this purely economic description misses a deep normative dimension, explored by the social historian E. P. Thompson and the political scientist and anthropologist James C. Scott: poor and peasant societies hold firm moral expectations about a "fair price" and a "guaranteed minimum subsistence," and it is the violation of these expectations, not merely a statistical rise in price, that triggers social protest.

## Thompson: The Moral Economy and the Bread Riots

E. P. Thompson's article "The Moral Economy of the English Crowd in the Eighteenth Century" (*Past & Present*, 1971) made the concept of the **moral economy** influential, studying food riots in eighteenth-century England. He showed that the rioting crowds did not act in hungry, random chaos. They targeted merchants and millers whom they saw as violating customary norms about the fair price and marketing of grain in times of scarcity. In some episodes the crowds seized grain and sold it at a price they considered fair, handing the proceeds to the owner, in a disciplined protest that enforced an older paternalist custom (sometimes called popular price-fixing) rather than indiscriminate looting. Thompson's point was that the crowd acted on a coherent popular consensus about legitimate and illegitimate practices in marketing and milling, which he contrasted with the emerging doctrine of the free grain market.

## Scott: The Peasant Moral Economy and Subsistence Politics

Scott developed this framework anthropologically in his study of peasants in Southeast Asia. He proposed that the logic of the subsistence peasant is not to maximize average profit over the years, but to avoid the danger of falling below the subsistence line in any given year, even if this costs a lower average return in the long run. Scott argued that this explains why peasants may prefer arrangements that are safer but less profitable over more profitable but riskier ones. He argued that when colonial states and market capitalism imposed fixed taxes and rents that disregarded the variability of the harvest, they violated an implicit set of norms about reciprocity and the right to subsistence, which helped to explain rebellions in colonial Burma and Vietnam in the 1930s. Samuel Popkin (*The Rational Peasant*, 1979) criticized this account, arguing that peasants behaved as rational individual calculators and that village institutions were less egalitarian than Scott suggested; the debate shows the limits of the concept.

## The Bridge with Contemporary Arab Subsidy Policies

This framework offers a useful lens for understanding the sensitivity of bread and fuel subsidies in Arab economies. When governments lift subsidies in response to financial pressure or recommendations from international institutions, the resulting popular resistance (such as the January 1977 bread riots in Egypt after a reduction of food subsidies, or similar later protests) cannot be understood apart from the fact that the subsidy has become entrenched over decades as part of an implicit "social contract" linking the legitimacy of the state to guaranteeing a minimum of subsistence. It is not a purely technical fiscal policy that can be adjusted by budget calculations alone.

## The Importance of the Bridge for Designing Subsidy Reforms

This anthropological intersection suggests that the success of subsidy reforms depends partly on preserving the "sense of fairness" even when the technical mechanism changes (for example, replacing in-kind subsidies with targeted cash transfers), and not on financial efficiency alone. This is a lesson often overlooked when economic reform programs are designed in isolation from this normative dimension in the consciousness of the affected societies.

## Price Rises and the Scale of Protest

Research on Arab subsidy cuts does not identify a single percentage rise in bread or fuel prices at which protest becomes a broad uprising. Ketchley, Eibl and Gunning (*Journal of Peace Research*, 2023) study Egypt's January 1977 riots, often called the Bread Intifada, matching an event catalogue to local employment data. Rioting spread after the government announced cuts on a range of subsidized consumables. The authors report that the announced measures were not a rise in the price of subsidized bread itself, but increases on goods such as bottled gas, sugar, fine flour and rice. Even with that wide shock, unrest was concentrated where formally employed labour-market insiders were more numerous. The authors link those workers to stronger expectations of public welfare, drawing on moral-economy arguments alongside an insider-outsider account of segmented labour markets. The episode ties the scale of protest to whose customary entitlement was broken, and to the suddenness of the announcement, rather than to a price threshold later reforms could treat as a rule.
