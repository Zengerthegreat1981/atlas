# Classification Systems

Classification systems are the organised ways in which societies sort the world into kinds — of plants and animals, of people and kin, of colours and diseases, of the sacred and the profane. Every society classifies: it names species, divides people into groups, and draws boundaries between what is clean and unclean, edible and inedible, human and non-human. These systems are not merely labels for a world that is already divided; they are tools for thinking and acting. To classify is to decide what goes with what, what is like what, and what must be kept apart, and those decisions shape everything from diet to marriage to law.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish several kinds of classification. Folk taxonomies are the systems by which people name and group plants and animals: they typically recognise life forms (tree, bird, fish), generic kinds (oak, robin, salmon), and specific varieties, in a hierarchy that often resembles scientific taxonomy but is organised by usefulness, appearance, and cultural significance rather than by evolutionary descent. Social classifications sort people — by age, gender, kinship, caste, ethnicity, or occupation — and assign them rights, duties, and places. Symbolic classifications divide the world into opposed pairs — left and right, raw and cooked, pure and impure — that carry moral and cosmological weight.

Classification is both cognitive and social. It is cognitive because it reflects how the human mind groups similar things together; it is social because the categories a society uses reflect its history, its economy, and its power relations.

## Origins and Development

The anthropology of classification begins with Emile Durkheim and Marcel Mauss's *Primitive Classification* (1903), which argued that the categories of thought — genus and species, space and time — were not given by nature but derived from social organisation. In the societies they studied, the classification of the natural world mirrored the classification of the clan: just as the tribe was divided into clans, so the world was divided into classes of animals, plants, and spirits, each belonging to a clan. Thought, in this view, was social through and through.

Claude Levi-Strauss revived this tradition in *The Savage Mind* (1962), arguing that so-called primitive thought was not a lesser form of reasoning but a different one: a science of the concrete, which used the sensible qualities of things — the colour of a flower, the cry of a bird — to build systems of classification as rigorous as any science. For Levi-Strauss, a totemic classification, in which each clan is identified with an animal or plant, was not a confusion of nature and society but a way of thinking about social differences through natural differences.

Mary Douglas, in *Purity and Danger* (1966), added that classification is also a matter of order and disorder. Things that do not fit their category — the pig, which is cloven-hoofed but does not chew the cud; the pangolin, which is neither fish nor beast — are experienced as anomalous, dangerous, or sacred, and societies respond to them with taboo, ritual, or reverence. Dirt, for Douglas, was simply matter out of place: a sign that a classification had been violated.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One major debate concerns universality. Brent Berlin and his colleagues, in a classic study of folk biology published in 1973, argued that folk taxonomies of plants and animals show striking regularities across cultures — the same levels of classification, the same perceptual groupings — suggesting that human cognition imposes a common structure on the natural world. Critics replied that this underplays cultural difference: what counts as a kind depends on what matters to a society, and the same plant may be classified as food, medicine, or poison in different contexts.

A second debate concerns the relationship between classification and power. Michel Foucault, in *The Order of Things* (1966), showed how the great classifications of Western thought — of nature, of language, of wealth — changed with the political and epistemic order, and how each new system of classification produced new kinds of knowledge and new forms of control. The census, the map, and the museum are all instruments of classification, and all have been used to govern. To classify a people is, in part, to rule them.

A third dimension is the classification of persons. Kinship terminologies, racial categories, gender binaries, and legal statuses are all classification systems, and all have been contested. George Lakoff, in *Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things* (1987), showed that categories are not neat boxes with clear boundaries but radial structures organised around prototypes, with fuzzy edges and surprising members — the Dyirbal category that groups women, fire, and dangerous things together is not a mistake but a window into a different logic of association.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Classification systems are at the centre of contemporary life: the algorithms that sort us into consumers and risks, the medical categories that decide who is ill and who is not, the legal categories that decide who is a citizen, a refugee, or an illegal immigrant. Anthropology's contribution is to show that these categories are made, not found, and that they can be unmade. The decolonisation of museums, the revision of psychiatric classifications, and the recognition of non-binary genders are all, in part, struggles over classification.

The critique of classification also warns against its dangers. To classify is to simplify, and simplification can become stereotype, stigma, and violence. Yet we cannot think without categories. The study of classification systems remains a study of how human beings make the world intelligible — and of the price of that intelligibility.
