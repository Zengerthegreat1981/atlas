# Virtue Development

## Meaning and Definition

The concept of "virtue development" examines the mechanism by which the human soul moves from a state lacking virtue (or endowed only with raw innate predispositions) to a state of firmly established moral mastery, through stages of learning, training, and practice. Unlike the conception of virtue as theoretical knowledge that is acquired simply by understanding, this concept stresses that virtue is a settled disposition (Greek *hexis*, Latin *habitus*) formed through repeated action rather than through instruction alone. A person does not become courageous by knowing the definition of courage, but by repeatedly confronting situations that require overcoming fear.

## The Educational Path in Ibn Miskawayh and al-Farabi

In *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, Ibn Miskawayh offered a complete educational theory on this matter. He held that character is not fixed but can be improved, and that developing virtue depends on sound upbringing from an early age. Following the Greek tradition, he stressed habituation, training the child in good actions before the age of rational discernment, so that these actions become a second nature that later requires no arduous struggle. Al-Farabi, in *Tahsil al-Sa'ada*, distinguished theoretical virtues, acquired through learning and demonstration, from deliberative, moral and practical virtues, acquired through habituation and training, and made human perfection depend on both, under the guidance of a virtuous ruler or educator. Al-Ghazali, in the *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, emphasized *mujahada* (striving against the self) and the gradual practice of opposing a blameworthy trait by its opposite until the corresponding virtue becomes habitual.

## Aristotle and Virtuous Education in Western Philosophy

The theoretical origin of this concept goes back to Aristotle in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, who held in Book II that the moral virtues arise in us neither wholly by nature nor against nature, but are perfected by habit, using a well-known analogy: we become builders by building and lyre players by playing the lyre. Similarly, we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave acts. Aristotle stressed that this training alone is not sufficient without the guidance of practical reason (*phronesis*), which regulates the limits of the right action in each situation. In the contemporary period, Alasdair MacIntyre, in *After Virtue* (1981), revived interest in the role of communal "practices" in forming the virtues, and Nancy Sherman, in *The Fabric of Character* (1989), examined Aristotle's account of moral development.

## Objections and Illustrative Example

The method of habituation has been objected to on the ground that it may produce superficial conformity without a true understanding of the purpose of the act. Aristotle himself addressed this by requiring that a virtuous act be done knowingly, from choice and for its own sake, and from a firm and unchanging character (Book II, ch. 4). Illustrative example: a child who is initially made to share his toys with peers may, after years of repetition, find himself sharing spontaneously and with genuine joy, not because he fears punishment, but because generosity has become part of the fabric of his stable character.
