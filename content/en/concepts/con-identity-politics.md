# Identity Politics

## Definition

Identity politics refers to political action and claims-making organised around shared identities, such as race, gender, sexuality, ethnicity, disability, or religion, rather than around class or universal citizenship alone. The term describes movements in which people mobilise as members of a group defined by a shared experience of marginalisation, demanding recognition, rights, representation, and redress. Identity politics is therefore both a descriptive term for how political mobilisation happens and a contested label in public debate, used approvingly by some as a name for democratic inclusion and pejoratively by others as a name for division and fragmentation. Anthropologists and sociologists study it as a social phenomenon: how identities become politically salient, how groups define their boundaries and interests, and how states and institutions respond to identity-based claims.

## Origins and Development

The phrase emerged from the radical movements of the 1970s. The Combahee River Collective, a group of Black feminists in Boston, many of them lesbians, issued a statement in 1977 declaring that "the most profound and potentially most radical politics come directly out of our own identity," arguing that their experience at the intersection of racism, sexism, and homophobia could not be addressed by movements organised around any single axis. This statement is an important forerunner of intersectionality, the framework later named by Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989, which holds that systems of oppression interlock and that people positioned at their intersections face distinctive forms of disadvantage. Identity politics developed alongside and sometimes against earlier universalist politics: the civil rights movement's appeal to shared citizenship, the feminist movement's appeal to sisterhood, and the labour movement's appeal to class solidarity. Sociologists such as Mary Bernstein have stressed that identity politics can be understood not only as a fixed ideology but also as a strategic form of collective action, in which groups deploy identity to gain recognition, to challenge stigma, or to secure material resources, and in which the meaning of an identity is itself contested within the group.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The central debate pits recognition against redistribution. Nancy Fraser argued that struggles for recognition of cultural identity can displace struggles for economic redistribution, and that justice requires both: participatory parity, in which all people can take part in social life as peers, demands cultural respect and material resources together. A second debate concerns essentialism: whether identity politics fixes people into rigid categories, treating identities as natural and unchanging, or whether it can work with identities understood as fluid, constructed, and internally diverse. Critics within movements have repeatedly challenged the assumption that any group speaks with one voice, pointing to differences of class, age, and politics within identity categories. A third debate concerns the relationship between identity and solidarity. Critics argue that organising around particular identities fragments the common ground needed for broad political coalitions, while defenders reply that universal appeals have historically masked the interests of dominant groups, and that solidarity built on the acknowledgement of difference is more honest and more durable. A fourth question is institutional: how states should respond to identity claims, through multicultural policies, affirmative action, language rights, or constitutional recognition, and whether such policies entrench the very divisions they aim to remedy.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Identity politics is now one of the most contested terms in public life. It shapes debates over university curricula, corporate diversity programmes, electoral strategy, and international human rights, including the rights of Indigenous peoples, linguistic minorities, and LGBTQ people. Social media has transformed its dynamics: identities can be claimed, performed, and contested online, and movements can mobilise rapidly across borders, but online spaces also reward simplified and polarised versions of identity. The term is also used as a weapon: in much political commentary, "identity politics" is applied only to the claims of marginalised groups, while the identity politics of dominant groups, such as nationalism or the defence of traditional values, passes unmarked. Scholars increasingly argue that all politics involves identity, and that the interesting question is not whether identity politics exists but whose identities are treated as normal and whose as political. The anthropological contribution has been to show how identities are made and remade in practice, through ritual, language, memory, and everyday interaction, and therefore how identity politics is never simply the expression of a pre-existing identity but part of the process by which identities themselves are formed.
