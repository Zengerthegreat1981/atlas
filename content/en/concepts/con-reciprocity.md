# Reciprocity

Reciprocity is the mutual obligation to give, to receive, and to return. It is one of the most universal principles of social life: a gift creates a bond, and the return gift confirms it. Anthropologists treat reciprocity not as a quaint custom of small-scale societies but as a foundational mechanism through which relationships are made, obligations are recorded, and social order is maintained without courts, contracts, or police. Wherever people exchange things, services, words, or favours, reciprocity is at work — sometimes openly calculated, sometimes disguised as generosity, and sometimes enforced by the quiet threat of shame.

## The Gift and the Obligation to Return

The classic formulation comes from Marcel Mauss, whose essay *The Gift* (1925) examined gift exchange among the peoples of Polynesia, Melanesia, and the Indigenous nations of the Pacific Northwest. Mauss argued that a gift is never free. It carries three obligations: the obligation to give, the obligation to accept, and the obligation to repay. To refuse a gift is to refuse a relationship; to accept without returning is to accept a subordinate position. The gift therefore binds giver and receiver together in a continuing cycle, and the objects exchanged carry something of the giver's person with them — a spiritual force Mauss, following Māori thought, called *hau*.

Bronisław Malinowski's fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands gave this argument its ethnographic flesh. In *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922), he described the *kula*, a ceremonial exchange of shell valuables that circulated between islands in opposite directions. Kula valuables were not used or consumed; their whole point was to move, carrying fame and obligation with them. Alongside the kula ran the more practical exchange of yams, fish, and services, so that ceremonial generosity and everyday subsistence were woven into a single system of mutual obligation.

## Three Forms of Reciprocity

Marshall Sahlins, in *Stone Age Economics* (1972), distinguished three forms of reciprocity along a spectrum of social distance. Generalized reciprocity is the open-ended giving typical of close kin: parents feed children, and no account is kept, though the obligation to return may fall due years later or be discharged toward the next generation. Balanced reciprocity is direct exchange between social equals — a gift for a gift of roughly equal value, with the return expected within a known time. Negative reciprocity is the attempt to get something for nothing: haggling, theft, trickery, and the sharp dealing reserved for strangers and enemies. The same act — handing over food — can be any of the three, depending on who gives to whom.

Claude Lévi-Strauss extended the idea further, arguing in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949) that the exchange of women in marriage was the supreme form of reciprocity, the mechanism by which separate groups became kin and society itself was founded. For Lévi-Strauss, reciprocity was not merely an economic practice but the elementary structure of social life: the rule that one must give in order to receive is what turns nature into culture.

## The Norm of Reciprocity

Sociologist Alvin Gouldner (1960) reformulated the anthropological insight as a universal moral norm: people should help those who have helped them, and should not injure those who have helped them. The norm is enforced less by law than by gratitude, guilt, and reputation. Its negative side is equally important — the expectation of retaliation in kind, the "return of injuries," which keeps conflicts within bounds in societies without centralized authority. Evolutionary theorists have since argued that this norm underlies human cooperation itself: groups whose members reliably reciprocate out-compete groups whose members do not, and the emotions of gratitude, anger at cheating, and shame at ingratitude are the psychological machinery that makes the norm stick.

## Reciprocity Beyond the Gift

Reciprocity is not confined to pre-industrial societies. It shapes modern life in less obvious forms: the expectation that a dinner invitation will be returned, the favour owed to a colleague, the tip left for a waiter, the open-source programmer contributing code in the expectation of help later. Anthropologist David Graeber (2011) argued that even money and debt are best understood against this background — that the impersonal market is a recent and unusual arrangement, and that most human exchange through history has been reciprocal rather than commercial. Digital life has made the pattern visible again: social media runs on reciprocal attention, likes exchanged for likes, and the "gift economy" of the internet echoes the kula in unexpected ways.

Yet reciprocity is not always benign. It can lock people into exploitative relationships, as when a powerful patron's "gifts" create debts that can never be repaid and bind the recipient into dependence. It can sustain cycles of revenge as faithfully as cycles of generosity. And its emphasis on return can make genuinely free giving — charity without expectation — hard to recognise or to trust. Understanding reciprocity therefore means seeing both its power to create society and its power to constrain it: the same obligation that makes cooperation possible can also make freedom impossible.
