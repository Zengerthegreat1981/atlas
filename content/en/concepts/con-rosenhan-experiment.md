# Rosenhan Experiment

A well-known study in clinical psychology conducted by the psychologist David Rosenhan, professor of psychology and law at Stanford University. He published its findings in 1973 in the journal *Science* under the title "On Being Sane in Insane Places." It is one of the most influential studies in the critique of the reliability of psychiatric diagnosis in the twentieth century.

## Experimental Design

Between 1969 and 1972, Rosenhan sent eight "pseudopatients" (including Rosenhan himself) to twelve psychiatric hospitals in five different American states. None of them had a genuine psychiatric disorder. Each requested admission, claiming to hear a vague voice saying words such as "empty," "hollow," or "thud." Once admitted, the pseudopatients immediately stopped claiming any symptoms, behaved completely normally, and recorded their observations of the hospital experience.

## Results

All eight participants were admitted, making twelve admissions in all: eleven carried a diagnosis of schizophrenia and one of manic-depressive psychosis. They stayed in the hospitals for periods ranging from 7 to 52 days (an average of 19 days), despite their completely normal behavior after admission. No member of the hospital staff detected that they were healthy, although, as Rosenhan reported, a number of fellow patients voiced suspicions. Ordinary behaviors, such as taking notes, were even interpreted in the medical records as pathological symptoms ("engages in writing behavior"). On discharge, their diagnosis was recorded as "schizophrenia in remission" rather than "sane," meaning the diagnostic stigma persisted even after their mental health had been demonstrated.

In a second part of the study, Rosenhan told a teaching hospital that he would send pseudopatients during the following months. Over the following three months, of 193 patients admitted, staff judged 41 to be probable pseudopatients, although Rosenhan had in fact sent no one. He interpreted this as showing the opposite kind of error.

## Impact and Criticism

The results were interpreted as evidence that psychiatric diagnosis in that era (before DSM-III in 1980) lacked validity, and that the institutional context, being a "patient in a psychiatric hospital," shapes the interpretation of a person's behavior more than the behavior itself. The study is often cited, alongside other critiques, as one of the pressures behind the more operational criteria of DSM-III. From the critical side, the study was objected to from the time of its publication: Robert Spitzer (1975) argued that the study's logic was flawed, since clinicians reasonably relied on the reported symptoms, and that discharge as "in remission" showed diagnostic caution rather than failure; the sample was also very small. Susannah Cahalan's book *The Great Pretender* (2019) raised further doubts: she could not independently identify most of the pseudopatients, and she found discrepancies between Rosenhan's published account and his own notes. These questions about the study's reliability have not been resolved by controlled follow-up research.
