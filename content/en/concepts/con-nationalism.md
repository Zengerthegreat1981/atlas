# Nationalism: The Nation and the Claim to a State

Nationalism is **the ideology and political movement that holds that the nation — a community imagined as sharing history, language, culture, or descent — should be congruent with the political unit that governs it, the state**. It is at once a doctrine of political legitimacy, a source of collective identity, and a repertoire of symbols, rituals, and narratives through which people come to feel themselves part of a single people. Anthropologists study nationalism less as an abstract doctrine than as a lived practice: how national belonging is taught in schools, performed in ceremonies, contested at borders, and remade by migrants, minorities, and diasporas.

## Definition & Historical Development

The modern idea of the nation took shape in Europe and the Americas in the late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, alongside print capitalism, mass schooling, conscription, and the decline of dynastic empires. The French Revolution proclaimed popular sovereignty in the name of the nation, and over the following two centuries nationalist movements redrew the map of Europe, Latin America, Asia, and Africa. Decolonization after 1945 made the nation-state the near-universal form of political organization, so that even movements opposing a particular state usually framed their claims in national terms.

Early scholarship treated nations as ancient, natural communities awakening to self-consciousness. Later work reversed this view: nations are modern creations, and nationalism often precedes the nation it claims to express. From this perspective, national identity is not discovered but made — through censuses, maps, museums, anthems, flags, school textbooks, and the standardization of language. Anthropology has been central to this shift, showing in ethnographic detail how ordinary people learn to imagine themselves as members of a national community they will never meet in person.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

**Benedict Anderson** (1936–2015) argued in *Imagined Communities* (1983) that the nation is an imagined political community — imagined because its members never meet most fellow members, yet limited, sovereign, and experienced as a deep, horizontal comradeship. Anderson traced this imagination to print capitalism: newspapers and novels allowed readers to picture thousands of strangers sharing the same present, and thus to feel national simultaneity. His account explains why people willingly die for nations, and why nationalism could spread so rapidly across the colonial world through Creole officials, students, and newspapers.

**Ernest Gellner** (1925–1995) offered a contrasting, materialist account in *Nations and Nationalism* (1983). For Gellner, nationalism is a functional requirement of industrial society, which needs a mobile, literate workforce sharing a standardized high culture. Nationalism, in this view, invents nations where they do not exist; it is the state and its school system that produce national homogeneity, not the other way around. Gellner thus explained why nationalism arose with industrialization rather than earlier.

**Eric Hobsbawm** (1917–2012) emphasized invention and tradition. In the volume he co-edited with Terence Ranger, *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), and in *Nations and Nationalism since 1780* (1990), he and his collaborators showed how many supposedly ancient traditions — such as clan tartans (in Hugh Trevor-Roper's chapter) and British royal ceremonial (in David Cannadine's) — were shaped or created between the late eighteenth and twentieth centuries, often to give states and nations a usable past. **Anthony D. Smith** (1939–2016) pushed back against the strongest constructivist claims, arguing in *National Identity* (1991) that modern nationalism builds on pre-existing ethnic cores, myths, and memories — the *ethnie* — which constrain what nationalist elites can invent.

## Ethnographic Examples

Anthropologists have documented nationalism in everyday life. In postcolonial states, school curricula, independence-day parades, and national museums have been key sites where citizens learn who belongs to the nation and who is marked as foreign. In the Middle East and North Africa, Arab nationalism mobilized shared language and anti-colonial sentiment across state borders, while also clashing with local, tribal, and religious loyalties. In South Asia, partition and its aftermath showed how nationalist projects could produce both solidarity and catastrophic violence, as religious communities were reimagined as nations. Among diaspora communities, long-distance nationalism — funding, lobbying, and cultural activism from abroad — demonstrates that national belonging does not end at the border.

Nationalism also shapes exclusion. Minorities, migrants, and indigenous peoples frequently experience the nation as a boundary: citizenship laws, language policies, and census categories decide who counts as a full member. Anthropological fieldwork among Kurds, Palestinians, Basques, and other stateless peoples shows how national feeling can persist without a state, sustained by poetry, song, memory, and everyday practices of cultural reproduction.

## Contemporary Debates

Current scholarship debates whether nationalism is waning under globalization or resurgent in new forms. Populist movements invoke the nation against elites and immigrants; digital media enable both transnational solidarity and nationalist mobilization at unprecedented speed. Feminist and postcolonial scholars ask whose nation is being imagined — pointing out that nationalist narratives have often silenced women, minorities, and colonized peoples. Anthropology's contribution is to keep the analysis grounded: nationalism is not only an ideology of elites but a sentiment woven into ordinary life, capable of both binding communities together and licensing exclusion and violence.
