# Survey: Asking Questions to Measure a Population

A **survey** is a method of collecting information from a sample of people, by asking them standardized questions, in order to describe or explain the characteristics of a larger population. Surveys are the workhorse of modern social research: they measure attitudes, behaviours, and conditions at a scale no other method can match, and they underpin much of what sociology, political science, and public policy claim to know about the social world. A survey is not simply a list of questions; it is a measurement instrument, and its quality depends on how it is designed, administered, and interpreted.

## Definition and Development

The modern survey took shape in the 1930s and 1940s, alongside commercial polling and the development of probability sampling. A landmark of attitude measurement was Rensis Likert's "A Technique for the Measurement of Attitudes" (1932). Likert's innovation was the scale that still bears his name: a set of statements to which respondents indicate agreement on a graded range, from strongly agree to strongly disagree. The Likert scale made attitudes measurable in a standardized way, so that the opinions of thousands of people could be compared and aggregated. It also made visible a central problem of survey research: that the answer depends on how the question is asked.

The survey grew alongside the sampling theory that made it possible to generalize from a few thousand respondents to a population of millions. The key insight was that a well-drawn probability sample — in which every member of the population has a known chance of being selected — can represent the whole with measurable precision. Survey research thus became a science of inference: from the sample to the population, from the answers given to the attitudes and behaviours they are meant to measure.

## Key Contributions: Design, Error, and the Interview

Don Dillman, in *Mail and Telephone Surveys* (1978), showed that the quality of a survey depends as much on its administration as on its questions. His total design method — careful questionnaire layout, repeated follow-ups, personalization — raised response rates and reduced the bias that comes from those who do not answer. Dillman's work made clear that a survey is a social interaction: respondents decide whether to cooperate, how much effort to give, and how honestly to answer, and the surveyor's job is to make cooperation easy and rewarding.

Floyd Fowler, in *Survey Research Methods* (1984), systematized the craft: how to write questions that respondents understand in the same way, how to order them so that earlier questions do not distort later answers, and how to train interviewers so that they do not influence the responses they record. Robert Groves and his colleagues, in *Survey Methodology* (2004), brought the field together under the concept of total survey error — the idea that every stage of a survey, from the definition of the population to the coding of the answers, can introduce error, and that the researcher's task is to manage the trade-offs between them. A survey with a perfect sample but confusing questions is no better than a survey with clear questions and a biased sample.

## Examples

Surveys shape public life in ways most people never notice. Election polls are surveys; so are the unemployment figures, the health statistics, and the customer satisfaction ratings that guide policy and business. The World Values Survey, which has interviewed people in a large and growing number of countries since 1981, has shown how values shift with economic development — from survival values toward self-expression — and has become one of the most cited sources in comparative sociology.

Surveys also reveal their own limits in practice. Sensitive questions — about income, drug use, or sexual behaviour — produce under-reporting; questions about the future produce optimistic guesses; and the very act of asking can change what people think. Good survey research therefore treats answers not as transparent windows onto the mind but as products of the survey situation itself.

## Online Surveys and Non-Probability Samples

Web surveys are fast and cheap, and answering them privately on a screen can reduce the pressure to give socially approved answers. Their central difficulty is selection. Many rely on opt-in panels or open links, so that people choose themselves and no one's chance of inclusion is known; people without internet access, or with little interest in the topic, are easily missed. Mick Couper's review of web surveys (2000) stressed that they vary enormously in quality and that coverage and sampling must be judged for each design. Size does not repair the problem: the *Literary Digest* poll of 1936 gathered more than two million replies and still wrongly predicted the defeat of Franklin Roosevelt. Researchers try to correct non-probability samples by weighting them to population benchmarks, by matching respondents to a reference sample, or by statistical modelling. A task force of the American Association for Public Opinion Research (2013) concluded that such methods rest on assumptions that must be stated and defended, and that the usual margin of sampling error does not straightforwardly apply. Some panels avoid the problem partly by recruiting members through probability sampling and providing access to those who lack it.

## Surveys in Low-Literacy Populations

Where many respondents read little, self-completed questionnaires exclude or mislead, so surveys usually rely on interviewers. This brings its own effects, since answers may be shaped by the interviewer's gender, status, language, or perceived link to government or aid agencies. Designers use short questions in everyday spoken language, translate and test them with members of the population, and replace long numerical scales with fewer options, pictures, show cards, or countable objects such as beans or stones to express proportions and likelihoods. Cognitive interviewing, in which respondents explain how they understood a question, helps reveal misunderstandings before fieldwork begins. Audio computer-assisted self-interviewing lets people hear questions through headphones and answer by pressing marked keys, offering privacy without requiring reading. It is not automatically superior: a methodological experiment among adolescents in Kenya by Barbara Mensch, Paul Hewett, and Annabel Erulkar (2003) found that its effect on the reporting of sensitive sexual behaviour was mixed and differed between groups. Low literacy should therefore be treated as a design condition to be tested locally, not as a reason to omit people from research.

## Survey Ethics and Informed Consent

Respondents give time and information, often about matters that could harm them if disclosed. The Belmont Report (1979) set out three principles widely applied to research with human participants: respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. In surveys, respect for persons means informing people who is conducting the study, what it concerns, how long it will take, what risks it carries, how the data will be used, and that they may decline to take part, skip questions, or stop. Consent is often given orally or implied by completing a questionnaire, particularly where a signed form would itself create a record linking a person to sensitive answers. Eleanor Singer's experiments, reported in 1978, showed that the way consent is requested, including a request for a signature, can affect cooperation. Researchers must also distinguish anonymity, where no one can link answers to a person, from confidentiality, where the link exists but is protected. Detailed variables can identify individuals even after names are removed, so data release requires care. Children and dependent or vulnerable groups need additional safeguards, and selling or campaigning under the guise of a survey is a breach of professional codes.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Surveys have been criticized on several grounds. Pierre Bourdieu argued, in his 1973 essay "Public Opinion Does Not Exist", that opinion polls manufacture the public opinion they claim to measure, by asking people questions they have never considered and treating their answers as settled views. Others note that surveys flatten social life: they capture what people say, not what they do, and they struggle with meaning — the same answer can mean different things to different respondents. And falling response rates, as people grow wary of unsolicited calls and messages, threaten the representativeness on which the whole method depends.

Surveys remain indispensable. They now frame research on digital life, where online panels and social media offer cheap samples of uncertain quality; on global health, where surveys track disease and well-being; and on the measurement of attitudes in societies where face-to-face interviewing is dangerous or impossible. How to ask, whom to ask, and what an answer means are still the questions that decide whether a survey tells the truth.
