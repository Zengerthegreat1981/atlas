# Concept of Moral Luck

"Moral luck" refers to cases in which our moral judgment of an agent depends on factors outside the agent's full voluntary control, such as the outcomes of the agent's act, the circumstances in which he or she was born, the temperament he or she inherited, or situations the agent encountered without choosing them. The paradox is that this conflicts with the Kantian principle that moral responsibility should be built solely on what lies within the agent's will alone, since, in Kant's words in the *Groundwork*, the good will is good in itself and not because of what it effects or accomplishes.

## Moral Luck in Islamic Thought

This term was not formulated as such in the theological (*kalam*) or jurisprudential heritage of Islam, but its substance is strongly present in the question of moral obligation (*taklif*) and capacity (*qudra*). Jurists distinguished between obligation to what is within one's capacity and obligation to what is beyond it, establishing that God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity, and that a person prevented from acting by compelling causes (such as coercion or incapacity) is relieved of sin. This is an implicit acknowledgment of the effect of circumstances beyond the will. Theologians also discussed the question of the children of polytheists and those who died before the call of faith reached them. This question relates directly to the effect of the circumstances of birth (geographic and historical luck) on eschatological destiny, and the schools differed widely on it, between those who referred the matter to a test to be presented to them on the Day of Resurrection, and those who made it subject to God's prior knowledge of what they would have chosen had they lived. These debates show an early awareness of the problem of divine justice when people's circumstances differ.

## Origins of the Concept in Western Philosophy

The term was coined by Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel in two essays published together in 1976 (*Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*, Supplementary Volume 50), both entitled "Moral Luck." Williams gave the example of the painter Gauguin, who abandoned his family to devote himself to art in Tahiti. Our final moral judgment on his decision depends partly on his later artistic success, which was not guaranteed at the time he made the choice. Nagel classified moral luck into four types: outcome luck (for example, the difference between a careless driver who hits a child and an equally careless driver who encounters no one); circumstance luck (for example, someone who lived in Nazi Germany and faced a moral test that someone living elsewhere did not face); constitutive luck (the individual's temperament and inherited disposition); and causal luck (the determination of our actions by a chain of causes preceding our existence).

## The Philosophical Problem and Objections

The heart of the problem lies in what Nagel called the gradual "erosion" of moral responsibility. The more closely we analyze, the more we find that every element of a moral act ultimately depends on factors beyond the agent's will, to the point that the idea of "moral desert" almost vanishes. Some philosophers rejected this radical conclusion and called for revising our understanding of responsibility rather than abandoning it. Others, following Kant (long before the term was formulated), insisted that luck does not affect the moral worth of a good will. On that view, the weight we give to outcomes belongs to our practical, legal or social reactions rather than to the moral assessment of the agent.

## Illustrative Example

Two drivers, equally negligent in running a red light, are involved in the same situation: one reaches home safely because the road was clear, and the other hits a child who was crossing. Despite the equality of negligence and intention, the second is charged with manslaughter, while the first is at most lightly reproached. This clearly reveals the effect of luck on our practical judgments, despite its contradiction with the principle of equal assessment of intention.
