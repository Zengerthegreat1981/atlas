# The Documentary Hypothesis (JEDP) of Wellhausen

The central thesis of the higher criticism school in the nineteenth century: the five books of the Torah (the Pentateuch) are not a single composition but a late compilation of four textual sources, distinct in style and theology, edited together at a later stage.

## The Four Sources

- **J (Yahwist, Jahwist)**: uses the divine name "YHWH" (Yahweh); a vivid and anthropomorphic narrative style; its composition is generally dated to the southern Kingdom of Judah.
- **E (Elohist)**: uses "Elohim"; more reticent about describing God directly; its composition is generally dated to the northern Kingdom of Israel.
- **D (Deuteronomist)**: corresponds mainly to the Book of Deuteronomy; a rhetorical, homiletic style; linked to the religious reforms of King Josiah (around the seventh century BCE).
- **P (Priestly)**: concerned with ritual, genealogies and priestly organization; its composition is generally dated to after the Babylonian exile. In Wellhausen's scheme it is the latest of the four sources, and the Pentateuch reached its final form through the combination of the sources by later redactors.

## Internal Evidence Used

The argument rests on: the repetition of stories in conflicting versions with differences in detail (the two creation accounts in Genesis 1 and 2; two intertwined accounts of Noah's flood with contradictory details about the number of animals and the duration of the flood); variation in the divine name used; variation in vocabulary and stylistic formulation between successive passages; and contradictions in the legislation (such as differences in where sacrifices are to be offered between different texts).

## Later Revisions

The chronological ordering and evolutionary linkage proposed by Wellhausen (from a "primitive" non-centralized religion to an "advanced" priestly system) have been the subject of later criticism, as they reflect evolutionary cultural assumptions prevalent in nineteenth-century Europe more than purely textual evidence. Hermann Gunkel's form criticism (`con-form-criticism-gunkel`) shifted attention to the oral genres behind the texts, and Martin Noth's tradition-historical work emphasised oral traditions and the formation of the Pentateuch's themes. Since the 1970s, scholars such as Rolf Rendtorff and John Van Seters have challenged the classical J and E sources and favoured models of smaller units or later composition, while others, such as Richard Elliott Friedman and Baruch Schwartz, defend refined source models. Most scholars still accept that the Pentateuch is composite, but whether its sources can be precisely separated and dated remains a live debate, not a settled consensus.

## Reception of the Hypothesis in Contemporary Judaism

The hypothesis was largely rejected by traditional Orthodox Judaism, which adheres to the doctrine of "Torah from Heaven" (*Torah min ha-Shamayim*), according to which the five books of Moses are a single, continuous divine revelation dictated to Moses word for word. Reform and modern Conservative Jewish movements, by contrast, have adopted it to varying degrees within a broader historical-critical approach to sacred texts. This made the hypothesis a dividing line between different contemporary Jewish movements in their stance towards the critical study of the Torah.

## Its Impact on Comparative Islamic Studies

In the same period Theodor Nöldeke applied philological-critical methods to the history of the Qur'anic text in his *Geschichte des Qorans* (1860). Such approaches have been widely contested by Muslim scholars and debated among Western scholars, given the fundamental difference in the nature of historical transmission and early written documentation between the case of the Hebrew Bible and the case of the Qur'an. This methodological distinction should be taken into account in any comparison between the two fields.
