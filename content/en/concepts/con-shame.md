# Shame (Existential Perspective)

Shame is the state of being seen and judged unfavorably, or of anticipating such judgment. It differs from guilt, which concerns an action, in that shame concerns the self: shame says "I am unacceptable," while guilt says "I did something wrong." Existential thinkers treat it as a revealing emotion that exposes how a person stands in relation to others and to their own sense of who they are.

## Definition

In psychology, shame is generally described as a painful self-conscious emotion in which the whole self is evaluated as flawed, often with an impulse to hide or withdraw. Guilt, by contrast, focuses on a specific behavior and tends to motivate repair. Helen Block Lewis's *Shame and Guilt in Neurosis* (1971) was an influential early clinical account of this distinction, and June Tangney and Ronda Dearing's *Shame and Guilt* (2002) summarized later empirical research.

## Origins

The existential treatment begins with Sartre. In *Being and Nothingness* (1943) he describes shame as the experience of being caught as an object in another's gaze: a person absorbed in what they are doing suddenly realizes they are seen, and in that moment recognizes themselves as the kind of being the other sees. For Sartre shame discloses the existence of the other and of one's own being-for-others. Erik Erikson placed an early form of shame in the child's struggle for autonomy, and later writers such as Gershen Kaufman (*The Psychology of Shame*, 1989) examined shame as shaped by early relationships. Bernard Williams's *Shame and Necessity* (1993) argued from the Greek tradition that shame concerns who one is in the eyes of others one respects.

## Shame and Authenticity

Existentially, shame often arises from a gap between the actual self and the performed self. If identity rests on appearing competent or invulnerable, exposure of weakness brings shame, and the exposure reveals the performance. This suggests a paradox: shame can sometimes signal that authenticity is becoming possible. Dropping a false self-presentation, though acutely painful, can open the way to genuine connection and self-acceptance. Many people first meet shame in therapy just as defenses loosen and the actual self becomes visible.

## Social Shame vs. Existential Shame

Not all shame concerns inauthenticity. A person who feels shame for violating cultural norms, or from stigmatization because of poverty, illness, sexuality, race or abuse, experiences social shame that may be a response to real injustice rather than a personal failing. The existential therapist distinguishes shame that points to avoidance of something the person needs to face from shame that has been imposed by others' contempt, and does not treat the latter as something the person must simply accept as deserved.

## In Practice

Therapists of many orientations work with shame by offering a relationship in which the client can be seen without being judged, by naming shame when it appears, and by helping the client tell apart "I did something I regret" from "I am worthless." Compassion-focused approaches, associated with Paul Gilbert, address shame through developing self-compassion. Because shame prompts hiding, it can be hard to talk about, and clients may mask it as anger or withdrawal. Shame that is severe or linked to trauma may call for specialist support.

## Criticisms and Limits

The idea that shame signals emerging authenticity should not be generalized. Research reviewed by Tangney and Dearing found that a tendency toward shame is more often associated with difficulties such as depression, anxiety and interpersonal problems than guilt-proneness is, so shame cannot be treated as simply constructive. Some writers question the sharp line between shame and guilt, which are often experienced together and vary across cultures. Sartre's account, finally, is philosophical description rather than clinical theory, and moving from it to therapy requires care.
