# Sacred Time and Historical Eras

## The Hijri Calendar: Sacred Time Founds History

In Islam, religious time became the basis for ordering history. The Qur'an makes the year twelve lunar months, of which four are sacred (Al-Tawba 36), and forbids *nasi'* (the postponement of months so that they match the solar year) (Al-Tawba 37), so that the seasons of worship (Ramadan and the Hajj) move through the seasons. In the caliphate of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab the Hijri calendar was adopted, with its starting point set at the Prophet's migration (*hijra*), not at his birth or his first revelation, so the community took its founding event as the measure for dividing time. As for Abu al-Rayhan al-Biruni, in *al-Athar al-Baqiya ʿan al-Qurun al-Khaliya* (c. 1000), he compared the calendars and eras of nations (Persians, Greeks, Jews, Christians and Arabs) and calculated the starting points of their chronologies; it is among the earliest comparative works on religious time. It was translated into English by C. Edward Sachau in 1879 under the title *The Chronology of Ancient Nations*.

## Eliade: The Eternal Return and Cyclical Time

In Mircea Eliade's *The Myth of the Eternal Return* (1949; in English *Cosmos and History*, 1954), ancient humanity distinguished sacred time, the time of origin in which the first events are re-enacted through ritual, from ordinary connected time. New Year and Easter re-create the world symbolically, and ritual transforms linear time into a permanent presence. Eliade acknowledges that the Abrahamic traditions, including Islam, introduced a linear historical dimension (creation, revelation, then resurrection), so that the two times coexisted in them: one unrepeatable history, and periodic rituals that re-enact it. A loose parallel, not a documented influence, is Ibn Khaldun's reflection on the cycles of states from beginning and growth to decline.

## Collective Memory and Historians

Hubert and Mauss, in their study of the representation of time in religion and magic ("Étude sommaire de la représentation du temps dans la religion et la magie," 1905, collected in *Mélanges d'histoire des religions*, 1909), put forward the idea that time as lived by a group is the product of ritual calendars, and that days have different qualitative values: festivals and days of ill omen. Edmund Leach, in his study of the symbolic representation of time (1961), developed the view that time is perceived through an alternation of sacred and non-sacred intervals. Fernand Braudel introduced the concept of the *longue durée* in 1958 in the journal *Annales*, to describe slow-changing enduring structures (including religion) that govern history in depth, as opposed to rapid events. Jan Assmann, in *Cultural Memory* (1992), showed how collective memory is fixed through festivals and sacred texts. Reinhart Koselleck, in *Futures Past* (1979), argued that modernity changed the relationship between the "space of experience" and the "horizon of expectation," which reduced the presence of theological time in the making of history.

## Eras and Theology

Sacred time influences the division of eras: traditional Islamic historiography orders events according to the Hijra and the caliphs, and Christian historiography according to the Nativity (*Anno Domini*, devised by Dionysius Exiguus around 525), dividing history into the years before and after Christ. Eschatological times also have a role: Jewish, Christian and Islamic traditions define history by an anticipated end, and generate messianic movements that claim to know their place in time. All this makes history a meaningful narrative.

## Limits of the Approach

The comparison raises difficulties. Eliade's division between cyclical and linear time is considered exaggerated by his critics, because ancient societies also knew linear time. Anthropologists have likewise shown a variety of conceptions of time that cannot be reduced to a single binary. As for Braudel's *longue durée*, it is a methodological concept for the historian and does not in itself indicate that religion is more stable than other factors. Finally, one must distinguish between the religious principle of the calendar and scientific historiography, which depends on independent evidence.
