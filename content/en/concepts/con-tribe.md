# Tribe

A tribe is a social group larger than a clan or lineage, whose members share a common name, territory, language or dialect, and a belief in common descent or origin, and who recognise a shared political identity without necessarily having a centralised government. Tribes are typically composed of several clans or lineages, united by kinship idioms, ritual obligations and, often, a council of elders or a chief whose authority is limited and negotiable. The tribe is distinct from the clan: a clan is a descent group defined by (real or putative) kinship, whereas a tribe is a political community that may contain many clans, some of them unrelated, bound together by territory, alliance and shared identity. The concept has been one of anthropology's most contested, precisely because it names a real form of social organisation while also carrying a long history of colonial misuse.

## Origins and Development

The tribe entered anthropology as an evolutionary stage. Nineteenth-century theorists, above all Lewis Henry Morgan in *Ancient Society* (1877), arranged human societies in evolutionary sequences in which tribes, organised by clans and kinship, preceded the state. Mid-twentieth-century neo-evolutionists such as Elman Service later formalised the sequence of band, tribe, chiefdom, and state, with the tribe as the political form of peoples who had outgrown the band but not yet built kingdoms. The tribe was thus imagined as a rung on a ladder — a primitive condition that modern states would supersede.

Twentieth-century ethnography gave the concept empirical content while beginning to undo its evolutionary framing. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) showed how a tribe could function as a political system without government: among the Nuer of South Sudan, tribes were the largest groups within which feuds were settled and against which other tribes were opposed, and their unity emerged from the segmentary opposition of their constituent clans and lineages rather than from any central authority. The tribe, in this account, is not a stage but a structure — a way of organising political life through balanced opposition.

Marshall Sahlins, in *Tribesmen* (1968), synthesised the comparative material. He defined the tribe by its segmentary organisation: a population of autonomous local groups — villages, clans, lineages — integrated by kinship, ritual and exchange rather than by command. Sahlins stressed the tribe's flexibility and its limits: tribal organisation can mobilise large numbers for war or ceremony, but it cannot sustain permanent hierarchy, taxation or standing armies, and it tends to fragment once the occasion for unity passes.

## Key Debates

The most consequential debate concerns whether the tribe is a real indigenous form or a colonial invention. In "The Illusion of Tribe" (1970), Aidan Southall argued that many of the "tribes" recorded by administrators and anthropologists in Africa were not pre-colonial political units at all but artefacts of colonial rule: administrators needed bounded, named groups to govern, tax and recruit, and they froze fluid identities into tribal categories, appointed chiefs where none had existed, and drew boundaries across peoples who had lived in overlapping networks. Archie Mafeje, in "The Ideology of Tribalism" (1971), went further, arguing that "tribalism" was an ideology that blamed African political problems on primordial loyalties while obscuring the colonial and class forces that produced them.

Morton Fried, in *The Notion of Tribe* (1975), gave the critique its sharpest formulation. He argued that tribes, as bounded political units, are typically secondary phenomena — products of interaction with states, whether through trade, warfare or administration — rather than pristine survivals of an earlier stage. Peoples become tribes when states need them to be tribes: as buffers, as allies, as tax units, as enemies.

Defenders of the concept reply that the critique, while salutary, overstates its case. People in many parts of the world do identify as members of tribes, mobilise as tribes, and are recognised by others as tribes; the fact that tribal identity is historically produced and politically shaped does not make it unreal. The contemporary consensus treats the tribe as a historically contingent political identity — real, but made rather than given — and insists on studying how tribal identities are formed, maintained and transformed rather than assuming them as natural units.

A second debate concerns the tribe's relationship to the state. Tribes have been incorporated into states as administrative units, resisted states as autonomous polities, and been reinvented within states as vehicles of electoral mobilisation and cultural identity. The same tribal name can denote a kinship network, a territorial claim and a political party, depending on context.

## Contemporary Relevance

The tribe remains politically salient. In Libya, Yemen, Iraq and Afghanistan, tribal affiliation shapes conflict, negotiation and state-building; in East Africa, tribal identity structures electoral politics; in North America, Australia and New Zealand, the language of tribe has been reclaimed by Indigenous peoples as a basis for sovereignty, land claims and cultural revival. Meanwhile the word itself is contested: many anthropologists avoid "tribe" as a technical term because of its colonial associations, preferring "ethnic group" or "people", while others retain it precisely because the peoples concerned use it. The debate over the word is part of the phenomenon: to call a group a tribe is itself a political act, with a history that the anthropology of the tribe must keep in view.
