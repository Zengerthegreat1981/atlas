# Disability Anthropology

Disability anthropology is the study of how disability is understood, experienced, and lived across cultures. It asks what counts as impairment in different societies, how disabled people are included or excluded from social life, and how they build identities, communities, and political movements. Its central insight is that disability is not only a property of individual bodies, but a relationship between bodies, environments, and social worlds — and that this relationship varies profoundly across cultures.

## History and Origins

Anthropologists long encountered disability in the field, but for decades it remained marginal to the discipline. Early ethnographies noted disabled people in passing, often as figures of misfortune or spiritual significance, without making disability itself an object of analysis. A turning point came with Robert Edgerton's The Cloak of Competence: Stigma in the Lives of the Mentally Retarded (1967), a study of people released from a California institution for the intellectually disabled. Edgerton showed how stigma shaped every aspect of their lives, and how they managed their identities in a world that marked them as incompetent.

In the 1980s, Robert F. Murphy's The Body Silent (1987) brought disability into the anthropological mainstream. Murphy, an anthropologist who became quadriplegic, wrote a pioneering ethnography of his own experience, combining personal narrative with cultural analysis of how American society treats the disabled body. At the same time, the disability rights movement and the passage of the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990) gave disability a political visibility that anthropology could no longer ignore.

The field consolidated in the 1990s and 2000s. Benedicte Ingstad and Susan Reynolds Whyte's edited volume Disability and Culture (1995) demonstrated that disability is interpreted through local cosmologies, kinship systems, and moral economies — that in some societies it is read as fate or spirit possession, in others as misfortune to be managed, and in others still as a source of special status. Devva Kasnitz and Russell Shuttleworth's 2001 essay on engaging anthropology with disability studies called for a sustained dialogue between the two fields, while the Society for Disability Studies, founded in the 1980s, provided an institutional home for the wider interdisciplinary field.

## Key Figures and Works

Faye Ginsburg and Rayna Rapp are the field's leading figures. Their 2001 article "Enabling Disability: Rewriting Kinship, Reimagining Citizenship" and their 2013 review "Disability Worlds" in the Annual Review of Anthropology argued that disability should be foundational to anthropology, not a specialised topic, because it reveals how societies organise personhood, kinship, and belonging. Their book Disability Worlds (2024) draws on three decades of ethnographic research in New York City, following families, activists, artists, and educators, and develops the idea of "new kinship imaginaries" — the creative ways families remake kinship around disability.

Susan Reynolds Whyte's work in Uganda examined how disability is understood in a context where illness and misfortune are interpreted through kinship and spiritual idioms, and how disabled people navigate charity, rehabilitation, and the state. Nora Groce, an anthropologist and disability researcher, documented in *Everyone Here Spoke Sign Language* (1985) how, on Martha's Vineyard, a hereditary form of deafness was once so common that hearing and deaf islanders alike used sign language. Lennard Davis's Enforcing Normalcy: Disability, Deafness, and the Body (1995), though written from literary and cultural studies, deeply influenced anthropological thinking about the "normal" body as a cultural construct. Henri-Jacques Stiker's A History of Disability (1982 in French; English translation 1999) traced how Western societies have classified, confined, and rehabilitated disabled people across the centuries.

## Method

Disability anthropology uses long-term ethnography to document the everyday lives of disabled people — in homes, schools, workplaces, clinics, and activist networks — and to record how they experience their bodies, their environments, and other people. It pays close attention to language: to the terms people use for themselves, to the categories imposed by medicine and the state, and to the gap between the two. Many disability anthropologists are themselves disabled, or the parents or kin of disabled people, and the field has embraced this "situated" knowledge, arguing that lived experience is a legitimate source of anthropological insight.

The field is closely allied with disability studies, and shares its commitment to the social model of disability — the idea, developed by British activists in the 1970s, that people are disabled less by their impairments than by barriers in the social and built environment. Anthropologists have both applied and complicated this model, showing that the distinction between impairment and disability is itself culturally variable, and that in many societies the relevant categories are spiritual, moral, or relational rather than medical.

## Influence and Contemporary Directions

Disability anthropology has changed how anthropology thinks about the body, personhood, and inequality. It has influenced medical anthropology, the anthropology of the state, and the study of humanitarianism, and it has supplied ethnographic evidence for debates about accessibility, inclusive education, genetic screening, and reproductive choice. Its emphasis on lived experience has also shaped research ethics, pushing anthropology toward participatory methods in which disabled people are collaborators rather than subjects.

Contemporary work extends the field in several directions: the anthropology of neurodiversity and autism; studies of disability in the Global South, where war, poverty, and limited health care shape the experience of impairment; research on assistive technologies, prosthetics, and digital access; and the ethnography of disability activism and the disability arts. A Wenner-Gren Foundation symposium organised by Ginsburg and Rapp produced a Current Anthropology supplement, "Disability/Anthropology: Rethinking the Parameters of the Human" (2020), that marked the field's maturity. Its guiding conviction remains: disability is not a marginal topic but a universal human condition, and any anthropology that ignores it is incomplete.
