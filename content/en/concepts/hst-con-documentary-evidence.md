# Documentary Evidence in Ancient History

In Greco-Roman historiography, documentary evidence occupied a complex and secondary status compared to direct eyewitness observation and oral inquiry. While ancient historians valued primary documents—such as stone inscriptions, public decrees, senatorial records, and archival treaties—they operated within an intellectual tradition that privileged personal autopsy (*autopsia*) and contemporary living memory over archival research. Nevertheless, several prominent historians actively gathered, cited, and scrutinized documentary texts to verify disputed narratives, establish chronological precision, and adjudicate diplomatic conflicts.

## Oral Testimony versus Documentary Archives

Classical Greek historiography was fundamentally an oral and rhetorical medium. Both Herodotus and Thucydides founded historical investigation (*historia*) upon personal travel, direct visual observation, and the interrogation of living witnesses. Archival records existed in city-states—such as the Athenian *Metroon*, where public decrees (*psephismata*) and laws were preserved on papyrus and bronze—but historians rarely engaged in systematic archival searches. In part, this was due to the logistical difficulty of navigating unindexed civic archives, but it also reflected an epistemological conviction: living informants could be cross-examined, whereas static documents could not defend their authenticity or clarify ambiguities.

## Documentary Usage in Greek Historiography

Despite the dominance of oral testimony, documentary sources were utilized in significant historical contexts. Herodotus recorded and analyzed inscriptions during his travels, including Cadmean tripods in Thebes and Egyptian stelae, though he was occasionally misled by local dragomans.

Thucydides made more critical and formal use of documents. In Book 5 of his *History of the Peloponnesian War*, he reproduced the texts of official diplomatic treaties, including the Peace of Nicias (421 BCE) and the subsequent alliance between Athens, Argos, Mantinea, and Elis. Thucydides reproduced these treaties with their exact stipulations, oaths, and lists of signatories, demonstrating how documentary texts could establish legal precision and expose discrepancies between formal diplomatic compacts and actual political conduct.

In the Hellenistic period, Polybius placed documentary research at the heart of his historical polemics. In Book 3 of his *Histories*, Polybius refuted previous pro-Carthaginian historians (such as Philinus of Agrigentum) regarding the origins of the Punic Wars by locating and translating ancient bronze tablets preserved in the treasury of the aediles on the Capitoline Hill. These documents contained the early commercial and territorial treaties between Rome and Carthage dating back to the late sixth century BCE. Polybius criticized earlier historians who either failed to consult these primary texts or asserted that they never existed.

## Roman Historiography and Antiquarian Scholarship

In Rome, the tradition of aristocratic record-keeping provided historians with a rich documentary foundation. Early Roman annalists drew upon the *Annales Maximi*—the annual boards kept by the *Pontifex Maximus* recording magistrates, food shortages, eclipses, and religious prodigies—as well as the consular fasti (*fasti consulares*) and family funeral eulogies (*laudationes funebres*).

Livy utilized these sources in *Ab Urbe Condita*, although he acknowledged the profound difficulty of reconstructing early Roman history after the Gallic sack of Rome in 390 BCE destroyed many archaic records. Moreover, Livy (8.40) also warned that family funeral eulogies and records had been falsified to flatter aristocratic lineages.

By contrast, imperial biographers and later historians relied heavily on administrative documentation. In *De Vita Caesarum*, Suetonius drew on his access to imperial records (he held the post *ab epistulis*, in charge of imperial correspondence, under Hadrian) to quote directly from Augustus's personal letters, wills, and handwritten imperial decrees. Similarly, Tacitus consulted the *acta senatus* (senatorial minutes) and the daily gazette (*acta diurna*) while composing his *Annals*.
