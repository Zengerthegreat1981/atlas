# Evolutionary Ethics

Evolutionary ethics is an attempt to explain the origin of the moral sense and of the rules of human behavior (such as cooperation, altruism, and justice) as products of natural selection, not as purely rational givens or divine commands separate from the natural history of the species. It assumes that the basic moral inclinations, such as care for offspring, empathy, and social punishment of cheaters, gave our ancestors an adaptive advantage in survival and reproduction, and so became entrenched both genetically and culturally.

Premodern Muslim thinkers did not propose an evolutionary theory in the Darwinian sense. However, some commentators and social scientists, such as Ibn Khaldun in the *Muqaddimah*, noted that cooperation and social solidarity (*'asabiyya*) are a necessity imposed by the nature of human society and its need to survive. This is a functional explanation that approaches the logic of evolutionary explanation insofar as it regards morality as serving the continuation of human civilization, although its teleological framework remained divine rather than purely material.

In Western philosophy, the idea began with Charles Darwin himself, in *The Descent of Man* (1871), where he linked the "moral sense" to social instincts shared by humans and other animals. It was later developed by Edward Wilson in *Sociobiology: The New Synthesis* (1975), which drew on W. D. Hamilton's (1964) concept of "inclusive fitness" to explain altruism, that is, that helping relatives serves the spread of shared genes even if the individual sacrifices himself. Robert Trivers (1971) explained "reciprocal altruism" between non-relatives as a stable evolutionary strategy that benefits both parties in the long term. Michael Ruse and Edward Wilson, in a 1986 article, took a more radical position, holding that morality is an adaptation whose sense of objectivity is an illusion fostered by our genes to promote cooperation.

Evolutionary ethics faces fundamental philosophical objections, the most prominent being the is-ought gap associated with David Hume and the "naturalistic fallacy" formulated by G. E. Moore in *Principia Ethica* (1903). According to these objections, the move from a description of what *is* (the emergence of a moral inclination through evolution) to what *ought to be* (the validity of a moral rule) is an unjustified logical leap. A related point is that knowing the origin of a belief does not by itself show it to be false (the genetic fallacy). Moral realists also object that the existence of an evolutionary explanation for the moral sense does not rule out the existence of objective moral truths that this sense arrived at, even if by evolutionary chance.

As an example: the innate human tendency to punish someone who cheats in a commercial transaction, even at a personal cost (the behavior of "altruistic punishment," studied experimentally by Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter in 2002), is explained by evolutionary scientists as a mechanism for protecting the stability of long-term collective cooperation.

Researchers distinguish between two positions: a moderate "explanatory" position, which is content to explain how our capacity for moral discrimination arose without judging the truth of its content, and an extreme "debunking" position, which seeks to undermine moral realism altogether. The philosopher Sharon Street, in "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (2006), argues that natural selection shaped our moral judgments according to what serves survival, not according to what matches independent moral truths. This places a heavy burden of proof on anyone who claims that our evolved intuition tracks moral truth rather than merely adaptive benefit. The debate remains open between naturalists and moral realists to this day, and intersects with deeper questions about the nature of moral knowledge and the reliability of human intuition in general. This branch reminds us that understanding the biological origin of the moral phenomenon does not by itself replace the traditional philosophical question of its independent rational justification.
