# r > g (Piketty's Fundamental Inequality of Capitalism)

The **"r greater than g" law** is a central explanatory formulation put forward by the economist **Thomas Piketty** in his book *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (French original 2013, English translation 2014). It states that when the rate of return on capital (*r*) exceeds the growth rate of the economy as a whole (*g*) over a long period, wealth tends to become concentrated in the hands of those who already own capital, growing faster than general income.

## The arithmetic logic behind the law

The arithmetic logic of this law rests on a simple observation. If capital yields an annual return (*r*) higher than the growth rate of the economy as a whole (*g*), then capital's share of national income will rise gradually over time, at the expense of the share of labour and wages. This is because accumulated wealth grows faster than the economy itself, against which its share is calculated. Over generations, this means that those who inherit wealth or capital achieve gains that gradually exceed what someone relying only on income from their own work can achieve, regardless of the effort expended.

## Based on long-run historical data

To justify this law, Piketty draws on historical tax data that he collected with colleagues, chief among them Anthony Atkinson, covering more than a century in several countries. He shows that this relationship (*r* greater than *g*) was the natural historical pattern prevailing in most periods of modern capitalism. The exception was an extraordinary period in the mid-twentieth century (between the two world wars and immediately after), when the rate of return on capital fell because of exceptional shocks (the destruction of war and high post-war taxes), while economic growth rose exceptionally through reconstruction. This temporarily reversed the relationship in favour of reduced inequality during that particular era.

## Deep distributive implications

Piketty argues that this law explains a broader phenomenon: the return of what he calls "patrimonial capitalism", in which family inheritance becomes a more important determinant of an individual's economic position than personal effort and work. This is similar to the situation in Europe during the "golden age" of the late nineteenth century. He holds that the world has been moving back toward this pattern since the 1980s, after the exceptional shocks that had temporarily restrained it in the mid-twentieth century subsided.

## A methodological critique of the inevitable conclusion

The law faced technical criticism from economists who questioned an implicit assumption needed for the conclusion that capital's share of income rises as wealth accumulates: that the "elasticity of substitution" between capital and labour (the ease with which one can be replaced by the other in production) exceeds one. Matthew Rognlie and others also argued that much of the recent rise in capital income reflects housing rather than productive capital. They regard this assumption as a modelling premise open to serious debate, not as an empirically proven fact, and they argue that the theoretical conclusion does not follow automatically from the historical data alone without this additional assumption.

## A proposed policy response

From this law Piketty drew an ambitious policy recommendation: a coordinated global progressive tax on wealth itself, not on income alone. He argues that this is the only instrument capable of restraining this long-run structural trend, while explicitly acknowledging the difficulty of achieving the international coordination needed to implement such a tax in practice in the foreseeable future.
