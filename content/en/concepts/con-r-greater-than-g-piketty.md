# r > g (Piketty's Fundamental Inequality of Capitalism)

The relation **r > g** is a central formulation in **Thomas Piketty**'s *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (French edition 2013; English translation 2014). It states that when the rate of return on capital (*r*) exceeds the economy's growth rate (*g*) for a long period, wealth already owned tends to grow faster than income, and inherited wealth can become a larger part of the distribution.

## The arithmetic

If the return on capital exceeds the growth of income, a fortune that is largely saved will rise relative to average earnings. Over generations, heirs of large fortunes can pull further ahead of people who rely only on labour income. Piketty also links a lower *g* to a higher wealth-income ratio, because in his steady-state accounting that ratio moves toward the saving rate divided by *g*. The share of income going to capital is the product of *r* and that ratio. A higher wealth-income ratio raises capital's share only if *r* does not fall by enough to offset it. That step is separate from the claim that *r* greater than *g* concentrates wealth, and it is the step critics have challenged most directly.

## Based on long-run historical data

Piketty draws on historical tax records assembled with colleagues, among them Anthony Atkinson and Emmanuel Saez, covering more than a century in several countries. On his reading, *r* above *g* was the usual pattern in modern capitalist economies. The main exception was the mid-twentieth century, when wars, wartime destruction and high postwar taxation reduced returns on capital, while reconstruction raised growth. Inequality of wealth and income was lower in that interval than before 1914 or after about 1980.

## Deep distributive implications

Piketty argues that the relation helps explain what he calls "patrimonial capitalism", in which inheritance weighs more heavily than labour income in determining economic position. He compares the late twentieth-century rise in wealth inequality with the Belle Époque before 1914, and he holds that rich countries have moved back toward that pattern since the 1980s, once the shocks of the mid-twentieth century had faded.

## A methodological critique of the inevitable conclusion

Critics have separated the historical series from the theoretical inference. A rising capital share, when capital accumulates, requires that the elasticity of substitution between capital and labour be greater than one, so that *r* falls only modestly as capital becomes more abundant. That elasticity is a modelling premise, not a settled measurement. Matthew Rognlie has also argued that much of the recent rise in net capital income in rich countries is income from housing, rather than from productive business capital. The historical concentration of wealth can be discussed apart from either claim.

## A proposed policy response

Piketty's main policy proposal is a progressive annual tax on net wealth, coordinated across countries so that mobile fortunes cannot avoid it by changing residence. He presents national income taxes and one-off levies as weaker restraints on the long-run divergence he describes, and he notes that the international agreement such a tax would require is difficult to obtain.
