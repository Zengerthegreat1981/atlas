# Moral Responsibility

## Meaning and Definition

Moral responsibility is the state in which an agent deserves praise or blame, reward or punishment, for an act they have performed. It differs from "moral agency" in that it concerns a particular act that has actually occurred, not the general capacity to act. The core of the philosophical debate revolves around the thorny relationship between responsibility and freedom of the will. Does fair accountability require that the agent be genuinely able to have done otherwise? Or is it enough that the act issued from their own will without external coercion, even if that will is itself governed by a prior causal chain?

## The Theory of Acquisition and the Principles of Accountability in Islamic Theology

The question of reconciling absolute divine power with human responsibility has been the most fertile ground for theological debate in the Islamic tradition. The Mu'tazila held that divine justice requires the human being to be the true creator of their voluntary acts; otherwise, imposing an obligation to do what one cannot do would be an injustice from which God is exempt. They therefore grounded responsibility in a created capacity that God grants the servant before the act. The Ash'ari school, from al-Ash'ari himself and developed by al-Baqillani and later theologians, formulated the theory of "acquisition" (*kasb*) as an alternative reconciliation. God is the sole true creator of every act, but the servant "acquires" the act through the concurrence of their partial, created capacity. This acquisition is the ground of praise and blame, even though the act itself is, in its very existence, created by God. A well-known objection to this theory is that it leaves obscurity about the meaning of "acquisition" itself. Ibn Rushd approached the question from a philosophical angle. In *Tahafut al-Tahafut*, he explained that comprehensive universal causality does not negate the meaning of responsibility, because what the human being is held accountable for is the act's issuing from their own will, regardless of the causes that precede that will.

## Western Philosophy and Contemporary Compatibilism

In his influential essay "Freedom and Resentment" (1962), Peter Strawson proposed a solution that moves beyond the traditional metaphysical debate about determinism and freedom. He suggested that moral responsibility is rooted in natural "reactive attitudes", such as resentment, gratitude, and moral indignation, and that these social practices do not depend on a philosophical resolution of universal determinism. Harry Frankfurt, in his 1969 paper, developed an influential critique of the principle of alternate possibilities through hypothetical scenarios (known as Frankfurt cases) in which an agent acts on their own and could not have done otherwise because of a hidden counterfactual intervener; he argued that the agent is nevertheless responsible. The force of these cases remains debated.

## Illustrative Example

A driver who speeds and strikes a child is questioned, morally and legally, about their act because they were able to control their speed. By contrast, a driver who suffers a sudden heart attack and loses consciousness entirely is not held responsible, since there is no conscious will at that moment.
