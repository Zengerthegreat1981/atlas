# Life Course Theory in Elder

An influential theoretical framework developed by Glen Elder beginning with his landmark study *Children of the Great Depression* (1974), helping to establish the field of "life-course sociology", whose basic principles were later formalised as four (Elder 1998).

## The Four Principles

Starting from his long-term tracking of the impact of the Great Depression (1929) on the lives of the children who lived through it, Elder established four basic theoretical principles. "Historical time and place" (Historical Time and Place): an individual's life is fundamentally shaped by the historical era and geographic place in which he lived, so the experience of the Great Depression generation differs radically in its long-term effects from that of a later generation that did not live through it. "Timing" (Timing): the impact of any given life event on the individual differs fundamentally according to his developmental age when it occurs (hardship experienced in early childhood can have different long-term consequences from the same hardship met in adolescence). "Linked lives" (Linked Lives): an individual's life is structurally linked to the lives of those around him across generations and family relations, so an economic crisis striking the father transmits its psychological and social effects to his children and wife. And "human agency" (Human Agency): individuals make their own active choices within the constraints and opportunities of their historical and structural circumstances, and are not merely passive victims of those circumstances.

## Influence

This work helped establish the field of "life-course sociology" (Life Course Sociology) as an alternative perspective to the earlier fixed "life stages" models (such as traditional developmental psychology models), stressing the changing historical and social context in shaping individuals' life paths across different generations, and it became a basic methodological reference in longitudinal studies of the life course and aging.

## The Children of the Great Depression Study

Elder built his theory on rare longitudinal data originally collected in the "Oakland Growth Study", which followed a group of children born in 1920–21 across decades of their lives. This allowed him to compare the life paths of those whose families suffered severe economic deprivation during the Great Depression with those whose families did not. Later work compared them with a younger cohort born in 1928–29, drawn from the Berkeley Guidance Study. He found that the effect of economic deprivation varied with the child's age when the crisis occurred and with how the family responded to it, rather than being a uniform and inevitable effect for everyone. The influence of this founding work later extended to similar studies that tracked the effect of other historical events (war, migration, contemporary economic crises) on different generations, applying its principles to other historical contexts.
