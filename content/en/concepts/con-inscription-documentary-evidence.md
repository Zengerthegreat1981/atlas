# Inscriptions and Documentary Evidence

Inscriptions are written evidence carved or engraved on durable hard materials such as stone, metal and fired clay, in contrast to documents written on perishable materials such as papyrus, parchment or paper. Inscriptions are among the oldest categories of historical evidence and among the most likely to survive for thousands of years. They form a specialized scholarly field known as epigraphy, complementing archaeology and paleography (the study of ancient writing).

## Source Advantages of Inscriptions

Inscriptions are generally regarded as relatively highly reliable, for several methodological reasons:

- **Material stability**: they are not subject to the textual corruption that manuscripts transmitted by hand accumulate over the centuries (each new copy carries the possibility of a scribe's error).
- **Contemporaneity**: many inscriptions (especially official ones) are made at the time of the event or close to it, unlike later narrative sources, which may be written decades or centuries afterward.
- **Official institutional character**: since an inscription is often produced by an official body (royal, temple or administrative), it has a specific historical context that makes it easier to date and to identify its issuing authority.

## Limits of Inscriptional Reliability

Despite these advantages, the method of historical criticism warns against treating inscriptions as "raw truth" without filtering, because:

- **The official inscription is usually a propaganda product**: royal inscriptions in particular (such as the stelae celebrating victories) serve an ideological purpose that glorifies the ruler and legitimizes his authority. They are therefore not a "neutral" description of events but a political message addressed to an audience.
- **Selectivity**: what is recorded in an inscription is what the patron authority chose to record. Inscriptions usually document victories rather than defeats, and remain silent about whatever does not serve the image of the ruler.
- **Decay and fragmentation**: many ancient inscriptions have reached us broken or damaged, which requires the researcher to reconstruct missing parts by inference.

## Non-Inscriptional Documentary Evidence

Beyond stone and metal inscriptions, "documentary evidence" in the broader sense includes cuneiform clay tablets (contracts, letters and administrative records), papyri (such as those of Egypt and the Greco-Roman world), coins bearing inscriptions and symbolic images, and cylinder seals. These categories vary in their degree of officialness. Private contracts and personal letters found in administrative archives (such as the archives of Mari or Tell el-Amarna) sometimes present a less filtered and more "everyday" picture than ceremonial royal inscriptions, because they were never designed for a propaganda purpose.

## The Importance of Inscriptions for Comparative History

Inscriptions have been an important tool for cross-checking against later literary and religious sources. In ancient Near Eastern studies, comparing the epigraphic annals of Assyrian and Babylonian kings with biblical narratives enables the cross-verification and chronological recalibration of historical events. Similarly, in early Islamic studies, contemporary epigraphic evidence—such as the monumental 72 AH / 691–692 CE inscription in the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem, Umayyad milestones, and early Arabic rock inscriptions across the Syrian desert and the Hijaz—provides valuable contemporary documentary evidence that allows scholars to test and contextualize later narrative chronicles compiled in the Abbasid era.
