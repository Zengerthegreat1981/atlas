# Kuznets Curve

The **Kuznets curve** is an economic hypothesis put forward by the economist **Simon Kuznets** in 1955. It describes a hypothetical relationship between the level of economic development and the level of inequality in income distribution, in the shape of an inverted U: inequality rises in the early stages of industrialization, then falls automatically in later stages of growth.

## The logic behind the hypothesis

Kuznets based his hypothesis on an observation about the dynamics of economic transition. In the early stages of industrialization, part of the labour force moves from a low-productivity, low-wage agricultural sector to a higher-productivity, higher-wage industrial sector. This creates a growing gap between those who have already moved and those who remain in agriculture, so overall national inequality rises. As industrialization advances further, and the modern sector expands to include a larger share of the population, alongside the spread of education and a slowdown in population growth, the gap begins to close gradually, and inequality falls again in the mature stages of growth.

## An optimistic policy implication

This optimistic hypothesis dominated development policy thinking for decades. It provided an implicit justification: the temporary inequality accompanying rapid industrialization is an acceptable price worth paying for a broader prosperity that will spread automatically later, without active redistributive political intervention. It was sometimes used to justify rapid industrialization policies that tolerated high temporary levels of inequality in many developing countries.

## A challenge from Piketty and Atkinson

**Thomas Piketty** reversed this optimism using long-run historical tax data that he compiled together with **Anthony Atkinson** and others. They showed that the decline in inequality seen in many Western countries in the mid-twentieth century was not the product of an automatic and inevitable mechanism, as Kuznets had suggested. It resulted from exceptional, non-recurring shocks: the two world wars, the Great Depression, and high post-war taxation. Inequality then rose sharply again from the 1980s onward, despite continued economic growth. This contradicts the Kuznets curve's expectation that growth alone is enough to reduce inequality automatically over the long term.

## Later use in other contexts

The shape of the Kuznets curve was later borrowed in other literatures, most notably the "environmental Kuznets curve", which hypothetically describes a relationship of similar shape between income level and environmental pollution. This later use is the subject of a separate methodological debate, entirely distinct from Kuznets's original hypothesis on income distribution. It is not a direct extension of his thesis, but a formal borrowing of the curve's shape; it was popularized in the early 1990s by studies such as Grossman and Krueger's on trade and the environment.

## A balanced contemporary assessment

Kuznets himself drew on limited data for the United States, Britain and Germany and described his findings as speculative. The curve is today assessed as at most a partial empirical regularity in specific historical contexts, and it is not a general deterministic law that applies automatically to every development path. Alvaredo, Assouad and Piketty's study of the Middle East (2019) also estimated very high inequality in that region, which does not obviously fit the simple Kuznets expectation.
