# Adolescent Depression

Applying the criteria of **major depressive disorder** in **DSM-5-TR** to adolescents, with one essential diagnostic difference unique to this age group: the manual accepts **irritable mood (anger or quick irritability)** as an equivalent substitute for the depressed mood as the core mood criterion for diagnosis. This exception does not apply to the diagnosis of depression in adults, who must have a depressed mood or loss of pleasure (or both), with no substitute.

## Why Irritability Rather Than Sadness?

This exception reflects a clinical and developmental observation: many depressed adolescents do not report clear sadness but rather **impatience, frequent quarrels, and aggressive withdrawal**. This expression is linked to a limited capacity to verbally express complex internal emotion at this age, and to hormonal and neurodevelopmental changes accompanying puberty. Nevertheless, this depressive irritability must be distinguished from **disruptive mood dysregulation disorder**, a separate diagnosis in DSM-5-TR with recurrent severe temper outbursts and persistently irritable or angry mood between outbursts; it is diagnosed between ages 6 and 18, with onset before age 10. That diagnosis was introduced originally to restrict the excessive diagnosis of bipolar disorder in severely irritable children.

## Developmental Significance

Early-onset depression (before adulthood) is associated with a heavier clinical course: a greater number of recurrent episodes over a lifetime, a higher rate of suicidal thoughts and behaviors, and a higher rate of co-occurring disorders (anxiety, conduct disorders, substance use), compared with depression that first appears in adulthood. Twin and family studies suggest that the relative contribution of genetic and environmental factors varies with age of onset, which implies that biological and environmental pathways to depression differ across the stages of life.

## Assessment and Treatment

Clinical assessment of adolescents requires careful distinction between the normal transient sadness of this stage and an actual depressive disorder, with systematic assessment of suicide risk (which rises sharply in this age group) and of contextual factors (bullying, family and academic pressure, sexual and gender identity). First-line recommended treatments include **cognitive behavioral therapy** and **interpersonal therapy for adolescents** (IPT-A). SSRI antidepressants are used with caution, given the **boxed warning** issued by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2004 about a possible increase in suicidal thinking and behavior in children and adolescents during the early stages of antidepressant treatment (extended in 2007 to young adults up to age 24). Fluoxetine is the best-studied SSRI for this age group and is FDA-approved for depression from age 8; escitalopram is approved from age 12.
