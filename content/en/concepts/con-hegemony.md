# Hegemony: Rule by Consent as Well as Coercion

**Hegemony** is the rule of one group over others through a combination of consent and coercion, in which the dominant group's worldview comes to be accepted as common sense by those it dominates. The term, developed by the Italian Marxist Antonio Gramsci, explains why subordinate groups often accept arrangements that work against their interests: domination is not maintained by force alone, but by the shaping of culture, education, religion, and everyday ideas so that the existing order appears natural, inevitable, and in everyone's interest. Anthropologists use hegemony to analyse how power operates in ordinary life — in schools, media, rituals, and language — and not only in armies, police, and parliaments.

## Definition and Development

Gramsci developed the concept in his *Prison Notebooks*, written between 1929 and 1935 while he was imprisoned by Mussolini's fascist regime, and published in English as *Selections from the Prison Notebooks* in 1971. He distinguished two forms of supremacy: *dominio*, direct domination by force through the state and its repressive apparatus, and *egemonia*, leadership exercised through civil society — the churches, schools, trade unions, newspapers, and voluntary associations in which consent is organized. A ruling group is hegemonic, Gramsci argued, when it leads rather than merely dominates: when its interests are presented as the interests of society as a whole, and when subordinate groups actively consent to its leadership.

This was a break from the cruder Marxist view that the ruling class rules only through the economy and the police. Gramsci insisted that the battle for power is fought first in culture and ideas. The decisive struggle, in his phrase, is a "war of position" — the slow work of building an alternative common sense — rather than a single "war of manoeuvre", the seizure of the state. A class that wins only the state without winning civil society, he argued, will not hold power for long.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Raymond Williams, in *Marxism and Literature* (1977), gave the concept its most influential elaboration in English. Williams defined hegemony as a "whole body of practices and expectations" — a lived system of meanings and values that saturates everyday life and is experienced as reality itself. Hegemony, for Williams, is not a fixed ideology imposed from above but a process: it must be continually renewed, defended, and modified, and it is always open to resistance and to alternative and oppositional forms. His distinction between dominant, residual, and emergent cultural forms gave anthropologists a way to study hegemony historically, tracing how older values persist and new ones emerge within a dominant order.

Edward Said extended the concept to colonialism in *Orientalism* (1978), showing how Western scholarship, literature, and administration together produced a hegemonic image of "the Orient" as exotic, backward, and in need of Western rule. Said's point was Gramscian: colonial rule depended on a system of knowledge that made domination seem justified and natural, and that system was reproduced in universities, museums, and novels as much as in colonial offices. Anthropology itself, Said argued, had helped to build the very hegemony it later had to criticize.

Ernesto Laclau and Chantal Mouffe, in *Hegemony and Socialist Strategy* (1985), radicalized the concept by detaching it from any single class. Hegemony, in their account, is the discursive construction of a collective will: different demands — for wages, for national independence, for women's rights — are linked together into a chain of equivalence under a shared project. Joseph Femia, in *Gramsci's Political Thought* (1981), clarified Gramsci's own meaning, distinguishing hegemony as consent-based leadership from the broader use of the term as a synonym for domination, and showing how Gramsci's analysis of the Italian Risorgimento and of the Catholic Church informed the concept.

## Ethnographic Examples

Anthropologists have found hegemony a powerful tool for fieldwork. In the study of schooling, researchers have shown how curricula and examinations present the history and values of dominant groups as neutral knowledge, while the knowledge of subordinate groups is treated as folklore or superstition. In the study of religion, hegemony helps explain how state churches and official theologies secure consent for inequality by framing it as divinely ordained. In the study of development, it illuminates how the language of "modernization" and "progress" makes particular economic policies appear as technical necessities rather than political choices.

The concept also explains resistance. Where hegemony is incomplete, subordinate groups develop what James Scott later called hidden transcripts — criticisms voiced offstage, in jokes, songs, and rumours. Peasants, workers, and colonized peoples may publicly consent while privately holding a different account of the world. Hegemony is therefore never total: it is a field of struggle, and the consent it secures is always conditional, partial, and liable to break down in crisis.

## Contemporary Debates

Current scholarship debates whether hegemony remains useful in an age of fragmented media and global capitalism. Some argue that the concept, developed for the nation-state, must be rethought for a world of transnational corporations, international institutions, and digital platforms, where consent is organized across borders. Others argue that hegemony has become more, not less, relevant: the power of global brands, development agencies, and social media to define what counts as normal and desirable is a hegemonic power of a new kind. Feminist and postcolonial scholars have asked whose consent counts, pointing out that hegemonic projects have often depended on the subordination of women and colonized peoples. Anthropology's contribution is to keep the analysis grounded in lived experience: hegemony is not an abstraction but a daily practice, reproduced in classrooms, workplaces, and households, and contested there too.
