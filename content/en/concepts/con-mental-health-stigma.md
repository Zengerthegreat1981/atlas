# Mental Health Stigma Across Cultures

## Definition

Mental health stigma is the set of stereotypes, prejudices, and discrimination directed at people with psychological disorders, their families, and those who treat them. It operates at three levels: general social stigma, self-stigma when the patient internalizes the negative image, and structural stigma in policies and funding. Its clearest effect is delayed help-seeking.

## Theoretical Foundations

Erving Goffman, in *Stigma* (Prentice-Hall, 1963), defined stigma as a deeply discrediting attribute that transforms the individual from a whole person into a tainted one, and distinguished between the "discredited" and the "discreditable" (see the entry on the discredited and the discreditable). Link and Phelan, in the *Annual Review of Sociology* in 2001, developed a definition combining labeling, stereotyping, separation, loss of status, and discrimination within a context of power. Corrigan, in *American Psychologist* in 2004, explained how stigma impedes treatment, and distinguished between self-stigma and public stigma. Thornicroft, in *Shunned* (Oxford University Press, 2006), presented data on discrimination within the family, at work, and in the health system itself. The INDIGO study (Thornicroft and colleagues, *Lancet*, 2009) surveyed patients with schizophrenia in 27 countries and found that experienced and anticipated discrimination were widely reported across sites.

## Cultural Variation

Yang and colleagues noted in *Social Science & Medicine* in 2007 that stigma is shaped by what they call "moral experience": what is at stake for people in a given culture. In societies where an individual is measured by the standing of their family, illness is tied to the family's reputation and to the marriage prospects of its daughters, so the family bears the stigma of association. In the Arab context, Dardas and Simmons, in the *Journal of Psychiatric and Mental Health Nursing* in 2015, analyzed the "stigma of mental illness in Arab families," showing the influence of fear of shame, family reputation, and the belief that illness is a trial, possession, or weakness of faith. However, stigma is neither fixed nor exclusive to Arab societies, as Western societies are also full of it. In popular imagination the term "majnun" (the madman) functions as a blanket judgment that does not distinguish between symptoms, whereas the medieval Islamic medical and legal tradition distinguished between different conditions and degrees of impaired reason (see the entry on mental health and human rights).

## Reducing Stigma

Reviews indicate that direct contact with people who have recovered from illness is more effective than education alone. National campaigns such as "Time to Change" in England have been conducted. In the Arab world, involving religious leaders and the media, and delivering care within primary care, may reduce stigma, because a general clinic appointment does not reveal the reason for the visit (see the entry on community mental health).

## Critique of the Concept

It is criticized for confining the problem to "public stigma" and overlooking the role of structural conditions: poverty, scarcity of services, and institutional power. Measuring stigma with questionnaires is also affected by the desire to give socially acceptable responses.
