# Monotheism

Monotheism is the belief in, and worship of, a single god. In anthropology the term names both a theological doctrine and a social form: a religious system in which one deity holds supreme, exclusive authority, in contrast to polytheism, in which many gods share the cosmos, and animism, in which spiritual power is diffused through persons, animals, places and objects. The word itself comes from the Greek *monos* (one) and *theos* (god), and it was coined in the seventeenth century, but the phenomenon it describes — exclusive devotion to one god — has a much longer and more contested history. Anthropologists treat monotheism less as a stage of progress than as a particular way of organising the sacred, one with distinctive consequences for law, morality, community and power.

## Origins and Development

The earliest anthropological theories of religion placed monotheism at the end of a long evolutionary ladder. Edward Tylor argued in *Primitive Culture* that religion began in animism, the belief in spirits, and only gradually advanced through polytheism toward monotheism, which he regarded as the most rational form of belief. This scheme treated Judaism, Christianity and Islam as the culmination of religious evolution, and so-called primitive religions as its childhood. Later scholars rejected the ladder. Émile Durkheim argued that the sacred is fundamentally social: gods embody the moral authority of the community, and the form a religion takes reflects the form of the society that practises it. From this perspective monotheism is not more rational than polytheism; it is the religious expression of a particular kind of social unity, in which one god mirrors one people, one law and one moral order.

Historical research complicated the evolutionary story further. The Hebrew Bible itself preserves traces of an earlier polytheistic world — references to a divine council, to the goddess Asherah, and to rival gods — suggesting that Israelite monotheism emerged gradually out of a polytheistic background rather than arriving fully formed. Egypt offers a famous parallel: the pharaoh Akhenaten's imposition of exclusive worship of the sun-disc Aten in the fourteenth century BCE is often cited as an early experiment in monotheism, though it collapsed soon after his death. Sigmund Freud, in *Moses and Monotheism*, controversially traced Jewish monotheism back to this Egyptian episode, a claim historians have largely rejected but which shows how powerfully the origins of monotheism have preoccupied scholars. Jan Assmann has argued that what was genuinely new about biblical monotheism was not the idea of one god, which existed elsewhere, but the "Mosaic distinction" — the sharp division between true and false religion, which made religious difference a matter of truth and error for the first time, with far-reaching consequences for intolerance and for the very idea of religious identity.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns whether monotheism is a belief or a practice. Theologians emphasise doctrine, but anthropologists note that many people who affirm one god also venerate saints, ancestors or local spirits, a pattern sometimes called practical polytheism. In Catholic and Orthodox Christianity, in Sufi Islam and in many African churches, devotion to intermediaries coexists with formal monotheism, suggesting that exclusive monotheism is often an ideal enforced by religious specialists rather than a description of everyday devotion. A second debate asks whether monotheism encourages violence. Critics argue that the claim to possess the one true god makes religious difference intolerable, while defenders reply that monotheistic traditions also produced universalist ethics — the idea that all humans are equal before one god — which underpins modern human rights. A third debate concerns the cognitive roots of belief: Pascal Boyer has argued that religious concepts of powerful, knowing agents are easily acquired because they fit ordinary human intuitions about minds and agency; how such intuitions interact with the institutional spread of monotheism, tied to states and empires, remains disputed.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Monotheism remains the dominant religious form for more than half the world's population, and its anthropological study has shifted from origins to lived practice. Ethnographers now study how monotheistic faith is experienced in migration, in secular states, and in dialogue — and conflict — with other religions. The category itself has also come under critique: scholars argue that "monotheism" is a Western theological concept imposed on traditions that understand the divine differently, and that Hindu traditions, for example, can be simultaneously monotheistic and polytheistic, with one ultimate reality expressed through many deities. The term remains indispensable, but anthropologists increasingly use it as a starting point for ethnographic inquiry rather than as a fixed box into which religions can be sorted.
