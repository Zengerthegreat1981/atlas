# Globalization: Flows, Connections, and Uneven Worlds

Globalization is **the intensification of connections across the world — of goods, people, money, images, and ideas — so that events and decisions in one place increasingly shape lives in distant places**. It is not a single process but a bundle of them: trade liberalization, migration, transnational media, global finance, and the spread of institutions, tastes, and political movements. Anthropologists study globalization from the ground up, asking how global flows are experienced, resisted, and remade in particular places, and who gains and who loses when the world becomes more connected.

## Definition & Historical Development

Connections across continents are old — trade routes, empires, religions, and migrations have linked distant societies for centuries. What changed in the late twentieth century was speed, scale, and simultaneity: container shipping and air travel moved goods and people faster; satellites and then the internet moved information instantly; and the deregulation of finance after the 1970s allowed capital to cross borders at the click of a key. The fall of the Soviet bloc and the opening of China and India to world markets in the 1980s and 1990s extended these circuits further, producing a world economy in which production is routinely split across many countries.

Anthropology's contribution has been to insist that globalization is not a steamroller flattening local difference. The same global product — a soft drink, a television format, a development programme — is taken up differently in different places, given local meanings, and sometimes turned against its makers. Globalization produces both homogenization and new forms of difference, often at the same time.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

**David Harvey** (born 1935) analyzed globalization as a new regime of capitalist accumulation in *The Condition of Postmodernity* (1989). He introduced the idea of time-space compression: faster transport and communication shrink the experience of distance, but they also intensify competition, accelerate fashion and finance, and disorient people whose lives are reorganized by forces they cannot see. For Harvey, globalization is driven by capital's need to overcome spatial barriers and to find new markets, labour, and resources.

**Roland Robertson** (1938–2022) popularised the term glocalization and, in *Globalization: Social Theory and Global Culture* (1992), argued that the global and the local produce each other. Global institutions adapt to local conditions, and local cultures assert themselves on global stages; neither pole can be understood without the other. Robertson also emphasized that globalization is cultural as well as economic — a growing consciousness of the world as a single place.

**Arjun Appadurai** (born 1949) mapped globalization as overlapping landscapes of flow in *Modernity at Large* (1996): ethnoscapes of moving people, mediascapes of circulating images, technoscapes of technology, financescapes of capital, and ideoscapes of political ideas. These flows move at different speeds and along different routes, and their disjunctures — the gaps between them — produce the tensions of the contemporary world. Appadurai's framework gave ethnographers a way to study globalization without reducing it to economics alone.

**Saskia Sassen** (born 1949) showed in *Globalization and Its Discontents* (1998) that globalization is produced in particular places, above all in global cities such as New York, London, and Tokyo, where finance, law, and corporate services concentrate. Globalization, she argued, does not make the state or the city disappear; it reorganizes them, creating new forms of inequality within cities as well as between countries, as high-paid professionals and low-paid service workers inhabit the same urban space under sharply different conditions.

## Ethnographic Examples

Fieldwork has shown globalization's uneven effects. In export-processing zones, young women assemble electronics for world markets under conditions that link their wages to distant consumers and shareholders. Migrants sustain families across borders through remittances and phone calls, building transnational households that span continents. In rural Africa and Asia, structural adjustment programmes imposed by international lenders have reshaped farming, health care, and schooling, often with devastating local consequences. Meanwhile, indigenous movements use global media and international law to defend local land — going global in order to stay local.

Consumption offers another window: the spread of fast food, beauty products, and smartphones is not simple Westernization. People adopt, adapt, and indigenize global goods, using them to mark status, youth, or modernity in locally meaningful ways.

## Contemporary Debates

Scholars now debate whether globalization is retreating. Trade wars, pandemic border closures, and nationalist movements have slowed some flows, leading some to speak of deglobalization. Others argue that globalization is not ending but changing form — digital platforms, data flows, and climate change create new connections even as goods trade stalls. For anthropology, the key insight endures: globalization is never a single force acting on passive locals. It is made, contested, and lived by people in particular places, and its benefits and burdens are distributed with striking inequality.
