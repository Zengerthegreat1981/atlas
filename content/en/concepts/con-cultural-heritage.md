# Cultural Heritage

## Definition

Cultural heritage is the legacy of tangible and intangible expressions that a group inherits from the past, values in the present, and seeks to transmit to the future. Tangible heritage includes monuments, buildings, archaeological sites, artworks, and objects; intangible heritage includes oral traditions, performing arts, rituals, festivals, crafts, and knowledge systems. The concept is broader than old buildings and museum pieces: it encompasses the practices, meanings, and skills through which people make sense of who they are and where they come from. Heritage is therefore not simply something that survives from the past; it is something that the present selects, interprets, and manages. What counts as heritage, who decides, and whose past is preserved are political questions, and anthropology studies heritage as a social process rather than as a fixed inventory of valuable things.

## Origins and Development

The modern concept of heritage emerged in the nineteenth century, when European states began to protect monuments and to found national museums as part of nation-building. After the Second World War, heritage became an international concern. The UNESCO Convention Concerning the Protection of the World Cultural and Natural Heritage, adopted in 1972, created the World Heritage List and established the idea that some sites are of outstanding universal value to all humanity. In 2003, the Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage extended protection to living traditions, responding to criticism that the earlier convention privileged the stone monuments of Europe over the practices of other cultures. David Lowenthal's *The Past Is a Foreign Country* showed how attitudes to the past have changed over time, and how heritage reflects present anxieties as much as past realities. Barbara Kirshenblatt-Gimblett analysed heritage as a mode of cultural production, arguing that heritage does not simply preserve the past but creates something new, as when a living practice is staged for tourists or a neighbourhood is redeveloped as a historic district. Laurajane Smith developed the concept of authorised heritage discourse, the set of professional and institutional assumptions that define what heritage is and who is entitled to speak for it, and showed how it marginalises alternative understandings.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns whose heritage counts. The World Heritage List has long been dominated by European sites, and critics argue that the criteria of outstanding universal value encode Western ideas about monumentality and authenticity. The 2003 convention was a response, but debates continue about whether listing protects living traditions or freezes them, turning dynamic practices into performances for outsiders. A second debate concerns authenticity and change. Must heritage remain unchanged to be genuine, or can it evolve? Many traditions survive precisely by adapting, and the insistence on frozen authenticity can prevent the very communities that practise them from innovating. A third debate concerns ownership and restitution. Museums in Europe and North America hold vast collections acquired under colonial rule, and demands for the return of objects such as the Benin Bronzes or the Parthenon Marbles raise questions about who owns the past and whether heritage belongs to a nation, a community, or humanity as a whole. A fourth dimension concerns heritage and conflict: heritage sites are deliberately targeted in war, from the destruction of the Bamiyan Buddhas to the shelling of historic cities, because destroying a group's heritage is a way of attacking its identity and its claim to a place.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Heritage is now a major global industry and a key arena of politics. Tourism built around heritage sites generates income but can also displace residents, inflate prices, and turn neighbourhoods into stage sets. UNESCO listing, while conferring prestige and protection, can create new conflicts over who benefits and whose version of the past is recognised. Digital technologies are reshaping heritage: three-dimensional scanning preserves endangered sites, online archives make collections accessible, and social media allows communities to document and assert their own heritage outside official channels. Climate change threatens heritage directly, from rising seas endangering coastal sites to changing rainfall damaging mud-brick architecture, and heritage professionals increasingly argue that protecting heritage must be part of climate adaptation. Critics of the heritage concept argue that it can essentialise culture, presenting traditions as timeless rather than as products of history, and that the language of preservation can be used to legitimise exclusion, as when heritage is invoked to resist new building or new residents. The anthropological perspective insists that heritage is best understood not as a collection of objects but as a relationship between people and their past, and that its study must attend to the voices of those whose heritage it claims to be.
