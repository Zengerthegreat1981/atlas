# Culture-Nature Distinction

The distinction between culture and nature is one of the most consequential ideas in Western thought — and, anthropologists have come to argue, one of the most peculiar. In the modern West, nature is commonly understood as the given, non-human world of plants, animals, geology, and physical forces, while culture is understood as the human-made world of language, art, technology, and social institutions. Nature is what we are born into; culture is what we make of it. This opposition seems obvious and universal, but ethnographic research has shown that many peoples do not divide the world this way at all: they treat animals as persons, forests as societies, and spirits as neighbours, and they see human social life as continuous with the non-human world rather than separate from it. The culture-nature distinction is therefore not a fact about the world but a cultural idea with a history — and anthropology has become the discipline that questions it most radically.

## Definition

The distinction rests on a series of paired oppositions: nature is wild, raw, and given; culture is tamed, cooked, and made. Human beings are seen as crossing from one to the other — through cooking, through language, through the incest taboo — and becoming cultural beings by separating themselves from nature. Claude Lévi-Strauss made this opposition the foundation of structural anthropology. In *Totemism* (1962) and *The Raw and the Cooked* (1964), he argued that the opposition between nature and culture was the fundamental structure of human thought, expressed in myths, food prohibitions, and kinship rules across the world. The incest taboo, as analysed in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949), was for Lévi-Strauss the hinge between nature and culture: it is universal, like what belongs to nature, yet it is a rule, like what belongs to culture, and therefore marks the passage from one domain to the other.

## Origins and Development

The modern distinction has its roots in Enlightenment philosophy and in the nineteenth-century separation of the natural sciences from the human sciences, but its anthropological career began with Lévi-Strauss. His structuralism treated the nature-culture opposition as a universal of the human mind. An influential challenge came from feminist anthropology. Marilyn Strathern, in her 1980 essay "No Nature, No Culture: The Hagen Case," showed that the Hagen people of Papua New Guinea do not divide the world into nature and culture, and that the opposition could not be used to explain their gender relations, as it had been used to explain gender in the West. Tim Ingold, in *The Perception of the Environment* (2000), argued from a different direction, proposing that human beings do not live in a cultural world imposed on a natural one, but dwell in a single environment, perceiving and acting within it through skilled practice. A wide-ranging synthesis came from Philippe Descola. In *Beyond Nature and Culture* (2005, in French; English translation 2013), Descola argued that the nature-culture dualism is a specifically Western and recent invention — naturalism, one of four possible ontologies, alongside animism, totemism, and analogism — and that most peoples of the world understand the relations between humans and non-humans differently. Bruno Latour, in *We Have Never Been Modern* (French 1991; English 1993), made a parallel argument: modernity's claim to have separated nature from culture, science from politics, was never actually achieved, and the modern world is full of hybrids — climate, epidemics, genetically modified organisms — that belong to neither domain.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns universality. Is the nature-culture distinction a feature of the human mind, as Lévi-Strauss argued, or a historical product of Western modernity, as Descola argues? Many ethnographers read the evidence as supporting the latter: many Amazonian peoples treat animals as persons with souls and societies; Australian Aboriginal peoples understand the land as the product of ancestral action, neither natural nor cultural in the Western sense. A second debate concerns the political uses of the distinction. To declare something natural — women's subordination, racial hierarchy, the market — has been a way of presenting it as inevitable; feminist and postcolonial anthropologists have shown how the appeal to nature has legitimated inequality. A third debate concerns what follows from abandoning the distinction. If there is no separate nature, how should anthropologists study human relations with animals, plants, and landscapes? Some propose a multispecies ethnography, studying the entanglements of humans and other beings; others propose an ontology of relations, in which what matters is not what things are but how they are related.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The critique of the nature-culture distinction has become urgent in the age of climate change. If nature is separate from culture, then climate change is a technical problem for the natural sciences; if the two are entangled, then it is a political, moral, and cultural problem that requires new ways of thinking about human responsibility. The same applies to conservation, where the idea of a pristine nature untouched by humans has justified the eviction of indigenous peoples from their lands, and to biotechnology, where genetically modified organisms and lab-grown meat blur the line between the natural and the artificial. The enduring contribution of the debate is to have shown that the most basic categories of thought are themselves cultural, and that anthropology's task includes questioning the categories with which it thinks — including its own founding distinction between the human world and the world beyond it.
