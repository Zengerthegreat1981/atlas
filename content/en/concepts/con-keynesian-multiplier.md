# Keynesian Multiplier

The **Keynesian multiplier** is a central economic concept developed by **John Maynard Keynes** in *The General Theory* (1936), building on an earlier idea formulated by his student Richard Kahn in 1931 (Kahn's multiplier concerned employment; Keynes generalised it to income in chapter 10 of the *General Theory*). It describes how an initial increase in one component of total spending (especially investment or government expenditure) can generate an increase in the economy's total income that exceeds the original increase by several multiples.

## The Mechanism: Successive Rounds of Spending and Income

The multiplier operates through a series of successive rounds. When the government spends a certain sum on an infrastructure project, for example, that sum becomes direct income for the workers and suppliers involved in the project. They in turn spend part of this new income on other goods and services, generating new income for a third party, and so the cycle repeats throughout the economy. The amount spent in each later round diminishes, because part of it is saved, spent on imports, or taken away as taxes.

## The Marginal Propensity to Consume: The Mathematical Determinant of the Multiplier's Size

The size of the multiplier is determined mathematically by the **marginal propensity to consume**: the proportion of each additional unit of income that individuals spend, as opposed to the proportion they save or lose to taxes and other leakages (such as imports). In the simplest case the multiplier equals 1/(1 − MPC), where MPC is the marginal propensity to consume, and it is smaller when taxes and imports leak out of the spending stream. The higher the marginal propensity to consume, the larger the multiplier, because each round of spending generates a next round of additional income that is relatively larger and is spent again in the domestic economy.

## The Theoretical Basis for Fiscal Intervention

This concept provided the central theoretical argument for justifying expansionary government spending policies to combat recession and unemployment. If the government, through a relatively limited expenditure, can generate a much larger increase in aggregate income and employment through the effect of the multiplier, then fiscal intervention becomes a relatively efficient tool for pulling the economy out of a depression or recession. Keynes had argued for such public spending in the early 1930s (including in his 1933 open letter to President Roosevelt), and the multiplier became a standard tool of fiscal analysis after the Second World War. Its actual size varies across economies and periods and is the subject of continuing empirical debate: estimates depend, for example, on spare capacity and on how monetary policy responds, and no single figure holds for every case (Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh, for instance, found multipliers after 2010 larger than forecasters had assumed).

## Later Criticisms and Reservations

The multiplier concept faced theoretical challenges from monetarism and the later new classical school, which questioned its practical effectiveness on several different grounds. Friedman argued that individuals tend to treat a temporary increase in income with greater caution (the permanent income hypothesis, 1957), so they do not spend from it to the degree that simple Keynesian theory assumes. Robert Barro (1974) revived the idea of "Ricardian equivalence": rational individuals might expect higher future taxes to finance current government spending, and would therefore reduce their current consumption as a precaution. This would weaken the actual effect of the multiplier compared with the earlier, more optimistic Keynesian estimates.
