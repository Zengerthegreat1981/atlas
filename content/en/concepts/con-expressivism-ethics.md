# Expressivism

Expressivism in ethics is a theory in metaethics (Metaethics) holding that moral judgments such as "lying is wrong" do not describe objective facts that can be true or false. Rather, they express emotional attitudes, desires or psychological states on the part of the speaker, in the way that the phrase "how wonderful!" expresses an emotion rather than describing an event. Expressivism belongs to the family of non-cognitivist positions (Non-Cognitivism), which deny that moral values are properties existing in the world independently of our attitudes.

## Roots and Historical Context

Expressivism emerged in the twentieth century, drawing on Hume's sentimentalism, as a response to the difficulties of both naturalist and non-naturalist (intuitionist) moral realism, especially within logical positivism. A. J. Ayer, in *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936), held that moral statements carry no cognitive meaning because they are not empirically verifiable, and that they do no more than express emotional approval or disapproval. This view became known as the "Boo/Hurrah theory" (Boo/Hurrah Theory, or Emotivism). Charles Stevenson developed the idea by adding a functional dimension: a moral statement not only expresses an emotion but also seeks to influence the emotions of the listener and to bring them to adopt the same attitude, so it is a persuasive rather than a descriptive instrument. In the second half of the twentieth century, Richard Hare developed "Universal Prescriptivism" (Universal Prescriptivism), which combines the expressive dimension with a commitment to logical consistency, regarding moral judgments as universal commands that can be generalized to all similar cases.

## Contemporary Development: Quasi-Realism

Classical expressivism faced a sharp logical objection known as the "Frege–Geach problem": how can the validity of a logical inference such as "If theft is wrong, then getting your friend to steal is wrong; theft is wrong; therefore getting your friend to steal is wrong" be explained, given that moral sentences carry no truth value that would allow such a logical conclusion? In response, Simon Blackburn developed the theory of "Quasi-Realism" (Quasi-Realism), which tries to show how a purely expressive attitude can generate moral discourse that looks and grammatically and logically behaves as if it were genuinely descriptive, without committing to the existence of independent moral facts. Allan Gibbard offered a similar model that links moral judgments to systems of "norms" (Norms) accepted by the individual.

## Objections and Example

Expressivism is objected to on the grounds that it makes it difficult to explain moral progress and genuine disagreement between parties. If a moral judgment is merely the expression of a personal attitude, how can we understand a moral debate in which one of the two sides appears to be genuinely mistaken rather than merely differing in taste? Example: when someone says "slavery is injustice," the expressivist holds that this statement expresses a deep condemnation of slavery and invites others to adopt the same attitude, rather than describing an objective property inhering in the act itself, apart from human attitudes.
