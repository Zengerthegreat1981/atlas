# Attachment Theory & Development

## Definition

Attachment theory is a developmental psychological framework formulated by the British psychiatrist and psychoanalyst John Bowlby. It holds that the infant is biologically predisposed to bond with a caregiver, and that the quality of this early bond shapes an internal pattern for later relationships and for the regulation of emotions. This entry addresses its psychopathological dimension: how disordered attachment contributes to mental illness and how it is used in treatment. Attachment patterns and Bowlby himself are covered in separate entries.

## Early Roots

Bowlby began from his observations of delinquent young people and of children separated from their mothers. He prepared a report for the World Health Organization, published in 1951 under the title *Maternal Care and Mental Health*. He then published the trilogy *Attachment and Loss*, beginning with volume one in 1969 from Hogarth Press. Support came from animal behavior science: Harry Harlow reported in *American Psychologist* in 1958 that infant rhesus monkeys preferred a cloth-covered surrogate to a wire surrogate that provided milk, evidence that contact comfort is not reducible to feeding. Mary Ainsworth stood out, developing in the 1960s and 1970s the "Strange Situation" procedure to measure secure, avoidant, and resistant (ambivalent) patterns (*Patterns of Attachment*, Erlbaum, 1978). Main and Solomon then added the disorganized attachment pattern in 1986.

## The Psychiatric Dimension

Disorganized attachment, linked to caregivers who are frightening or frightened, is associated with later dissociative symptoms and aggressive behavior in children. DSM-III (1980) included reactive attachment disorder; DSM-IV distinguished inhibited and disinhibited types, and DSM-5 made them two separate diagnoses, reactive attachment disorder and disinhibited social engagement disorder. A major test came from the English and Romanian Adoptees study, led by Michael Rutter, of children adopted into the United Kingdom from Romanian institutions in the early 1990s, after the end of the Ceaușescu regime. Follow-up of that cohort found that institutional deprivation lasting more than about six months was associated, even after placement in family care, with difficulties persisting into young adulthood, including disinhibited social engagement, autism-like symptoms, and inattention and overactivity, whereas the early cognitive impairment largely remitted by young adulthood (Sonuga-Barke and colleagues, *The Lancet*, 2017). George, Kaplan, and Main presented the Adult Attachment Interview in 1985 to link childhood representations with the adult narrative. Peter Fonagy then expanded the theory in *Attachment Theory and Psychoanalysis* (Other Press, 2001) through the concept of mentalization (reflective functioning) (see the entry on Mentalization).

## Arabic and Older Readings

Arab civilization did not formulate a theory under this name, but Arabic medicine paid attention to the upbringing of young children. Ibn al-Jazzar al-Qayrawani, in *Siyasat al-Sibyan wa-Tadbirihim*, detailed the choice of a wet nurse and the management of breastfeeding and weaning. Islamic jurisprudence set out rulings on breastfeeding (Qur'an 2:233) and custody, and the classical legal schools generally gave the mother priority in the custody of young children when the parents separate. Contemporary researchers have studied attachment across cultures: van IJzendoorn and Sagi-Schwartz discuss, in the *Handbook of Attachment* (Guilford), whether the categories are universal.

## Limits of the Theory

The theory is criticized for its focus on the mother and its neglect of the father's role and of collective care in many societies, for the cultural bias of the Strange Situation measure, and for its apparent determinism in linking childhood to destiny. Attachment is a risk factor, not a predetermined verdict, and the evidence indicates that "earned" secure attachment is possible after later relationships or psychotherapy.
