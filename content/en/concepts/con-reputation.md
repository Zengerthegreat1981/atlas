# Reputation

Reputation is the public estimation of a person's character, conduct, and worth — what others say about you when you are not present. It is a form of social capital that can open doors or close them: a good reputation brings trust, credit, marriage prospects, and office, while a damaged one brings exclusion, suspicion, and shame. Unlike status, which is often inherited or formally conferred, reputation must be continuously earned and defended through conduct, and it is always vulnerable to gossip, rumour, and slander. Anthropologists study reputation because it reveals how communities regulate behaviour without courts or police: the fear of what others will say is often a more powerful sanction than the fear of punishment.

## Definition

Reputation is best understood as a collective judgement, produced and maintained through talk. It is not a property of the individual alone, but a social fact created by the community's ongoing evaluation. Erving Goffman showed that social life is a kind of performance, in which people manage the impressions others form of them, and reputation is the residue of those performances over time. Julian Pitt-Rivers defined honour as the value of a person in their own eyes and in the eyes of their society; reputation is its public, negotiable side. It has moral, competence, and relational dimensions: is this person honest and loyal, skilled at their work, and faithful to obligations toward kin and neighbours? Reputation is also gendered in many societies: men's reputations often turn on courage, generosity, and the defence of honour, while women's reputations turn on modesty, fidelity, and the management of the household. Because reputation is built from talk, it is inseparable from gossip, which anthropologists treat not as idle chatter but as a central mechanism of social control — a way for a community to define its norms, police its boundaries, and share information about who can be trusted.

## Origins and Development

The anthropology of reputation grew out of the study of honour in Mediterranean and Middle Eastern societies in the 1960s, where scholars found that honour was not a quaint survival but a living system that governed violence, marriage, hospitality, and politics. In small communities where everyone knows everyone else, reputation is dense and inescapable: a single act can be witnessed, retold, and remembered for a generation. The concept travelled widely, applied to the "face" of Chinese social life, the prestige economies of Melanesia, and the street reputations of urban neighbourhoods. Pierre Bourdieu's analysis of symbolic capital gave reputation a theoretical place within a general theory of power: reputation, honour, and prestige are forms of capital that can be accumulated, converted into economic advantage, and lost through miscalculation. The rise of the modern state and the market did not eliminate reputation; it displaced it into new arenas — professional references, credit ratings, and, most recently, online reviews and social media profiles, where reputation is quantified, searchable, and increasingly detached from personal acquaintance.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns whether reputation is a resource or a constraint. For the individual, a good reputation is an asset; but the need to protect it can also be a burden, forcing people into costly displays of generosity or into violence to avenge insult. In societies governed by honour, the imperative to defend reputation has been linked to feuding and to the control of women's behaviour, raising difficult questions about the human costs of the system. A second debate concerns the relationship between reputation and truth. Reputation is built on what is said, and what is said may be false; yet in practice, a reputation, once established, tends to shape how new evidence is interpreted, so that the well-regarded are given the benefit of the doubt and the ill-regarded are not. A third dimension is the scale of reputation. In face-to-face communities, reputation is personal and local; in larger societies, it becomes institutionalised in titles, certificates, and records. A fourth question is agency: how much control do people have over their reputations? Goffman's dramaturgy suggests a great deal — people stage-manage their self-presentation carefully — but gossip, envy, and malice mean that reputation is never fully under one's own control.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Reputation has acquired new urgency in the digital age. Online ratings, social media profiles, and background checks mean that reputation is now produced by strangers and algorithms, and that a single viral accusation can destroy a livelihood overnight. Anthropologists note that the internet has amplified rather than invented these dynamics. The phenomenon of "cancel culture" can be read as a modern form of reputational sanction, operating without formal courts. In business and politics, reputation management has become an industry, and credit-scoring systems convert reputation into a number that determines access to housing, loans, and employment — raising concerns about bias and accountability. Critics of the concept warn against reducing social life to impression management: reputation matters, but so do material conditions, legal rights, and structural power. The enduring insight is that reputation is a social infrastructure — invisible, powerful, and maintained by talk — and that understanding it is essential to understanding how communities hold together without formal enforcement.
