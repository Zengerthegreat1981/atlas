# Peace Building

Peace building is the set of activities undertaken to prevent the recurrence of violent conflict after a war or crisis has ended, by addressing its root causes and rebuilding the institutions, relationships, and trust that make peaceful coexistence possible. It extends beyond the negotiation of ceasefires and peace agreements to the longer work of disarming combatants, reforming security forces, restoring the rule of law, reviving economies, and reconciling divided communities. For anthropologists and peace researchers, peace building is a central concept because it links the politics of international intervention to the everyday lives of people emerging from war.

## Origins and Development

The term peacebuilding came into wide use after the Cold War, when the United Nations began to undertake complex missions in countries emerging from civil war. The UN Secretary-General Boutros Boutros-Ghali defined it in *An Agenda for Peace* (1992) as action to identify and support structures that tend to strengthen and solidify peace in order to avoid a relapse into conflict. This definition marked a shift from peacekeeping — the monitoring of ceasefires by neutral forces — to a more ambitious agenda of rebuilding states and societies.

The intellectual foundations of peace building reach further back. Johan Galtung, a founding figure of peace research, argued in "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research" (1969) that peace requires more than the absence of direct violence: it also requires the removal of structural violence — such as poverty, inequality, and exclusion — and he called this wider condition positive peace. In "Three Approaches to Peace: Peacekeeping, Peacemaking and Peacebuilding" (1976) he argued that peacebuilding should create structures that address the causes of war. Galtung's distinction between negative peace (the absence of war) and positive peace (the presence of justice and well-being) remains the conceptual backbone of the field, and his later work, *Peace by Peaceful Means* (1996), elaborated the approach in detail.

## Approaches and Practice

Peace building takes many forms, and its practice has been shaped by both theory and experience. John Paul Lederach, in *Building Peace: Sustainable Reconciliation in Divided Societies* (1997), argued that peace building must work at multiple levels simultaneously: with top leaders who negotiate agreements, with middle-range leaders — religious figures, academics, civil society activists — who can bridge divides, and with grassroots communities where peace must be lived day by day. Lederach emphasised that sustainable peace depends on local ownership: outsiders can support peace building, but they cannot impose it.

In practice, peace building missions have included the disarmament, demobilisation, and reintegration of former combatants; the reform of police and armed forces; the organisation of elections; the establishment of truth commissions and war crimes tribunals; and the promotion of economic recovery. Anthropologists have studied these activities on the ground, documenting how they are received by local populations — how, for example, a truth commission is experienced by survivors, or how a new police force is judged by the communities it patrols. This ethnographic work has shown that peace building is not a technical exercise but a political and cultural process, in which international models meet local realities and are transformed by the encounter.

## Key Debates

One debate concerns the liberal peace model: the assumption, dominant in the 1990s and 2000s, that peace building should promote democracy, market economics, and the rule of law, on the grounds that liberal states do not go to war with one another. Roland Paris, in *At War's End: Building Peace After Civil Conflict* (2004), argued that rapid liberalisation could itself destabilise fragile societies, and that peace building should proceed gradually, building effective institutions before introducing competitive elections and free markets. Oliver Richmond, who examined competing conceptions of peace in *The Transformation of Peace* (2005), later argued in *A Post-Liberal Peace* (2011) for an approach that takes local practices, identities, and conceptions of peace seriously rather than treating them as obstacles to be overcome.

A related debate concerns the role of external actors. Critics argue that international peace building can amount to a new form of trusteeship, in which foreign experts and organisations govern post-conflict societies in the name of peace. Defenders argue that many post-conflict states lack the capacity to rebuild on their own and need sustained external support. Anthropologists have contributed to this debate by documenting both the achievements of peace building missions and their unintended consequences — the dependency they can create, the local elites they can empower, and the resentments they can generate.

## Contemporary Relevance

Peace building remains a pressing concern in the twenty-first century, as conflicts in the Middle East, Africa, and elsewhere continue to generate demands for intervention and reconstruction. The concept has also been extended beyond post-war settings: to the prevention of violence in fragile states, to the management of climate-related conflict, and to the building of peace in cities divided by gang violence and social exclusion. The anthropological contribution is to insist that peace is not only a matter of treaties and institutions, but of everyday relationships — of trust rebuilt between neighbours, of livelihoods restored, and of the slow work of imagining a shared future after violence. That work, as the record of the past three decades shows, is difficult, contested, and never finished.
