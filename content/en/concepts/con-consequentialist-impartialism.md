# Consequentialist Impartiality

## Meaning and Definition

Impartiality in consequentialist ethics is the principle that the interests of every individual affected by an act must be counted with equal weight in the calculation of overall consequences. No preference is given to the interests of the agent, their relatives or their own kind over the interests of strangers. For consequentialist thinkers, the criterion of moral rightness is maximizing the overall good, from what Henry Sidgwick called "the point of view of the universe," rather than maximizing the good of one particular party.

## Roots and the Islamic Comparison

This principle partly intersects with the Qur'anic concept of justice, as in the verse "And when you speak, be just, even if it concerns a near relative." It requires setting aside the advantages of kinship in judgment and testimony. However, Islamic jurisprudence does not treat all levels of moral priority as equal. It arranges concentric circles of benevolence (parents, close relatives, neighbors, and then Muslims in general), in accordance with the verse "And to parents, good treatment, and to relatives." For Islamic jurisprudence, justice does not cancel the priority of those nearest. Consequentialist impartiality, by contrast, systematically treats all affected parties equally in the calculation of benefit.

## Philosophical Context and Objections

The principle is associated with the dictum, which John Stuart Mill attributed to Bentham, that each is to count for one and nobody for more than one. Henry Sidgwick formulated it philosophically in *The Methods of Ethics* (1874), speaking of "the point of view of the universe," which transcends the perspective of the partial agent. Bernard Williams offered an influential critique of this principle through the concept of "integrity" (personal integrity). He argued that neutral consequentialism sometimes requires the individual to give up their personal projects and even their family obligations if overall calculations so demand, which empties personal moral life of its meaning. The philosopher Peter Singer defended an extreme version of impartiality in his essay on famine (1972). He concluded that geographical distance does not justify neglecting to save strangers when we are able to do so without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance.

## Relationships and Applications

This concept is closely linked to the school of utilitarianism and consequentialism, in which it forms a structural pillar. It appears in debates about global distributive justice, such as the question of whether spending money on local luxury can be morally justified when the same amount could save lives in a poor country. An example is the contemporary "Effective Altruism" movement.

## Limits of Practical Application

Strict application of impartiality faces a significant practical objection. Must an individual actually devote all of their surplus income to needy strangers as long as that increases overall good? Peter Singer holds that logic requires this unless one sacrifices something of comparable moral importance. Moderates such as Samuel Scheffler, however, argue for the need to recognize "agent-centered prerogatives," which allow the individual to give relatively greater weight to their own projects and loved ones without fully violating the principle of impartiality. This is an attempt to reconcile the demands of universal morality with the realities of personal life.
