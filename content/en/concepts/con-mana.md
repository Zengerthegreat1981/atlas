# Mana

Mana is a concept originating in Melanesia and Polynesia, where it refers to a supernatural force, power, or efficacy that people, spirits, objects, and places may possess in varying degrees. A chief with mana commands respect and succeeds in what he undertakes; a sacred object with mana is dangerous to handle; a place with mana is potent and must be approached with care. Mana entered anthropology in the late nineteenth century and became one of the discipline's most debated concepts, a test case for how far a term borrowed from one cultural world can travel — and how far it can be distorted in the travelling.

## Definition

The term was introduced to anthropology by R. H. Codrington, an Anglican missionary and ethnographer who worked in Melanesia. In his book The Melanesians, published in 1891, Codrington described mana as a force or influence, itself not physical and in a way supernatural, that shows itself in physical force or in any kind of power or excellence that a person possesses. A person with mana succeeds where others fail; his words carry weight, his gardens flourish, his canoes are swift. Mana is not evenly distributed: it can be gained through success, through contact with spirits or ancestors, through proper ritual, and through descent from powerful forebears, and it can be lost through failure, wrongdoing, or pollution. It is also transferable: it can reside in objects such as weapons or ornaments, and it can be transmitted through touch, speech, or inheritance. Crucially, mana is both a quality and an explanation: to say that someone succeeded because he had mana is to explain success by the power that success itself demonstrates.

## Origins and Development

Codrington's account was taken up by the French sociological school. Émile Durkheim, in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life, published in 1912, treated mana as the prototype of the sacred: the impersonal force that, in his theory, was really society itself, experienced by individuals as an external power. Marcel Mauss developed the idea further, arguing that mana was a category of the mind, a notion that fused the ideas of power, action, quality, and state. In Britain, R. R. Marett, in The Threshold of Religion, published in 1909, proposed that belief in mana — which he called animatism, to distinguish it from belief in spirits, or animism — preceded belief in gods and spirits in the evolution of religion. Mana thus became a cornerstone of early theories of religion, used to explain the origins of the sacred, the nature of magic, and the foundations of social authority. By the mid-twentieth century, however, the concept had fallen into disrepute as a vague catch-all, applied so widely that it seemed to explain everything and nothing.

## Key Debates

The most important modern debate about mana was opened by Roger Keesing, whose essay "Rethinking Mana," published in 1984, argued that anthropologists had created a false concept. Keesing showed that in the languages of the Pacific, terms translated as mana had specific, varied meanings — efficacy, truth, success, spiritual power — and that the abstract, impersonal force of the textbooks was largely an invention of European theorists. He did not argue that the Pacific concepts were unimportant, but that they had been flattened into a single idea that served European theories of religion better than it described Pacific realities. Other scholars have defended a more careful use of the term, arguing that the notion of a transmissible, graded power does capture something real about how people in Oceania think about efficacy and authority, even if the textbook definition needs revision. The debate over mana has become a classic illustration of a wider problem in anthropology: the translation of Indigenous concepts into analytic terms, and the risk that the analytic term takes on a life of its own, detached from the language and the world from which it came.

## Contemporary Relevance

Mana has survived its academic critics, and it now circulates far beyond anthropology. In New Zealand, mana is a living concept in Māori life and politics, where mana motuhake — separate authority, or self-determination — is a central political claim, and manaakitanga, the ethic of hospitality and care, is a public value. In Hawai'i, mana remains a significant religious and political concept. In global popular culture, mana has been borrowed by fantasy games and literature as a measure of magical energy, a use that owes little to its Pacific origins and that some Pacific commentators regard as trivialising. Within anthropology, the debate over mana continues to shape how scholars handle Indigenous concepts: the current preference is to attend closely to the words people actually use, in their own languages, and to the contexts in which they use them, rather than to extract a single portable essence. Mana's history is thus both a chapter in the anthropology of religion and a cautionary tale about the discipline itself.
