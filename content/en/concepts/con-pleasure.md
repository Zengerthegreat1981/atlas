# Pleasure

The subjective positive experience of sensory or psychological comfort that accompanies the satisfaction of a need or drive, or the relief of pain or tension. It is among the oldest subjects of philosophy and psychology, and it intersects ethics, medicine and neuroscience.

## Pleasure in the Arab Heritage

Ibn Sina, in *Remarks and Admonitions* (*al-Isharat wa-al-Tanbihat*), defined pleasure as the perception of the agreeable as agreeable, and pain as the perception of the incompatible as incompatible; pleasure is thus a relation between a perceiving power and a perfection suited to it, not merely a bodily state. From this he concluded that intellectual pleasures are higher than sensory ones, because what is apprehended in them is more noble and more lasting. Abu Bakr al-Razi, in *The Spiritual Physick* (c. 920), took a different view, regarding pleasure as a return to the natural state after pain, a position that brings him close to Plato in the *Philebus* and to Epicurus's definition of pleasure as the absence of pain. This was translated by Arberry in *The Spiritual Physick of Rhazes* (1950). Al-Ghazali, in *The Revival of the Religious Sciences* (*Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*), placed the highest pleasures in knowing God and gazing upon His face, and warned that sensory pleasure is brief and is followed by regret, thus adopting the same hierarchy between transient and enduring pleasure.

## Pleasure in Greek and Modern Philosophy

Epicurus regarded pleasure as the goal of life, but in the sense of the absence of bodily pain and disturbance of the soul (*ataraxia*). Aristotle, in the *Nicomachean Ethics* (Book X), presented pleasure as the completion of a complete activity rather than an independent end. Jeremy Bentham, in *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), treated pleasures and pains as quantities, and John Stuart Mill, in *Utilitarianism* (1861), distinguished between higher and lower pleasures.

## Pleasure in Psychoanalysis and Neuroscience

Freud formulated the "pleasure principle" in *Formulations on the Two Principles of Mental Functioning* (1911) as a primary law of the psychic apparatus that seeks satisfaction and avoids pain, and then modified it in *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920) when he introduced the death drive and repetition compulsion. James Olds and Peter Milner (1954) discovered brain centres that drive a rat to stimulate itself electrically. Olds and Milner's finding was long interpreted as the discovery of a "pleasure centre", though later work, notably by Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson (1998), argued that dopamine is more closely involved in incentive salience ("wanting", the motivation to pursue rewards) than in hedonic impact ("liking"). Berridge and Kringelbach (2008) review evidence that hedonic "hot spots" in the nucleus accumbens shell and other regions, involving endogenous opioid and related systems, contribute to the experience of pleasure. These are findings largely from animal studies, and their translation to human pleasure remains partly open.

## Clinical Importance

The loss of the capacity for pleasure (anhedonia) is a central symptom of depression and schizophrenia, while changes in reward processing and in the balance between wanting and liking are prominent in theories of addiction (such as Robinson and Berridge's incentive-sensitization theory). Anna Lembke's popular book *Dopamine Nation* (2021) discusses this balance for general readers. Brickman and colleagues (1978) reported that lottery winners were not markedly happier than comparison groups, a study often cited in support of the "hedonic treadmill" (adaptation) idea formulated by Brickman and Campbell (1971); the original study was small and its interpretation has been qualified by later research.

## The Distinction from Neighbouring Concepts

Pleasure differs from happiness and flourishing (eudaimonia): pleasure is a momentary experience tied to a stimulus, whereas happiness is an evaluation of a whole, meaningful life. Epicurus built his preference between transient and stable pleasures on this very distinction. It also differs from the "hedonic principle," which is an explanatory rule attributing every behaviour to the pursuit of pleasure, whereas pleasure itself is an experience.

## Limits of the Concept

Pleasure suffers from the difficulty of subjective measurement, and from the fact that "pleasure" covers heterogeneous experiences, from taste to intellectual enjoyment to comfort. Dopamine research faces the challenge that its results in animals do not transfer directly to humans.
