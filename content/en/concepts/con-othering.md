# Othering

## Definition

Othering is the process by which a group defines itself by constructing another group as fundamentally different, inferior, or alien. Through othering, the "us" gains its identity and coherence by contrast with a "them" who are imagined as exotic, threatening, backward, or inhuman. The process operates through language, images, institutions, and everyday interaction: stereotypes that fix people into a few simple traits, binaries such as civilised and savage or modern and traditional, and practices of exclusion that mark some people as not belonging. Othering is not merely a matter of individual prejudice. It is a social and cultural mechanism that legitimises unequal treatment, from colonial domination to immigration controls, by making inequality appear natural, as if it followed from the essential difference of the other rather than from relations of power. Anthropology has been centrally concerned with othering because the discipline itself was historically built on the study of peoples constructed as others.

## Origins and Development

The concept draws on several intellectual traditions. Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* showed how woman is constructed as the Other in relation to man, who occupies the position of the universal subject, while woman is defined only in relation to him. Edward Said's *Orientalism* demonstrated how Western scholarship, literature, and colonial administration produced "the Orient" as a timeless, sensual, and despotic other, a representation that served colonial power while claiming to describe reality. Johannes Fabian's *Time and the Other* showed how anthropology itself contributed to othering by placing the peoples it studied in a different time, treating them as living survivals of an earlier stage of human development rather than as contemporaries. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak developed the concept further, analysing how colonial discourse constructs the subaltern as an other who cannot speak within the dominant frameworks of knowledge, and how even sympathetic representation can reproduce the silencing it claims to overcome. Together, these works turned othering from a vague observation about prejudice into a precise analytical tool for studying power.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns whether othering is inevitable in the formation of identity. Some theorists argue that all identity requires difference, and that the self is necessarily constituted through contrast with what it is not. Others argue that difference need not become othering: recognising difference is not the same as ranking it, and the problem lies not in distinction itself but in the hierarchical and essentialising form it takes under conditions of inequality. A second debate concerns the relationship between othering and representation. Said's argument implies that representations do not merely reflect reality but actively produce the other, raising the question of whether any representation of a different culture can escape othering. Anthropologists have responded with reflexive ethnography, collaborative methods, and attention to how the people studied represent themselves and the anthropologist in return. A third dimension concerns the multiple forms othering takes: racial othering, gendered othering, the othering of migrants and refugees, of disabled people, of the poor, and of religious minorities. These forms overlap and reinforce one another, and the same group can be othered in different ways in different contexts, as exotic in one setting and threatening in another.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Othering remains a powerful lens on contemporary politics. Media coverage of migration frequently constructs migrants as a flood or an invasion, a language that turns people into a natural disaster rather than fellow human beings. Security discourse after 2001 constructed the Muslim as a suspect other, with consequences for surveillance, detention, and everyday harassment. Digital platforms have created new mechanisms of othering: algorithms that sort users into echo chambers, online harassment campaigns that target women and minorities, and the viral spread of stereotypes in memes and short video. At the same time, the concept has been criticised for being overextended: if every distinction counts as othering, the term loses its critical edge. Critics also note that focusing on discourse can underplay material relations, such as the economic structures that make some groups vulnerable to being othered in the first place. The strongest uses of the concept therefore combine attention to representation with attention to the institutions and inequalities that give othering its force, asking not only how others are imagined but who benefits from the imagining and what it makes possible.
