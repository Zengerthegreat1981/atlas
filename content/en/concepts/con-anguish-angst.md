# Existential Anxiety (Angst): Confronting the Indefinite in the Self

In Kierkegaard's *The Concept of Anxiety* (*Begrebet Angest*, 1844), anxiety (Danish *Angest*, German *Angst*) is a state of mind that differs from fear. Fear has a specific object (one fears a particular animal or danger); anxiety has no determinate object. Kierkegaard calls it the "dizziness of freedom": the self's awareness of its own possibilities. Heidegger reformulated the idea in *Being and Time* (1927) as a fundamental mood (*Grundbefindlichkeit*) in which the world loses its familiar significance and Dasein is confronted with its own being-in-the-world and its ownmost, non-relational possibility, death (*Sein-zum-Tode*). In the later lecture "What Is Metaphysics?" (1929), anxiety is the mood in which "the nothing" (*das Nichts*) is disclosed.

## Philosophical Meaning

For Kierkegaard, anxiety accompanies the awareness that one is free: it arises before the possibility of acting and the possibility of failing to act, and it is therefore bound up with the individual's relation to sin, spirit and the "leap" of decision. Heidegger sharply distinguishes anxiety (*Angst*) from fear (*Furcht*): fear is directed at a particular entity within the world that is threatening, whereas anxiety is directed at nothing in particular and discloses the world as such and Dasein's own possibility of being. For Heidegger, anxiety individualizes Dasein and can call it back from absorption in everyday "falling" toward authenticity. In Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) anguish (*angoisse*) is likewise distinguished from fear: fear concerns external things that threaten me, while anguish is the awareness of my own freedom and of what I might do (the vertigo before a precipice is his example).

## Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

In existential psychotherapy, Irvin Yalom (*Existential Psychotherapy*, 1980) treats anxiety as arising from confrontation with the "ultimate concerns" of existence: death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness. Existential therapists distinguish "normal" existential anxiety, which is proportionate to the situation and can be a source of growth, from "neurotic" anxiety, which is a defensive and disproportionate response to it (a distinction associated with Rollo May and Paul Tillich). Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), a contemporary behavioural therapy, similarly aims to change a person's relationship to anxious experience (acceptance, defusion) rather than to eliminate it.

Clinical anxiety disorders are a different level of description. In DSM-5-TR, generalized anxiety disorder is defined by excessive, hard-to-control worry about a number of events or activities, present on more days than not for at least six months, together with associated symptoms and impairment; panic disorder and social anxiety disorder have their own criteria. Whether a given patient's anxiety expresses existential concerns, a diagnosable disorder, or both is a matter for clinical judgment; the existential concept is not a diagnostic category and does not replace assessment.

## Distinction from Adjacent Concepts

Existential anxiety differs from **fear**, which has a specific object. It differs from **clinical anxiety** (anxiety disorders as defined in DSM-5-TR and ICD-11), which is a classification of symptom patterns and impairment, not a philosophical account of what anxiety discloses. It also differs from **worry** in the everyday sense: worry is a repetitive, verbal thought process about possible future problems, whereas existential anxiety is a mood or state said to disclose something about one's existence as such.
