# Informal Economy

Productive, commercial, and service activities that take place outside the state's legal, tax, and administrative organization, yet still generate income and are socially organized according to their own rules. They are not merely marginal economic chaos.

## The Origin of the Concept in Keith Hart

The British anthropologist **Keith Hart** coined the term "informal sector" in 1971 in a field study of migrant workers from the countryside to Accra in Ghana. It was later published in his landmark article "Informal Income Opportunities and Urban Employment in Ghana" (1973). Hart distinguished between wage labor in large institutions and government on one hand, and self-employment in small-scale activities on the other. He divided informal activities into "legitimate" (such as crafts and small trade) and "illegitimate" (such as smuggling). Hart concluded that many urban migrants, despite the constraints of the dominant capitalist economy, were actually earning independent income through these activities. They were not merely surviving on the margins until the whole economy "transitioned" into the formal sector, as the prevailing modernization theories had predicted at the time.

## Evolution of the Concept

The International Labour Organization (ILO) adopted the term in its 1972 report on Kenya. Its use then expanded in development and economic literature to include integrated production networks that intersect with the formal economy (such as home-based work within global supply chains), not merely street vendors and independent artisans. The network **WIEGO** (Women in Informal Employment: Globalizing and Organizing) developed this framework to document the scale of informal employment worldwide, especially among women in developing economies, and linked it to questions of social protection and labor rights.

## Anthropological Significance

The concept represents a break from the linear modernization view, which assumed that economic development necessarily means the disappearance of unorganized activities in favor of a comprehensive formal economy. Later urban ethnography showed that the informal economy is not a traditional remnant but a continuous product of the relationship between global capitalism and local markets, and that it overlaps without clear boundaries with the formal economy rather than being a separate sector.

## Limits

- The boundaries between "formal" and "informal" are variable and methodologically contested. Many activities combine both (for example, a worker who is officially registered but also does unreported additional work).
- Researchers disagree about whether the informal economy is a positive livelihood solution for the poor or a form of exploitation of workers deprived of legal protection.
