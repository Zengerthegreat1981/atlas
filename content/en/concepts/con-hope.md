# Hope

Hope, in existential thought, is not optimism or the belief that things will work out well. It is, rather, a commitment to meaning-making and to engagement with life despite the absence of guarantees. Frankl called it the "will to meaning"—the capacity to find purpose even in suffering, even in circumstances that appear meaningless.

## Hope Born from Despair

Authentic hope arises not from naiveté but from the confrontation with despair. Frankl's concentration camp experience offers the starkest illustration: those who maintained hope (defined not as optimism but as a sense of purpose) were more likely to survive than those who lost it. But that hope was not based on the belief that liberation would come; some maintained it while still imprisoned. Rather, hope was a choice—the choice to see one's suffering as meaningful, to maintain dignity and compassion even in dehumanizing circumstances, to live for something beyond immediate survival.

This hope is robust precisely because it has been tempered by the acknowledgment of despair. Someone who has truly confronted meaninglessness and still chooses to care, to commit, to create meaning, is more deeply hopeful than someone who has never questioned optimistic assumptions about life's inherent direction.

## Hope and Futility

May distinguishes between hope and wishful thinking. Wishful thinking is passive: the fantasy that circumstances will change without one's effort. Hope is active: the commitment to a vision of what could be, combined with the willingness to work toward it, even knowing that failure is possible.

Therapeutic hopelessness—the sense that nothing can change—often masks a more fundamental despair about one's own agency and responsibility. The existential therapist's role is not to reassure ("things will get better") but to clarify: What is within your power to change? What meaning can you create with what is available to you? What possibilities remain open?

## The Practice of Hope

Hope, in existential terms, is a practice rather than a feeling. It involves:

1. **Commitment to values** despite uncertainty about outcomes.
2. **Engagement with the present** rather than postponement to an imagined better future.
3. **Connection to others** and to projects larger than oneself.
4. **The tolerance of both hope and despair** as legitimate responses to human existence.

Many people in therapy are not suffering from insufficient hope but from excessive or misplaced hope—the belief that once they achieve a certain goal, find a partner, reach a status level, then life will finally be meaningful. This displacement of meaning into an imagined future is a form of inauthenticity that therapy helps to address.
