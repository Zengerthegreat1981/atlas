# Hope

## Definition and the Existential Conception

In existential psychology and philosophy, hope is not uncritical optimism, passive wishful thinking, or an unfounded expectation that external circumstances will inevitably turn out well. Rather, authentic hope is an active commitment to meaning-making and purposeful engagement with life despite uncertainty, vulnerability, and the absence of guarantees. It represents a conscious decision to act and care within a finite, precarious world.

## Hope Born from Despair

Existential thinkers assert that authentic hope is forged directly through the honest confrontation with despair, finitude, and the threat of meaninglessness:
- **Viktor Frankl**: In *Man's Search for Meaning* (1959), Frankl drew upon his experiences in Nazi concentration camps to illustrate that survival and psychological endurance were tied to the "will to meaning." Hope was not an illusion of guaranteed liberation; rather, it was an existential choice to preserve personal dignity, care for others, and orient oneself toward a meaningful future task or transcendent value regardless of outer degradation.
- **Søren Kierkegaard**: In *The Sickness unto Death* (1849), Kierkegaard analyzed despair (*Fortvivlelse*) as a fundamental misrelation within the self. For Kierkegaard, overcoming despair requires moving beyond both temporal resignation and defiant self-assertion into genuine faith and hope grounded in transcendent reality.
- **Rollo May**: In *Love and Will* (1969), May carefully distinguished between "wishing" and "willing." Wishing is imaginative, emotionally alive play with possibilities that lacks commitment, whereas willing is the organization of the self toward action. May held that each needs the other, and authentic hope synthesizes both: it envisions what could be while mobilizing personal responsibility and agency to bring that vision into being.

## The Phenomenological Perspective: Gabriel Marcel

The French existential philosopher Gabriel Marcel offered a profound phenomenological analysis of hope (*espérance*) in *Homo Viator* (1944). Marcel sharply contrasted hope with desire:
- **Desire** (*désir*) is calculative, possessive, and directed toward a specific, concrete outcome (e.g., hoping that a particular transaction succeeds).
- **Hope** (*espérance*) is an ontological virtue rooted in communion and open availability (*disponibilité*). For Marcel, true hope is lived as an appeal: it asserts that in the very heart of trial, captivity, or darkness, there exists a transcendent dimension of reality that cannot be destroyed by death or despair.

## Cognitive and Psychological Formulations: Snyder's Hope Theory

In positive and cognitive psychology, C. R. Snyder and colleagues (1991) operationalized hope as a measurable, cognitive-motivational construct. According to Snyder's model, hope comprises two reciprocal and interrelated thinking processes:
1. **Pathways Thinking**: The perceived cognitive capacity to generate plausible routes and actionable strategies to reach desired goals, including the ability to create alternative routes when facing obstacles.
2. **Agency Thinking**: The perceived motivational self-efficacy and determination to initiate and sustain movement along those chosen pathways ("the will and the ways").

Snyder developed the **Adult Hope Scale** (AHS), and subsequent research has consistently demonstrated that higher hope scores correlate with superior academic and athletic performance, effective problem-focused coping, lower distress during physical illness, and enhanced psychological well-being.

## Clinical and Therapeutic Implications

In psychotherapeutic practice, the existential therapist does not offer superficial reassurance or platitudes ("everything will be fine"). Instead, therapy serves to:
- Disentangle genuine hope from **defensive postponement**—the inauthentic belief that life will only truly begin after some future milestone is reached (e.g., marriage, wealth, status).
- Confront therapeutic demoralization by clarifying what genuine choices and values remain within the client's agency.
- Cultivate the tolerance for both hope and grief as legitimate, coexisting facets of an authentic human life.
