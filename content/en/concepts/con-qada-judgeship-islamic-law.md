# Qada' (The Institution of the Islamic Judge)

**Qada'** (judgeship) is the institution through which the **judge** (*qadi*), appointed by the ruling authority, issues binding rulings on disputes brought before him between litigants, on the basis of Islamic jurisprudence and its established procedures. Classical jurists distinguished judgeship precisely from two other sharia-legal functions: ifta' (issuing legal opinions) and siyasa shar'iyya (governance in accordance with sharia).

## Distinction from Ifta' and Siyasa Shar'iyya

In al-Qarafi's authoritative theorization (in *al-Ihkam*), the judge's ruling is distinguished from the mufti's fatwa by being **binding** in the specific dispute brought before him (not merely a non-binding announcement about God's ruling in general). It is distinguished from **political or imamic action** (the powers of the ruler within siyasa shar'iyya) by being restricted to the procedures of the claim and the formal evidence (*bayyina*) between two specific contesting parties, not a general act in public affairs based on considering the public interest, as the imam or ruler does.

## Conditions for the Judge and the Development of the Office

Jurists stipulated for the judge conditions such as majority (*bulugh*), sound mind, integrity (*'adala*), and sufficient knowledge of the principles and branches of jurisprudence needed to decide disputes. The office of judgeship developed historically from individual judges working in separate cities into a centralized hierarchical structure, after the creation of the office of **chief judge** (*qadi al-qudat*) in the Abbasid era. The first to hold this office is attributed to Abu Yusuf, a student of Abu Hanifa, who became a reference for the judges below him throughout the state.

## Binding the Judiciary to an Official School

At a later historical stage, some Islamic states (especially the Ottoman state) required judges officially to rule according to one approved official school of jurisprudence (the Hanafi school, in the Ottoman case), rather than leaving the individual judge free to exercise unrestricted ijtihad, as had been the case in the earlier periods. This institutional development is sometimes compared with the movement towards unifying the judiciary and stabilizing precedent in other legal traditions.

## The Transformation in the Modern Era

With modern codification projects (such as the *Majallat al-Ahkam al-Adliyya*), the judge's role gradually changed from a jurist who derives the ruling directly from the jurisprudential texts to an applier of pre-established codified legal texts. This is a fundamental transformation in the nature of the Islamic judicial function, and it has provoked wide debate as to whether it represents a continuation of the jurisprudential tradition or a rupture with it.
