# Assimilation: Becoming Part of the Mainstream

Assimilation is **the process by which immigrants and minority groups come to resemble the dominant group of a society — in language, customs, social networks, and ultimately identity — and are accepted as full members of it**. It is one of the oldest concepts in the study of migration, and one of the most contested. For some, assimilation describes a natural and desirable path to inclusion; for others, it names a demand that minorities abandon their cultures in exchange for acceptance. Anthropologists study assimilation not as a single ladder but as a varied, uneven, and often resisted process, shaped by race, class, gender, and the policies of the receiving society.

## Definition & Historical Development

The concept was developed by the Chicago School of sociology in the early twentieth century, in a city transformed by immigration from Europe and migration from the American South. Robert Park described a race relations cycle — contact, competition, accommodation, and finally assimilation — through which groups would gradually merge into a common culture. The model assumed a one-way movement: immigrants would shed old ways, adopt American norms, and eventually disappear as distinct groups.

That assumption was challenged as it became clear that assimilation worked very differently for different groups. European immigrants and their children largely assimilated into whiteness, while Black Americans, Native Americans, and later Latino and Asian immigrants faced barriers that no amount of cultural adaptation could overcome. Assimilation, scholars concluded, is not only about what newcomers do; it is also about what the host society permits, and about the racial hierarchies that decide who can become "American," "French," or "British" and on what terms.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

**Robert E. Park** (1864–1944) and **Ernest W. Burgess** (1886–1966) laid the foundations in *Introduction to the Science of Sociology* (1921), presenting assimilation as the final stage of group contact and as the process through which a common culture emerges. Their framework treated the city as a laboratory of social change and made the immigrant experience central to sociology. Its optimism — that contact would eventually produce unity — reflected the assimilationist faith of its time.

**Milton M. Gordon** (1918–2019) refined the concept in *Assimilation in American Life* (1964), distinguishing seven stages: cultural assimilation (adopting the host culture), structural assimilation (entering its institutions and social networks), marital assimilation, identificational assimilation, and others. Gordon argued that cultural assimilation could occur without structural assimilation: a group might speak English and celebrate American holidays yet remain excluded from jobs, neighbourhoods, and friendship circles. Structural assimilation, he insisted, is the decisive stage, and it is the one that has most often been blocked.

**Richard Alba** and **Victor Nee** reformulated the theory in *Remaking the American Mainstream* (2003), arguing that assimilation is not a one-way absorption but a mutual remaking: immigrants change the mainstream even as they join it. They showed that the American mainstream itself has been repeatedly redefined — by the incorporation of Catholics, Jews, and Southern Europeans — and that contemporary immigrants are reshaping it again. Their account restores agency to immigrants and treats assimilation as an open-ended process.

**Rogers Brubaker** (born 1956) questioned the concept itself in "The Return of Assimilation?" (2001), noting that scholars had abandoned assimilation in the 1970s and 1980s as ethnocentric, only to return to it when multiculturalism seemed to fail. Brubaker argued for a leaner use of the term — as a general, abstract process of becoming similar — stripped of its normative demand that minorities must conform, and distinguished from the specific policies that states call assimilation.

## Ethnographic Examples

Fieldwork complicates any simple story of assimilation. Second-generation youth often navigate between worlds: speaking the host language at school and the heritage language at home, adopting new tastes while keeping family obligations. In France, the republican model has demanded cultural assimilation — above all secularism in public schools — while North African-origin citizens report persistent exclusion from jobs and housing. In the United States, segmented assimilation theory (Portes and Zhou, 1993) describes three paths: upward assimilation into the middle class, downward assimilation into marginalized urban poverty, and selective acculturation, in which immigrants preserve community ties and bilingualism as resources for advancement. Which path a group follows depends less on culture than on the resources, networks, and reception it encounters.

## Contemporary Debates

Today, assimilation is debated against multiculturalism and integration. Critics of assimilation policies argue that demanding cultural conformity punishes difference and masks inequality; critics of multiculturalism argue that it can entrench separation. Scholars increasingly distinguish between assimilation as a descriptive process and assimilationism as a political demand, and they ask who bears its costs — above all women, whose dress and family practices are often the focus of assimilationist pressure. For anthropology, the enduring question is not whether assimilation happens but on whose terms, at what price, and with what room for people to remain themselves while becoming part of a larger whole.

## Religious Identity

Religion complicates the expectation that distinct identities fade. Will Herberg's *Protestant–Catholic–Jew* (1955) argued that in the United States the descendants of immigrants tended to lose ancestral languages while retaining or recovering a religious identification, which served as an accepted way of being American. Later research shows that congregations can offer immigrants community, practical help, and respectability while also sustaining difference. Acceptance is uneven: where a religion is treated as foreign or suspect, as many Muslims experience in Europe and North America, religious identity may become a boundary that other forms of cultural adaptation do not remove.
