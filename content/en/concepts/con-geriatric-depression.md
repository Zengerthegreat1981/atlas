# Geriatric Depression

Depression in late life is a mood disorder that affects people in late adulthood, usually after age sixty-five. It has clinical and epidemiological characteristics that differ from depression in young and middle-aged adults. It is among the most undiagnosed psychiatric disorders in this age group, because its symptoms are disguised as physical or cognitive complaints.

## Epidemiology

Clinically significant depressive symptoms are reported in roughly 8–16% of older adults living in the community (not in care institutions), whereas the proportion meeting the full diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorder is considerably lower, on the order of 1–4% (Blazer, 2003; Fiske et al., 2009). Rates rise markedly among residents of nursing homes and among people with accompanying chronic physical illnesses, such as stroke, dementia, heart disease, and cancer.

## Distinctive Clinical Features

The clinical presentation of depression in older adults differs from that in young people, which makes diagnosis more complex. Older adults tend to report physical symptoms (such as pain, fatigue, and sleep disturbance), anxiety, and health-related worries (hypochondriasis) more than they directly report a sad mood. They rarely express guilt compared with younger patients. Depression also commonly overlaps with **cognitive impairment**. Cognitive symptoms may improve when the depression is treated, a presentation formerly called "pseudodementia" (a term now discouraged, because depression with cognitive impairment is also associated with a higher later risk of dementia). This makes it difficult to distinguish it diagnostically from actual neurodegenerative diseases.

## Risk Factors and Suicide

The main risk factors include female sex, chronic physical illness, disability and pain, alcohol and substance use, medical conditions and medication effects (polypharmacy), family history, social isolation, and bereavement. In the United States, suicide mortality is highest in later life among men. Older adults make fewer attempts per death than younger people, so attempts are more often fatal. A prior statement of intent is also less consistently reported than in younger groups.

## Assessment and the vascular hypothesis

Because somatic symptoms of medical illness overlap with depressive symptoms, screening instruments written for older adults, notably the Geriatric Depression Scale (Yesavage et al., 1982–83), emphasize mood and cognition rather than appetite, sleep, and fatigue. George Alexopoulos and colleagues (1997) proposed a "vascular depression" hypothesis: late-onset depression accompanied by cerebrovascular risk and white-matter changes on imaging. The hypothesis remains a clinical research frame. It does not by itself separate late-life depression from early-onset depression that has persisted into old age, or from depression secondary to dementia. Executive dysfunction, psychomotor slowing, and a weaker response to some antidepressants have been described more often in this late-onset, vascular-risk group.

## Management

In broad terms, management of late-life depression may include antidepressant medication, evidence-based psychotherapies (such as problem-solving therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy), and, for severe or treatment-resistant cases, electroconvulsive therapy, with attention to medical comorbidity, drug interactions, and social support. Treatment decisions are individual and belong with a clinician; this entry is educational and is not clinical advice. Anyone at risk of suicide should seek urgent professional help.

## Limits

- Late or mistaken diagnosis, whether as dementia or as merely "normal aging," remains a common clinical problem that prevents timely effective treatment.
- Distinguishing precisely between depression and dementia accompanied by depressive symptoms requires longitudinal clinical follow-up in many cases, rather than a single momentary diagnosis.
