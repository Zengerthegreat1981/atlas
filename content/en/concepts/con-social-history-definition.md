# Social History: Definition and Scope

A branch of historical writing that takes as its subject **the collective life of ordinary people** (their social relations, material living conditions and daily cultural practices) as its focus. This contrasts with traditional political history, which concentrates on states, rulers, wars and official institutions.

## Definition

In its broadest form, social history is defined as the study of the collective experiences, social relations and cultural practices of past societies, as opposed to political history, which focuses on elites, state action and institutional events. This definition covers diverse topics such as the structure of the family, social class, work and occupations, daily life in the city and the countryside, customs and popular rituals, mass protest movements, and the experiences of marginalized groups, which have rarely left direct documents written in their own hands.

## Thompson's Formulation: "History from Below"

An influential formulation of this definition was made by the British historian E. P. Thompson in 1966 under the term "History from Below." It became one of the most widely used definitions of the whole field. Thompson put this method into practice in his landmark work *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963), which traced the emergence of a political class consciousness among English workers in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries by reconstructing their forgotten daily experiences from neglected documents, rather than narrating decisions made about them by politicians and elites. Thompson's approach treated ordinary people as active agents of historical change rather than assuming that change is made only from above.

## From "From Above" to "From Below": The Methodological Shift

Before this shift, writings classified as social history tended to study the social and economic structure "from above," as a background to major political events, or as aggregate statistics on classes and occupations without examining how individuals lived within them. Thompson's work, among others after the Second World War, contributed to a methodological shift toward the "from below" approach, which was regarded as a necessary correction to the dominance of traditional "from above" historical writing, which ignored the experience of the non-elite majority as unworthy of systematic historical documentation.

## Methodological Tools

Social history in this sense relies on sources and methods different from those of traditional political history. These include **oral history**, which collects the testimonies of people who lived the events directly; **quantitative history**, which uses administrative records (such as tax, birth and death registers) to statistically reconstruct patterns of collective life; and the study of **popular culture** (art, music, rituals and celebrations), treated as sources that reveal the values and worldview of ordinary people, not just elites.

## Limitation

This definition raises a fundamental methodological problem: how can the "voices" of broad social groups that left no written documents in their own hands be accessed, when most available historical sources (administrative records, newspapers, correspondence) were produced largely by educated classes or by state institutions themselves? This tension between the ambition of "writing from below" and the nature of the sources available "from above" remains a central unresolved methodological debate. It leads some historians to read official documents "against the grain" to extract unintended traces of the experiences of marginalized groups.
