# Legal Personality

**Legal personality** is the capacity that a legal system grants to an entity (whether an individual or a collective) to be **a bearer of rights and responsible for obligations**, that is, to be a "person" in the legal sense regardless of whether it is a natural human being in fact. This distinction between the "natural person" (the human being) and the "juridical person" (such as a company, association or state) is among the deepest structural abstractions in modern legal theory.

## Dhimma (Patrimony) in Islamic Jurisprudence

Islamic jurisprudence carries a parallel concept of deep roots: **dhimma**, a legal attribute that makes a person capable of bearing and discharging obligations. The concept was originally tied to the natural person, but later jurists extended practical applications resembling juridical personality to collective entities. Examples are the **waqf** (endowment), which is treated as having an independent patrimony, so that it can own, rent, sue and be sued in its own name, separate from the patrimony of its founder or administrator; and the **bayt al-mal** (public treasury), which jurists treated as a public patrimony distinct from the private property of the ruler. This provided an early jurisprudential basis for the idea of a patrimony independent of the immediate natural person. Scholars disagree about how far this amounts to juridical personality in the modern sense; some argue that classical Islamic law never developed a corporation as such, and the position of the waqf varied among schools and periods.

## Juridical Personality in Modern Arab Civil Laws

Modern Arab civil laws, influenced by the French and German traditions through the work of jurists such as Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri, have settled on granting **juridical personality** explicitly to entities such as the state, public bodies, companies, associations and endowments, specifying for them the basic elements of legal personality: an independent patrimony, capacity to litigate, a legal domicile, a name that distinguishes them, and a legal representative who expresses their will. This technical framework combines the Islamic jurisprudential heritage of the concept of *dhimma* with modern technical formulation derived from the European civil tradition.

## Competing Western Theories: Fiction versus Reality

Western legal scholars have differed about the theoretical basis of juridical personality. Proponents of the **fiction theory** (*Fiktionstheorie*), among them Friedrich Carl von Savigny, hold that the juridical person is purely a technical legal fiction that the state confers on the entity to facilitate the organization of its affairs, and has no real existence outside this technical fiction. On the other hand, proponents of the **realist theory**, among them Otto von Gierke, hold that organized human groupings (such as associations and cooperatives) possess a real, objective collective will prior to the legal recognition of them, and that law does not invent them but acknowledges a social reality that actually exists.

Other approaches include the **purpose-patrimony theory** associated with Alois Brinz, which treats the juridical person as property bound to a purpose, and Hans Kelsen's view of the juridical person as a personification of a set of legal norms. In English law, the House of Lords in *Salomon v. A. Salomon & Co. Ltd* ([1897] AC 22) confirmed that a duly incorporated company is a legal person distinct from its members.

## The State as a Legal Person: Jellinek's Contribution

Georg Jellinek, in *Allgemeine Staatslehre* (General Theory of the State, 1900), developed an influential analysis of the state as a unified **general legal person** that bears rights and obligations independent of the individuals who successively govern it. This explains why international obligations and treaties continue to bind the state despite changes of government, and this analysis deeply influenced the theory of the state in public international law and in comparative constitutional law.

## Contemporary Challenges: Beyond the Human and the Corporation

The concept of legal personality faces new questions today, with proposals to grant legal personality to unconventional entities, such as rivers (for example, New Zealand's Te Awa Tupua (Whanganui River Claims Settlement) Act 2017), and, in speculative debate, advanced artificial intelligence systems. In 2017 the Uttarakhand High Court in India declared the Ganges and Yamuna legal persons, but the Supreme Court of India stayed that decision. These developments reopen the fundamental question: is legal personality a purely technical fiction that can be extended without theoretical limit to any entity for any practical purpose, or is it tied to a minimum set of attributes (such as will or consciousness) that cannot be crossed arbitrarily?
