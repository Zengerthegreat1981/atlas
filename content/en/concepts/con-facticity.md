# Facticity: The Given Reality That Cannot Be Changed

In Sartre's *L'Être et le Néant* (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943), facticity refers to the given aspects of my situation that I did not choose and cannot simply cancel: my body, my past, the place and time of my birth, my language, my social position and the existence of other people. These facts precede my choices, and my consciousness cannot abolish them. Yet my freedom remains in **how** I take them up: how I live them, interpret them and give them meaning. For Sartre, the human being (the for-itself) is always both facticity and transcendence: it is what it has been and also what it projects beyond that.

The term has an earlier life in Heidegger, who in *Being and Time* (1927) speaks of the facticity (*Faktizität*) of Dasein: the fact that Dasein is always already delivered over to a world it did not choose. Sartre adapts this vocabulary, with more emphasis on freedom and the for-itself.

## The Philosophical Meaning

Sartre uses facticity to resist determinism without denying the weight of circumstances. Social class, for example, is part of one's facticity, but it does not settle what one makes of it. Place of birth is a given, but its meaning for a life is not fixed in advance. Freedom does not mean the power to change any fact at will; Sartre insists that freedom exists only within a situation, and that the situation is neither pure fact nor pure choice. He also holds that the meaning of a fact (for example, a disability or a social origin) is bound up with the project in which it is taken up.

## The Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

Existential therapists draw on this vocabulary loosely. Irvin Yalom speaks of the "givens of existence" (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness), and Rollo May, in *Freedom and Destiny* (1981), uses "destiny" (not in a fatalistic sense) for the limits and givens of a person's life, which freedom must work with. In this tradition, therapy does not aim to change the facts of a client's body, past or circumstances, but to help the client to change their relation to them. This is a conceptual tradition and not a standardized treatment protocol. Some writers have also taken up facticity and situation in discussions of race and colonial history (for example, in debates around Frantz Fanon's reading of Sartre), but these applications, outlined below, are contested and lie outside the core concept.

## Liberation and Postcolonial Psychology

Frantz Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952) supplies the central postcolonial test. Its fifth chapter, on the lived experience of the Black man (rendered in an early English translation as "The Fact of Blackness"), describes a body whose meaning has been fixed in advance by the white gaze and colonial history. Fanon argues that Sartre's account does not capture this: such a given is historically imposed, not simply one more feature of the human condition. Lewis Gordon's *Bad Faith and Antiblack Racism* (1995) later used Sartrean categories to analyse racism.

Liberation thought raises a related problem. Paulo Freire's *Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (English 1970) speaks of "limit-situations", which can appear as unalterable until critical reflection shows them to be historical and open to action. Ignacio Martín-Baró, a founder of liberation psychology in El Salvador, analysed Latin American fatalism as a response to oppressive social conditions and an ideology that helps sustain them, not a fixed trait of character. Reading these works through facticity is interpretive, since they do not all use the term. Their shared lesson is to ask which givens cannot be changed and which only appear so.

## Distinction from Neighboring Concepts

Facticity is closely related to Heidegger's **thrownness** (*Geworfenheit*): in Heidegger, facticity is the character of Dasein as an entity that finds itself already in a world, while thrownness names how that being-already-in-a-situation is disclosed in moods; the two are often treated as nearly equivalent. Facticity differs from **destiny** understood as an external, overpowering fate; and it differs from **determinism**, which denies freedom, whereas facticity is the set of conditions within which freedom operates.

## Facticity, Bad Faith and Authenticity

Sartre links facticity directly to **bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*) in *Being and Nothingness*: bad faith exploits the duality of facticity and transcendence, either by treating oneself as merely a thing defined by one's facts (role, class, past), or by pretending to a freedom that ignores one's facts. **Authenticity** is not worked out systematically in that book (Sartre refers to a future ethics). In later texts such as *Anti-Semite and Jew* (1946) he describes it as lucidly accepting one's situation while taking responsibility for it.

## Within Existential Therapy as a School

Facticity is one of several philosophical concepts that existential therapy (May, Yalom and others) borrowed from Sartre and Heidegger to describe the limits within which clients exercise freedom.
