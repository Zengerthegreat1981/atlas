# Body Schema: The Unconscious System of Movement and Posture

The body schema is a largely non-conscious sensorimotor representation of the body's posture and spatial configuration that guides movement and the adjustment of posture without deliberate attention. The notion goes back to the British neurologists Henry Head and Gordon Holmes, who in "Sensory disturbances from cerebral lesions" (*Brain*, 1911-12) described a "postural schema" or "body schema" that is continually updated by afferent signals and against which new postures and movements are registered. Head's later work, including *Aphasia and Kindred Disorders of Speech* (1926), extended his account of cortical organization. The Austrian psychiatrist Paul Schilder, in *The Image and Appearance of the Human Body* (1935), developed a related notion of the "body image" in a psychoanalytic and phenomenological register.

## Philosophical Meaning

Maurice Merleau-Ponty, in *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945), took up the French term *schéma corporel* (drawing on Head, Schilder and Gestalt neurology) and reinterpreted it: the body is not an object in the world among others but the medium through which we have a world at all. The body schema, for him, is not a mere summary of bodily sensations but a practical, motor "I can": a dynamic, pre-reflective way of being oriented toward tasks and surroundings. When I reach for a cup, I do not compute distances; my body is already attuned to the reachable space. His case study of the patient Schneider, who could carry out habitual movements but not abstract ones on command, illustrates how the schema supports concrete action.

## Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

Clinical and experimental work links disturbances of body representation to several conditions. In **phantom limb** experience after amputation, the person continues to feel the missing limb, and pain may accompany it; Ramachandran and colleagues' mirror-box procedure (reported in the mid-1990s) has been studied as a treatment, with mixed evidence. In **unilateral spatial neglect**, typically following damage to the right parietal region, patients fail to attend to the contralesional side of space or body. **Somatoparaphrenia** is a delusional disownership of a limb, often with neglect, and is distinct from **anosognosia** (unawareness of a deficit, as in Anton-Babinski syndrome). The **rubber hand illusion** (Botvinick and Cohen, 1998) shows that the felt boundaries of the body can be shifted experimentally by synchronous visual and tactile input. Disorders such as body dysmorphic disorder are usually described as disturbances of body image (the person's perception of and beliefs about appearance) and not as primary schema disorders. Descriptions here are conceptual and are not treatment guidance.

## Distinction from Adjacent Concepts

In the influential clarification by Shaun Gallagher (*How the Body Shapes the Mind*, 2005), the **body schema** is a system of sensorimotor capacities that functions without awareness or the need for perceptual monitoring, whereas the **body image** is a system of conscious perceptions, beliefs and attitudes about one's own body. It also differs from Merleau-Ponty's **lived body**: the schema is better understood as one aspect of the lived body (its motor-practical dimension), which also includes affect, sensation and intentionality. The neuroscientific usage treats the schema as a neural representation (involving parietal and premotor areas) that can be experimentally probed, while the philosophical usage emphasizes pre-reflective, first-person bodily experience; the two are related but not interchangeable.
