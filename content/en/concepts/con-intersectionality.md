# Intersectionality

Intersectionality is the idea that systems of inequality — race, gender, class, sexuality, disability, and others — do not operate separately but intersect, producing experiences that cannot be understood by considering any single axis alone. A Black woman's experience of discrimination, for example, is not simply the sum of racism plus sexism; it is shaped by the interaction of the two, in ways that can be invisible to movements organised around only one of them. The concept began as a tool of legal and feminist critique and has become one of the most widely used frameworks in the social sciences.

## Origins and Development

The term was coined by the legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in "Demarginalizing the Intersection of Race and Sex" (1989). Her starting point was a court case, *DeGraffenreid v. General Motors* (1976), in which Black women workers alleged discrimination in hiring. The court dismissed the claim: General Motors employed women (who were white) and Black people (who were men), so no discrimination could be shown against Black women as such. The legal framework, Crenshaw argued, could recognise race discrimination and sex discrimination, but not the intersection of the two — leaving Black women in a gap that neither anti-racist nor feminist law could see.

The insight had earlier roots. Anna Julia Cooper wrote in *A Voice from the South* (1892) of the Black woman's position at the crossroads of race and gender, and the Combahee River Collective's statement (1977) described interlocking systems of oppression decades before the term existed. bell hooks' *Ain't I a Woman* (1981) developed the same critique within feminism, arguing that a movement speaking for women in general too often spoke only for white, middle-class women. Crenshaw gave the idea a name and a precise analytical form, extending it in "Mapping the Margins" (1991) to violence against women of colour. Patricia Hill Collins then broadened it sociologically in *Black Feminist Thought* (1990), developing the concept of a "matrix of domination" in which intersecting systems of power organise social life as a whole.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns what kind of thing intersectionality is: a theory of identity, a method of analysis, or a political project. Catharine MacKinnon (2013) argued that it is best understood as a method — a way of analysing how systems of domination interact — rather than a general theory of identity, a reading that preserves its critical edge without reducing it to a catalogue of personal attributes. A related question is whether the framework should remain centred on race, gender, and class, or expand to include disability, age, religion, citizenship, and other axes. Expansion promises inclusiveness but risks diluting the concept into a list of differences with no account of how power operates among them.

A second dimension is methodological: how to study intersectionality empirically without flattening it. Quantitative researchers debate whether interaction effects in statistical models can capture what the theory describes, while qualitative researchers use life histories and case studies to show how intersections are lived. A third debate concerns its travels beyond its original context. Developed in the United States around race, the framework has been adapted to societies where the salient divisions are religion, caste, ethnicity, or sect — a productive extension, but one that raises questions about whether the concept can be detached from the specific history of Black feminism that produced it.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Intersectionality now shapes research, policy, and practice far beyond its origins. It informs public health research on how overlapping disadvantages produce health inequalities, legal scholarship on discrimination, and social work and psychotherapy, where practitioners use it to understand clients whose difficulties arise from several forms of marginalisation at once. It has also entered public and institutional language, adopted by universities, governments, and corporations — a spread that its critics view with suspicion, arguing that the concept has been domesticated into a bureaucratic vocabulary of diversity, stripped of its critique of power.

Other criticisms come from different directions. Some liberal critics argue that an emphasis on intersecting identities fragments shared political ground and weakens solidarity; others, from within feminism and critical race theory, worry that the framework can be used to rank oppressions or to treat identity as destiny. Defenders reply that the concept was never about ranking, but about seeing what single-axis thinking misses. Its enduring contribution is a simple discipline of attention: whenever an analysis, a policy, or a movement speaks of "women" or "workers" or "the oppressed" in general, intersectionality asks who is left out of that general category — and what the world looks like from their position.
