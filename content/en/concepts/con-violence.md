# Violence: Direct, Structural, and Cultural Forms

**Violence** is the intentional or structural infliction of harm on persons, groups, or their conditions of life. It takes many forms: the blow, the bullet, and the bomb; the law that denies people food, medicine, or schooling; the insult that makes a group seem less than human. Anthropology and sociology study violence not only as an individual act but as a social phenomenon — organised, legitimated, remembered, and resisted. To understand violence is to understand how power works when persuasion and consent fail, and how societies normalise harms that no single person chose.

## Definition and Development

The modern social-scientific concept of violence was transformed by Johan Galtung's essay "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research" (1969). Galtung distinguished **direct violence**, in which an identifiable actor harms another, from **structural violence**, in which harm results from the social structure itself — unequal distributions of income, education, health care, and political power that cause people to die earlier or live worse lives than they otherwise would. His central example was stark: if people starve when food is objectively available, violence has been committed even though no one struck a blow. The definition of violence as "the cause of the difference between the potential and the actual" made poverty, racism, and preventable disease visible as forms of violence rather than as mere misfortune.

In 1990 Galtung added a third term, **cultural violence**: the symbolic systems — religion, ideology, language, art, science — that legitimise direct and structural violence, making them seem natural, necessary, or deserved. The three forms reinforce one another in what Galtung called the triangle of violence: cultural violence justifies structural violence, which in turn produces direct violence, which is then justified again by culture.

## Key Contributions

Frantz Fanon, in *The Wretched of the Earth* (French original 1961), offered the most influential account of violence in anti-colonial struggle. Writing from the Algerian war of independence, Fanon argued that colonialism is itself a system of violence — the settler's violence of dispossession and humiliation — and that the violence of the colonised is a response that can restore dignity and create a new national consciousness. His argument remains controversial: critics reply that it risks romanticising violence, while defenders insist that it describes the violence already present in colonial rule.

Hannah Arendt, in *On Violence* (1970), drew a sharp distinction between power and violence. Power, for Arendt, rests on collective consent and disappears when people withdraw their support; violence is instrumental, a tool that appears when power is in jeopardy. Her distinction explains why even the most heavily armed regimes collapse when their subjects stop obeying, and why violence, however effective in the short term, cannot by itself create lasting legitimacy.

Pierre Bourdieu added the concept of **symbolic violence**: domination exercised not through physical force but through the imposition of categories, classifications, and standards that the dominated come to accept as natural. Developed with Jean-Claude Passeron in *Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture* (French original 1970; English 1977), the idea explains how schools, for example, can reproduce class inequality while appearing neutral and meritocratic: children from privileged backgrounds find their own cultural capital recognised as talent, while others are judged deficient.

## Ethnographic Perspectives

Anthropologists have documented how violence is lived and made meaningful. Nancy Scheper-Hughes and Philippe Bourgois, in their edited volume *Violence in War and Peace* (2004), gathered ethnographies showing that violence is not confined to war: it operates in homes, streets, clinics, and bureaucracies, and people develop everyday routines for surviving it. Their work introduced the idea of a continuum of violence, from the intimate violence of the household to the spectacular violence of war, with structural violence connecting them.

Ethnography also shows that the meaning of an act depends on context. A slap may be punishment, play, or insult depending on who gives it and where. Ritualised fighting, blood feuds, and duelling follow elaborate rules that limit and channel violence, while state violence — police beatings, military occupation, forced displacement — claims the monopoly of legitimate force. The anthropological contribution is to show that violence is always culturally coded: what counts as violence, who may use it, and how it is judged are social questions, not merely physical ones.

## Critique and Contemporary Questions

The broadened concept of violence has been criticised for stretching the term too far. If poverty is violence, critics ask, does the word lose its force? Galtung's defenders reply that the point of the expansion is precisely to show that preventable suffering is not natural, and that naming it violence assigns responsibility. A second debate concerns the relationship between violence and resistance: when, if ever, is violent resistance legitimate, and who decides? A third concerns gender: feminist scholars have shown that domestic and sexual violence, long treated as private matters, are among the most widespread forms of violence in the world, and that their privatisation is itself a political act.

Contemporary research examines violence in digital environments — harassment, doxxing, and algorithmic discrimination — and the violence of borders, where detention and deportation inflict harm without any blow being struck. The concept of violence remains, for the social sciences, both an analytical tool and a moral question: to call something violence is to say that it should not be accepted as the way things are.
