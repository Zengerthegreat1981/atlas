# Steady-State Economy

**The steady-state economy** is a central concept in ecological economics, developed by the economist Herman Daly in his book *Steady-State Economics* (1977). The idea has a precursor in John Stuart Mill's "stationary state". It refers to an economy that maintains a relatively constant size of physical capital and population over time, rather than continually pursuing ever-increasing material accumulation.

## A Physical Basis: Thermodynamics

The concept rests on the second law of thermodynamics (entropy). Every economic activity converts low-entropy natural resources into high-entropy waste and dissipated heat, a process that is physically irreversible. From this, Daly and Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen treat the economy as a subsystem of a larger ecological system that is finite in its sources of low-entropy matter and energy. On their view, endless growth of physical throughput runs into those limits. How far substitution and technical change can loosen the constraint is disputed in the later growth-and-environment literature; the steady-state argument does not treat the dispute as closed.

## The Stability Criterion: Balance with Carrying Capacity

The "stability" of the economy in this framework is defined by a specific criterion: the rate of consumption of natural resources and the rate of waste generation must remain within the capacity of surrounding ecological systems to regenerate and absorb them, without long-term cumulative degradation. This is a physical criterion that is relatively measurable, although its precise determination for each specific resource and ecosystem remains a subject of ongoing scientific debate. It is not a vague general slogan.

## Distinguishing Growth from Development

Daly makes a careful conceptual distinction between "growth" (a quantitative increase in the physical size of the economy) and "development" (a qualitative improvement in the quality of life, not necessarily involving material increase). He argues that a physically stable-sized economy can continue to "develop" qualitatively (better education, higher arts) without any need for continuous further material growth. This distinction seeks to avoid the charge that the concept is a call for comprehensive civilisational stagnation or for abandoning human progress in its broader sense.

## Distinction from the More Radical "Degrowth"

The concept of the steady-state economy differs from the later European degrowth movement in an important respect. Daly's goal is "stability" (reaching a sustainable level and then remaining at it) rather than a continuous reduction in the size of the economy. Daly and many degrowth writers agree that rich countries have passed the point where further growth is beneficial, but degrowth advocates emphasise deliberate contraction, while Daly emphasises a permanent balance at a sustainable scale.

## Proposed Policy Instruments

Daly proposed specific mechanisms for achieving this stability. Among them are a system of tradable resource depletion permits, which sets an upper ceiling on the consumption of a given resource and then allows its quotas to be traded among users. Others include limits on inequality through a minimum and a maximum income, and a population policy, for which he suggested transferable birth licences. He attempted to design these so that they are internally consistent with the logic of the steady-state system he advocates.

## A Lasting Influence on Later Ecological Economics

The steady-state argument was one reference point for the International Society for Ecological Economics, founded in 1989. Kate Raworth's *Doughnut Economics* (2017) later combined a similar idea of an outer ecological ceiling with an inner social foundation, a minimum of well-being that the economy should meet.
