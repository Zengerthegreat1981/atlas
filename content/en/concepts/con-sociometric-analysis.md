# Sociometric Analysis

## Definition

Sociometric analysis is the quantitative study of social relationships within a group, measuring who chooses whom — for friendship, collaboration, leadership, or rejection — and mapping the resulting pattern of ties. Its basic instrument is the sociometric test, in which members of a group name the others they would prefer as partners in a given activity. The answers are assembled into a sociogram, a diagram of the group's structure, revealing stars who attract many choices, isolates who attract none, cliques of mutual choice, and bridges between subgroups. Sociometric analysis thus makes the informal structure of a group visible: the pattern of affection, influence, and exclusion that formal organisation charts conceal.

## Origins and Development

Sociometry was founded by Jacob L. Moreno, a psychiatrist and social scientist who argued that the emotional currents between people — attractions and repulsions — constitute a real, measurable structure of society. In *Who Shall Survive?* (1934), he introduced the sociometric test and the sociogram, and proposed a science of social relations grounded in the measurement of choice. Moreno developed the method further in *Sociometry, Experimental Method and the Science of Society* (1951), extending it from small groups to communities and proposing sociometric techniques for the study of organisations and the reorganisation of institutions.

The method was taken up by group dynamics researchers and in studies of classrooms and organisations. In the later twentieth century, sociometric analysis was absorbed into the wider field of social network analysis. John Scott's *Social Network Analysis* (1991) consolidated the methods of the field, while Stanley Wasserman and Katherine Faust's *Social Network Analysis: Methods and Applications* (1994) became its standard reference, systematising measures of centrality, density, and structural equivalence. Ronald Burt's *Structural Holes* (1992) showed how a sociometric position — spanning the gap between disconnected groups — confers competitive advantage, linking the measurement of ties to the explanation of power and opportunity.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

Sociometric analysis has several dimensions. The study of choice distinguishes positive choices, such as friendship, from rejections; the study of structure distinguishes the pattern of ties from the attributes of the individuals tied. Measures of centrality identify those who are most connected, while measures of brokerage identify those who link otherwise separate parts of the network. A long-standing question concerns what sociometric data actually measure: whether a stated preference reflects a durable relationship or a passing mood, and whether the choices people report correspond to the interactions they actually have. Observational studies of interaction have been used to check and supplement reported choices.

Critics have also noted that early sociometry focused on small, bounded groups — classrooms, work teams, wards — and struggled with larger, open-ended structures. Network analysis answered this by treating ties as the fundamental unit and developing methods for large and incomplete networks. Another debate concerns explanation: a sociogram describes a structure, but explaining why that structure takes the form it does requires theories of homophily, reciprocity, status, and the institutional context in which ties form.

## Contemporary Relevance

Sociometric analysis is more relevant than ever. Its methods underpin the study of friendship and peer influence in schools, of collaboration and advice networks in organisations, and of the spread of information, behaviour, and disease through populations. Digital platforms generate sociometric data at unprecedented scale, from social media connections to email traffic, and the same measures — centrality, clustering, brokerage — are used to analyse online communities, to identify influential actors, and to understand how misinformation travels. The method's founding insight survives intact: social life is structured by relationships, and those relationships can be measured, mapped, and used to explain what groups do. It also carries a practical lesson for anyone who manages a group. A classroom, a team, or a community has an informal structure that no formal chart captures, and interventions that ignore it — seating plans, team assignments, leadership appointments — often fail for reasons that a sociogram would have made visible. Sociometric analysis remains, in this sense, both a research method and a diagnostic tool: a way of seeing the group as its members actually experience it, rather than as its official description claims it to be.

## Comparisons across Schools

Related traditions put sociometric choice to different uses. Moreno's own programme was therapeutic and reforming: choices were to be used to regroup people so that their living and working arrangements matched their preferences, an aim developed in Helen Hall Jennings's research on leadership and isolation (1943). Group dynamics researchers in the line of Kurt Lewin studied cohesion and leadership partly through experiment. Developmental psychologists classify children as, for example, popular, rejected, neglected or controversial from peer nominations, as in work by John Coie, Kenneth Dodge and Heide Coppotelli (1982). Structural network analysts shifted attention from individual preference to positions and patterns, including blockmodels that group actors with similar relations. Interpretive researchers ask what a named choice means to the person making it, since friendship or respect may be understood differently across settings. The schools thus differ over whether the sociogram is mainly a tool for intervention, a measure of individual standing or evidence of social structure.
