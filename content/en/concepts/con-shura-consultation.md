# Shura and Consultation

A Quranic and political principle that requires consultation among Muslims (or between the ruler and the ruled) in public affairs before a decision is made. It derives principally from two central Quranic verses.

## The Textual Basis

The first verse is in Surat al-Shura (42:38), which lists "their affair is consultation among themselves" among the traits of the believers, alongside prayer and spending. The second is in Surat Al 'Imran (3:159), in the passage on the Battle of Uhud: "consult them in the affair; then when you have resolved, rely upon God." The verse commands consultation. Many later jurists read "when you have resolved" as leaving the final decision with the one who consults. That reading is argued in the section below; the verse does not itself describe an institution.

## Early Historical Practice

After the Prophet's death in 632 CE, senior Companions met at the **Saqifa of Banu Sa'ida** in a contested succession assembly. Later jurists and exegetes cited that meeting as an early practical application of consultation. They likewise cited the six-man council, also called a *shura*, which the caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab named before his death in 644 CE to choose his successor. Political jurisprudence later spoke of **ahl al-hall wa-al-'aqd** (those who loosen and bind) as the body that acts for the community in choosing the ruler. Al-Mawardi, in *al-Ahkam al-sultaniyya*, sets the contract of the imamate among that body. The phrase is not Quranic, and classical writers do not describe it as an elected assembly of a modern kind.

## Divergent Interpretations

Classical and contemporary exegetes and jurists have disagreed about the nature of binding consultation. Is it **non-binding advice**, after which the final decision remains with the ruler once opinions have been heard? Many traditional jurists understand it this way, based on "then when you have decided" in the verse of Al 'Imran. Or is it a **binding mechanism** that restricts the ruler to what the consultative body agrees upon?

The disagreement acquired new political weight in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi (d. 1902), in *Taba'i' al-istibdad wa-masari' al-isti'bad* (The Nature of Despotism), published in Cairo around 1900, treated consultation as a check on absolute rule. Muhammad Rashid Rida, in *al-Khilafa aw al-imama al-'uzma* (The Caliphate or the Supreme Imamate), wrote the essays in *al-Manar* in 1922–1923 and issued them as a book from Matba'at al-Manar in 1923, before the abolition of the caliphate in March 1924. He envisaged a caliphate limited by the counsel of those qualified to bind and loose. Other writers, among them Abu al-A'la Mawdudi, held that consultation does not hand sovereignty to the people, because legislation remains subject to the Sharia. The dispute concerns the authority of the consultative body.

## Caveats

- The Quranic text does not specify a clear organizational mechanism for consultation (who participates, and how disagreement among those consulting is resolved). This has left the door open to multiple jurisprudential and political interpretations throughout history.
- The contemporary comparison between shura and modern democracy is a subject of sharp ideological dispute, not merely of neutral interpretive debate.
