# Shura and Consultation

A Quranic and political principle that requires consultation among Muslims (or between the ruler and the ruled) in public affairs before a decision is made. It derives principally from two central Quranic verses.

## The Textual Basis

The first verse is in Surat al-Shura (42:38), which describes "their affair being [decided by] consultation among themselves" as a characteristic of the believers, alongside prayer and spending. The second is in Surat Al 'Imran (3:159), a direct address to the Prophet Muhammad after the Battle of Uhud: "and consult them in the affair; then when you have decided, rely upon God." It is a divine command to the Prophet himself to consult his Companions, while the final decision remains in his hands after consultation.

## Early Historical Practice

Jurists and exegetes cited the event of **the Saqifa of Banu Sa'ida** after the Prophet's death (632 CE), when senior Companions gathered to consult on choosing a successor, as an early practical application of the principle of consultation. They likewise cited the six-man consultative council (also called *shura*) named by the caliph 'Umar ibn al-Khattab before his death in 644 CE to choose his successor. They also cited the institution of **ahl al-hall wa-al-'aqd** (those who loosen and bind), which later developed in political jurisprudence as the body that exercises consultation on behalf of the community in choosing the ruler and holding him accountable.

## Divergent Interpretations

Classical and contemporary exegetes and jurists have disagreed about the nature of binding consultation. Is it **non-binding advice**, after which the final decision remains with the ruler once opinions have been heard? Many traditional jurists understand it this way, based on "then when you have decided" in the verse of Al 'Imran. Or is it a **binding mechanism** that restricts the ruler to what the consultative body agrees upon?

This disagreement acquired renewed political importance in the twentieth century, when Islamic reformist thinkers used the principle of consultation to justify modern democratic institutions such as parliaments and elections. They treated consultation as an authentic Islamic basis for popular political participation. Other jurists argued that consultation differs fundamentally from Western democracy, because it is bound by the rulings of the Sharia and not by absolute popular sovereignty.

## Caveats

- The Quranic text does not specify a clear organizational mechanism for consultation (who participates, and how disagreement among those consulting is resolved). This has left the door open to multiple jurisprudential and political interpretations throughout history.
- The contemporary comparison between shura and modern democracy is a subject of sharp ideological dispute, not merely of neutral interpretive debate.
