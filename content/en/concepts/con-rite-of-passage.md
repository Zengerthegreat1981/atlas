# Rites of Passage

Rites of passage are rituals that mark a person's movement from one social status to another: from childhood to adulthood, from single to married, from living to dead, from outsider to member of a group. Birth, puberty, marriage, and death are biological facts, but every society surrounds them with ceremony, and the ceremonies do real social work: they announce the change, teach the new status, and make the transition publicly recognised. Rites of passage are among the most universal features of human social life, and their study has shaped anthropology's understanding of ritual itself.

## Definition

The classic model comes from Arnold van Gennep, who in *Les Rites de Passage* (1909) argued that all rites of passage share a threefold structure. First comes separation: the person is removed from their old status, often physically — taken from the village, stripped of ordinary clothing, forbidden ordinary food. Then comes the liminal or transitional phase, in which the person is between statuses, neither what they were nor what they will become. Finally comes incorporation: the person is reintegrated into society in their new status, with new rights, obligations, and marks of identity. The model applies far beyond the classic life-cycle events. It can describe joining a profession, entering a religious order, taking citizenship, or even the rituals of a modern university graduation. What matters is not the biological event but the social passage: the movement across a boundary between recognised statuses. Rites of passage are often secret or restricted: initiations may be conducted away from the uninitiated, and the knowledge imparted may be forbidden to outsiders. Secrecy is part of the rite's power, marking the boundary between those who have passed and those who have not.

## Origins and Development

Van Gennep's book was published in French in 1909 and was little read outside France for decades; the English translation did not appear until 1960. Its influence came largely through Victor Turner, who took up the liminal phase — the middle, in-between stage — and made it the centre of a theory of ritual and society. In *The Forest of Symbols* (1967), based on fieldwork among the Ndembu of Zambia, Turner described how initiands in the liminal phase are stripped of rank, property, and ordinary identity, and experience a form of egalitarian fellowship he called communitas. In *The Ritual Process* (1969), he argued that this experience of anti-structure — the temporary suspension of hierarchy — is a permanent feature of social life, and that societies need periodic liminal moments to renew themselves. Later scholars extended the model in new directions. Catherine Bell, in *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (1992), argued that ritual is not a separate category of action but a way of acting — ritualisation — that produces power relations and embodied dispositions. Ronald Grimes, in *Deeply into the Bone* (2000), studied rites of passage in contemporary North America, showing how people improvise new rituals — for divorce, for coming out, for retirement — when traditional ones are absent or inadequate.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns universality. Van Gennep claimed a universal structure, but critics ask whether the threefold pattern is genuinely found everywhere or whether it is imposed by the analyst. Some societies mark transitions without elaborate ritual; others mark them with rituals that do not fit the three phases neatly. A second debate concerns gender. Initiation rites for boys and girls often differ sharply: male initiation is frequently public, painful, and collective, while female initiation may be private, domestic, and tied to menarche or marriage. Feminist anthropologists have argued that rites of passage do not merely mark a change of status but produce gendered persons, teaching boys and girls different forms of embodiment, sexuality, and authority. A third debate concerns the relationship between ritual and social change. Do rites of passage conserve the social order, by inducting each generation into existing roles, or can they be sites of resistance, where initiands and ritual specialists challenge the established order? Turner argued for the latter possibility: the liminal phase, in which ordinary rules are suspended, can generate new symbols and new social forms.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Rites of passage have not disappeared in modern societies; they have changed form. Graduation ceremonies, military basic training, fraternity initiations, citizenship ceremonies, and even the rituals of the workplace — the first day on the job, the retirement party — all follow the logic of separation, transition, and incorporation. At the same time, many people experience the absence of rites: adolescents in secular societies may pass into adulthood with no ceremony to mark it, and migrants may find that the rites of their home community have no meaning in their new country. Rites of passage also remain politically charged: debates about female genital cutting, framed by its practitioners as initiation and by its critics as a human rights violation, show how a rite of passage can become the focus of global moral argument. The enduring value of the concept is that it links the most intimate moments of a life — birth, puberty, marriage, death — to the largest structures of society, showing that becoming a person is never a private matter but a social achievement, accomplished through ritual.
