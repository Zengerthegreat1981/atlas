# Professional Conflicts of Interest

## Meaning and Definition

A professional conflict of interest is a situation in which a person who holds a position or practices a profession that requires them to make judgments on behalf of another party (a client, a patient, an institution or the public) faces two conflicting considerations: their own interest (financial, familial or any other private interest) on one side, and the requirements of professional integrity and the duty of loyalty to the person they serve on the other. As a result, professional judgment may be swayed in favor of the private interest. What matters here is that the existence of a conflict does not require that corruption actually occur. The mere possibility of influence is enough to make the situation an ethical problem that calls for disclosure or recusal.

## Historical Context

Systematic attention to conflicts of interest developed alongside modern professional ethics in medicine, law and public administration during the twentieth century. Professions became more complex and more intertwined with major economic interests, such as pharmaceutical companies and the financial sector. The concept gained particular momentum after major financial and medical scandals, such as the Enron collapse of 2001, which showed how auditors could lose their independence when their income depended on the company they were supposed to oversee. This prompted strict legislation, such as the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 in the United States.

## Main Positions and Approaches

The professional literature distinguishes three types of conflict. An *actual* conflict affects the decision in fact. An *apparent* conflict appears to an outside observer to be capable of influencing the decision, although it is not necessarily so. A *potential* conflict may arise in the future.

Many codes of professional conduct, from medicine to law to journalism, agree that the appropriate remedy proceeds through three graduated steps. First, full disclosure of the relationship that may cause the conflict. Second, recusal from making the decision if the conflict is substantial. Third, prohibiting the relationship altogether if the severity of the conflict makes disclosure and recusal insufficient, as in the case of judges being barred from hearing cases involving their relatives.

## Objections and Challenges

Some researchers, such as Daylian Cain, George Loewenstein and Don Moore ("The Dirt on Coming Clean," 2005), found in experiments that disclosing a conflict of interest may have perverse effects. After disclosure, advisers may feel morally licensed to give more biased advice, while recipients often do not discount the advice enough. It is also practically difficult to draw the line between a conflict that requires recusal and one that can be managed by disclosure alone.

## Illustrative Example

A physician who receives gifts or financial support from a particular pharmaceutical company and then prescribes its drugs to patients, even when the drug is genuinely effective, falls into a conflict of interest. That physician must disclose the relationship to their patients and to the regulatory authorities.
