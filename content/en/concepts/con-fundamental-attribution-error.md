# Fundamental Attribution Error (FAE)

A social-psychological bias formulated by the psychologist **Lee Ross in 1977** ("The Intuitive Psychologist and His Shortcomings"), in which people tend to attribute the behaviors and mistakes of others to flaws in their personalities and innate dispositions ("he is reckless or foolish"), while attributing their own mistakes and similar behaviors to the pressure of circumstances and overwhelming environmental situations ("I was in a hurry").

## Experimental roots

The concept builds on **Fritz Heider's** attribution theory (*The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations*, 1958), which drew on his work with Marianne Simmel (1944) showing that people readily ascribe intentions to the movements of simple geometric shapes, and on the study by **Edward Jones and Victor Harris in 1967**, which found that evaluators still inferred some real attitude from essays that they knew had been written under assigned, constrained conditions.

## The cultural difference between East and West

Research indicates that the attribution error is more common in individualistic Western cultures than in collectivistic Eastern cultures, which give greater consideration to the environmental context when explaining the behavior of others.

## Its relation to self-serving bias

The concept is related to the actor-observer asymmetry (Jones & Nisbett, 1972), the tendency to explain others' behavior by dispositions and one's own by circumstances, and is distinct from self-serving bias, the tendency to credit oneself for successes and blame circumstances for failures.

## Ross's study in the quiz game

In the classic quiz-game study by Ross, Amabile and Steinmetz (1977) at Stanford University, participants were randomly divided into "questioners" and "contestants" in a question-and-answer game resembling television quiz shows, in which the "questioner" chooses difficult questions from his own knowledge. Although observers and contestants knew that the roles had been assigned at random and gave the "questioner" a clear structural advantage, they still rated the "questioner" as more knowledgeable than the "contestant", underweighting the effect of the role in favor of an explanation based on personal traits.

## Limits of the concept and the name

Some researchers have criticized the label "fundamental error", because the tendency is not universal or inevitable so much as a strong probabilistic bias influenced by culture, by the nature of the situation, and by how much the judge knows about the actor. The effect is reported to weaken when the judge knows the actor well or is asked to consider the actor's circumstances before judging.
