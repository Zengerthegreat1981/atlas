# The General Theory of Contract in Islamic Law (Nazariyyat al-'Aqd)

The **general theory of contract** is the general jurisprudential framework within which Islamic law formulates the common rules governing every contractual act, whether sale, lease, agency or other, rather than treating each named contract in isolation. The theory gradually took shape in jurisprudential and usul works, through the extraction of general pillars and conditions that serve as an analytical umbrella for all named contracts. Its theorization as a "general theory" in the modern sense is a development partly linked to the exchange between Islamic jurisprudence and modern civil law in the twentieth century, in works such as al-Sanhuri's *Masadir al-Haqq fi al-Fiqh al-Islami* and Mustafa al-Zarqa's *al-Madkhal al-Fiqhi al-'Amm*.

## The pillars of the contract

Jurists generally agree that a contract cannot exist without certain elements, though they differ on which are pillars (*arkan*) and which are conditions. The Hanafis count only the **form** (offer and acceptance) as the pillar, treating the parties and the subject matter as conditions, while the majority count three pillars: the **form** (offer and acceptance), which is the expression of two matching wills to create the obligation; **the two contracting parties**, who must have legal capacity to contract; and **the subject matter** (the object of the contract), the thing or benefit to which it is directed, which must exist or be capable of delivery, must be lawful, and must be known in a way that prevents excessive uncertainty.

## Conditions of validity and defects of consent

Validity requires conditions beyond mere formation. The most prominent are freedom from excessive **gharar** (uncertainty about the subject matter or its existence), freedom from **riba** (usury), and the soundness of consent from **compulsion** (ikrah) that overcomes the will of the contracting party and corrupts it. Hanafi jurisprudence distinguishes precisely between the **void contract** (defective in its pillar itself, so that its effect never arises at all) and the **invalid (fasid) contract** (which satisfies its pillars but is defective in an external attribute: it forms from the outset but produces an incomplete effect that must be removed). This distinction is particular to the Hanafi school; the other three Sunni schools generally treat invalid and void contracts alike, with some exceptions in particular matters.

The schools also differ on how far parties may add their own stipulations to a contract: Hanbali jurists, and Ibn Taymiyya in particular, allowed more freedom to stipulate conditions than most other schools, which restricted it more.

## Formation, validity, enforceability and binding force

Hanafi writers arrange the contract in four ascending stages. **Formation** (*in'iqad*) requires matching offer and acceptance in one session, a party with at least discernment, and a lawful, existing subject matter. **Validity** (*sihha*) requires freedom from vitiating defects such as serious uncertainty, riba, certain extraneous stipulations and, for most Hanafis, duress. **Enforceability** (*nafadh*) requires ownership or authority and full enough capacity; without it the contract is suspended (*mawquf*) until ratified. **Binding force** (*luzum*) requires that neither party hold an option to rescind.

The other schools draw these lines differently. The **Malikis**, like the Hanafis, generally allow an unauthorized sale of another's property to await the owner's ratification, whereas the dominant **Shafi'i** and **Hanbali** positions treat it as void. The **Shafi'is** emphasize verbal offer and acceptance and were reluctant to accept sale by mere exchange (*mu'ata*), which the other three broadly admit; they also generally deny effect to a sale concluded by a minor. On binding force, the Shafi'is and Hanbalis affirm an option while the parties remain in the contractual session (*khiyar al-majlis*), while the Hanafis and Malikis hold that completed offer and acceptance bind without it.

## Named contracts as applications of the general theory

Named contracts in jurisprudence, such as sale, lease, agency, partnership and pledge, are treated as particular applications of these general rules. To each is added specific provisions according to its nature and subject matter, such as the provisions on options (khiyar) in sale, or the provisions on the termination of agency upon the death of the principal.

## Contemporary importance in Islamic finance

The theory of contract has acquired great contemporary importance in engineering the contracts of **Islamic finance**. Classical named contracts, such as murabaha, ijara and mudaraba, are redeployed under the same jurisprudential validity conditions to build contemporary financial instruments. This has made familiarity with the pillars, conditions and invalidating factors of the contract a cornerstone of modern Islamic banking fatwa practice.
