# Abortion Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Abortion ethics is the philosophical inquiry into the legitimacy of ending a pregnancy. It revolves around two intertwined questions: when does the fetus acquire moral status (personhood) that requires its protection, and does this status restrict the woman's right to decide about her own body? In Islamic jurisprudence, the jurists did not agree on a single position. Many Hanafi jurists, and a number of Shafi'i jurists, permitted abortion before the ensoulment (*nafkh al-ruh*), which most of them set at one hundred and twenty days, in some cases only for a valid excuse. They based this on the view that before that point the fetus has not yet received the soul. The dominant Maliki position, by contrast, was stricter, prohibiting abortion even in the early stages, and the Hanbali and Shafi'i schools also contain a range of views, from permission to disapproval. After ensoulment, the jurists almost unanimously agreed on its prohibition except in cases of extreme necessity, such as a certain threat to the life of the mother, applying the maxim "harm is to be removed" and giving precedence to averting the greater harm.

## Historical Context

In contemporary Western philosophy, the question came to the fore strongly around the 1973 ruling of the United States Supreme Court in *Roe v. Wade* (overruled in 2022 by *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*). In her famous essay "A Defense of Abortion" (1971), Judith Jarvis Thomson put forward the argument of the "violinist," to show that even if the fetus's full personhood is granted, this does not oblige the woman to donate her body to keep it alive. She distinguished between the fetus's right to life and its right to use the body of another. On the other side, conservatives such as Don Marquis hold that abortion is wrong because it deprives the fetus of "a future like ours," regardless of the degree of its neurological development. Gradualist positions sit between the two sides, linking moral status to the development of the nervous system, the fetus's capacity to feel pain, and its viability outside the womb.

## Applications

These debates appear in practice in legislation on therapeutic abortion (in cases of a health risk or a congenital malformation), and in the controversy over sex-selective abortion, which both jurisprudence and modern law reject as discrimination. A frequently discussed case is the discovery of a lethal congenital malformation during pregnancy, in which legal and religious bodies in some jurisdictions weigh the continuation of the pregnancy against the mother's health, and some jurists reason by the principle of the lesser harm.

## Divergence of Schools on the Timing of Ensoulment

The jurists' disagreement over when ensoulment occurs is not a marginal detail; it is the axis of the entire ruling. Jurists who permitted early abortion relied on the hadith of Ibn Mas'ud, which describes three forty-day stages (drop, clot, and lump of flesh) before the soul is breathed in. Some contemporary scholars, by contrast, relying on the progress of embryology, which shows the early onset of neural activity, argue that the ruling should be reconsidered in light of new medical findings. This debate reflects the dynamism of legal reasoning (*ijtihad*) in facing modern empirical science without abandoning fixed legal principles.

## Primary Sources of the Juristic Debate

The classical positions can be traced in the legal manuals of the schools, usually in chapters on marriage, birth control, and the compensation (*ghurra*) owed for causing a miscarriage. On the Hanafi side, Ibn al-Humam's *Fath al-Qadir* and Ibn 'Abidin's *Radd al-Muhtar* report permission before ensoulment, together with the view of some Hanafis that it requires an excuse. For the Malikis, al-Dardir's *al-Sharh al-Kabir*, with al-Dasuqi's gloss, forbids expelling what has settled in the womb even before forty days. Among Shafi'i authors, al-Ghazali, in the book on marriage in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, distinguishes withdrawal (*'azl*) from abortion: the latter is an offence against something already existing, and its gravity increases as development advances. Al-Ramli's *Nihayat al-Muhtaj* records the later Shafi'i discussion, and Ibn Qudama's *al-Mughni* treats the Hanbali rules on compensation and expiation. Basim Musallam's *Sex and Society in Islam* (1983) is a useful modern guide to this material.
