# Matter Out of Place

A concept formulated by Mary Douglas in *Purity and Danger* (1966) to explain the cultural meaning of "dirt" and taboos.

## Central Idea

Douglas argues that "dirt" is not an intrinsic property inherent in a thing in itself, but the result of violating a symbolic classification system that a particular culture imposes on the world in order to organise its experience. A shoe is not dirty in itself, but becomes so when placed on the dining table, because the cultural classification system assigns the table to food and not to shoes. In this sense, dirt is "matter out of place" within a classificatory system, not an objective property independent of it.

## Application to Food Taboos

Douglas applied this framework to the analysis of food taboos in the Biblical Book of Leviticus (such as the prohibition of pork and certain sea creatures). She proposed that the forbidden animals are often those that do not clearly fit the expected natural classification categories of the cultural system concerned (for example, a land animal that does not chew the cud although it has cloven hooves). Such animals become classificatorily "anomalous" and therefore symbolically dangerous, requiring prohibition.

## Impact

This concept became an influential analytical tool in the anthropology of religion and in studies of symbolic pollution and taboos across cultures, and its influence extended to the sociology of the body and the sociology of deviance (how certain social groups are classified as "dangerous" because they do not fit the dominant categories of social classification).

## The Islamic Heritage Connection

Islamic jurisprudence offers a parallel classificatory system for organising impurity and purity (in the chapters on purity, *ṭahāra*, of the classical manuals of the Sunni and other legal schools), in which specific substances (such as pork, flowing blood and certain bodily excretions) are classified as ritually impure, and must be avoided or whatever they touch must be purified. This forms part of a comprehensive system that links bodily cleanliness with the spiritual state of the worshipper (ablution being a condition for the validity of prayer). Researchers in the anthropology of religion have used Douglas's framework to analyse these legal classificatory systems as mechanisms for regulating the boundaries between the pure and the impure, socially and symbolically, rather than as purely hygienic rules.

## Criticism and Development

Some later researchers criticised Douglas's interpretation as excessively formalist, one that sometimes neglects the specific historical context of each prohibition (why pork in particular and not another animal?). They preferred explanations that combine the classificatory logic Douglas proposed with the historical, economic and environmental factors specific to each case, as Marvin Harris later did in his cultural-materialist explanation of the prohibition of pork among Jews and Muslims.

## Her Own Later Development

Douglas further developed this framework in her other works. For example, in *Risk and Culture* (1982, with Aaron Wildavsky), she studied modern societies' perceptions of environmental risks, showing that these perceptions follow a cultural classificatory logic similar to the logic of traditional pollution, rather than a neutral scientific assessment of probabilities alone.
