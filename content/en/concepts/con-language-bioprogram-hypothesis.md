# Language Bioprogram Hypothesis

A hypothesis formulated by Derek Bickerton (1981, 1984) to explain a striking phenomenon: creole languages around the world, although they arose from source languages that are entirely different and on distant continents, resemble one another in a set of basic grammatical features, such as the system of verb inflection for tense, aspect and mood, and basic word order.

## The Proposed Mechanism

Bickerton assumes that children who acquire a pidgin (a linguistic system poor in grammatical input) as a first language do not merely transmit it as it is. Instead they "fill in" its grammatical gaps automatically with a hypothetical innate grammatical program (a *bioprogram*) that is biologically shared by all humans. According to him, this explains the cross-linguistic grammatical resemblance among creole languages despite the radically different source languages.

## The Main Criticism

The hypothesis has faced wide methodological criticism, most prominently from Salikoko Mufwene, who questioned the accuracy of the generalization itself (do creole languages actually resemble one another to the extent claimed?) and proposed alternative explanations based on the languages in contact (both the lexifier and the substrate languages of the original speakers) and on the gradual, population-level development of creoles, rather than on a single universal bioprogram.

## Theoretical Importance

Despite the debate surrounding it, the hypothesis has been cited as field-based evidence for an innate grammatical capacity in humans, alongside similar evidence from studies of the emergence of new sign languages (such as Nicaraguan Sign Language).

## Bickerton's Original Field Evidence

In formulating his hypothesis, Bickerton drew primarily on his field study of Hawaiian Creole English. He observed that the first generation of immigrant laborers (from China, Japan, the Philippines and elsewhere) developed a pidgin with a grammatically impoverished structure for common communication on sugar plantations, while their children, the generation that acquired this pidgin as a first language in childhood, developed a creole with a fully developed grammatical structure within just one generation, even though their parents had not provided them with a rich grammatical model. Bickerton regarded the speed of this transition (one generation, not centuries) as decisive evidence for the intervention of an innate capacity, not a purely environmental social explanation. Later historical work on Hawaiian Creole English has questioned how abrupt this development actually was.

## The Hypothesis in the Debate over Universal Grammar

The hypothesis is linked to the "poverty of the stimulus" argument developed by Chomsky to support the idea of an innate universal grammar. The creole case provides a "natural" field test (not easily repeatable under laboratory conditions) of the idea that children impose a rich grammatical structure even when the linguistic input available to them is very poor. This led many linguists to regard creole cases and recently emerged sign languages (such as Nicaraguan Sign Language) as "natural laboratories" for studying the emergence of language and innate grammar.
