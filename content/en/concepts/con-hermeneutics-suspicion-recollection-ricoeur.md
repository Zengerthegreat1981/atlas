# Hermeneutics of Suspicion vs. Hermeneutics of Recollection (Ricœur)

A methodological distinction formulated by Paul Ricoeur to describe two opposing paths that can be applied to interpreting the symbol and the religious text. It became a central analytical tool in contemporary religious hermeneutics.

## The Hermeneutics of Suspicion

Ricoeur calls Freud, Marx and Nietzsche together "the three masters of suspicion" (les trois maîtres du soupçon): three thinkers who, despite radically different projects, agreed in treating the apparent consciousness (including religious consciousness) as a **mask** concealing a deeper truth (repressed desire in Freud, class relations of production in Marx, the will to power in Nietzsche). Applying this method to the religious symbol means reading it as a concealment of a psychological, economic or power-related interest that the surface of the text does not disclose.

## The Hermeneutics of Recollection (Recovery)

In contrast, Ricoeur describes another path, inspired by religious phenomenology (Otto, Eliade) and the comparative study of religion, which listens to the religious symbol as carrying a **true meaning** that deserves to be rediscovered and recovered, not merely exposed and stripped of its mask. His central thesis is: "the symbol gives rise to thought" (*Le symbole donne à penser*). That is, the religious symbol is a source of meaning, not merely a surface to be reduced to what lies beneath it.

## Second Naïveté: Going Beyond the Dichotomy, Not Choosing One Side

Ricoeur refuses to treat the two paths as mutually exclusive, where one is chosen against the other. His thesis is that mature interpretation passes **through** suspicion (it does not ignore it with a direct naive faith) to arrive at a "second naïveté": a reflective faith that comes after criticism rather than before it, absorbing the lessons of suspicion without surrendering to their complete reduction of the religious symbol. This places him in a unique conciliatory position within the debate on reduction in the study of religion.

## Application to the Religious Symbol: The Example of Sin

Ricoeur's earlier work *The Symbolism of Evil* (1960), where the notion of a second naïveté first appears, anticipates this framework in its treatment of the symbols of fault and the Adamic myth. Instead of reading the story of the Fall (Adam and Eve) literally and historically (a first naïveté), or stripping it of its mask as merely a psychological projection of the feeling of guilt (a reductive suspicion), Ricoeur proposes a third reading. It passes through suspicion in order to recover from the symbol an existential meaning about the nature of human evil that cannot be reduced to a purely psychological or social explanation, affirming that the religious symbol carries a surplus of meaning that exceeds any single reductive explanation.

## The Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

This distinction finds a loose methodological echo in Islamic interpretive traditions. Alongside "exegesis" (tafsir), which explains the apparent meaning of the text, Sufis such as Ibn Arabi and esoteric (Batini) exegetes developed forms of "interpretation" (ta'wil) that seek a deeper meaning behind the apparent sense. This roughly parallels Ricoeur's "hermeneutics of recollection," although the doctrinal framework differs radically. On the other side, modern rationalist critics (such as Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd in his critical reading of religious discourse) developed a method closer to Ricoeur's "hermeneutics of suspicion," by questioning the historical and ideological circumstances that produced particular juristic interpretations, without thereby necessarily denying the sanctity of the text itself.
