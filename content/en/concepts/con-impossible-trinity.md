# Impossible Trinity (Trilemma)

The **impossible trinity**, also known as the **trilemma**, is a principle of international economics. It states that a country cannot simultaneously achieve three desirable goals: independence of monetary policy, a stable exchange rate, and freedom of capital movement across borders. Any two of them can be achieved, but only at the cost of giving up the third.

## Mundell's Formulation

The principle derives from the Mundell–Fleming model developed by **Robert Mundell** in his paper on capital mobility and stabilisation policy (1963) and by Marcus Fleming (1962). The trilemma as a three-cornered formulation, and the label "impossible trinity" (often associated with Paul Krugman), became standard in later decades. Mundell showed that when a central bank attempts to fix the exchange rate while keeping its borders open to capital, it loses the ability to set the interest rate for domestic objectives. If it raises the interest rate to curb inflation, foreign money flows in seeking higher returns, raising demand for the domestic currency and threatening the peg itself. If it lowers the rate to stimulate growth, capital leaves in search of better returns abroad and presses the currency downward.

## The Three Choices in Practice

In practice each country chooses one corner of the triangle to sacrifice. China has, with a managed exchange rate, retained a measure of monetary independence by maintaining substantial restrictions on capital movement. Euro-area members keep free capital movement and irrevocably fixed exchange rates among themselves, and give up an independent national monetary policy. The euro itself floats against other currencies. The United States and the United Kingdom have chosen freedom of capital and monetary independence, at the cost of a fully floating exchange rate.

## The Gulf States as a Clear Example

The currency-peg regimes of the Gulf Cooperation Council states, such as the Saudi riyal and the UAE dirham pegged to the dollar, provide a very clear teaching example. By choosing a stable exchange rate together with freedom of capital movement (to attract foreign investment), these states effectively give up the independence of their monetary policy. Their central banks follow the decisions of the US Federal Reserve on raising and lowering rates in an almost automatic manner, regardless of local economic conditions.

## Significance and Limits

The principle explains why quasi-fixed pegs have repeatedly collapsed when they tried to combine all three goals, as in the European exchange rate mechanism crisis of 1992 and the Asian financial crisis of 1997. It also explains why the Bretton Woods system had to dissolve after the Nixon shock (1971), when it became impossible to maintain fixed exchange rates as international capital movements grew. Some economists nonetheless regard the trilemma as an oversimplification. Experience shows intermediate degrees of partial capital controls and managed flexibility that allow more room than the sharp model assumes, and how far such partial control can work remains an empirical debate.
