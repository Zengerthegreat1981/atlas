# Exogamy

Exogamy is the rule or practice requiring a person to marry outside their own group — whether that group is a family, clan, lineage, caste, or religious community. Its counterpart is endogamy, marriage within the group. Exogamy is one of the oldest and most widespread rules in the ethnographic record, and it has been central to kinship theory since the nineteenth century: it is the mechanism by which separate groups are linked into wider networks of alliance, and the rule that prevents marriage between close kin. In most societies the incest taboo and the rule of exogamy overlap, but they are not identical: the incest taboo forbids certain marriages, while exogamy positively requires marriage outside a defined group.

## Origins and Development

The term "exogamy" was introduced by the Scottish scholar John F. McLennan in *Primitive Marriage* (1865). Twentieth-century ethnographies of African kinship later examined the rule in detail. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) and later *Kinship and Marriage among the Nuer* (1951) described how rules of clan and lineage exogamy forbade marriage between recognised kin and so linked lineages across Nuerland through marriage and cattle payments. Meyer Fortes's *The Web of Kinship Among the Tallensi* (1949) showed a similar pattern in northern Ghana, where clan exogamy meant that marriages created ties of obligation between otherwise separate patrilineal groups.

Claude Lévi-Strauss transformed the subject in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949). For Lévi-Strauss, exogamy was not merely a negative prohibition but the positive foundation of society itself: groups exchange women (or men) as they exchange goods, creating bonds of reciprocity. He distinguished restricted exchange, in which two groups exchange spouses directly with each other, from generalised exchange, in which several groups participate in a cycle — group A takes wives from B, B from C, and C from A. On this view, the incest taboo is the rule that forces exchange to happen, and therefore the rule that makes culture possible. Rodney Needham's edited volume *Rethinking Kinship and Marriage* (1971) carried the debate forward by critically re-examining the categories of kinship and marriage theory.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

Exogamy takes several forms, and their differences matter. Clan exogamy, common in Africa and the Pacific, forbids marriage within the clan. Lineage exogamy is narrower, forbidding marriage only within the lineage. Family exogamy, which forbids marriage within the nuclear or extended family, is the narrowest form and underlies the marriage rules of most modern societies. The scope of the prohibited group varies enormously, and with it the social consequences: the wider the exogamous unit, the wider the network of alliance a marriage creates.

The second debate concerns the functions of exogamy. Anthropologists generally agree that it serves several purposes at once: it creates alliances between groups, reduces inbreeding, and extends each person's network of kin. But which of these is primary has been contested. Functionalists emphasised alliance and social cohesion; later scholars stressed the genetic argument; and Lévi-Strauss emphasised exchange itself. The most persuasive accounts treat these functions as interlocking rather than competing.

The third debate is feminist. Gayle Rubin's classic essay "The Traffic in Women" (1975) argued that exogamy, as Lévi-Strauss described it, is a system in which women are exchanged by men — a "traffic" that constitutes women as objects of exchange and underwrites male dominance. Rubin did not reject Lévi-Strauss's analysis but radicalised it, showing that the exchange of women is also the production of gender hierarchy. Later feminist anthropologists have complicated this picture, documenting cases in which women are active agents in arranging marriages and in which the movement of men, rather than women, is the norm.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

In modern societies the boundaries of exogamy have blurred. People increasingly marry across lines of religion, ethnicity, and class, and the rules that once governed marriage have weakened — though they have not disappeared. In India, caste endogamy remains strong even as inter-caste marriage grows; in many diaspora communities, the question of whom one may marry remains a flashpoint between generations. Exogamy also remains relevant to law: most legal systems retain prohibitions on marriage between close relatives, and debates about cousin marriage in Britain, the United States, and elsewhere replay, in legal language, the anthropological arguments about inbreeding and alliance.

Critics of the classical literature note that it often treated exogamy as a rule that societies "have," underplaying the negotiation, evasion, and conflict that surround real marriages. The strongest recent work studies exogamy as a contested field — a set of expectations that people invoke, bend, or defy — rather than as a mechanical system. On this view, the rule of marrying out remains one of the clearest windows onto how societies draw their boundaries and build their alliances.
