# The Monomyth / Hero's Journey (Joseph Campbell)

The central concept of Joseph Campbell's book *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949): the hypothesis that a single narrative structure, which he called the "monomyth" (a term he took from James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*), recurs across the myths of the world, historically and culturally distant from one another, embodied in the pattern of the "Hero's Journey".

## The Three Major Stages

Campbell divided the typical journey into three main stages, each subdivided into further stages:

1. **Departure**: the hero receives a "Call to Adventure", and often hesitates or initially refuses it ("Refusal of the Call"), before crossing the "Threshold" into another world with the help of a "guardian" or supernatural "mentor".
2. **Initiation**: the hero faces a series of trials ("the road of trials"), may meet a "goddess" or be reconciled with his father, and reaches the climax (the "Ultimate Boon") — often a revelation or a prize of value to the whole community.
3. **Return**: the hero returns, sometimes with rejection or pursuit, bearing a "boon" that benefits his society (Campbell's "Master of Two Worlds" and "Freedom to Live"), thus completing the cycle. Campbell himself glosses this boon as an "elixir", a label that later adaptations popularised.

Campbell modelled the three-part structure on the anthropological notion of rites of passage (separation, initiation, return, as in Arnold van Gennep's *Les rites de passage*, 1909). The middle stage thus resembles what Victor Turner later called the liminal phase, though Campbell did not develop the comparison himself.

## Examples Campbell Cited

Campbell applied this pattern to the stories of the Buddha, Moses, Christ, Prometheus and Aeneas, and to figures from Native American, African and Asian myths, concluding that the differences in cultural detail conceal a shared psychological-narrative structure, which he explained through a mixture of Jungian archetypes and anthropological rites of passage.

## Impact Beyond the Academy

The influence of this concept extended beyond the academic field into popular storytelling worldwide: George Lucas explicitly acknowledged his direct debt to Campbell's model in writing *Star Wars* (1977), and Christopher Vogler's memo and later book *The Writer's Journey* (1992) turned the pattern into a standard screenwriting guide in Hollywood.

## Methodological Limitation

This concept shares the general methodological limitation of universalising comparative approaches (Frazer, Jung, Campbell): the extraction of narrative elements from highly contrasting cultural and historical contexts in order to fit them into a single presumed universal pattern. Robert Segal and other scholars of myth have criticised its selective use of evidence, its dependence on Jungian psychology and its neglect of the particular functions of myths in their own societies (see `dbt-segal-critique-campbell-monomyth-methodology`).

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Arabic popular epics such as the *Sirat Antar ibn Shaddad* (the epic of Antara) and the *Sirat al-Zahir Baybars* include episodes of exile, trial and adventure, and a loose comparison with the pattern is possible. Such comparisons should be treated cautiously, since these narratives have their own cultural logic (such as the centrality of tribal honour and chivalry and their tradition of oral performance) and should not be reduced to a presumed universal Jungian scheme.
