# Attention

The central cognitive process that enables the human being to focus selectively on specific stimuli in the environment or on internal thoughts while excluding and filtering out other distracting stimuli, and to direct limited perceptual resources toward information processing and decision-making.

## Distinction and Theoretical Definition

William James defined attention in his book *The Principles of Psychology* (1890) as 'the taking possession by the mind, in clear and vivid form, of one out of what seem several simultaneously possible objects or trains of thought,' stressing that the essence of attention is 'withdrawal from some things in order to deal effectively with others.' Contemporary cognitive psychology distinguishes several dimensions of attention:
- **Selective Attention**: The ability to choose a specific sensory input and ignore distractions (Broadbent's filter model and Treisman's attenuation model).
- **Sustained Attention / Vigilance**: The ability to maintain focus and continuous performance over an extended period of time.
- **Divided Attention**: Distributing limited cognitive resources across more than one task at the same time (multitasking).
- **Executive Attention**: Regulating and controlling cognitive conflicts and inhibiting inappropriate automatic responses.

## Cognitive and Neural Structure and Mechanisms

The mechanisms of attention depend on complex brain neural networks that include the prefrontal cortex, the parietal cortex, and the thalamus; "bottom-up" processes (Bottom-up, driven by the salience of the external stimulus) interact with "top-down" processes (Top-down, driven by internal goals and expectations).

## Clinical and Applied Significance

Inattention is a core symptom domain of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and attentional biases are described in anxiety and depressive disorders (where attention is directed selectively toward threats). Attention training and conscious attention also form a pillar of mindfulness techniques (Mindfulness) and of mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT).

## Attention Networks

Posner and colleagues (for example Posner & Petersen, 1990) proposed that attention involves three partly distinct networks: alerting (achieving and maintaining readiness), orienting (selecting information from sensory input), and executive control (resolving conflict among responses).

## Difference from Neighboring Concepts

Attention differs from **consciousness** (Consciousness); consciousness is the general state of wakefulness and awareness of self and environment, whereas attention is the directed selective mechanism that focuses consciousness on specific content. It also differs from **working memory** (Working Memory), which represents the space of storage and active processing, whereas attention represents the entry gate and the control over that space.
