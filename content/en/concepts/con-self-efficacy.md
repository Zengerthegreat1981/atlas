# Self-Efficacy (Perceived Self-Efficacy)

A central concept in Bandura's social cognitive theory (SCT, 1986), referring to a person's belief in their capability to organize and execute the courses of action required to produce given attainments (Bandura, 1977, 1997).

## Definition

Self-efficacy is not a general self-evaluation but a **specific judgment of capability** in a particular context: "Can I overcome this situation now?" There is a fundamental difference between:
- **Outcome Expectancy**: "If I do X, then Y will happen."
- **Efficacy Expectancy**: "I am able to do X" (which is what matters).

## The Four Sources of Self-Efficacy

1. **Mastery Experiences**: the strongest source. Repeated success at a particular task builds a firm belief in one's capability.
2. **Vicarious Experiences**: watching others succeed at a task (especially if they are "like me").
3. **Verbal Persuasion**: encouragement from others and affirmation of capability (relatively weak).
4. **Physiological/Emotional States**: anxiety, fatigue and pain can weaken the perception of efficacy.

## Its Place in Bandura's Broader Theory

Self-efficacy arises within the framework of "Triadic Reciprocal Determinism," which Bandura put forward in *Social Foundations of Thought and Action* (1986): the person, behavior and environment each influence the others reciprocally, and perceived self-efficacy is one of the most important personal factors in this loop. One of the main vicarious sources for building this efficacy is "Modeling," the observational learning that Bandura first detailed in *Social Learning Theory* (1977) before it was later incorporated into self-efficacy theory as the "vicarious experiences" source.

## The Difference from Similar Concepts

- **Self-Concept**: a general, global evaluation of the self (different from "efficacy in a particular domain").
- **Self-Esteem**: an overall evaluation of one's worth as a person.
- **Locus of Control**: a general belief that events are controlled internally or externally (Rotter, 1966), dimensionally different.
- **Empowerment**: a broader socio-political concept.

## Clinical Importance

Self-efficacy has been linked to treatment engagement and outcomes in many areas, though it is one factor among several and associations vary:
- In the treatment of depression, anxiety, addiction and eating disorders.
- In rehabilitation after illness or injury and in chronic pain management.
- In the self-management of chronic diseases such as diabetes and asthma.

Building self-efficacy through graded mastery experiences is a common element of cognitive-behavioral treatments, including internet-delivered programs, and of some school-based interventions.

## Limitations

- "Excessive" self-efficacy (overconfidence) may hinder help-seeking.
- Self-efficacy does not guarantee behavior if the environment is unsupportive.
- Difficulty of measuring it objectively (most measures are self-report).
