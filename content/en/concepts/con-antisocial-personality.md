# Antisocial Personality Disorder

## Definition

**Antisocial personality disorder** (ASPD) is a personality disorder characterized by a stable and enduring pattern, present since age 15, of **disregard for and violation of the rights of others**, according to the **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision** (DSM-5-TR, American Psychiatric Association). The individual must be 18 years or older at diagnosis, with evidence of **conduct disorder** with onset before age 15. That requirement records a developmental continuity from childhood conduct problems into adult antisocial behavior. It is a diagnostic rule, not a claim that every child with conduct disorder later meets criteria for ASPD.

## Diagnostic Criteria

DSM-5-TR requires three or more of the following: failure to conform to social and legal norms; repeated deceitfulness; impulsivity or failure to plan ahead; irritability and aggressiveness; reckless disregard for the safety of self or others; consistent irresponsibility (such as failure to sustain work or meet financial obligations); and lack of remorse (indifference to, or rationalization of, having hurt others). In the **International Classification of Diseases, Eleventh Revision** (ICD-11, World Health Organization), there is no separate classification that matches exactly. Instead, the equivalent condition is diagnosed within the **dimensional framework for personality disorders**, by specifying the domain of **"dissociality"** as one of the five basic trait domains (alongside negative affectivity, detachment, disinhibition and anankastia), with the severity of the disorder specified as mild, moderate or severe rather than as a separate categorical diagnosis.

## Historical Background

The DSM category is narrower than older writing on "psychopathic" personality. Hervey Cleckley's *The Mask of Sanity* (1941) described people who could appear socially competent while showing shallow affect, unreliability, and a lack of remorse. Lee Robins's follow-up study *Deviant Children Grown Up* (1966) traced adult "sociopathy" to childhood conduct problems and supplied much of the behavioral checklist that entered the Feighner criteria and then DSM-III (1980). Critics of that operationalization, including researchers working with Hare's Psychopathy Checklist, argued that a list of antisocial acts underweights the interpersonal and affective traits Cleckley had emphasized. DSM-5-TR keeps the behavioral criteria in its main section and offers a trait-based alternative in Section III.

## Key Dimensions

- **Relationship to psychopathy**: ASPD overlaps with the clinical concept of "psychopathy" (as in Hare's Psychopathy Checklist, PCL-R), but the two are not synonyms. Psychopathy focuses more on interpersonal-affective personality traits (such as emotional callousness and manipulation), whereas ASPD in DSM focuses primarily on observable behavior patterns.
- **Developmental continuity**: the diagnosis requires a history of conduct disorder in childhood or adolescence, which makes it one of the few disorders in DSM explicitly linked to a specific predetermined developmental course.
- **Alternative dimensional model (AMPD)**: Section III of DSM-5-TR presents an alternative model for personality disorders. For antisocial personality disorder it requires moderate or greater impairment in personality functioning (typically in self-functioning, meaning identity or self-direction, and in interpersonal functioning, meaning empathy or intimacy) together with pathological traits in the domains of antagonism (manipulativeness, callousness, deceitfulness, hostility) and disinhibition (irresponsibility, impulsivity, risk taking). Unlike the categorical Section II criteria, it does not require a documented history of conduct disorder, and it converges with the dimensional logic of ICD-11. The categorical Section II criteria remain the official diagnostic method.
- **The criminal context**: the diagnosis is much more common among prisoners than in the general population, but it is not synonymous with criminality, and many people with antisocial traits do not commit violent crimes.
