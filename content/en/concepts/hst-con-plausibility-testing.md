# Plausibility and Rational Evaluation

Plausibility and rational evaluation (*to eikos* and *to pithanon* in Greek; *verisimilitudo* and *probabilitas* in Latin) formed the primary epistemological tools through which ancient historians distinguished credible historical narrative from myth, poetic invention, and popular hearsay. In the absence of institutionalized archives, systematic documentation, or modern forensic archaeology, ancient historians relied heavily on the standard of probability to evaluate the veracity of conflicting reports and past events.

## Epistemological Foundations in Classical Greece

The emergence of plausibility testing was closely linked to early Ionian rationalism and fifth-century BCE rhetoric. Hecataeus of Miletus formulated the foundational principle of rational scrutiny: "I write what seems to me to be true, for the stories of the Greeks are manifold and, in my opinion, ridiculous." Herodotus of Halicarnassus applied this principle throughout his *Histories*. Although committed to recording what was told to him (*legein ta legomena*), Herodotus frequently suspended belief or questioned reports that violated physical or logistical plausibility, such as the idea of a river Ocean encircling the earth (2.23, 4.36), which he rejected for lack of evidence.

Thucydides elevated plausibility testing into a systematic historical method. In the "Archaeology" of Book 1 of the *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Thucydides reconstructed early Greek history through deductive inferences based on material resources, naval infrastructure, and economic surplus. Rejecting the exaggerated numbers of poets like Homer and the sensationalism of chroniclers (*logographoi*), Thucydides posited that historical actions must be evaluated according to immutable principles of human nature (*anthropeia physis*, 3.82). For Thucydides, political actors respond consistently to fear (*deos*), honor (*time*), and self-interest (*opheleia*), providing historians with a psychological benchmark for assessing the plausibility of reported motivations and political decisions.

## Hellenistic and Roman Methodological Applications

In the Hellenistic era, Polybius established pragmatic history (*pragmatike historia*) on rigorous plausibility testing. In Books 2 and 12 of his *Histories*, Polybius sharply criticized historians such as Phylarchus and Timaeus of Tauromenium for introducing fabricated, melodramatic scenes into historical works. Polybius maintained that historical credibility required practical familiarity with politics, geography, and military tactics. An author who had never commanded troops could not plausibly describe a cavalry battle, nor could one ignorant of Mediterranean terrain assess marching routes or supply lines.

Roman historians adapted plausibility testing within a framework deeply informed by legal rhetoric and senatorial politics. In *Ab Urbe Condita*, Livy acknowledged that early Roman legends preceding the city's foundation were embellished by poetic fiction rather than authenticated by incorrupt documents; he therefore evaluated early episodes by their moral and psychological plausibility. Tacitus rigorously deployed probability to unmask political rumor and dynastic propaganda in imperial Rome. In the *Annales*, Tacitus systematically juxtaposed official senate records (*acta senatus*) with widespread court rumors, often reporting sensational allegations (such as rumors that Germanicus was poisoned, *Annals* 2.73, 3.14–16) while expressing reserve about them.

## Historiographical Significance and Inherent Limitations

While plausibility testing marked a crucial transition from mythography to empirical historiography, modern historians identify significant methodological limitations in ancient practice. Ancient historians often conflated what was historically true with what was culturally expected. Because plausibility judgments rested on common-sense assumptions of their own elite social milieu, ancient authors routinely rejected genuine historical anomalies that contradicted their cultural presuppositions regarding foreign peoples, women, or lower-class behavior. Consequently, plausibility testing could become a conservative filter that reinforced cultural stereotypes rather than an objective instrument of empirical verification.
