# Transference and Countertransference in the Therapeutic Relationship

A central psychodynamic phenomenon described by Sigmund Freud: the patient transfers onto the therapist feelings, expectations and conflicts that originated in relationships with important early figures (such as the parents). Freud noted the phenomenon in *Studies on Hysteria* (1895, with Josef Breuer) and analysed its role in treatment in the case of "Dora" (published 1905), where he reflected on his failure to recognise it in time. It makes the therapeutic relationship a living stage on which earlier relational patterns can be observed and worked through.

## Positive Transference, Negative Transference, and Neurotic Transference

Interpreting transference became a central tool for producing insight within the psychoanalysis that Freud founded. In "The Dynamics of Transference" (1912) Freud distinguished positive transference (affectionate feelings toward the analyst) from negative transference (hostile feelings), and observed that transference is at once the strongest resistance to treatment and the main means by which it works, which is why it must be recognised and interpreted. In classical theory, "transference neurosis" refers to the development, in the course of analysis, of the patient's conflicts around the person of the analyst. Later schools adapted the concept: for example, in the intensive short-term dynamic psychotherapy (ISTDP) developed by Habib Davanloo, transference feelings are addressed directly together with the patient's defenses.

## Countertransference

The psychotherapist's unconscious emotional response to the patient, and the need for the therapist to undergo deep personal analysis in order to manage it. Freud introduced the term in 1910 and regarded it as an obstacle arising from the therapist's own unresolved conflicts, which is why analytic training includes the analyst's own analysis. Paula Heimann, in her influential article "On Counter-Transference" (1950), broadened this concept from an "obstacle" to be overcome into a valuable diagnostic tool: the therapist's apparently unjustified emotions toward the patient may reveal unconscious dynamics that the patient reproduces in the therapeutic relationship without being aware of it.

## Development within the Object Relations School

The British object relations tradition, including Melanie Klein and Donald Winnicott, developed the concept of transference to include the projection of very early relational patterns from infancy, and not only the Oedipal conflicts on which Freud focused, regarding the therapy room as reactivating the deepest layers of the patient's relational experience with the earliest caregivers.

## The Importance of Transference in the Training of Contemporary Therapists

The analysis of transference and countertransference remains a cornerstone in the training of dynamic psychotherapists to this day, since trainees are usually required to undergo their own personal analysis or ongoing clinical supervision, specifically to develop their ability to distinguish their own legitimate feelings toward the patient from those the patient evokes in them as an echo of the patient's own relational dynamics, a delicate distinction that requires extended experience and training.
