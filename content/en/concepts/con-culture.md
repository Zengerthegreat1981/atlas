# Culture: Shared Meaning and the Fabric of Social Life

**Culture** is the shared system of meanings, values, norms, symbols, and practices through which people make sense of the world and coordinate their lives together. It includes language, religion, art, food, dress, and manners, but also the taken-for-granted assumptions about what is right, beautiful, and real. Culture is learned, not inherited biologically; it is shared, not private; and it is always changing, even when it presents itself as tradition. It is one of the most widely used and most contested concepts in the social sciences, precisely because it names something so central: the medium in which all social life takes place.

## Definition and Development

The classic anthropological definition was given by Edward Burnett Tylor in *Primitive Culture* (1871): culture is "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society". Tylor's definition was deliberately broad. It treated culture as everything learned through membership in society, and it made the study of culture the study of human life as a whole, rather than of art and literature alone.

In the twentieth century, the concept was refined in several directions. Alfred Kroeber and Clyde Kluckhohn, in *Culture: A Critical Review of Concepts and Definitions* (1952), collected more than 160 definitions and showed how the term had been used to mean everything from a way of life to a system of ideas. Their review established that culture is best understood as patterns of behaviour acquired and transmitted by symbols, and as the distinctive achievements of human groups, including their embodiment in artefacts.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Clifford Geertz, in *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), gave the most influential account of culture as meaning. Culture, Geertz argued, is "a system of inherited conceptions expressed in symbolic forms by means of which men communicate, perpetuate, and develop their knowledge about and attitudes toward life". His method was thick description: the detailed interpretation of a cockfight, a funeral, or a joke, showing how a single event carries layers of meaning. For Geertz, the analysis of culture is not an experimental science in search of law but an interpretive one in search of meaning — and the anthropologist's task is to read the text a society writes about itself.

Raymond Williams, in *Culture and Society* (1958) and *Keywords* (1976), showed that the word culture itself has a history. It first meant the tending of crops and animals, was extended to the cultivation of the mind, and by the nineteenth century had come to mean both a whole way of life and the arts. Williams distinguished three uses that still structure debate: culture as a process of intellectual and spiritual development, culture as a particular way of life, and culture as the works and practices of intellectual and artistic activity. His point was that the argument over what culture means is itself part of culture — a struggle over whose way of life counts as civilized.

Pierre Bourdieu added a sociological dimension by showing that culture is also a resource. Taste in music, art, and food, he argued, is not a matter of personal preference but of cultural capital: the knowledge and dispositions acquired through upbringing and education that mark social position and reproduce class distinctions. What counts as high culture is itself a product of power, and the ability to recognize and appreciate it is unequally distributed.

## Examples

Culture is visible in the smallest details of daily life. The way people greet one another, the food they consider edible, the clothes they wear to a wedding, the jokes they find funny — all are cultural. It is visible in institutions: schools transmit culture through curricula, courts through rituals of justice, and media through the stories they tell about who we are. It is visible in conflict: disputes over the veil, over language rights, or over the curriculum are disputes over whose culture will be recognized as legitimate.

It is also visible in change. Migration brings cultures into contact, producing new hybrid forms: food, music, and language that belong fully to neither parent culture. Digital media create new cultural forms — memes, hashtags, online communities — that spread across the world in days. Culture, in this view, is not a fixed inheritance but a continuous process of making and remaking meaning.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Critics have argued that the concept of culture can be used to stereotype, treating whole peoples as if they shared a single, unchanging essence — "Arab culture", "African culture" — and ignoring the diversity and conflict within them. Others have argued that it can be used to explain away inequality, attributing poverty or violence to culture rather than to power and material conditions. These criticisms have led scholars to speak of cultures in the plural, to emphasize internal diversity and contestation, and to study how cultural categories are themselves produced and used.

The concept remains indispensable. Contemporary research applies it to the culture of organizations, asking how workplaces develop shared norms; to the culture of science, asking how laboratories produce knowledge; to consumer culture, asking how shopping and branding shape identity; and to political culture, asking how shared assumptions shape what is possible in politics. Culture remains the name for the shared meanings that make social life intelligible — and for the struggle over those meanings.
