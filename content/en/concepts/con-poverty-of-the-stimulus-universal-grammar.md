# Universal Grammar and Poverty of the Stimulus

A central argument in generative grammar that justifies the postulate of an innate "Universal Grammar" (UG) shared by all humans. It rests on an observation about the gap between what a child actually hears and the grammatical knowledge the child acquires.

## The Core of the Argument

Proponents of this argument observe that in childhood the child is exposed to a limited and often unstructured sample of speech (incomplete sentences, performance errors, and the absence of explicit negative examples that tell the child what is *not* grammatically correct). Yet by a very early age the child attains precise grammatical knowledge that is uniform across all children of the speech community, more precise and far richer than could be logically derived from the available input alone. From this "gap" (the poverty of the stimulus) it is concluded that the child must already possess an innate grammatical structure that constrains the possible hypotheses and guides the acquisition process.

## A Classic Example: Structure-Dependence Rather Than Linearity

The most famous example concerns the formation of questions. Children appear from the outset to apply rules that are sensitive to the hierarchical structure of the sentence (structure-dependent) rather than to the simple linear order of words, for instance in forming yes/no questions with auxiliaries (experimental evidence from Crain and Nakayama, 1987), even though the input does not logically rule out a simpler linear alternative. This is presented as evidence of an innate prior constraint. How far such input is actually lacking is the point disputed below.

## The Usage-Based Critique

Critics such as Tomasello argue that estimates of the input's "poverty" are statistically exaggerated, and that general statistical learning mechanisms (not specific to language) may suffice to explain the observed pattern without assuming a specialized innate grammatical structure.

## Additional Examples of the Argument

Other constructions have been offered as further cases, but they do not all behave like auxiliary inversion. Constraints on pronoun interpretation are a useful caution. Children are often reported to know early that a reflexive such as *himself* must find its antecedent nearby, while knowledge that a plain pronoun such as *him* cannot take a local antecedent (Principle B of the binding theory) is delayed well into the early school years (the "Delay of Principle B"). A poverty-of-the-stimulus argument cannot simply list every adult constraint as something children know before the input could teach it. Defenders of the argument therefore rest it on cases, such as structure-dependent inversion, where early adult-like behavior and thin input are both claimed.

## Contemporary Statistical Responses

Geoffrey Pullum and Barbara Scholz (2002) examined several classic poverty-of-the-stimulus claims and argued that they had not been supported by empirical evidence about the input, since the relevant constructions can be found in ordinary text. Julie Legate and Charles Yang (2002) replied with corpus counts from child-directed speech (CHILDES) and argued that, for auxiliary-inversion questions, the evidence available to the child is in fact very sparse, so the argument survives on that case. The exchange shows that the argument's premise can be, and is being, tested empirically, and the question remains contested.
