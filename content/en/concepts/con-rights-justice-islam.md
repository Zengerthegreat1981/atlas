# Rights & Justice in Islamic Thought

Justice in Islamic thought is a Quranic value: "God commands justice and benevolence" (Q. 16:90), and "when you judge between people, judge with justice" (Q. 4:58). The Mu'tazila made it one of their five principles, in the sense that God does not do what is ugly and does not impose what is beyond endurance. The Ash'arites understood it as meaning that God disposes of His own dominion as He wills. From this follows the question of rights: what does a person deserve from another person, and from the state?

## Rights in Jurisprudence

Jurists divided rights into the rights of God (*huquq Allah*) and the rights of human beings (*huquq al-'ibad*), and derived rulings on penalties and transactions from this division. The rights of human beings in property, life and honour lapse by the waiver of their holder, whereas what is a right of God does not lapse by the waiver of individuals. The objectives of the Sharia (*maqasid al-shari'a*) placed the preservation of life, property and honour among the essentials, as al-Shatibi elaborated. Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 751 AH/1350 CE) stated in *I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in* that the Sharia is "all justice, all mercy," and that any ruling that departs from justice into tyranny is not part of the Sharia.

## The State and Justice

Al-Mawardi (d. 450 AH/1058 CE) wrote in *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* about the duties of the imam and the office of *mazalim*, a supreme court that considered complaints of injustice by governors. In the *Muqaddima*, Ibn Khaldun (d. 808 AH/1406 CE) established a social rule: "injustice heralds the ruin of civilization" (*al-zulm mu'dhin bi-kharab al-'umran*). He also treated a chapter whose title states that when taxation exceeds its proper limit, it corrupts the state. Rosenthal translated the *Muqaddima* (1958). Miskawayh (d. 421 AH/1030 CE), in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, presents justice as a comprehensive virtue that balances the faculties of the soul. Constantine Zurayk edited the book (1966).

## The Modern Trend

The *Universal Islamic Declaration of Human Rights* was issued by the Islamic Council of Europe in 1981. The *Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam* was issued by the Organisation of the Islamic Conference in 1990, and it made the Sharia the only reference for interpreting its articles. Majid Khadduri wrote *The Islamic Conception of Justice* (1984), and Muhammad Hashim Kamali presented *Freedom, Equality and Justice in Islam* (2002).

## Critique

Ann Elizabeth Mayer, in *Islam and Human Rights: Tradition and Politics* (1991), criticized these declarations for restricting rights by the Sharia in matters such as freedom of religion and gender equality. Defenders reply that the concept of right in the tradition is linked to duty. There is a gap between theorizing about justice and the historical practice of government. Studies differ on how effective the *mazalim* institution was in practice and on how its condition varied from era to era. It is also not valid to regard the modern idea of "human rights" as a direct extension of the tradition, since it is the product of a different context.
