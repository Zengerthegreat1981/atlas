# The Replication Crisis

The replication crisis, sometimes called the credibility revolution, refers to the discovery in the 2010s that many published findings in psychology, and in other fields, could not be reproduced when independent researchers repeated the studies. Replication, repeating a study to see whether the result holds, is a basic safeguard of empirical science. When it fails often, it raises questions about how results were produced and published in the first place.

## How the concern emerged

Several events converged around 2011. Daryl Bem published experiments in a leading journal purporting to show evidence of precognition, using conventional methods, which led many researchers to ask what standards the field was actually applying. The same year, Diederik Stapel, a prominent social psychologist, was found to have fabricated data in many papers, showing that fraud was possible and could go undetected for years. Soon after, Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn published "False-positive psychology", demonstrating that ordinary flexibility in analysis could make almost any result appear statistically significant. Earlier, John Ioannidis had argued in 2005 that most published research findings might be false, owing to small samples, flexible analyses and publication bias.

## The Open Science Collaboration

In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration, led by Brian Nosek, reported attempts to replicate 100 studies published in three psychology journals in 2008. While most original studies reported significant results, a much smaller share of replications did, and the replication effect sizes were on average about half the size of the originals. The project did not show that all failed studies were false, but it indicated that the published record was overstated. Later "many labs" projects tested effects across many sites and found that some classic findings replicated robustly while others did not.

## Causes

- **Publication bias.** Journals favour positive and novel results, so null findings stay in file drawers, inflating the apparent evidence.
- **Questionable research practices.** These include analysing data in many ways until significance appears (sometimes called p-hacking), deciding when to stop collecting data after looking at results, dropping conditions or outcomes, and presenting post-hoc hypotheses as if they had been planned, a practice known as HARKing. A survey by Leslie John, George Loewenstein and Drazen Prelec in 2012 suggested that such practices were common.
- **Low statistical power.** Small samples produce unreliable estimates, and the significant ones tend to exaggerate true effects.
- **Incentives.** Careers depend on publication and novelty, so researchers have reasons to favour striking results over careful verification.
- **Misunderstanding of statistics.** Misinterpretation of p-values and over-reliance on a threshold of significance contribute to the problem.

## Responses

The field has adopted a number of reforms:

- **Preregistration** of hypotheses and analysis plans before data collection, which separates confirmatory from exploratory analysis.
- **Registered Reports**, a journal format in which methods are peer-reviewed and accepted in principle before results are known.
- **Open data, materials and code**, so others can check and reuse work.
- **Larger samples and power analysis**, and greater attention to effect sizes and confidence intervals.
- **Direct and multi-site replications**, and journals dedicated to publishing replications and null results.
- **Guidance from statistical bodies**, such as the American Statistical Association's 2016 statement on p-values.

## Debates

Not everyone agrees about the scale or the diagnosis. Some researchers argue that failed replications can reflect differences in context, sample or procedure, a view often called the hidden-moderators argument, while others reply that this makes theories unfalsifiable if invoked only after failure. Critics of the Open Science Collaboration, such as Gilbert and colleagues, argued that statistical analysis of its data was misleading, and the authors responded; the exchange showed that interpreting replication rates is itself not straightforward. Others worry that reform burdens early-career researchers or that rigid rules could reduce creativity. There is also a question of what counts as a successful replication, since criteria such as statistical significance, effect size comparison and confidence-interval overlap can give different answers.

## Why it matters

The crisis is generally seen as a self-correcting episode: it exposed weaknesses and prompted practical improvements. For readers, it suggests treating single studies, especially small ones with surprising results, with caution, and giving more weight to preregistered, well-powered and replicated findings.
