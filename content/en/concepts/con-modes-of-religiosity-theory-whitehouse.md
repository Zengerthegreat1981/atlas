# Modes of Religiosity Theory: Doctrinal vs. Imagistic

A theoretical framework formulated by Harvey Whitehouse that distinguishes between two structural modes for transmitting religion and its experience. It rests on the difference between two kinds of long-term human memory: semantic memory (general, abstract knowledge) and episodic memory (specific memories of events we lived through personally).

## The Doctrinal Mode

This mode is characterized by rituals and teachings **repeated at high frequency** (daily prayers, weekly sermons, regular religious instruction). These are relatively low in momentary emotional arousal and are stored in semantic memory as abstract doctrinal knowledge. They can be transmitted by organized verbal instruction and spread quickly through large institutions (churches, mosques, religious schools) with clear leadership hierarchies. Its typical example is the large institutional monotheistic religions.

## The Imagistic Mode

This mode is characterized by rituals that are **rarely repeated** (they may occur only once in a lifetime, such as harsh initiation rites), but are **highly emotionally and sensorially arousing** (bodily pain, fear, shock). They are stored in episodic memory as an unforgettable personal experience that produces a deep collective bond (cohesion) among those who underwent the experience together. However, they spread more slowly (they cannot easily be transmitted verbally) and tend to be tied to smaller groups without strictly centralized leadership. Its typical example is the initiation rites of the small societies that Whitehouse studied in the field in Papua New Guinea.

## Theoretical Impact

This dichotomy provides a cognitive-social explanation for a question that historians of religion have long observed without precise theoretical framing: why do religions differ so greatly in their institutional structure (centralized or decentralized), and in the kind of bond they generate among their followers (abstract doctrinal loyalty versus an intimate collective connection based on shared experience)? The theory ties this variation directly to the structure of human memory and the frequency of ritual, not to the content of the doctrine itself.

## Field Case Study: The Pomio Kivung Cult

Whitehouse developed his theory from fieldwork in the late 1980s and early 1990s in East New Britain, Papua New Guinea, where he studied the Pomio Kivung, a local religious movement that combines elements of Christian missionary teaching with beliefs about ancestors and their return. The Pomio Kivung relies on frequently repeated teaching and ritual, which he took as a model of the doctrinal mode, and he contrasted it with other movements in the region whose rituals are rare, dramatic and intensely arousing. He first set out the contrast in an article of 1992 and then in *Inside the Cult* (1995).

## The Critique of Ritual Competence

The cognitive scholars Robert McCauley and E. Thomas Lawson object to limiting the explanation to the two variables of frequency and arousal. They propose instead the theory of "ritual competence," which focuses on the internal formal structure of the ritual act itself (who performs the act, on whom it is performed, and the position of the supernatural agent within the sequence of the act) as the more important variable in shaping how rituals are performed and experienced. This debate between the two theories remains one of the most lively discussions within the cognitive science of religion.

## Responses from Social Anthropology and Ethnography

Anthropologists working outside the cognitive programme have tested the theory chiefly against their own field material. The collection *Ritual and Memory* (2004), edited by Whitehouse and James Laidlaw, invited scholars with detailed ethnographic knowledge to assess how far the two modes illuminate particular cases. The results were mixed: the distinction proved useful for posing comparative questions, but several cases combined features of both modes or did not fall neatly into either. Laidlaw's own discussion of Indian renouncer religions, Jainism in particular, questioned whether frequent routine and intense experience can be assigned to separate tendencies when each is embedded in the other. In a later essay (2007) he set out, from a sympathetic but critical position, a social anthropologist's difficulties with the cognitive science of religion more broadly: explanations built on memory and universal mental mechanisms capture only part of what makes religious life what it is, and give limited place to history, reflection, interpretation and the deliberate self-formation of practitioners. The disagreement therefore concerns not only whether the predicted correlations hold, but also what a theory of religion ought to explain.
