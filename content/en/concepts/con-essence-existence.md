# Essence vs Existence Debates

## Definition

The debate over essence and existence is one of the central metaphysical problems in Arabic-Islamic philosophy. It turns on the question of ontological priority: which constitutes the concrete ground of reality: **essence** (*mahiyya*, literally "whatness"), meaning the definition and formal intelligibility of a thing, or **existence** (*wujud*), meaning its actual instantiation outside the mind? Ibn Sina (Avicenna, 370–428 AH / 980–1037 CE) formulated the classical distinction between the two, laying the groundwork for a debate that culminated centuries later in the opposing doctrines of the primacy of essence (*asalat al-mahiyya*) and the primacy of existence (*asalat al-wujud*).

## Historical Context

In *Kitab al-Shifa'* (The Book of Healing) and *al-Isharat wa-l-Tanbihat* (Remarks and Admonitions), Ibn Sina distinguished between a thing's conceptual quiddity (such as "horseness" or "triangularity") and its factual instantiation in reality. For every contingent being (*mumkin al-wujud*), essence does not contain its own existence. Existence is additional to the quiddity and depends on an external cause (*sabab*). Later readers often called that addition an accident (*'arad*), a label only partly supported by Avicenna's own wording. The only exception is the **Necessary Existent by Virtue of Itself** (*wajib al-wujud bi-dhatihi*, God), whose essence is identical with His existence. This distinction passed into post-Avicennan scholasticism (*kalam* and *hikma*), prompting extensive debate regarding whether existence in external things is an actual extramental reality or merely a secondary intelligible (*ma'qul thani*).

## Main Dimensions

- **Primacy of Essence (*asalat al-mahiyya*)**: In his Illuminationist (*Ishraqi*) philosophy, Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi (549–587 AH / 1154–1191 CE) rejected the Peripatetic view that existence is a real attribute added to essence in external reality. In *Hikmat al-Ishraq* (The Philosophy of Illumination, 1186), Suhrawardi argued that existence is merely a mental consideration (*amr i'tibari*) abstracted by the intellect; what truly exists externally are concrete, differentiated essences structured by degrees of luminous intensity (*nur*). In the Safavid period, Mir Damad (d. 1041 AH / 1631 CE), a leading figure of the School of Isfahan, likewise defended the priority of essence over existence.
- **Primacy of Existence (*asalat al-wujud*)**: Sadr al-Din Muhammad al-Shirazi, known as Mulla Sadra (979–1050 AH / 1571–1640 CE), reversed this hierarchy, constructing his "Transcendent Philosophy" (*al-Hikma al-Muta'aliya*) on the foundation that **existence is the sole objective reality**. Essences, for Sadra, are not self-standing realities but epistemic limits, determinations, and mental forms derived from the boundaries of specific degrees of existence.
- **Modulation of Existence (*tashkik al-wujud*)**: Sadra linked the primacy of existence to *tashkik* (analogy, gradation, or modulation). Existence is a single, all-encompassing reality that varies intrinsically in intensity (*shidda*), deficiency (*da'f*), priority (*taqaddum*), and posteriority (*ta'akhkhur*). From the divine ground down to prime matter, reality is a unified spectrum of being rather than a collection of atomized quiddities. This insight also supported his doctrine of trans-substantial motion (*al-haraka al-jawhariyya*), according to which the material world undergoes continuous, intrinsic transformation.
- **Modern Trajectory**: The Sadrian synthesis remains a basic part of the philosophical curriculum in Shi'i seminaries (*hawzat*). Muhammad Husayn Tabataba'i (1904–1981) and Mehdi Ha'iri Yazdi (1923–1999) taught and extended it. Toshihiko Izutsu (1914–1993) compared Sadra's account of existence with Heidegger's, and Henry Corbin (1903–1978) presented the school to European readers through a phenomenology of the imaginal world.
