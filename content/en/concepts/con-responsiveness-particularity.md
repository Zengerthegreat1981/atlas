# Responsiveness & Particularity

## Meaning and Definition

The concept of "responsiveness and particularity" in moral philosophy refers to a critical stance. It holds that true morality does not consist in mechanically applying abstract universal rules to every case. It consists instead in the capacity to respond sensitively to the concrete and unique details of each moral situation, and to persons in their non-repeating individual particularity. The proponent of this approach holds that one who deals with each human being merely as a "case" to which a general rule applies misses the essence of the moral relationship. That relationship requires us to see the other as this particular person, not as a sample of a category.

## Roots in Islamic Thought: Wisdom, Discernment, and Juristic Reasoning

Islamic jurisprudence recognized a precise distinction between the general juristic rule and attention to the particularity of the case. Jurists determined that legal opinion changes with the change of times, circumstances, intentions, and customs. Imam al-Shatibi established this in *al-Muwafaqat*, distinguishing between the stable universals of the Shariah and the requirements of juristic reasoning in applying a ruling to a particular event. He insisted that the true jurist is not merely one who memorizes texts, but one who possesses the skill of applying a general ruling well to the particularity of each case. Sufism likewise celebrated the virtue of discernment (*firasa*) and insight into people's states, rather than treating them according to outward appearance alone. A saying reported from Ali ibn Abi Talib advises speaking to people in terms they can understand. This reflects an early awareness of the need to take into account the particularity of the addressee in moral and da'wa guidance, rather than applying one discourse to everyone.

## The Western Context: Care Ethics and Particularity in Murdoch's Thought

The Irish-born British philosopher Iris Murdoch developed this concept in her book *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970). She used the term "attention" to describe the genuine moral effort required to see another person fairly and free from the egoism and illusions of the self. She gave the example of a mother who gradually revises her judgment of her daughter-in-law, freeing herself from prejudgments in order to see her actual personality. On this foundation, a current known as care ethics was built by Carol Gilligan and Nel Noddings. It criticizes the tendency of Kantian and utilitarian ethics toward abstraction and the general rule, calling for ethics to be founded on concrete caring relationships between specific individuals. Bernard Williams also criticized the notion of impartiality in modern ethics. He argued that special personal relationships, such as the relationship of a husband to his wife, generate special moral obligations that cannot be reduced to a neutral utilitarian calculation.

## Applications and Objections

This approach is applied in critiquing routine medical ethics that treat patients as standardized cases, calling on physicians to attend to the particular circumstances of each patient. It is objected that the approach may open the door to favoritism and bias if particularity is given excessive precedence over universal justice. It is also objected that the absence of a clear general standard may leave moral judgment vulnerable to whim. Its proponents reply that responsiveness to particularity does not mean abandoning general principles. It means supplementing them with a perceptual sensitivity that prevents their blind application.
