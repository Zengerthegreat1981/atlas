# Land Tenure

Land tenure is the system of rules that determines who may use land, for what purposes, and for how long. It is one of the most fundamental institutions in any society, because land is at once a source of food, a home, a store of wealth, and a marker of identity. Tenure systems vary enormously: land may be owned outright by individuals, held in common by a village, controlled by a lineage or chief, leased from the state, or occupied under customary rules that no written law recognises. Anthropologists study land tenure to understand how rights in land are claimed, defended, inherited, and lost — and how struggles over land produce and reproduce inequality, conflict, and belonging.

## Definition

Land tenure is best understood not as a single right but as a "bundle of rights." Different people may hold different rights over the same plot: one person may farm it, another may collect firewood on it, a third may hold the right to sell it, and a fourth may inherit it. Tenure systems are usually classified into four broad types. Private tenure gives an individual or corporation exclusive rights, including the right to sell. Communal tenure vests rights in a group, with members holding use rights that cannot be sold outside the group. State tenure places land under government ownership, with users holding leases or permits. Open access, where no one holds enforceable rights, is often confused with communal tenure but is a different arrangement. In practice, most societies combine elements of several types, and the same plot may be governed by overlapping customary, religious, and statutory rules.

## Origins and Development

In small-scale societies, land was typically held by kin groups, and rights were established by clearing, cultivating, and defending territory. Henry Maine argued in 1861 that early law moved from status to contract, from rights held by virtue of membership in a group to rights held by individuals — a claim that shaped a century of debate. Colonial rule transformed tenure across Africa, Asia, and the Americas: colonial governments imposed private property and state ownership on systems they barely understood, often declaring "vacant" land that was in fact used under customary rules. The twentieth century saw repeated waves of land reform, from the redistribution of estates in Latin America and Asia to the collectivisation of agriculture in socialist states and its later reversal. In 1990, Elinor Ostrom challenged the conventional wisdom that common land is inevitably degraded, showing that communities around the world manage shared resources sustainably through locally devised rules — work that earned her the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2009.

## Key Debates

The most heated debate concerns privatisation: should customary and communal land be converted into individual, titled property? Hernando de Soto argued in 2000 that formal titles would unlock the "dead capital" of the poor, allowing them to borrow against their land. Critics reply that titling often dispossesses the vulnerable — women, tenants, and pastoralists whose rights are invisible to surveyors — and concentrates land in the hands of elites. A second debate concerns gender: in many tenure systems, women farm land they cannot inherit or sell, leaving them dependent on male relatives. A third debate concerns the commons. The "tragedy of the commons," popularised by Garrett Hardin in 1968, held that shared resources would inevitably be overused; Ostrom's research showed that the tragedy is avoidable when communities can set and enforce their own rules. Finally, there is the question of indigeneity: Indigenous peoples around the world claim land rights based on prior occupation and spiritual connection, challenging states that treat their territories as empty or available.

## Contemporary Relevance

Land tenure is at the centre of many of the world's most urgent conflicts. Large-scale land acquisitions — "land grabs" — by foreign investors and domestic elites have displaced farming communities across Africa, Asia, and Latin America. Urbanisation is creating new tenure problems in cities, where millions live in informal settlements without secure rights. Climate change is intensifying competition for land, as rising seas, desertification, and shifting rainfall make some areas uninhabitable and others newly valuable. Meanwhile, governments and development agencies are experimenting with new forms of tenure, from community land trusts to digital land registries. The anthropological lesson is that tenure is never merely technical: it is about who belongs, who decides, and whose claims count.
