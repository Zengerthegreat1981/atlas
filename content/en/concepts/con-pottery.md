# Pottery

Pottery — vessels and objects made from clay that is shaped, dried, and fired until hard — is among the oldest and most widespread technologies in human history, and one of the richest sources of evidence for archaeologists and anthropologists. Because fired clay survives in the ground long after wood, cloth, and skin have rotted, broken potsherds are often the most abundant trace of past societies. But pottery is more than a dating tool: the study of how pots are made, used, exchanged, and discarded reveals how people organise labour, mark identity, cook and store food, and understand their world.

## Definition

Anthropologists often analyse pottery through its chaîne opératoire, the sequence of technical choices that turns raw clay into a finished vessel: selecting and preparing clay, adding temper such as sand or crushed shell, forming the pot by hand-building, coiling, or the wheel, decorating its surface, and firing it at a temperature that transforms soft clay into durable ceramic. Each step involves decisions that are learned within a community of practice, so that two villages making functionally identical cooking pots may use visibly different techniques. This is why archaeologists can trace cultural boundaries, trade routes, and learning networks from potsherds alone.

Pottery is also defined by function. Vessels store grain and water, cook food over fire, serve meals, hold offerings, and carry goods to market. Function shapes form: a cooking pot needs walls that resist thermal shock, a storage jar needs a narrow mouth to keep contents dry, and a serving bowl needs a surface that displays decoration. Anthropologists therefore read pots as compromises between technical constraints, available materials, and social demands.

## Origins and Development

Pottery appears independently in several parts of the world. The earliest known ceramic vessels come from East Asia: fragments from Xianrendong cave in Jiangxi and Yuchanyan cave in Hunan, southern China, date to roughly 18,000-20,000 years ago, and the Jomon pottery of Japan, beginning at least 15,000 years ago, includes some of the most elaborately decorated early vessels. In the Near East pottery appeared around 8,000-9,000 years ago, alongside farming villages, while in the Americas it emerged independently in the Amazon and the Andes several thousand years ago.

The spread of the potter's wheel, in use in Mesopotamia by the fourth millennium BCE, transformed production. Wheel-throwing allowed faster, more standardised vessels and supported the rise of specialist workshops and markets. Yet hand-building never disappeared: many societies kept coiling and paddle-and-anvil techniques for millennia, and potters' choices between wheel and hand often marked ethnic or gendered identities rather than simple technological progress.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate in pottery studies concerns how much a pot's style tells us about the people who made it. Early archaeology treated distinctive decoration as the signature of a "culture" or even an ethnic group. Later work, including that of Prudence Rice and the ethnoarchaeologists represented in Longacre (1991), showed that style is shaped by many factors: the potter's training, the demands of the market, the availability of clays and pigments, and the social messages that decoration carries. A painted design may signal a potter's lineage, a household's status, or a ritual occasion — or it may simply be what sells.

A second dimension is the organisation of production. Anthropologists distinguish household production, in which potters make vessels mainly for their own families, from workshop and industrial production, in which specialists produce for exchange. Dean Arnold's ethnoarchaeological research in Mexico and Peru showed that pottery production is sensitive to the agricultural calendar, the cost of fuel, and access to clay sources, and that potters constantly weigh the risks of firing against the demands of farming. The shift from household to workshop production is therefore a social and economic change, not merely a technical one.

Gender is a third key dimension. In many societies pottery is women's work, learned from mothers and grandmothers; in others, wheel-thrown pottery is a male craft. These divisions affect who controls the income from pottery, who owns the knowledge, and how innovations spread. The study of pottery has therefore become a window onto the division of labour and the transmission of skill.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Pottery remains a living craft as well as an archaeological subject. Studio potters, heritage revival projects, and fair-trade cooperatives sustain traditional techniques in places from the Andes to sub-Saharan Africa, while also reshaping them for tourist and export markets. Anthropologists studying these projects ask who benefits when a "traditional" pot is sold in a gallery, and how the demand for authenticity affects potters' choices.

The field has also become more critical of its own categories. The term "pottery" itself lumps together an enormous range of objects — from a rough cooking pot to a finely painted ceremonial vessel — and the assumption that pots equal peoples has been used to draw ethnic boundaries that the evidence does not support. Contemporary research therefore combines laboratory analysis of clays and firing temperatures with ethnography of living potters, treating pottery as a material record of skilled practice, social relations, and economic life rather than as a passive marker of identity.
