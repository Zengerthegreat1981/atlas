# Integrity Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Integrity ethics (Integrity Ethics) is the system of values that makes the preservation of rights, the fulfillment of covenants, and truthfulness with oneself and with others the pillars of upright conduct. The person of integrity is consistent between what they conceal and what they display, and between their words and their deeds. In this sense, integrity goes beyond returning material deposits to a broader meaning: inner uprightness and not changing one's colors according to circumstances.

## Foundations in the Islamic Tradition

The Holy Qur'an treats trust (*amanah*) as a central axis. It describes it as something that "the heavens and the earth and the mountains refused to bear, and were apprehensive of it, but the human being bore it," which indicates its existential weight. In *Adab al-Dunya wa al-Din*, al-Mawardi discussed trustworthiness among the qualities of upright character and social order. Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, linked trustworthiness to soundness of the heart, regarding betrayal of trust as one of the signs of practical hypocrisy, as stated in the prophetic hadith: "and when he is entrusted, he betrays."

## The Western Philosophical Context

In Western philosophy, the concept of "personal integrity" (Personal Integrity) has been associated with the idea of consistency between principles and action. Bernard Williams developed a critique of utilitarianism from this standpoint, arguing that requiring the individual to sacrifice their personal principles for the sake of overall utility deprives them of their "integrity" as a moral self. The moral act, on his account, must proceed from the individual's own project, not from a cold external calculation that strips them of their identity. Kant, for his part, discussed truthfulness as an absolute duty admitting no exception, because lying contradicts the dignity of the rational human being both in themselves and in others.

## Objections and an Example

Absolute trustworthiness is objected to on the grounds that it may conflict with other values, as in the case of someone entrusted with a secret who knows that keeping it would endanger the life of an innocent person. A familiar illustration is a person who finds lost property and returns it in full despite need, a theme common in Islamic moral literature as an example of uprightness prevailing over immediate advantage.

## Trustworthiness in Public Life

In Islamic political thought, trust extends to the trust of governance itself. Jurists regarded it as one of the greatest trusts, given the power it entails over people's interests and rights. Islamic political writing, drawing on the hadith that when affairs are entrusted to the unqualified one should await the Hour, treated appointing someone to office without competence as a betrayal of trust even without financial corruption; al-Mawardi's *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* sets out the qualifications required of officeholders. In contemporary philosophy, the concept of "institutional integrity" (Institutional Integrity) is linked to holding those who hold public office accountable, on the basis that the trust placed in them is a trust for which they are answerable if they neglect it. This makes integrity ethics a common foundation for the governance of individuals and of institutions alike.
