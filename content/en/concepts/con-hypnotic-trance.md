# Hypnotic Trance

## Definition and Core Characteristics

A hypnotic trance is an altered, attentive state of consciousness characterized by focused concentration, reduced peripheral awareness, and heightened responsiveness to suggestion. The American Psychological Association (APA) Division 30 (Society of Psychological Hypnosis) formally defines hypnosis as "a state of consciousness involving focused attention and reduced peripheral awareness characterized by an enhanced capacity for response to suggestion."

Core experiential and cognitive characteristics include:
- **Focused Attention**: Narrowing the field of awareness onto specific internal or external stimuli while ignoring irrelevant background sensations.
- **Attenuated Critical Monitoring**: Temporary suspension of active analytical judging, allowing suggested imaginative scenarios to be experienced without critical scrutiny.
- **Enhanced Suggestibility**: Increased willingness to accept verbal and symbolic suggestions regarding altered perception, sensation, emotion, or behavior.
- **Phenomenological Involuntariness**: Responses are frequently experienced as happening automatically or effortlessly rather than through deliberate volitional exertion. Individual hypnotic suggestibility is a relatively stable, measurable trait across the adult lifespan.

## Historical Evolution: From Mesmer to Modern Science

The scientific conceptualization of hypnosis evolved through critical historical controversies:
- **Mesmerism and Animal Magnetism**: In the late 18th century, Franz Anton Mesmer claimed to cure illness by manipulating an invisible, universal magnetic fluid ("animal magnetism"). In 1784, the Royal Commission headed by Benjamin Franklin and Antoine Lavoisier discredited the physical fluid theory, attributing Mesmeric effects to imagination and suggestion.
- **James Braid and Neuro-Hypnotism (1843)**: The Scottish surgeon James Braid rejected animal magnetism and coined the terms "neuro-hypnology" and "hypnotism" (from the Greek *hypnos*, sleep) in *Neurypnology* (1843). Braid established hypnosis as a physiological and psychological state induced by visual fixation and monoideism (sustained concentration on a single idea).
- **The 19th-Century Debate**: In the 1880s, two French schools clashed over the nature of hypnosis:
  - *The Salpêtrière School* (Jean-Martin Charcot in Paris) viewed hypnosis as an artificially induced pathological condition exclusive to individuals suffering from hysteria.
  - *The Nancy School* (Ambroise-Auguste Liébeault and Hippolyte Bernheim) argued that hypnosis is a normal psychological manifestation of universal suggestibility. The Nancy School's position ultimately prevailed in psychological science. Sigmund Freud studied at both schools before abandoning hypnosis in favor of free association.

## Major Theoretical Models

Contemporary hypnosis research is divided between state, non-state, and naturalistic perspectives:

1. **Neodissociation Theory (State Model)**: Formulated by Ernest Hilgard (*Divided Consciousness*, 1977), this model posits that hypnosis induces a genuine alteration in consciousness characterized by a functional division of cognitive control systems. Hilgard's famous **"hidden observer"** experiments demonstrated that when participants received hypnotic suggestions for analgesia during a cold-pressor test, their ordinary hypnotic report indicated little or no pain, yet when a "hidden part" was asked to report through special techniques (such as key presses or automatic writing), it described more pain, though typically less than in the normal waking state. Hilgard read this as evidence of dissociation; the effect appeared only in some highly hypnotizable participants, and critics argue it reflects suggestion and expectations.
2. **Sociocognitive and Response Expectancy Models (Non-State Models)**: Advanced by Nicholas Spanos, Theodore Sarbin, and Irving Kirsch, these models argue that hypnosis does not require a special trance state. Instead, hypnotic responses reflect normal psychological processes: motivated role enactment, positive attitudes, situational demand characteristics, and response expectancies (anticipating that a response will occur automatically).
3. **Ericksonian Naturalistic Hypnosis**: Milton H. Erickson pioneered an indirect, utilization-based approach, conceiving trance not as an artificial laboratory phenomenon but as a common, natural everyday experience (such as deep absorption in reading or daydreaming). Erickson utilized indirect suggestion, paradoxical interventions, and storytelling to bypass conscious resistance.

## Clinical Applications and Cautions

In clinical practice, hypnosis is utilized strictly as an adjunct to evidence-based medical and psychological interventions:
- **Empirical Indications**: Hypnosis has strong empirical support for acute and chronic pain management (hypno-analgesia), reducing procedural anxiety during medical interventions, and in specialized protocols such as gut-directed hypnotherapy for irritable bowel syndrome (the Whorwell protocol).
- **Forensic and Memory Cautions**: Hypnosis is **not** a reliable method for recovering forgotten or repressed memories. Extensive research (e.g., Steven Jay Lynn, Elizabeth Loftus) has conclusively demonstrated that hypnosis does not increase memory accuracy; instead, it increases suggestibility, confabulation, and the creation of pseudomemories (false memories) held with heightened subjective certainty. Consequently, hypnotically refreshed testimony is legally inadmissible in many jurisdictions.
