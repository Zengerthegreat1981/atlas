# Hypnotic Trance

A state of organized alteration in consciousness, ranging between modified wakefulness and deep relaxation, characterized by:
- **Narrowly focused attention**: narrowing the field of awareness so that the therapist or a single voice becomes the focus of attention.
- **Reduced conscious monitoring**: quieting the "critical factor" that measures and checks logically.
- **Heightened suggestibility**: a tendency to accept suggestions without the usual conscious review.
- **Partial dissociation**: a division within awareness, as in Hilgard's neodissociation account. People differ widely in **hypnotic suggestibility**, which is a fairly stable individual trait.

## Historical root

The contemporary term goes back to the Scottish surgeon James Braid, who coined the word "hypnosis" in 1843, deliberately breaking with the **Mesmerism** of Franz Anton Mesmer, which explained the phenomenon as a flow of "animal magnetism"; Braid reframed it as a physiological-psychological state resulting from focused attention, not an invisible fluid passing from therapist to patient.

## Theoretical dimensions

- **The classical school (Bernheim, Liébeault)**: Trance is not a special entity but a result of suggestion, with varying susceptibility.
- **Ernest Hilgard's school**: Trance is a real neuropsychological state of division, supported by the "hidden observer" test designed by Hilgard himself.
- **Milton Erickson's school**: Trance is a natural subjective experience that can be induced through indirect therapeutic communication, and is not an abnormal state.

These approaches developed within clinical hypnosis, which historically separated from psychoanalysis after Freud abandoned hypnosis in favor of free association.

## Therapeutic applications

Hypnosis is used as an adjunct in the management of acute and chronic pain and in some work on anxiety and phobia; the evidence is strongest for pain. It is not a reliable way of recovering accurate memories: memories produced under hypnosis can be distorted or false and may be held with high confidence, so it should be used only by trained clinicians.

## The hidden observer test

Ernest Hilgard designed the "Hidden Observer" experiment in the 1970s to test the dissociation hypothesis: he asked participants under deep hypnosis to endure pain (such as placing a hand in ice water) with a suggestion of feeling no pain, and then asked a presumed "hidden part" of their awareness to report the level of pain through automatic talking or a keypress. Hilgard found that this "hidden part" often reported high real pain despite the conscious report of no pain, which he regarded as evidence of a division in conscious processing; critics, including Spanos, argued that the reports reflect demand characteristics and the wording of the instructions.

## The controversy over the nature of trance

The fundamental question remains open in the contemporary hypnosis literature: is hypnotic trance a physiologically distinct state of consciousness (as Hilgard holds), or a social performance shaped by expectations and the therapeutic context (as Theodore Sarbin and Nicholas Spanos hold)? Some neuroimaging studies report changes in the activity of networks associated with attention and self-awareness during hypnosis, without settling the broader theoretical controversy.
