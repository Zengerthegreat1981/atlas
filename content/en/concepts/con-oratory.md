# Oratory and Rhetoric

Oratory is formal, public speech performed before an audience, and rhetoric is the art of persuading through it. In anthropology, oratory is studied as a social performance: the speech that opens a council, settles a dispute, honours the dead, or rallies a community. Unlike everyday conversation, oratory is marked by elevated language, rhythm, repetition, and gesture, and it is often the work of specialists, chiefs, elders, or professional speakers, whose skill with words is a source of authority. To study oratory is to study how words do things: how they bind agreements, confer status, and move people to act.

## Origins and Development

The Western tradition of rhetoric begins with Aristotle's *Rhetoric* (fourth century BCE), which classified persuasion into appeals to reason, character, and emotion, and treated the speech as a craft that could be taught. Anthropologists, however, found that rhetoric is not a Greek invention but a human universal, practised wherever people gather to decide. The volume *Political Language and Oratory in Traditional Society* (1975), edited by Maurice Bloch, argued that among the Merina of Madagascar, formal oratory was a highly stylised art in which speakers used proverbs, parallel phrasing, and respectful address to display deference and to make decisions appear as the product of consensus. The formality of the speech, Bloch argued, restricted what could be said and therefore who could challenge authority.

Richard Bauman's *Verbal Art as Performance* (1977) shifted attention from the text of the speech to its performance. A speech succeeds or fails in the moment, through the speaker's voice, timing, and command of the audience, and its meaning depends on the event in which it occurs. Donald Brenneis and Fred Myers developed this approach in *Dangerous Words: Language and Politics in the Pacific* (1984), showing that in Pacific societies, words are understood to have power in themselves: a careless or hostile word can cause illness, provoke violence, or disturb relations with spirits. Oratory is therefore not merely talk about power; it is an exercise of power, and a risky one.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns the relationship between oratory and political organisation. In egalitarian societies, oratory may be the main instrument of leadership, since leaders who cannot command obedience must persuade. Among the Samoan *fono*, the council of chiefs, oratory is the medium through which rank is displayed and disputes settled, and the speaking chiefs, the *tulafale*, hold a status distinct from the title-holders they serve. In chiefdoms and states, by contrast, oratory often becomes the voice of authority itself, with royal proclamations, sermons, and inaugural addresses ritualising power. Anthropologists ask whether rhetoric flourishes where power is contested and withers where it is secure, or whether every political order needs its speakers.

A second dimension is gender. In many societies, formal oratory is a male domain, and women's speech is confined to laments, gossip, or song. Michelle Rosaldo's work among the Ilongot of the Philippines showed how the public oratory of men was linked to headhunting, anger, and the assertion of adult status, while women's knowledge was expressed in other genres. Yet women have their own oratorical traditions, such as funeral laments, which show that rhetoric is not a male monopoly, and that its gendering is itself a political fact to be explained rather than assumed.

A third dimension is the relationship between speech and truth. Oratory is judged by its effects, not only by its accuracy, and skilled speakers may use ambiguity, flattery, or exaggeration to achieve them. Ethnographers document how audiences evaluate speeches, how speakers are trained, and how failed oratory, the speech that falls flat or offends, can damage a reputation. The study of oratory thus connects aesthetics, politics, and ethics: a good speech is beautiful, effective, and proper, and these qualities are inseparable.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Oratory remains central to public life. Political campaigning, courtroom advocacy, religious preaching, and protest speeches all depend on rhetorical skill, and the rise of social media has created new stages for it: the viral video, the TED talk, and the televised debate are oratorical genres with their own conventions. Anthropologists study how these forms travel, how political leaders borrow the styles of preachers or comedians, and how audiences in different cultures judge sincerity, eloquence, and authority. The study of misinformation has also drawn on rhetoric: persuasion works through narrative, repetition, and emotion as much as through evidence, and understanding that is essential to understanding public debate.

The concept's enduring value is that it treats language as action. Words do not merely describe the world; in oratory they constitute it, declaring war, sealing marriage, and conferring office. Contemporary anthropology therefore studies oratory alongside law, ritual, and performance, asking how speech acts are learned, authorised, and contested. In a world where public speech is increasingly mediated, recorded, and replayed, the old anthropological insight holds: to understand a society, listen to how its people speak when it matters most.
