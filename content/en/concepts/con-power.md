# Power: Getting Others to Do What They Would Not Otherwise Do

**Power** is the capacity to shape the actions, beliefs, and life chances of others — to get people to do what they would not otherwise do, to set the terms on which decisions are made, and to decide which questions are even raised. It is one of the oldest concepts in political and social thought, and one of the most contested, because every definition of power carries a theory of how society works: who rules, how rule is maintained, and whether it can be resisted.

## Definition and Development

The classic modern definition comes from Robert Dahl, who in "The Concept of Power" (1957) defined it as a relation: A has power over B to the extent that A can get B to do something B would not otherwise do. Dahl's definition made power observable — it could be studied by watching who wins in concrete decisions — and it shaped a generation of empirical research on community politics. Its strength was precision; its weakness, critics argued, was that it only saw power where open conflict existed.

Max Weber, in *Economy and Society* (1922; English edition 1968), drew a sharper distinction. Power (*Macht*) is the probability that an actor can carry out their will despite resistance; domination (*Herrschaft*) is power stabilized into legitimate authority, accepted as rightful by those who obey. Weber's point was that raw power is unstable and expensive to maintain, so rulers seek to legitimize it — through tradition, charisma, or rational-legal rules. The study of power therefore becomes the study of how domination is justified and how those justifications are challenged.

## Key Contributions: The Three Dimensions

Steven Lukes, in *Power: A Radical View* (1974), argued that Dahl's definition captured only the first dimension of power: decision-making in open conflict. He proposed two more. The second dimension, developed by Peter Bachrach and Morton Baratz, is agenda-setting: the power to keep issues off the table, so that some grievances never become decisions at all. The third dimension is the most far-reaching: the power to shape people's preferences and perceptions, so that they accept the existing order as natural or inevitable and do not even experience their interests as frustrated. A worker who believes low wages are the natural price of unskilled labour is, in Lukes's account, subject to the third dimension of power.

Michel Foucault, in *Discipline and Punish* (1975; English edition 1977), pushed the analysis further still. Power, for Foucault, is not only something the state or the rich possess and the poor lack; it circulates through institutions — schools, prisons, hospitals, factories — and through everyday practices of surveillance, examination, and classification. Modern power does not only forbid; it produces. It produces docile bodies, trained to sit still, work on time, and examine themselves. Foucault's account made power visible in places where earlier theories had not looked: the timetable, the examination hall, the medical record.

## Examples

The three dimensions can be seen in a single workplace. The first dimension appears when a manager overrules a worker's request. The second appears when the agenda of a staff meeting never includes pay, because pay is declared a matter for head office. The third appears when workers come to see their low pay as a reflection of their own worth rather than a feature of how the firm distributes its income. None of these is reducible to the others, and a full account of power in the workplace must trace all three.

Power also operates at the level of states. A government exercises the first dimension when it passes a law; the second when it controls which stories the press covers; the third when it shapes the categories through which citizens understand themselves — as consumers rather than workers, as taxpayers rather than citizens. The study of power is therefore also the study of language, media, and education.

## Critique and Contemporary Relevance

Lukes's third dimension has been criticized for a serious problem: if people's preferences can be shaped against their interests, who decides what their real interests are? Critics worry that the idea licenses the analyst to declare that people do not know their own good. Foucault's account faces a different objection: if power is everywhere, circulating through every relation, then resistance seems to have no outside from which to begin, and the theory offers little guidance for changing the world.

Power remains central to sociology and political science. It now frames research on digital platforms, where firms shape what users see and want; on global supply chains, where buyers in one country set the conditions of labour in another; and on intimate life, where feminist scholarship has shown how power operates in the household as much as in the parliament. The concept endures because every account of social order must answer the same question: whose will prevails, and how?
