# Colonialism: Domination, Extraction, and Rule

Colonialism is **the practice by which one people establishes political domination over another people and territory, ruling it from outside and reorganizing its economy, law, and culture to serve the colonizer's interests**. It differs from mere conquest or trade in its duration and its ambition: colonial rule builds administrations, settles populations, extracts resources, and remakes the colonized society itself — its land tenure, its languages, its schools, and its sense of who its people are. For anthropology, colonialism is not only a historical background but a constitutive condition: the discipline itself grew up studying peoples under colonial rule, and its central concepts were forged in that encounter.

## Definition & Historical Development

European colonialism expanded from the fifteenth century onward, first in the Americas, then across Asia, Africa, and the Pacific, so that by 1914 European powers and their settler offshoots controlled most of the world's land surface. Colonial rule took many forms: settler colonies that displaced indigenous peoples, plantation colonies built on enslaved labour, and indirect rule through local intermediaries. Everywhere it combined military force with administrative classification — censuses, maps, tribal registers, and legal codes that divided populations into races, tribes, and castes and then governed them through those categories.

Decolonization after 1945 ended most formal empires, but the anthropological study of colonialism did not end with independence. Former colonies inherited borders drawn by colonizers, economies organized around a single export, and bureaucracies built to control rather than to serve. Postcolonial scholars therefore distinguish colonialism, the historical system of direct rule, from coloniality, the persistence of colonial hierarchies of race, knowledge, and value after independence.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

**Aimé Césaire** (1913–2008) argued in *Discourse on Colonialism* (1950) that colonialism brutalized the colonizer as much as the colonized. Colonization, he wrote, dehumanizes even the most civilized man, because the habit of treating other human beings as inferior corrodes the colonizer's own humanity. Césaire's essay made the moral and psychological costs of empire central to its analysis, and it became a founding text of anti-colonial thought.

**Albert Memmi** (1920–2020) analyzed the colonial relationship from both sides in *The Colonizer and the Colonized* (1957). He showed that colonizer and colonized are locked in a single system: the colonizer legitimizes privilege by portraying the colonized as lazy, backward, and unfit to rule, while the colonized internalizes that image and then struggles against it. Memmi's portrait of the colonial situation — its economics, its racism, and its psychology — remains a classic account of how domination is lived.

**Frantz Fanon** (1925–1961) pushed the analysis toward liberation in *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961). Writing as a psychiatrist in Algeria, Fanon described colonialism as a Manichean world, split absolutely between colonizer and colonized, maintained by violence. Decolonization, for Fanon, therefore required a radical break — not only political independence but the remaking of the colonized subject, freed from the inferiority the colonial system had installed.

**Edward Said** (1935–2003) extended the critique to knowledge itself in *Orientalism* (1978). He showed how Western scholarship, literature, and art produced the "Orient" as an object — exotic, irrational, unchanging — and how that representation justified domination. Colonial power, Said argued, works through the authority to describe and define other peoples, making the study of representation itself a political act.

## Ethnographic Examples

Anthropologists have documented colonialism's everyday workings. In Africa, colonial administrators used anthropological knowledge to govern through "customary law" and invented tribal authorities, freezing fluid identities into fixed categories that later shaped postcolonial politics. In the Pacific, missionaries and officials remade kinship, gender, and land tenure, while islanders adapted, resisted, and reinterpreted colonial institutions in their own terms. In Latin America, the colonial categories of Indian, mestizo, and Spaniard organized labour, taxation, and status for three centuries, and their traces persist in contemporary inequalities of race and class.

Colonialism also shaped the conditions of anthropological fieldwork itself. Early ethnographers often depended on colonial officials for access, transport, and protection, and their writings were read by administrators seeking to govern more effectively. Recognizing this history has made contemporary anthropology more reflexive about who speaks for whom and to what ends.

## Contemporary Debates

Current scholarship debates whether colonialism is past or ongoing. Settler colonial studies argues that in places such as the United States, Canada, Australia, and Israel/Palestine, colonialism is a structure rather than an event — the elimination or marginalization of indigenous peoples continues through land policy, law, and settlement. Others emphasize neocolonialism: economic dependence, debt, and the power of international institutions that reproduce colonial inequalities without formal rule. For anthropology, the enduring lesson is that colonialism must be studied not only as conquest but as a durable system of classification, extraction, and representation whose effects still shape the world ethnographers inhabit.
