# Signifier and Signified

A central conceptual dyad in Saussure's theory of the linguistic sign. For Saussure, the linguistic sign is not a link between a word and a thing, but between a **signifier** (Signifiant: the sound image or written form of the word) and a **signified** (Signifié: the mental concept it evokes).

## Arbitrariness of the Relation

The relation between signifier and signified is **conventional, not natural**: there is no logical or imitative reason why the sound sequence *shajara* should evoke the particular concept of "tree" rather than some other sequence. The evidence is that different languages link the same concept to entirely different signifiers (tree, arbre, shajara). The partial exception that Saussure himself mentions is onomatopoeia, which is limited and does not overturn the general rule.

## The Difference Between Signified and Referent

Saussure's sign does not include the thing itself: the signified (Signifié) is distinct from what later theorists call the "referent" (the actual thing in the external world). The signified is a mental concept within consciousness, not the actual tree itself. This allows the linguistic sign to refer to abstract concepts (such as justice or freedom) that have no concrete material referent at all, in addition to tangible objects.

## Value Within the System

For Saussure, neither face of the sign has its value in isolation. Value (*valeur*) comes from difference within the system. His own comparison is lexical: French *mouton* covers both the animal and the meat, whereas English divides that range between *sheep* and *mutton*. The French sign and the English signs are not equivalent items with different labels. Each has the value fixed by what stands next to it in its own language. Later work on color vocabularies is a separate research tradition and is not evidence Saussure offered.

## Impact

This dyad became an influential model for structuralist semiology, as Roland Barthes and others borrowed it to analyze cultural sign systems in general (fashion, advertising, myth). It differs from the triadic sign model of Charles S. Peirce (representamen, object, interpretant), which was developed independently. Within linguistics itself, it remains the standard entry point for teaching the theory of the sign and the basis for every subsequent discussion of the relation between language, thought, and reality.

## The Context of Formulating the Concept

Ferdinand de Saussure developed this conception in his lectures at the University of Geneva in three courses between 1906 and 1911, but he did not publish them himself. After his death, his students Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye compiled them from student notes, and they appeared under the title *Cours de linguistique générale* in 1916. This indirect origin explains some of the ambiguity and the multiple interpretations that later surrounded his precise concepts, including the signifier/signified dyad itself.
