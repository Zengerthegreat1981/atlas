# Cultivation Practices

Cultivation practices are the organised ways in which people grow plants for food, fibre, medicine, and ritual — clearing land, preparing soil, sowing, tending, harvesting, and storing the crop. They range from the shifting cultivation of forest gardens to the irrigated rice terraces of Asia, from the kitchen gardens of a village to the vast plantations of the modern world. Anthropology studies cultivation not only as a technology but as a way of life: it shapes settlement, kinship, ritual, and politics, and it carries a body of knowledge — about soils, seasons, seeds, and spirits — that is among the most sophisticated that any society possesses.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish several broad types of cultivation. Shifting cultivation, also called swidden or slash-and-burn, involves clearing a patch of forest, growing crops for a few years, and then letting it return to fallow while the cultivator moves on. Intensive cultivation, by contrast, works the same land year after year, using irrigation, terracing, manuring, and careful rotation to sustain fertility. Horticulture, in the anthropological sense, is small-scale gardening with hand tools, while agriculture proper is usually taken to involve the plough, draft animals, and larger fields.

These types are not stages on a ladder of progress. Shifting cultivation, long dismissed as primitive and wasteful, is in many environments the most sustainable and productive way to farm, and its practitioners possess detailed knowledge of forest ecology. Harold Conklin's study of Hanunoo agriculture in the Philippines, published in 1957, showed that shifting cultivators could name hundreds of plants, distinguish dozens of soil types, and manage a complex rotation of crops and fallow — a system as intricate as any modern agronomy.

## Origins and Development

The study of cultivation was transformed by Ester Boserup's *The Conditions of Agricultural Growth* (1965). Against the Malthusian view that population growth was limited by food supply, Boserup argued that population pressure drove agricultural intensification: as more people needed to be fed from the same land, farmers adopted shorter fallows, better tools, and more labour-intensive methods. Intensification was not progress but a response to necessity, and it often meant more work for less return per hour — a finding that overturned the assumption that agricultural development was always an improvement.

Clifford Geertz's *Agricultural Involution* (1963) offered a parallel account from Java, where the introduction of Dutch colonial demands and the growth of population led to ever more elaborate and labour-intensive rice cultivation — an involution, in which the system became more complex without becoming more productive per person. Geertz's term captured a pattern found in many parts of the world: cultivation systems can elaborate themselves to absorb more labour without raising living standards.

James C. Scott, in *The Art of Not Being Governed* (2009), added a political dimension, arguing that in the uplands of Southeast Asia people deliberately chose shifting cultivation, dispersed settlement, and crops that were hard to tax — such as root crops that could be left in the ground — as a strategy of evading the state. Cultivation, in this view, is not only an economic choice but a political one: the way people farm can be a way of staying free.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One major debate concerns the relationship between cultivation and social organisation. Does intensive agriculture produce hierarchy, as early theorists argued, because irrigation and stored surpluses require managers and chiefs? Or can intensive cultivation be organised egalitarianly, as Robert McC. Netting showed in *Smallholders, Householders* (1993), where smallholder farming sustained stable, household-based societies without centralised power? The evidence suggests that cultivation enables hierarchy but does not determine it: the same technique can serve a chiefdom or a commune.

A second debate concerns gender. In many societies, women do most of the cultivation — clearing, planting, weeding, harvesting — while men control the land and the crop. Boserup was among the first to document this systematically, showing that development projects which gave land, credit, and training to men often undermined women's livelihoods. The gender division of cultivation remains a central concern of anthropology and development.

A third dimension is knowledge. Cultivation depends on what is now called traditional ecological knowledge: the accumulated understanding of soils, weather, pests, and varieties that farmers have built up over generations. This knowledge is increasingly recognised as a scientific resource in its own right — in the breeding of resilient crops, in agroforestry, and in the adaptation to climate change — and its loss, as rural populations shrink and seeds are replaced by commercial varieties, is a matter of global concern.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Cultivation practices are at the centre of the great questions of our time: how to feed a growing population, how to farm without destroying the soil and the climate, and who controls the food system. Industrial agriculture, with its chemical inputs and monocultures, has raised yields but at a heavy ecological cost, and there is growing interest in the practices that anthropologists have documented — intercropping, agroforestry, fallow management, and the use of diverse local varieties — as models for a more sustainable agriculture.

The critique of cultivation studies has also been important. Early anthropology sometimes romanticised the smallholder and ignored the hardship of farming, the hunger of the lean season, and the inequalities within farming communities. Contemporary work is more attentive to the politics of cultivation: to land grabs, to the power of seed and agrochemical companies, and to the struggles of farmers to keep their land and their knowledge. Cultivation, like all human practices, is a site of both ingenuity and conflict.
