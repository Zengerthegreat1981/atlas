# Austrian Business Cycle Theory

**Austrian business cycle theory** is a theoretical framework whose monetary setting was laid out by **Ludwig von Mises** in *The Theory of Money and Credit* (1912; *Theorie des Geldes und der Umlaufsmittel*), drawing on Knut Wicksell's distinction between the natural and the market rate of interest and on Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk's theory of capital. Mises stated the cycle argument more fully in later essays, and **Friedrich Hayek** elaborated it in *Monetary Theory and the Trade Cycle* (1929; English 1933) and *Prices and Production* (1931). Hayek shared the 1974 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel with Gunnar Myrdal for work on money, economic fluctuations, and the interdependence of economic and institutional phenomena. It links the causes of unsustainable investment booms and the economic crises that follow them to an artificial credit expansion created by banks through lowering the interest rate, without a real basis in the genuine voluntary saving of individuals.

## Interest as a signal of real saving

The theory starts from the idea that in a free, undistorted market the interest rate reflects individuals' real preferences between present consumption and saving for future investment. A low interest rate means that individuals save a great deal (preferring deferred consumption), which provides real resources sufficient to finance additional long-term investments without any shortage of resources. When banks artificially lower the interest rate through credit expansion, rather than in response to a real increase in actual saving, they send a misleading signal to entrepreneurs that additional saved resources are available, when in fact they are not.

## Misallocation of investment across stages of production

Entrepreneurs respond to this misleading signal by embarking on long-term investment projects (such as building factories or infrastructure) that require real resources (labour, raw materials, machinery) exceeding what is actually available from real saving in the economy. This creates competition for scarce resources between different stages of production (immediate consumer goods versus long-term capital goods), and raises prices and wages in the expanding sectors in an unsustainable way.

## The inevitable correction: depression

When it eventually becomes clear that real saving was not sufficient to complete all these ambitious investment projects, many firms are forced to halt unfinished projects or liquidate them at a loss. A painful correction, a depression, then occurs that re-aligns the structure of actual production with consumers' real preferences for saving and consumption. Austrians hold that liquidating the unsustainable projects is a necessary correction, because it removes the misallocation, even though the process is painful. They argue that trying to avoid it through further credit expansion, as Keynesian policies recommend, postpones the problem and magnifies it rather than solving it.

## A confrontation with Keynesian theory

This theory stands in direct contradiction to John Maynard Keynes's diagnosis of the causes of the Great Depression and his remedy. Where Keynes saw the solution in stimulating aggregate demand through government action (by public spending or tax cuts) to put idle resources back to work, the Austrians hold that the root of the problem predates the depression itself, in the artificial credit expansion that preceded it. They argue that additional government stimulus entrenches further structural distortions rather than addressing the root cause. This methodological disagreement was at the heart of the famous debate between Hayek and Keynes in the 1930s.

## Criticism from the mainstream

Many mainstream economists, including Milton Friedman, who was himself close to the liberal tradition, have criticized this theory, and it remains a minority view within academic macroeconomics compared with monetary, Keynesian and real business cycle accounts. A standard objection is that it does not convincingly explain why businessmen, who are assumed to be rational, repeatedly fail to anticipate the boom and bust if the mechanism is known. Friedman also stressed the weak empirical support for a link between the size of a boom and the size of the subsequent bust. The Austrians reply that credit expansion creates misleading price signals that are difficult to distinguish from genuine ones, even for rational agents, in real time.
