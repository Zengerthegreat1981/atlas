# Habit & Character Formation

Moral character formation is the process by which an individual's stable dispositions toward virtuous or vicious action are shaped through repetition and practice. Through this process, virtuous acts shift from a laborious act of will into a firm disposition from which action flows easily and with pleasure. The idea assumes that virtue is not abstract theoretical knowledge but a practical capacity (*hexis*) acquired through action, not through thinking alone.

This concept is central to the Islamic ethical tradition, especially in the work of Ibn Miskawayh in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (The Refinement of Character and the Purification of Dispositions). He defined character (*khuluq*) as "a state of the soul that calls it to its actions without deliberation or reflection," and divided character into natural dispositions and dispositions acquired through training and habit. He affirmed that repeating virtuous acts fixes a firm form in the soul. Al-Ghazali followed the same approach in *Ihya' Ulum al-Din*, likening the refinement of the soul to taming a wild animal that requires patient training until it becomes docile by nature. Al-Farabi, in *Tahsil al-Sa'ada* (The Attainment of Happiness), linked the acquisition of virtue to upbringing within a well-ordered city that provides a sound educational environment, in a framework influenced by Plato and Aristotle.

The deeper philosophical roots go back to Aristotle in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, who distinguished between intellectual virtue acquired through teaching and ethical virtue acquired through habit, and affirmed the well-known formulation: "We become just by doing just acts, and brave by doing brave acts." Aristotle held that virtuous action hits the mean between excess and deficiency, and that it be repeated within an educational environment (*paideia*) that directs the young person toward the proper pleasure accompanying virtuous action, rather than toward false pleasure. In recent times, the philosophers Philippa Foot and Rosalind Hursthouse have developed this perspective within the revival of virtue ethics, emphasizing that virtues are integrated dispositions of perception, emotion, and action together, not merely mechanical behavior.

This view is opposed by the "situationist" argument in social psychology, which relies on experiments such as Milgram's obedience studies (1960s) and, more controversially, the Stanford prison experiment; John Doris and Gilbert Harman are prominent philosophical proponents. It holds that moral behavior is influenced by situational context far more than by stable personality traits, which casts doubt on the existence of a stable "disposition" in the Aristotelian sense. Proponents of virtue ethics reply that genuine virtue is precisely what withstands situational pressure, and that the rarity of complete virtue does not invalidate its possibility.

Example: a physician who has become accustomed to honesty with patients in minor matters finds himself able to tell a difficult truth in a critical situation without hesitation, because honesty has become part of his character rather than a decision made anew each time.

This concept has important practical educational consequences. If virtue is acquired through habit, the responsibility of the family, school, and society in raising children to practice small virtuous behaviors early outweighs the mere instruction of them in theoretical rules, since a disposition formed in childhood is difficult to change in adulthood. This explains the insistence of Ibn Miskawayh and al-Ghazali on the importance of the earliest educational stage in a person's life, and on the necessity of choosing good companions and a suitable environment as two practical conditions indispensable for entrenching virtue in the soul.
