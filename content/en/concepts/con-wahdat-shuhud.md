# Wahdat al-Shuhud (Unity of Witness)

Wahdat al-shuhud is a position in Sufi monotheism holding that oneness lies in the witnessing of the wayfarer (*salik*), not in existence itself. The world truly exists and is other than God. But the gnostic, in the state of annihilation (*fana'*), witnesses only God, and so takes the oneness to be real when it is only in his witnessing. This position stands in contrast to **Wahdat al-wujud** (Unity of Being), attributed to Ibn Arabi and his followers, which holds that the only true existent is one.

## The Origin in 'Ala' al-Dawla al-Simnani

The first theoretical formulation of the distinction is attributed to 'Ala' al-Dawla al-Simnani (d. 736 AH/1336 CE), one of the shaykhs of the Kubrawiyya order in the Sufi lodge of Sufiabad near Simnan in Iran. He criticized Ibn Arabi from within the path, distinguishing between "existential monotheism" (*tawhid wujudi*) and "witnessing monotheism" (*tawhid shuhudi*), and held the second to be safer. Jamal Elias studied his life and thought in *The Throne Carrier of God* (1995). Rizvi, in *A History of Sufism in India* (1978–1983), traced the transmission of this distinction to the Indian Naqshbandiyya.

## Ahmad Sirhindi: The Renewal of the Second Millennium

Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi (d. 1034 AH/1624 CE), known as the "Renewer of the Second Millennium" (*mujaddid alf-i thani*) within the Naqshbandi order in India, became famous for this view. In his *Maktubat* (Letters) he describes the union the wayfarer experiences as a state of perception that gives way to "sobriety" (*sahw*), that God is distinct from the world, and that the relationship between them is not one of unity. Yohanan Friedmann's study *Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi: An Outline of His Thought and a Study of His Image in the Eyes of Posterity* (1971) showed that his position on Ibn Arabi was more composite than is commonly known.

## Comparison with Wahdat al-Wujud

The two positions differ in the following respect: Wahdat al-wujud makes annihilation an unveiling of a truth, while Wahdat al-shuhud makes it a subjective state that is followed by a return to multiplicity and to the Sharia. For this reason some regarded the second position as closer to the positions of al-Junayd, and to that of Ibn Taymiyya, who accused the proponents of the first of "union" (*ittihad*) in *Majmu' al-fatawa*. Yet Ibn Taymiyya himself also rejected an annihilation that leads to the denial of otherness.

## Criticism and Reservations

Some scholars argue that the difference between being and witnessing is less sharp than has been claimed. The distinction was a means of bringing Ibn Arabi closer to the Sharia. William Chittick, in *The Sufi Path of Knowledge* (1989), argues that Ibn Arabi himself affirms both transcendence (*tanzih*) and immanence (*tashbih*), so that he cannot be reduced to "union". Moreover, the disagreement rests on the translation of difficult terms, and scholars do not agree on what "being" (*wujud*) encompasses in Ibn Arabi.
