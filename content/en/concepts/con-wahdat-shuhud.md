# Wahdat al-Shuhud (Unity of Witness)

Wahdat al-shuhud is a position in Sufi monotheism holding that oneness lies in the witnessing of the wayfarer (*salik*), not in existence itself. The world truly exists and is other than God. But the gnostic, in the state of annihilation (*fana'*), witnesses only God, and so takes the oneness to be real when it is only in his witnessing. This position stands in contrast to **Wahdat al-wujud** (Unity of Being), attributed to Ibn Arabi and his followers, which holds that the only true existent is one.

## 'Ala' al-Dawla al-Simnani

An early formulation of the distinction is attributed to 'Ala' al-Dawla al-Simnani (659–736 AH / 1261–1336 CE), a shaykh of the Kubrawiyya who settled at the lodge of Sufiabad near Simnan in Iran. He criticized Ibn Arabi from within the path, distinguishing "existential monotheism" (*tawhid wujudi*) from "witnessing monotheism" (*tawhid shuhudi*), and held the second to be safer. Jamal J. Elias studied his life and thought in *The Throne Carrier of God* (1995). S. A. A. Rizvi, in *A History of Sufism in India* (1978–1983), followed the later life of this distinction in the Indian Naqshbandiyya. The wording *wahdat al-shuhud* became a technical slogan above all with Ahmad Sirhindi.

## Ahmad Sirhindi: The Renewal of the Second Millennium

Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi (d. 1034 AH/1624 CE), known as the "Renewer of the Second Millennium" (*mujaddid alf-i thani*) within the Naqshbandi order in India, became famous for this view. In his *Maktubat* (Letters) he describes the union the wayfarer experiences as a state of perception that gives way to "sobriety" (*sahw*), that God is distinct from the world, and that the relationship between them is not one of unity. Yohanan Friedmann's study *Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi: An Outline of His Thought and a Study of His Image in the Eyes of Posterity* (1971) argued that his position on Ibn Arabi was more composite than is commonly assumed.

## Comparison with Wahdat al-Wujud

As the contrast is usually drawn, wahdat al-wujud treats annihilation as the unveiling of a truth about existence, while wahdat al-shuhud treats it as a state of the wayfarer, followed by a return to multiplicity and to the Sharia. Some writers therefore placed the second view nearer to al-Junayd's preference for sobriety after ecstasy, and nearer to Ibn Taymiyya, who criticized wahdat al-wujud, together with doctrines of union (*ittihad*) and indwelling (*hulul*), in fatwas later gathered in *Majmu' al-fatawa*. Ibn Taymiyya also rejected an annihilation that denies the world's otherness.

## Criticism and Reservations

Some scholars argue that the difference between being and witnessing is less sharp than has been claimed. Some read the distinction as a way of bringing Ibn Arabi closer to the Sharia. William Chittick, in *The Sufi Path of Knowledge* (1989), argues that Ibn Arabi himself affirms both transcendence (*tanzih*) and immanence (*tashbih*), so that he cannot be reduced to "union". Moreover, the disagreement rests on the translation of difficult terms, and scholars do not agree on what "being" (*wujud*) encompasses in Ibn Arabi.
