# The Nag Hammadi Library and "Lost Christianities"

In December 1945, an Egyptian peasant near the town of Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt found a clay jar containing thirteen bound leather codices in Coptic, comprising about 52 texts, most of them previously unknown, including the *Gospel of Thomas*, the *Gospel of Philip*, the *Gospel of Truth*, and other Sethian and Valentinian texts. A widely held hypothesis is that monks of a nearby Pachomian monastery buried them in the later fourth century CE, perhaps in the aftermath of the Festal Letter of Athanasius of Alexandria (367 CE), which listed the books of the New Testament canon and condemned other writings; the codices themselves are fourth-century copies of texts composed earlier. Not all of the texts are Christian or Gnostic: the collection also includes Hermetic works and a fragment of Plato's *Republic*.

## From Archaeological Discovery to a Concept in Early Christian History

Nag Hammadi did not remain merely a philological discovery. It fed a central thesis in the critical historical study of early Christianity: that Christianity in its first two centuries was not a single doctrinal mass from which deviant "heresies" later branched off, but a broad field of competing communities (Pauline, Valentinian, Sethian, Ebionite). The communities that lost were later stamped "Gnostic" or "heretical" retrospectively by the victorious side, which became "orthodoxy." The thesis builds on Walter Bauer's *Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity* (1934). Elaine Pagels presented the Nag Hammadi texts to a wide audience in *The Gnostic Gospels* (1979), and Bart Ehrman later gave this reading a broad readership in *Lost Christianities* (2003), citing the Nag Hammadi texts as direct evidence of this plurality. The category "Gnosticism" itself has been questioned by scholars such as Michael Williams and Karen King as a modern construct that groups diverse texts too neatly.

## Methodological Boundary with the Philosophical Gnostic School

The atlas treats Gnosticism as a religious-philosophical movement in its own right, in a separate profile that discusses its doctrines and content (the aeons, Sophia, the unknown God). This entry does not repeat that. Instead, it addresses Nag Hammadi as a discovery of material evidence and its methodological impact on writing the history of early Christianity.

## The Actual Circumstances of the Discovery

The story of the discovery itself is told in details that attracted wide interest: the peasant Muhammad Ali al-Samman found the jar while digging for natural fertilizer (sabakh) at the foot of Jabal al-Tarif, and at first hesitated, fearing that the jar contained a jinn, before discovering the leather manuscripts it held. The manuscripts were then dispersed through local antiquities traders and middlemen, before their collection and academic study began through international efforts that lasted decades. James M. Robinson, general editor of the standard English edition, reconstructed this history in detail, though some elements of the traditional account are disputed.

## The Significance of the *Gospel of Thomas* in Particular

The *Gospel of Thomas*, a collection of 114 sayings attributed to Jesus without a narrative frame (unlike the four canonical Gospels), holds a special place in academic literature because it gives no account of the crucifixion or of the bodily resurrection (its one reference to the cross, in saying 55, concerns discipleship). This led some researchers (such as Helmut Koester) to ask whether it represents an early tradition parallel to the synoptic Gospel tradition rather than a later deviation from it. The debate on its dating (proposals range from the middle of the first century to the second century) remains open to this day. The surviving Coptic text is a fourth-century copy, and Greek fragments of earlier versions were found at Oxyrhynchus.
