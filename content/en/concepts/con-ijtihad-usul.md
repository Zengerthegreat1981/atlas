# Ijtihad (Independent Legal Reasoning)

**Ijtihad** is the qualified jurist's (*mujtahid*) exertion of utmost effort to derive a practical legal ruling from its detailed evidence, in a matter where there is no text that is definitive in its authenticity and meaning and no established consensus. Ijtihad is the central mechanism that keeps the Sharia living and able to address new events across the ages, serving as a continuation of the process of deriving rulings after the cessation of revelation. Its classical textual support includes the report of Mu'adh ibn Jabal, sent to Yemen, who said he would strive to form his own opinion when he found no ruling in the Qur'an or the Sunna, and the report that a judge who exerts himself and is correct has two rewards, and one reward if he errs.

## Conditions for the Mujtahid

Usul scholars required the mujtahid to meet demanding epistemic and methodological conditions: mastery of the Quranic verses and prophetic traditions that bear on legal rulings (not necessarily memorizing the whole Quran and Sunna, but what is relevant to rulings); knowledge of the points of consensus and of juristic disagreement, so as not to contradict established consensus; proficiency in the Arabic language and its semantic conventions; knowledge of usul al-fiqh and the methods of inference (such as analogy); and knowledge of abrogating and abrogated texts, so that no ruling is derived from an abrogated text. These conditions, taken together, are demanding. Most usulists regarded ijtihad as a communal obligation (*fard kifaya*), and many held that no age can be without a mujtahid, while others thought the highest degree of ijtihad had become rare. Whether fully independent mujtahids existed after the era of the founding imams is therefore a subject of debate among jurists and historians.

## Types of Ijtihad

Scholars distinguished between **absolute (independent) ijtihad**, which is reasoning about the principles of inference themselves, as the founding imams of the schools did, and **restricted or partial ijtihad (ijtihad within a school)**, which is reasoning carried out within the principles of an existing school to derive subsidiary rulings that the school's founding imam did not explicitly state, while adhering to its general rules. They also distinguished *ijtihad of preference and fatwa*, which is the selection of the more probable of existing school opinions without independent original derivation.

## The Debate over the Closing of the Gate of Ijtihad

Beginning roughly in the fourth century AH (tenth century CE), a juristic discourse emerged holding that the gate of absolute ijtihad had been closed once the four schools became established, and that what remained was restricted ijtihad within the schools. Contemporary historians, notably Wael Hallaq, have disputed the historical accuracy of this thesis, pointing to the continued practice of actual ijtihad even after what is called the "closing of the gate" in theory.

## Its Impact on Modern Legal Reform

Modern reformist and revivalist movements revived the call for ijtihad as an alternative to rigid taqlid, demanding direct reconsideration of the texts to address contemporary issues. This produced a sharp debate over the standards of contemporary ijtihad and the legitimacy of its exercise outside the traditional frameworks of juristic training.
