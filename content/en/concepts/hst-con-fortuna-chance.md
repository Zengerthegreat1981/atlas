# Fortune and Chance in Historical Causation

In Greco-Roman historiography, the tension between rational human agency and the unpredictable forces of chance (*tyche* in Greek, *fortuna* in Latin) formed one of the primary philosophical problems of historical causation. Classical historians recognized that even the most meticulously prepared plans, the most formidable armies, and the wisest political constitutions were vulnerable to unforeseen contingencies, unexpected weather, sudden reversals, and moral panic. Consequently, historians sought to delineate the boundaries between intelligible cause-and-effect relationships and the erratic interventions of fortune.

## Greek Historiography: Solonian Instability and Thucydidean Prudence

In the archaic and classical Greek imagination, the unpredictability of human affairs was rooted in the fragility of mortal prosperity. In Book 1 of Herodotus's *Histories*, the Athenian sage Solon warns the wealthy Lydian king Croesus that human life is entirely subject to chance (*pan esti anthropos symphore*, 1.32), and that no man can be called happy until he has brought his life to a noble close without catastrophe. Herodotus viewed fortune through a theological lens, where divine envy (*phthonos theon*) humbled excessive prosperity and restored equilibrium.

Thucydides fundamentally redefined the problem of chance by stripping it of theological overtones. In his *History of the Peloponnesian War*, Thucydides juxtaposed *gnome* (rational calculation, foresight, human intellect) against *tyche* (chance, irrational contingency, the unforeseen). In his speeches, Pericles argued that rational policy and naval discipline could manage risk and minimize the influence of chance.

However, Thucydides repeatedly depicted *tyche* upsetting rational expectations. The sudden outbreak of the plague at Athens (430 BCE) and the storm that drove Athenian ships to Pylos in 425 BCE, which led to the Spartan surrender at Sphacteria, are examples of contingencies that overturned calculation.

## Hellenistic Historiography and the Polybian Dilemma

During the Hellenistic period, *Tyche* was elevated from a literary concept into a ubiquitous Hellenistic divinity, personifying the sudden rises and catastrophic falls of Hellenistic monarchs and empires. In his *Histories*, Polybius engaged in a lifelong struggle to integrate *Tyche* into a rigorous, pragmatic historical methodology.

On one hand, Polybius insisted upon strict rational causation. In Book 36 (ch. 17), he formulated a clear methodological rule: whenever the causes of an event can be identified—such as military discipline, constitutional stability, or logistical preparation—the historian must never attribute the outcome to *Tyche* or the gods. Fortune or the gods may be invoked only where no cause can be found, such as unusual weather.

On the other hand, Polybius assigned *Tyche* a profound dual role in his overarching universal history. First, *Tyche* designated the genuinely unexpected or inexplicable accident, such as unseasonable frost or sudden plague. Second, and more importantly, Polybius personified *Tyche* as a quasi-providential force directing the disparate affairs of the entire inhabited world (*oikoumene*) toward a single teleological conclusion: the universal empire of Rome (Book 1.4). Furthermore, Polybius observed that *Tyche* was the ultimate agent of tragic reversal (*peripeteia*). Recording Scipio Aemilianus weeping over the ruins of Carthage in 146 BCE, Polybius quoted Scipio reciting verses from Homer about the fall of Troy, reflecting that the same reversal might one day befall Rome.

## Roman Historiography: Fortuna and Virtus

In Roman culture, *Fortuna* was revered both as an unpredictable deity and as a divine ally of the Roman people (*Fortuna Populi Romani*). Roman historians explored how *fortuna* interacted with Roman *virtus* (manly courage, moral excellence). In *Ab Urbe Condita*, Livy presented Roman imperial expansion as a harmonious collaboration between divine *fortuna* and ancestral *virtus*: fortune favored the brave and the pious, while deserting the reckless and the unjust.

Julius Caesar developed a distinctive personal conception of *fortuna*. In *De Bello Gallico* and *De Bello Civili*, Caesar acknowledged that fortune held immense power in war, frequently turning minor tactical incidents into decisive routs. Yet Caesar claimed a unique personal bond with fortune (*Fortuna Caesaris*), using his boldness (*audacia*) and speed (*celeritas*) to seize opportunities created by chance before his adversaries could react.

By contrast, Sallust offered a cynical assessment in *Bellum Catilinae* (ch. 8), declaring that "Fortune rules in everything; she celebrates or obscures all things according to her whim rather than truth." Under the early Principate, Tacitus viewed the unpredictable terror of the Julio-Claudian dynastic court through profound skepticism. In *Annals* 6.22, Tacitus reflected on whether human events were governed by unalterable fate (*fatum*), random chance (*fors* or *casus*), or complete divine indifference, leaving the question open.
