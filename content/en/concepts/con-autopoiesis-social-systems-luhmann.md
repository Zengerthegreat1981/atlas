# Autopoiesis and Operational Closure (Luhmann)

A central concept in Niklas Luhmann's social theory, borrowed from biology, that redefines what counts as the basic "component" of a social system in a way radically at odds with earlier sociological tradition.

## Content

Luhmann borrowed the concept of "autopoiesis" from the Chilean biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela, who used it to describe the capacity of living organisms to produce and reproduce their own components from within themselves, preserving their organizational unity despite continuous exchange with the external environment. Luhmann applied this concept to society in a radical way: he claimed that a social system does not consist of human individuals (contrary to almost the entire classical and modern sociological tradition, from Durkheim to Parsons, which assumes the individual or individual action to be the basic unit of analysis) but consists exclusively of "communications," the reciprocal units of meaning that link one element to another within the system. These communications, according to Luhmann, produce and reproduce themselves autopoietically according to their own internal logic, in a state of "operational closure" from their external environment, including the individuals themselves, who in this conception become merely an "environment" surrounding the social system and not an internal component of it. This environment (individual consciousness, the biological body) is nevertheless a necessary condition for the system to continue operating, without being part of it analytically.

## Impact

This conception offered a radical and highly abstract reformulation of the concept of the "social system" that provoked wide controversy over its explanatory usefulness and philosophical limits, especially in the long-running exchange with Jürgen Habermas. Their 1971 joint volume *Theorie der Gesellschaft oder Sozialtechnologie* predates the autopoietic turn, but Habermas later argued, in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985), that systems theory leaves no room for normative critique of society. Luhmann's reply was that sociology should observe and describe society rather than take a normative standpoint within it. The theory deeply influenced many fields beyond sociology proper, from the theory of autopoietic law (notably Gunther Teubner) to the analysis of organizations.

## Specialized Subsystems

Luhmann applied this framework to the analysis of the functional subsystems of modern society (law, economy, politics, science, religion), holding that each of these subsystems reproduces itself through its own binary code that cannot be reduced to the code of another system: the legal system processes the world through the code "legal/illegal," the economy through "payment/non-payment," and science through "true/false." Hence one system cannot "understand" another through its own internal logic; it can only "irritate" or provoke another system through a communication that the latter processes according to its own code, not according to the code of its source.

## Psychic and Social Systems

Luhmann treats consciousness ("psychic systems") and society as two distinct autopoietic systems: consciousness operates with thoughts and society with communications. Each is part of the other's environment, and they are linked by "structural coupling," above all through language, which allows consciousness to irritate communication and vice versa without either determining the other. Communication itself is analyzed as a synthesis of information, utterance and understanding. Critics have objected that removing human beings from society is counterintuitive and that the theory is hard to test empirically.
