# Communicative Competence

A central concept in linguistic anthropology, developed by Dell Hymes (1927–2009) in his foundational article "On Communicative Competence" (written in 1966, published in 1972). It refers to the ability to use language in a socially appropriate way: not knowledge of grammatical rules alone, but knowing when, where, with whom and how one may speak, and in what style and register suits the situation.

## Content

Hymes formulated the concept as a direct response to Noam Chomsky's distinction between **competence** (the abstract grammatical knowledge latent in an ideal speaker of a homogeneous language community) and **performance** (the actual realisation, marred by errors and extra-linguistic factors). Hymes argued that this distinction arbitrarily excludes the social dimension of language from the domain of "competence" itself, whereas a child acquiring language learns not only to construct grammatically correct sentences, but also, with equal necessity, when speech is socially **appropriate**: when to remain silent, in what form to address someone older, and how to open and close a conversation according to the cultural context. Communicative competence thus includes four integrated dimensions by which any utterance may be evaluated: whether it is possible grammatically (possible), whether it is socially appropriate (appropriate), whether it is feasible in practice (feasible), and whether it is actually performed in a real context (performed).

## Applications

- **Ethnography of communication**: The concept became the cornerstone of Hymes's approach to the ethnography of communication, which studies "speech events" across cultures with his analytical framework SPEAKING.
- **Foreign language teaching**: From the 1970s, the field of language teaching adopted the concept of communicative competence as the basis of what is known as "Communicative Language Teaching", in which a learner is assessed by his ability to communicate effectively in real contexts, rather than by memorising isolated grammatical rules alone.
- **Sociolinguistics**: Later researchers (such as John Gumperz, in interactional sociolinguistics) studied how speakers from different cultural backgrounds interpret one another's conversational cues, extending Hymes's concern with appropriateness to everyday interaction.

## Limitations

The concept has sometimes been criticised as too loose to be operationalised with enough precision for rigorous experimental measurement. It has also been criticised on the grounds that the standards of "social appropriateness" are themselves variable and relative to such a degree that a full definition of "competence" is difficult even within a single language community, let alone its generalisation across cultures that differ radically in their communicative norms.
