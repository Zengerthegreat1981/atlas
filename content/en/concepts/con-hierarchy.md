# Hierarchy: The Ranking of Persons, Groups, and Things

**Hierarchy** is the arrangement of persons, groups, offices, or categories in a ranked order, in which some stand above others and are owed deference, obedience, or privilege. Hierarchy is everywhere in human life: in age and generation, in the ranks of an army or a bureaucracy, in the grades of a school, in the seating of a feast, and in the castes and estates that have ordered whole civilisations. It differs from stratification, its close neighbour, in emphasis: stratification concerns the unequal distribution of wealth, power, and prestige, while hierarchy concerns the principle of ranking itself — the idea that the world is ordered from higher to lower, and that this order is right, natural, or sacred. Anthropologists study hierarchy both as a social fact and as a value, asking how ranking is justified, how it is experienced, and how it is resisted.

## Definition and Development

The classic sociological treatment of hierarchy comes from Max Weber, whose *Economy and Society* (written 1911–1920, published posthumously 1922, English edition 1968) described bureaucracy as a hierarchy of offices: each office stands under the supervision of a higher one, with fixed duties, written rules, and a career ladder of promotion. Weber's hierarchy is a rational instrument, designed for efficiency and predictability. But Weber also knew that hierarchy has other sources: in traditional authority, rank is inherited and sanctified by custom; in charismatic authority, it follows the extraordinary person. In all three cases, hierarchy is a way of organising obedience — of making it clear who gives orders and who takes them.

Louis Dumont, in *Homo Hierarchicus* (1966, English edition 1970), gave hierarchy its most famous anthropological treatment, through the Indian caste system. For Dumont, hierarchy is not primarily a ladder of power but an encompassing value: the pure encompasses the impure, the whole encompasses its parts, and rank expresses a religious order of the world. Castes are not merely unequal; they are ordered by a principle — purity and pollution — that everyone in the system recognises, including those at the bottom. Dumont's argument was controversial: critics said it took the Brahmanical view of caste for the view of the whole society, ignoring the power, violence, and resistance that sustain ranking. But his insight endures: hierarchy is not just who has more; it is a way of seeing the world as ordered, in which every being has its proper place.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

Morton Fried, in *The Evolution of Political Society* (1967), placed hierarchy in a comparative and historical frame. Fried distinguished egalitarian societies, in which no one has permanent authority over others, from rank societies, in which some positions carry higher prestige, and from stratified societies, in which rank is backed by differential access to resources. Marked hierarchy, in this scheme, is a product of history rather than a human constant, and it deepens as societies develop unequal access to resources and, eventually, states. Fried's sequence has been much revised, but the distinction between rank and stratification remains useful: a society can rank its members without making some of them rich and others poor.

Pierre Bourdieu, in *Distinction* (1979, English edition 1984), showed how hierarchy works in modern societies through taste. Preferences in food, music, clothing, and art are not innocent matters of personal liking but markers of rank: the tastes of the dominant classes are treated as refined, those of the dominated as vulgar, and the hierarchy of culture legitimises the hierarchy of class. Christopher Boehm, in *Hierarchy in the Forest* (1999), added the other side of the story: in egalitarian hunter-gatherer societies, hierarchy is actively prevented. People use ridicule, gossip, ostracism, and, in the extreme, assassination to cut down anyone who tries to dominate — a "reverse dominance hierarchy" in which the group as a whole dominates would-be dominators.

## Ethnographic Examples

Ethnography is rich in hierarchies of different kinds. Among the Nuer of South Sudan, hierarchy is minimal: age sets and lineages order social life, but no chief can command obedience, and leadership is a matter of persuasion. Among the chiefdoms of Polynesia, hierarchy is elaborate and sacred: chiefs are descended from gods, their persons are taboo, and the order of seating, speaking, and eating at feasts maps the order of the cosmos. In the villages of highland Madagascar, hierarchy is expressed through tombs: the placement, size, and decoration of ancestral tombs rank descent groups in a visible, permanent landscape of inequality.

Hierarchy is also expressed through the body. Who bows to whom, who speaks first, who eats first — these small acts of deference are the daily enactment of rank. In many societies, language itself is hierarchical: Javanese, Korean, and Japanese have elaborate levels of speech that mark the relative rank of speaker and listener, so that every sentence is also a statement about where each person stands.

## Contemporary Debates

Hierarchy is at the centre of current debates about inequality. One debate concerns whether hierarchy is natural or historical: evolutionary psychologists argue that humans have an innate tendency to form dominance hierarchies, while anthropologists point to the many societies that have lived without them and to the constant work required to maintain them.

A second debate concerns the hierarchies of the present: the corporate ladder, the academic pecking order, the ranking of nations and universities by league tables. Anthropologists argue that these are not neutral measures of merit but cultural artefacts, built on particular values and serving particular interests.
