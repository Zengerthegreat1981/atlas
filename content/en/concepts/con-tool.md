# Tool Making

Tool making — the manufacture and use of implements to extend the body's capacities — has long been treated as a defining human trait. The word "tool" comes from the Old English *tōl*, and the archaeological record of stone tools reaches back more than three million years, far earlier than the first fossils of our own species. For anthropology, tools are not merely practical objects: they are the material record of cognition, social learning, and cultural transmission. A handaxe, a spear-thrower, or a smartphone each embodies knowledge accumulated over generations and reveals how its makers think, cooperate, and imagine the world. To study tool making is to study how humans make themselves through making things.

## Origins and Development

The scientific study of tool making began with the recognition of deep human antiquity. In the nineteenth century, the discovery of stone tools alongside extinct animals established that humans had existed far longer than biblical chronology allowed. The twentieth century filled in the sequence: Oldowan choppers and flakes (around 2.6 million years ago), Acheulean handaxes (from about 1.7 million years ago), and the hafted, composite tools of the later Palaeolithic. Each stage implied new cognitive and social capacities — planning, teaching, and the coordination of many hands.

André Leroi-Gourhan, in *Gesture and Speech* (1993, first published in French in 1964–65), gave tool making its classic anthropological theory. For Leroi-Gourhan, tools are the externalisation of human action: the hand and the face, freed by bipedalism, project their operations outward into instruments. A tool is therefore not an add-on to a ready-made human but part of what makes us human — a second, artificial body. His work linked technology to the evolution of language, memory, and social life, and it remains a foundation of the anthropology of technology.

Kathy Schick and Nicholas Toth's *Making Silent Stones Speak* (1993) brought the study of early tool making to a wide audience, showing how experiments in knapping stone — reproducing ancient techniques by hand — reveal the skill, planning, and learning involved in even the simplest flake. Thomas Wynn, in "The Intelligence of Later Acheulean Hominids" (1979), argued that the symmetry of the handaxe implies spatial cognition of a kind once thought unique to modern humans, pushing the origins of complex thought far back in time.

Tim Ingold, in "Tool Use, Sociality and Intelligence" (1993), challenged the view of tools as mere instruments for achieving pre-formed goals. Tool use, he argued, is a skilled engagement with materials, learned through apprenticeship and embedded in social relations. The craftsperson does not impose a design on passive matter but works with the properties of stone, wood, or clay — a perspective that has reshaped the study of technology in anthropology.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns uniqueness: are humans the only tool makers? Chimpanzees termite-fish, crows bend wire, and sea otters crack shells with stones. Anthropologists now accept that tool use is widespread among animals, but argue that human tool making is distinctive in its cumulative character — each generation builds on the last, producing technologies no single individual could invent. This "ratchet effect" depends on social learning, language, and institutions of apprenticeship.

A second dimension is skill and the body. Making a tool is a bodily achievement: the knapper's hands, the potter's fingers, the weaver's rhythm. Anthropology of skill, developed by Ingold and others, studies how knowledge lives in the body rather than in the head — in gesture, habit, and attention. This perspective has been applied to crafts from blacksmithing to surgery, showing that expertise is a form of embodied cognition.

A third dimension is the social life of tools. Tools circulate as gifts, commodities, heirlooms, and ritual objects; they carry histories of ownership and use. A spear may be a weapon, a brideprice payment, and an ancestral relic at once. Studying tools therefore means studying exchange, value, and meaning as well as function.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Tool making remains central to anthropology's engagement with the present. The study of digital technologies — smartphones, algorithms, artificial intelligence — is now a major subfield, asking how new tools reshape attention, labour, and social relations. At the same time, the climate crisis has revived interest in low-technology crafts and repair cultures, and in the knowledge of makers who work with local materials.

Critics warn against technological determinism — the idea that tools drive history on their own — and against treating technology as separate from society. Tools are made by people, in particular social and political conditions, and they distribute power: who has access to a technology, who controls its production, and who bears its costs are political questions. The anthropology of tool making insists on keeping those questions in view, treating every implement as a social fact as well as a material one.
