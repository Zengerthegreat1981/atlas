# The Iron Law of Oligarchy

A principle formulated by Robert Michels holding that any large organization, regardless of its declared democratic principles, inevitably tends toward the concentration of real power in the hands of a small leadership.

## Content

In *Political Parties* (1911), Michels drew on his study of the German Social Democratic Party and the labour movement (organizations that formally proclaimed equality and internal democracy) to conclude that power was in fact concentrated in them in the hands of a stable leadership despite all the opposing slogans. He explained this tendency by structural organizational necessities, not individual ill will. The technical specialization required for effective administration gives those who possess it a permanent cognitive advantage; control over internal channels of communication (bulletins, meetings) gives the leadership the capacity to frame discourse before the rank and file; and the leadership's job stability (remaining for years), against the constant turnover of the ordinary rank-and-file membership, gives it an accumulation of knowledge and networks that the rank and file can hardly rival democratically.

## The Psychological and Social Roots of the Phenomenon in Michels

Michels was not content with the structural organizational explanation but added a complementary psychological-social dimension. He observed that the rank and file itself often looks forward to the existence of a strong leadership and feels a psychological need to be led by it and to trust in its wisdom, which he called the "psychological need for organization and leadership" among unorganized masses, so that oligarchy is not imposed only from above through the monopoly of technical knowledge, but is partly summoned from below through a mass desire for guidance and decisive leadership. Michels drew in this idea on his reading of the collective psychology of Gustave Le Bon and on the influence of the elitist inclination of his contemporaries Vilfredo Pareto and Gaetano Mosca.

## Michels's Relation to Elite Theory

Michels is usually classed with Pareto and Mosca within the "Italian elite school" (Classical Elite Theory), which was united by a shared conviction that every society, regardless of its declared political system (democratic or despotic), is in fact ruled by an organized, cohesive minority (an elite) that dominates the unorganized majority, and that "democracy" in the literal sense of direct rule by the people is an illusion impossible to realize in practice in any large and organizationally complex society, although the degree of openness and renewal differs from one elite to another.

## Impact and Criticism

This "law" became a basic reference in the sociology of organizations, parties and trade unions, cited even today to explain the gap between declared internal democracy and actual practice in many organizations. It was later criticized as an excessively deterministic generalization that ignores cases in which organizations succeeded in maintaining a relatively effective internal democracy through institutional mechanisms designed specifically to counter this tendency (such as compulsory rotation of offices and binding internal referendums). Lipset, Trow and Coleman's study of the International Typographical Union in the United States, *Union Democracy* (1956), is the best-known case of an organization that sustained a competitive internal party system, though they treated it as an exceptional case and explained it by unusual conditions.
