# Secure Attachment

The most adaptive of the infant attachment patterns identified in the "Strange Situation" procedure developed by Mary Ainsworth (patterns A, B and C, with a fourth, D, added later by Mary Main and Judith Solomon). It describes a relationship between child and caregiver in which the child feels safe and trusting, and so explores the world and returns for comfort. Secure attachment is pattern B.

## Characteristics

### In the Strange Situation
- **When the caregiver leaves**: exploration is reduced and the infant may show distress, though some secure infants show little.
- **When the caregiver returns**: the infant seeks contact or interaction and is readily soothed.
- **Play after soothing**: renewed exploration of the environment.
- **Response to a stranger**: wary but able to accept comfort or interaction, especially when the caregiver is present.

### In adulthood
Adult attachment research (Hazan and Shaver, Mikulincer and Shaver) associates secure attachment with:
- Trust and comfortable closeness in romantic relationships.
- Stable friendships and mutual support.
- Somewhat better emotion regulation and coping, and lower levels of anxiety and depression on average. These are group-level associations, not predictions for individuals.

## Other patterns

For contrast, Ainsworth's other patterns are **avoidant** (A: the infant shows little visible distress and avoids the caregiver on reunion) and **resistant or ambivalent** (C: the infant is highly distressed and not easily soothed). Main and Solomon later described **disorganized** attachment (D), marked by contradictory or fearful behavior toward the caregiver. Two concepts from Bowlby underlie the notion of security: the **secure base**, a caregiver from whom the child can explore and to whom the child can return, and the **internal working model**, the child's mental representation of the self and caregivers, which tends to guide later relationships.

## Prevalence

In studies of infants in Western middle-class samples, roughly two-thirds are classified as secure (van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg, 1988). Proportions differ across cultures and across risk contexts, and classification methods and caregiving norms differ as well.

## Influencing factors

### 1. Caregiver sensitivity
- Ainsworth and colleagues (1978) found that mothers who responded promptly and appropriately to their infants' signals were more likely to have securely attached infants. Later meta-analyses show a real but moderate association between sensitivity and security.
- On the Adult Attachment Interview (AAI), parents whose accounts of their own childhood are coherent are more likely to have secure children (Main and colleagues).

### 2. The child's characteristics
- Temperament appears to play a modest role. Attachment security depends mainly on the quality of the caregiving relationship.

### 3. Context
- Culture shapes caregiving norms and how attachment behavior is expressed.
- Poverty, war, migration and other stressors can affect caregiving.
- Social support helps families provide sensitive care.
- Secure attachment can also develop with other consistent, sensitive caregivers, and attachment classification can change over time, especially with changed circumstances.

## Intergenerational transmission

Parents' AAI classifications are associated with their children's Strange Situation classifications. Parents classified as autonomous (secure) on the AAI tend to have secure infants, while unresolved loss or trauma in the parent's account is associated with disorganized attachment in the child (Main and Hesse, 1990). A meta-analysis (van IJzendoorn, 1995) found a substantial association, though part of it remains unexplained by measured sensitivity, a puzzle known as the "transmission gap" (reviewed by Verhage and colleagues, 2016). "Earned security," in which adults with difficult childhoods come to represent their experience coherently, shows that these links are not deterministic.

## Stability

Attachment security shows modest continuity from infancy to adulthood (Fraley, 2002), with change more likely when life circumstances change. How stable it is across the lifespan remains debated.

## Applications

- **Parent-focused interventions**: programs that promote parental sensitivity and responsiveness.
- **Early intervention**: home-visiting programs for young parents, such as Minding the Baby.
- **Psychotherapy for adults**: the therapist serving as a secure base is a common theme in attachment-informed therapy.
- **Couple therapy**: Emotionally Focused Therapy (Sue Johnson) builds on attachment theory.
