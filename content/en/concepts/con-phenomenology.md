# Phenomenology: The Science of Describing Phenomena as Given

A philosophical movement founded by Edmund Husserl in *Logische Untersuchungen* (1900–01) and developed in *Ideen zu einer reinen Phänomenologie* (1913). It is concerned with the precise description of phenomena as they appear to consciousness, prior to any causal interpretation, scientific explanation, or metaphysical judgment. Its basic method is the "return to the things themselves" (zu den Sachen selbst): suspending preconceived judgments in order to arrive at what is "given" directly in experience.

## The Philosophical Meaning

Three methodological ideas are central:

- **Intentionality** (*Intentionalität*): all consciousness is consciousness of something (an idea Husserl took up from Brentano).
- **Epoché** and reduction: bracketing the "natural attitude" (the assumption that the world exists independently of consciousness) in order to examine how things are given to consciousness.
- **Description** (*Deskription*): describing the "given" (*das Gegebene*) as it appears, rather than explaining it through hidden causes.

Husserl conceived phenomenology as a "rigorous science" (strenge Wissenschaft) that would lay the foundations for all sciences, but his successors diverged: Heidegger transformed it into "fundamental ontology," Merleau-Ponty into "philosophy of the body," and Ricoeur into "hermeneutics."

## The Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

In phenomenological psychiatry, as developed by Karl Jaspers (*Allgemeine Psychopathologie*, 1913), and in the later work of Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss, patients' experiences are described "phenomenologically" (as they are lived) before or alongside being classified diagnostically. In Jaspers's usage, "static phenomenology" is the careful description of what patients actually experience. The aim is to understand how the world appears to the person, not only to name a disorder. In qualitative research, the method of Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (IPA), developed by Jonathan Smith, (from the 1990s) draws on phenomenological ideas (attention to lived experience and its description) together with hermeneutics and idiography.

## Distinction from Neighboring Concepts

Phenomenology differs from **empiricism**: the latter treats sensory experience as the sole source of knowledge, whereas phenomenology treats "description" as a complex mental act. It also differs from **structuralism** in the manner of Lévi-Strauss: structuralism studies unconscious structures, whereas phenomenology studies lived consciousness. And it differs from **introspectionism** in the manner of Wundt and Titchener: introspectionism seeks the "elements" of consciousness, whereas phenomenology seeks the "total structures" of experience.

The concept of intentionality draws on the idea of **conscious acts** developed by Franz Brentano, Husserl's teacher. Merleau-Ponty developed the concept of the **lived body** to extend phenomenological description to perceptual-motor experience, and Husserl developed the concept of the **lifeworld** in his later works to describe the lived horizon from which all knowledge emerges before its scientific abstraction.

## History of the Movement

After Husserl, phenomenology developed in several directions. Martin Heidegger (*Being and Time*, 1927) turned it toward the question of being and interpreted the analysis of Dasein as hermeneutic. In France, Jean-Paul Sartre (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943), Maurice Merleau-Ponty (*Phenomenology of Perception*, 1945) and later Emmanuel Levinas (*Totality and Infinity*, 1961) and Paul Ricœur adapted it to questions of freedom, embodiment, ethics and interpretation. The term "French phenomenology" is often used for this later development, which is more strongly existential and engaged with the human sciences than Husserl's own transcendental program; the contrast with the German tradition is a broad generalization and many figures cross it.
