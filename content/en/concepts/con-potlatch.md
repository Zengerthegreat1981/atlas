# Potlatch

A ceremonial exchange ritual among the peoples of the Northwest Coast of North America (the Kwakwaka'wakw, long called Kwakiutl in the literature, the Tlingit, Haida, and others). Franz Boas documented it in the field, and Marcel Mauss used it as a central example in *The Gift* (1925).

## Description

At a potlatch feast, a chief or local leader distributes large quantities of gifts (blankets, copper vessels, and food) to his guests. Sometimes he destroys them publicly, for instance by burning blankets or smashing valuable copper vessels, in a competitive display that enhances his social standing and influence. The implicit social obligation is that whoever receives the gifts must later reciprocate with equal or greater gifts on a future occasion, or else lose standing before the community.

## Maussian Interpretation

Mauss used the potlatch to support his general theory of the gift. Exchange here is not a purely utilitarian economic transaction. It is a mechanism for building social solidarity and hierarchical prestige through three interrelated obligations: giving, receiving, and reciprocating. The deliberate destruction of wealth shows that the goal is not economic accumulation but symbolic status.

## Colonial Context

The Canadian government legally prohibited the potlatch between 1885 and 1951, on the grounds that it was wasteful and an obstacle to the assimilation of Indigenous people. This example is later cited in critiques of colonial anthropology for suppressing local cultural practices in the name of progress.

## Variation Between Groups

Boas and his students documented concrete differences in the practice of the potlatch among the different groups of the Northwest Coast. Among the Kwakiutl, the potlatch was often associated with occasions for inheriting titles and social privileges within a system of ranks. Among the Tlingit, it took a form more closely tied to funeral rites and the commemoration of the dead. This shows that the potlatch was not a uniform, rigid practice, but a flexible system adapted to the social structure of each group.

## Contemporary Revival

The legal ban on the potlatch in Canada was lifted in 1951. The following decades saw a broad cultural revival of the practice among Northwest Coast peoples, as a symbol of reclaiming Indigenous cultural identity after decades of colonial repression. From the late 1970s, Canadian museums returned items confiscated during the ban to Kwakwaka'wakw communities, where cultural centres at Cape Mudge and Alert Bay were established to house them.

## Anthropological Debate over Interpretation

Interpreters of the potlatch have disagreed about its precise nature. Some, such as Stuart Piddocke, saw it in part as an ecological mechanism for redistributing food surpluses among groups with unequal resources. Helen Codere stressed rivalry, arguing that after European contact, wealth and population loss drove potlatching into an escalating competition she called "fighting with property". Others, Mauss among them, emphasised its symbolic and moral dimension in building status and alliance. The disagreement reflects the broader tension between materialist and symbolic approaches to exchange.
