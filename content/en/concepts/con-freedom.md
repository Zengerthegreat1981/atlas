# Freedom

The concept that apparently unites the whole school and in practice divides it — because each thinker sets a different limit on it.

## Liberating the Concept

There is no dispute that the human being is free in this school. The whole dispute is over the limits of this freedom: is it absolute or constrained by the situation? Is it individual or entangled with others? And is speaking of it to someone under real pressure liberation or burdening?

## The Core Disputes within the Concept

Two disputes. The first: is freedom absolute? Sartre says yes, nearly; Heidegger, Beauvoir and May say it is thrown, entangled and constrained. The second: is it safe to talk about it? This is not a philosophical dispute but a clinical one: the same sentence is liberation for one person and a burden for another, and the difference lies in the person's situation, not in the wording.

## Primary-Text Formulations

Primary existential texts present contrasting accounts of human freedom and its limitations:

- **Søren Kierkegaard** (*The Concept of Anxiety*, 1844, Ch. 2): describes freedom not as an abstract capacity for moral choice, but as the realm of pure possibility whose apprehension produces anxiety—the "dizziness of freedom" (*angest er frihedens svimmelhed*) when standing before open potentiality.
- **Martin Heidegger** (*Being and Time*, 1927, §§38, 58): characterizes freedom as always "thrown" (*geworfene Freiheit*). Human existence (*Dasein*) never begins from an unconstrained void, but always finds itself already delivered over into a specific, unchosen historical and cultural facticity.
- **Jean-Paul Sartre** (*Being and Nothingness*, 1943, Part IV, Ch. 1; *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, 1946): famously declares that humanity is "condemned to be free" (*condamné à être libre*). Because existence precedes essence, no external human nature or divine decree dictates values; even the refusal to choose constitutes an active choice.
- **Simone de Beauvoir** (*The Ethics of Ambiguity*, 1947, Part I): refines Sartrean freedom into a "situated" reality. She argues that individual freedom can only will itself by willing the freedom of others, and demonstrates that social, political, and material situations can genuinely crush or confiscate agency.
- **Viktor Frankl** (*Man's Search for Meaning*, 1946): writes from the context of concentration camps that the "last of the human freedoms" is the capacity to choose one's personal attitude and retain dignity in any set of circumstances, even when external agency is entirely removed.
- **Rollo May** (*Freedom and Destiny*, 1981): articulates freedom as existing in dialectical tension with "destiny"—the unchosen biological, historical, and cosmic givens of life.
- **Irvin D. Yalom** (*Existential Psychotherapy*, 1980, Part II): identifies freedom as the second ultimate concern, linking it directly to authorship of one's world and exploring how individuals evade the anxiety of responsibility through displacement, procrastination, and denial.

## How It Appears in the Session

Noticing the person describing their life in the passive voice: "things just went that way," "there was nothing to be done." The work is to gradually recover the agent — without hurry and without blame, and with an honest examination of what is actually constrained.

## The Most Common Misunderstanding

That existentialism says "you are completely free and responsible for everything." This is half the thesis. The other half — thrownness, situation and entanglement — is present in the philosophical source and was lost in transmission.

## Example and Counter-Example

Example: A woman feels "forced" to stay in a job she hates because of real financial obligations toward her family. The existential work does not tell her "you are free, quit the job" but distinguishes between the actual constraint (the financial obligation) and her free choice of her stance toward that constraint — how she carries it, and whether she looks for room for change within it. This is an honest respect for the mixture of thrownness and freedom in both Heidegger and Sartre.

Counter-example: A therapist tells a client suffering from severe depression and extreme poverty, "You are completely free, choose another life," ignoring the situation (facticity) that actually constrains his real choices. This flattens Sartrean freedom and ignores even Beauvoir, who stressed that freedom is always exercised within a situation, not in a vacuum.

## What Should Not Be Inferred from This Concept

It does not mean that every external constraint (poverty, illness, political oppression) is an illusion that can be overcome by a mere "decision." That turns a precise philosophical concept into disguised victim-blaming, and directly contradicts Beauvoir in her critique of systems that actually confiscate the individual's freedom (such as the patriarchal system).

Nor does it mean that the therapist dictates to the client what he "should" choose in the name of his liberation — this reverses the core of the idea, which transfers the responsibility of choosing to the client, not the therapist.

## The Arab and Cultural Context

Sartre's concept of absolute individual freedom — where no excuses or external authority absolve the individual of responsibility for his choice — was formulated within a post-religious European context that sees the individual as sole master of his moral decision. In Arab-Islamic contexts, the concept of freedom is often entangled with fate and divine will in a way that has no direct counterpart in secular European existential philosophy; the Arabic word "hurriyya" may not carry the same individualist-existential charge that Freiheit carries in Heidegger or liberté in Sartre.

This does not mean application is impossible, but it calls for caution: a religious Arab client may see no contradiction between believing in fate and bearing responsibility for his daily choices, and imposing a Sartrean framework of "you have no excuse, you are absolutely free" may collide with a coherent religious system of meaning that has no flaw requiring correction. Much empirical research on the perception of freedom and agency has relied on Western individualist samples, and measuring this perception in contexts of collective decision-making (the extended family, the clan) may require different instruments.

## Comparative Table

| Thinker | His exact formulation | What follows in the room | His disagreement with others |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kierkegaard | Freedom is possibility, and anxiety is its dizziness. Seeing that you can is what terrifies you, not the danger. | Anxiety is read as a sign that a real choice has become possible, not as a symptom to be removed. | The foundation for all that follows; no notable dispute here. |
| Nietzsche | Freedom is to create your values and bear that you created them, not to be exempt from constraints. | Working on the values by which the person lives without having chosen them. | Differs from Frankl: for Nietzsche values are created, for Frankl they are discovered. |
| Heidegger | Freedom is not a choice among options but an openness to possibilities. And it is always thrown freedom: in a situation you did not choose. | The constant balance: you are not responsible for what fell upon you, and you are responsible for your stance toward it. | Its second half — thrownness — is what was lost in the American transmission. |
| Sartre | "Condemned to be free" (*Existentialism Is a Humanism*, 1946). Even not choosing is a choice. And no excuse: circumstances are interpreted by the project in which I carry them. | The gentle confrontation with bad faith: "You say you are forced while you choose this every day." | The most extreme formulation, and the one rightly attacked when transferred to the clinic without reservation. |
| Simone de Beauvoir | Freedom is entangled: my freedom is not realized except through the freedom of those around me. And the situation — body, gender, class and history — constrains it actually, not theoretically. | Before saying "you still have a choice," the actual situation is examined: does this person have what makes the choice meaningful? | The most important correction of Sartre, and from within his own house. |
| Frankl | The last of the human freedoms: choosing one's attitude in any circumstance. He speaks from within an extreme experience, so his weight is heavy. | Working on attitude when changing the circumstance is impossible — in illness and loss specifically. | Often misused: turning a testimony from an extreme circumstance into a general rule for every circumstance. |
| Rollo May | Freedom and fate are two faces: there is what is not chosen (body, death, epoch), and freedom is what we do within this given. | A balanced practical formula used directly: what is given here? And what is available within it? | The most mature American formulation, and the least known compared with Yalom's. |
| Yalom | Freedom is the second ultimate concern, together with responsibility. And the terror of it is the terror of being the author of one's life rather than its witness. | Uncovering ways of avoiding responsibility: postponement, dependence, and attributing the decision to circumstances or to others. | Extends Sartre into practice, and inherits with him the problem of neglecting the situation. |
| van Deurzen | Freedom is exercised within four dimensions, one of which is necessarily social. So there is no sense in speaking of freedom without power, relationship and resources. | Surveying the four dimensions before any talk of choice: where does the constraint actually lie? | The institutional reply to the individualism of the American current. |
