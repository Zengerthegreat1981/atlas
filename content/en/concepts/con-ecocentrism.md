# Ecocentrism

## Meaning and Definition

Ecocentrism is an ethical position that makes the ecosystem as a whole, rather than individual living beings alone or humans alone, the direct object of moral consideration. Intrinsic value is attributed to the land, soil, waters and the web of environmental relations themselves, not merely to individual organisms. This position differs from biocentrism, which focuses on the value of each individual living organism. It also differs from anthropocentrism, which confines value to human beings.

## Early Traces in Islamic Thought

No term in the Islamic heritage corresponds exactly to this modern concept. However, the Qur'anic vision of the cosmos contains elements that approach it. Verses that describe the mountains, the stars and the trees as "glorifying God" give the natural world an existential status that goes beyond being raw material for human use. For example: "There is not a thing but glorifies Him with His praise." Jurists also discouraged wastefulness in water, even when performing ablution beside a flowing river, citing a report attributed to the Prophet. The Prophet ﷺ forbade cutting the trees of Medina and hunting its sanctuary game. Some contemporary writers read these rulings as protecting ecological balance for its own sake, though classical jurists framed them largely in terms of public benefit and divine ownership of creation. Some contemporary scholars see in the Qur'anic concept of "the balance" ("And the heaven He raised and imposed the balance... that you not transgress within the balance") a foundation for a comprehensive environmental outlook that prohibits disrupting the balance of the earth and its order.

## Western Roots and Contemporary Debate

Aldo Leopold laid the philosophical basis for this position in *A Sand County Almanac* through what he called the "land ethic." He stated its famous criterion: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise." He made the entire biotic community, including soil, water, plants and animals, the object of moral consideration rather than the individual alone. Arne Næss, in a 1973 article, introduced what he called "deep ecology," calling for a radical transformation in the human view of the self as part of a larger environmental web rather than its master. J. Baird Callicott, the leading philosophical defender of the land ethic, criticized individualist approaches (such as animal liberation) for neglecting the holistic ecological relations which, in his view, ground value.

## Objections and Examples

Ecocentrism is objected to on the grounds that it may justify sacrificing individual living beings (such as killing invasive animals in an ecosystem) in the name of "the overall balance." Advocates of individual animal rights criticize this. *Example:* a decision to cull a population of invasive deer on an island in order to protect the island's native plant diversity. Ecocentrism justifies this act by considering the whole, whereas someone who sees independent value in each individual animal would object to it.
