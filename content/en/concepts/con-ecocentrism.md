# Ecocentrism

## Meaning and Definition

Ecocentrism is an ethical position that makes the ecosystem as a whole, rather than individual living beings alone or humans alone, the direct object of moral consideration. Intrinsic value is attributed to the land, soil, waters and the web of environmental relations themselves, not merely to individual organisms. This position differs from biocentrism, which focuses on the value of each individual living organism. It also differs from anthropocentrism, which confines value to human beings.

## Early Traces in Islamic Thought

No term in the Islamic heritage corresponds exactly to this modern concept. However, the Qur'anic vision of the cosmos contains elements that approach it. Verses that describe the mountains, the stars and the trees as "glorifying God" give the natural world an existential status that goes beyond being raw material for human use. One verse states, for example, that everything without exception glorifies God in praise. Jurists also discouraged wastefulness in water, even when performing ablution beside a flowing river, citing a report attributed to the Prophet. The Prophet ﷺ forbade cutting the trees of Medina and hunting its sanctuary game. Some contemporary writers read these rulings as protecting ecological balance for its own sake, though classical jurists framed them largely in terms of public benefit and divine ownership of creation. Some contemporary scholars see in the Qur'anic concept of "the balance" (the verses relating that God raised the heaven, set the balance, and told people not to transgress it) a foundation for a comprehensive environmental outlook that prohibits disrupting the balance of the earth and its order.

## Western Roots and Contemporary Debate

Aldo Leopold laid the philosophical basis for this position in *A Sand County Almanac* through what he called the "land ethic." His famous criterion holds that actions are right insofar as they tend to preserve the biotic community's integrity, stability and beauty, and wrong insofar as they tend to do the opposite. He made the entire biotic community, including soil, water, plants and animals, the object of moral consideration rather than the individual alone. Arne Næss, in a 1973 article, introduced what he called "deep ecology," calling for a radical transformation in the human view of the self as part of a larger environmental web rather than its master. J. Baird Callicott, the leading philosophical defender of the land ethic, criticized individualist approaches (such as animal liberation) for neglecting the holistic ecological relations which, in his view, ground value.

## Objections and Examples

Ecocentrism is objected to on the grounds that it may justify sacrificing individual living beings (such as killing invasive animals in an ecosystem) in the name of "the overall balance." Advocates of individual animal rights criticize this. *Example:* a decision to cull a population of invasive deer on an island in order to protect the island's native plant diversity. Ecocentrism justifies this act by considering the whole, whereas someone who sees independent value in each individual animal would object to it.

## Primary-Source Excerpts

Leopold's essay "The Land Ethic" states the extension directly: the land ethic widens the community's boundaries to take in soil, water, plants and animals, which he groups together as the land. He adds that such an ethic turns human beings from conquerors of the land-community into ordinary members and citizens of it.

## Related Institutions in Islamic Law

Classical law contains several rules with environmental effects. The *hima* was a reserve in which grazing was restricted. A report in al-Bukhari states that there is no *hima* except for God and His Messenger, which jurists read as barring private appropriation and tying reserves to public need; the caliph 'Umar is reported to have maintained such land. Al-Mawardi's *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* treats reserves together with the revival of dead land (*ihya' al-mawat*), by which uncultivated land could be acquired through making it productive. A *harim*, or protective zone around wells and watercourses, limited nearby use. 'Izz al-Din ibn 'Abd al-Salam, in *Qawa'id al-Ahkam*, listed duties an owner owes to animals, including feeding them and not overburdening them. These rules rest on divine ownership, public welfare, and the avoidance of harm. They are therefore not ecocentric in Leopold's sense, although modern writers such as Mawil Izzi Dien draw on them in building an Islamic environmental ethic.
