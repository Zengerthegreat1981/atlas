# Leftist Arab Intellectualism

A broad intellectual and political current that arose in the Arab world at the beginning of the twentieth century, influenced by Marxism and European socialist thought. It engaged with the projects of national liberation from colonialism and the building of the modern state. It rose to prominence especially between the 1950s and the 1970s, at times intersecting with and at other times clashing with the two main rival currents: Arab nationalism and political Islam.

## Origins and Context

The spread of radical ideas among workers, the middle class and intellectuals went hand in hand with the growing integration of Arab societies into the global economy from the nineteenth century onward. The first Arab communist parties were founded in the 1920s (in Egypt, Palestine, Lebanon and Syria). The radical left was a central part of the anti-colonial projects between the 1950s and the 1970s, drawing supporters from various social strata, especially from religious and ethnic minorities.

## Notable Figures and Contributions

Among the most prominent thinkers of this current is the Lebanese **Mahdi Amil** (1936–1987), one of the most important theorists of Arab Marxism, who sought to develop an authentic Marxist reading of the structure of Arab societies, far from the literal copying of European theory. **Husayn Muruwwa** (1910–1987), with his reference work *al-Nazaat al-maddiyya fi-l-falsafa al-'arabiyya al-islamiyya* (Materialist Tendencies in Arab-Islamic Philosophy, 1978), offered a Marxist reading of the Islamic philosophical heritage. The Syrian **Tayyib Tizini** (1934-2019), from the 1970s, pursued a large project of re-reading the Arab-Islamic heritage from a Marxist perspective. The Moroccan thinker **Muhammad 'Abid al-Jabiri** (1935-2010), who came from the socialist left, produced the encyclopedic project *Naqd al-'aql al-'arabi* (The Critique of Arab Reason, four volumes), which became one of the most influential works of Arab thought in the late twentieth century, although his position relative to classical Marxism is a matter of debate.

## Intellectual Axes

- **Critique of dependency and imperialism**: Analysis of the structure of the Arab economy as an appendage to the capitalist centre of the world.
- **Re-reading the heritage**: An attempt to find material and rationalist roots within the Islamic philosophical heritage (the Mu'tazila, Ibn Rushd), in order to ground leftist thought in the Arab context rather than treat it as a purely foreign import.
- **The thorny relation with nationalism and political Islam**: Sometimes a tactical alliance (as in the national fronts against colonialism), and at other times a sharp ideological conflict. The new leftists accused the traditional religious and cultural heritage of irrationality and of obstructing liberation, while the left was in turn accused of atheism and of intellectual dependence on the communist West.

## Impact and Decline

The organized Arab left gradually lost its political momentum from the 1980s onward, with the collapse of the Soviet Union and the rise of Islamic movements. However, its intellectual legacy (particularly in the critique of authoritarianism and economic dependency) remained present in the following generation of Arab intellectuals, and was partly re-evaluated in debates after the Arab Spring about social justice.

## Limitations

The Arab left remained, in general, an organizationally small minority with limited mass influence compared with the nationalist and Islamist currents. Its internal divisions (the traditional communist party versus Maoist, Trotskyist and left-nationalist currents) weakened its capacity to build a single political project.
