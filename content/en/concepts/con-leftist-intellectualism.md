# Leftist Arab Intellectualism

**Leftist Arab intellectualism** is a varied philosophical and political tradition that took shape in the Arab world in the early twentieth century. It drew on historical materialism, Marxism, and other socialist currents, and it argued with anti-colonial nationalism, with critiques of landlordism and dependent capitalism, and with programmes for cultural and social change. Its widest public audience fell between the 1950s and the 1970s. In those decades it alternated between alliance and conflict with Arab nationalism and with political Islam.

## Origins and Historical Phases

The first phase, in the 1920s, included socialist clubs, trade unions, and early communist parties in Egypt, Lebanon, Syria, and Palestine. Salama Musa (1887–1958) wrote as a Fabian socialist on science and social reform. Joseph Rosenthal was among the founders of the Egyptian Socialist Party in Alexandria in 1921, a group that soon moved into the communist orbit. The current gained a larger public after the Second World War and the 1948 Palestine war, treating imperialism and local conservative elites as joint obstacles to independence.

A decisive turning point occurred after the June 1967 War (*al-Naksa*). The defeat of conventional Arab nationalist states (such as Nasserist Egypt and Ba'thist Syria) prompted the emergence of the Arab "New Left" (*al-yasar al-jadid*). Thinkers of this generation turned toward rigorous internal self-criticism, analyzing authoritarian state mechanisms, patriarchal structures, and the persistence of traditionalist religious mentalities.

## Prominent Figures and Contributions

- **Mahdi Amil** (pseudonym of Hassan Hamdan, 1936–1987): Lebanese Marxist philosopher. In *Muqaddimat Nazariyya li-Dirasat Athar al-Fikr al-Ishtiraki fi Harakat al-Taharrur al-Watani* (1972) he set out a "colonial mode of production" (*namat al-intaj al-kuluniyali*): capitalism in a colonized society, he argued, does not repeat a European sequence that ends in an independent bourgeoisie. In *Fi al-Dawla al-Ta'ifiyya* (1986) he linked that dependent structure to sectarian political rule, taking Lebanon as the case.
- **Husayn Muruwwa** (1910–1987): Lebanese communist whose *al-Naza'at al-maddiyya fi al-falsafa al-'arabiyya al-islamiyya* (Materialist Tendencies in Arab-Islamic Philosophy, 1978–1979) uses historical materialism to trace rationalist and materialist strands in the heritage, including the Mu'tazila, the Brethren of Purity, and the philosophers, and to place those strands in social history.
- **Sadik Jalal al-Azm** (1934–2016): Syrian philosopher who published *al-Naqd al-Dhati ba'd al-Hazima* (Self-Criticism After the Defeat, 1968) and *Naqd al-Fikr al-Dini* (Critique of Religious Thought, 1969). The second book, issued in Beirut by Dar al-Tali'a, led to his prosecution in Lebanon on a charge of insulting religion. The court dropped the charge. The two books made a secular reading of the 1967 defeat part of the public argument.
- **Tayyib Tizini** (1934–2019): Syrian philosopher who proposed a dialectical rereading of the heritage in *Mashru' Ru'ya Jadida lil-Fikr al-'Arabi fi al-'Asr al-Wasit* (A Project for a New View of Arab Thought in the Medieval Period, Damascus, 1971) and in *Min al-Turath ila al-Thawra* (From Heritage to Revolution, Beirut, 1976).
- **Abdallah Laroui** (b. 1933) and **Muhammad Abed al-Jabri** (1935–2010): Moroccan writers often placed beside the left, on different grounds. Laroui's *L'Idéologie arabe contemporaine* (Paris, 1967) criticizes modern Arab ideologies from a historicist position shaped by Marxism. Al-Jabri, who had been active in the Moroccan socialist movement, turned in the four-volume *Naqd al-'Aql al-'Arabi* (Critique of Arab Reason, 1984–2001) to an epistemological account of systems of knowledge in the tradition. That tetralogy is not itself a Marxist treatise.

## Intellectual Axes and Debates

1. **Critique of Imperialism and Underdevelopment**: Diagnosing Arab economies through dependency theory and unequal exchange (closely aligned with the political economy of Samir Amin).
2. **Reappropriation of Heritage (*al-Turath*)**: Contesting conservative claims over Islamic heritage by articulating a progressive, materialist, and rationalist counter-history.
3. **Critique of the State**: Deconstructing military dictatorships, clientelism, and sectarianism that subordinated social justice to empty nationalist rhetoric.
