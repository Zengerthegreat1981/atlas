# Stream of Consciousness: The Continuous Flow of Experience

The description of consciousness as a continuous flow, without sharp breaks, with each moment shading into the next. William James introduced the term in *The Principles of Psychology* (1890, Chapter IX, "The Stream of Thought") to argue that consciousness is not a chain of separate mental "states" but a continuous **stream**, interrupted only by gaps such as sleep. In the same work he described the "fringe" of vague relations surrounding focal experience and (in Chapter XV, borrowing the phrase from E. R. Clay) the "specious present," the short duration that is directly experienced as present.

## The Philosophical Meaning

James developed the description against "psychical atomism," which treated consciousness as separate granules (the associationist approach of nineteenth-century Britain). For him, thought has five basic characteristics: (1) every thought tends to be part of a **personal** consciousness; (2) within each personal consciousness thought is always **changing**; (3) within each personal consciousness thought is **sensibly continuous**; (4) it appears to deal with **objects independent of itself**; and (5) it is **selectively interested** in some parts of those objects to the exclusion of others. Husserl later analysed the temporal structure of experience phenomenologically in lectures of 1905, published in 1928 as *Vorlesungen zur Phänomenologie des inneren Zeitbewußtseins*.

## James, Husserl, and Bergson

The three thinkers are often grouped but differ in method and aim. James describes the stream introspectively, as a psychologist. Husserl describes the structure of time-consciousness (retention, primal impression, protention) as a phenomenologist bracketing questions about objective time. Bergson, in his *Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience* (1889), contrasts lived duration (*durée*), an indivisible qualitative flow, with the spatialised, measurable time of science. Their descriptions converge in rejecting atomistic accounts of the mind, but they are not a single doctrine, and direct historical influence should be claimed only where documented.

## Literary Usage

The phrase was taken up in literary criticism (it was applied to Dorothy Richardson's novels by May Sinclair in 1918) and is associated with writers such as James Joyce and Virginia Woolf. The literary technique of rendering a character's unbroken inner monologue is related to, but not the same as, the psychological concept.

## The Psychological and Therapeutic Meaning

In clinical psychology, the concept of the stream of consciousness plays a role in: (1) **free association** in Freud's therapy: the patient must say whatever comes to mind without selection, reproducing the stream of consciousness. (2) **Focusing**, developed by Eugene Gendlin, in which attention turns from the ongoing flow of thought to a bodily "felt sense." (3) **Disturbances of the experienced flow in schizophrenia**: early clinical studies of attention and perception (for example McGhie and Chapman, 1961) recorded patients reporting that their stream of experience felt broken or flooded.

## Distinction from Neighboring Concepts

The stream of consciousness differs from **reflective consciousness**, which stands above the stream in order to contemplate it: the former is immersed, the latter observes. It differs from **thinking**: the former includes emotions, sensations, and associations, while the latter is a more restricted symbolic activity. And it differs from the **unconscious**: the former is a descriptive phenomenon that can be observed, while the latter is a theoretical inference.
