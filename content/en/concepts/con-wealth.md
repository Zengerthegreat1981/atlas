# Wealth

Wealth is the accumulation of valued resources — goods, money, land, livestock, knowledge, relationships or prestige — that can be stored, displayed, exchanged or inherited. In anthropology, wealth is studied not as a universal quantity but as a cultural category: what counts as wealth, who may hold it, how it is displayed and what it obliges its holder to do vary profoundly between societies. Shells, cattle, yams, blankets, cars and bank balances have all served as wealth, and the same object can be wealth in one context and mere subsistence in another. The anthropological question is therefore not only how much people have, but what having means.

## Origins and Development

The classic anthropological account of wealth begins with the gift. Marcel Mauss, in *The Gift*, showed that in many societies wealth circulates through gift exchange rather than market sale, and that giving creates obligations: the recipient must receive and must repay, and the giver gains prestige precisely by parting with wealth. Among the Kwakwaka'wakw of the Northwest Coast, chiefs demonstrated their rank by giving away — even destroying — vast quantities of goods in the potlatch, a ceremony in which wealth was validated by its distribution rather than its retention. Bronisław Malinowski documented a parallel system in the Trobriand Islands, where the *kula* exchange circulated shell valuables around a ring of islands, and where a man's reputation depended on his generosity and his skill in the exchange rather than on what he kept.

Thorstein Veblen, writing about industrial America, offered a contrasting theory: wealth is displayed through conspicuous consumption, the wasteful display of expensive goods that signals status to others. Marshall Sahlins, in *Stone Age Economics*, challenged the assumption that hunter-gatherers were poor: he argued that they enjoyed affluence by wanting little, and that scarcity is created by the endless multiplication of wants rather than by any shortage of goods. Pierre Bourdieu extended the concept further, arguing that wealth takes several forms — economic capital (money and property), cultural capital (education, taste, knowledge) and social capital (networks and connections) — and that these forms can be converted into one another, so that a good education can become a good job and a good job can become social standing. Wealth, in this view, is not only what you own but what your ownership allows you to become.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns the relationship between wealth and inequality. In societies organised by kinship, wealth is often widely shared through obligations of reciprocity, and the accumulation of large personal fortunes is prevented by the demand to give. In stratified societies, wealth concentrates, and the concentration is legitimised by ideologies of merit, divine favour or market efficiency. A second debate asks whether wealth corrupts social relations: does the introduction of money and markets dissolve the bonds of reciprocity, as many classical social theorists feared, or do people incorporate money into existing moral economies, using it for gifts, bridewealth and ritual as readily as for purchase? Ethnography shows both: money can corrode relationships, but it can also be domesticated, as when wages are shared with kin or remittances sustain households across continents. A third debate concerns the measurement of wealth: development economists measure it in income and assets, while anthropologists insist that security, status, leisure and social standing are also forms of wealth, and that a society rich in relationships may be poor in cash without being poor in any meaningful sense.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Wealth is at the centre of contemporary anthropology. Ethnographers study billionaires and their philanthropy, the moral economies of debt, the global circulation of remittances, and the new forms of wealth created by digital platforms and cryptocurrencies. The anthropology of wealth also challenges dominant economic ideas: it shows that people everywhere think about wealth in moral terms — who deserves it, how it should be used, what obligations it creates — and that the economy is never separate from kinship, religion and politics. The critique of wealth extends to the discipline itself: anthropologists have noted that the wealth of museums, universities and research institutions was often built on colonial extraction, and that the study of other people's wealth cannot be separated from the history of how wealth was taken. Wealth remains, in short, one of the most revealing windows onto what a society values and how it distributes what it values.
