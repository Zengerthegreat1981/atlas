# Crossing and Dwelling Theory of Religion in Thomas Tweed's Work

A general theoretical framework for defining religion, developed by Thomas Tweed in his book *Crossing and Dwelling* (2006), drawing on his earlier ethnographic study of the Cuban Catholic exile community in Miami.

## Religion as Flow, Not System

Tweed rejects defining religion as a fixed doctrinal or institutional system bounded by clear limits. He proposes instead a definition in which religions are confluences of organic-cultural flows that intensify joy and confront suffering by drawing on human and suprahuman forces to make homes and cross boundaries. This definition deliberately emphasizes movement and change rather than stability and strict boundaries.

## The Two Dimensions: "Dwelling" and "Crossing"

The two central metaphors are complementary rather than opposed. "Dwelling" describes the ways religious people make homes and build a sense of belonging, in the body, the home, the homeland and the cosmos (as at the Miami shrine). "Crossing" describes movement across terrestrial, corporeal and cosmic boundaries, including the migration of the Cuban exiles themselves. In every concrete case, religion is the constant tension between the two acts together, not either one alone.

## Relation to the Field of Religion and Migration

The theory has been influential in the study of religion and migration, offering a framework that goes beyond the original Cuban-American case to other religious phenomena involving movement and diaspora.

## The Theory's Position Among Competing Definitions of Religion

Tweed contrasts his theory with other influential definitions of religion, notably two. The first is the functional definition of Emile Durkheim (religion as a system of beliefs and practices that unifies the community socially around "sacred" things). The second is the symbolic-cultural definition of Clifford Geertz (religion as a system of symbols that shapes moods and motivations by linking a conception of an order of existence to an aura of factual reality). Tweed shares both definitions' interest in the social and symbolic dimensions, but argues that such definitions give too little attention to movement and change. He proposes instead a definition more sensitive to movement, change, and geographic crossing, which makes it particularly suitable for studying religion in contemporary migration and diaspora contexts that challenge classical models based on a religious group fixed locally.

## The Ethnographic Origin of the Theory

The theory did not arise as a purely bookish theoretical abstraction. It was derived from prolonged fieldwork: Tweed studied the community of Cuban Catholic migrants around the shrine of "Our Lady of Charity" (Nuestra Señora de la Caridad) in Miami, documenting how these migrants turned the site into a meeting point between their original Cuban memory and their new American identity. This is a living embodiment of the crossing-and-dwelling duality on which he later built his general theoretical framework.
