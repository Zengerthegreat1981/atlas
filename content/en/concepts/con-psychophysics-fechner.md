# Psychophysics

## Definition

**Psychophysics** is the branch of experimental psychology that studies the quantitative relation between physical stimuli and the sensations and judgments they produce. The name and the program were established by **Gustav Theodor Fechner** in *Elemente der Psychophysik* (*Elements of Psychophysics*, 1860). Fechner, who was also a philosopher with a metaphysical interest in the unity of mind and matter, intended it as an "exact science of the functional relations between body and mind."

## The Basic Laws

### 1. Weber's Law

Formulated from the work of **Ernst Heinrich Weber** in the 1830s and 1840s (on touch and on the discrimination of lifted weights): the just noticeable difference between two stimuli is roughly proportional to the magnitude of the original stimulus.

Mathematically: **ΔI / I = k** (the Weber fraction, a constant).

- Illustration: if one can just detect the difference between 100 g and 102 g, one will need roughly a 20 g difference to detect a change from 1000 g.
- The Weber fraction differs from one sensory modality to another and the law holds approximately in the middle of the stimulus range, failing near the threshold.

### 2. Fechner's Law (1860)

Fechner took the just noticeable difference as the unit of sensation and, assuming that all such differences are equal increments of sensation, derived that the strength of a sensation is proportional to the logarithm of the stimulus intensity.

Mathematically: **S = k · log(I / I₀)**

Where S is the strength of the sensation, I the stimulus intensity, I₀ the threshold intensity and k a constant.

The form recalls Daniel Bernoulli's earlier logarithmic treatment of utility, and both express diminishing sensitivity to increments at higher magnitudes. Fechner's research took place in the same period as the physiology of sensory perception developed by Hermann von Helmholtz (the Young-Helmholtz trichromatic theory of colour vision and the 1850 measurement of the speed of nerve conduction). Wilhelm Wundt, who founded his Leipzig laboratory in 1879, took psychophysical methods as a central part of the new experimental psychology.

### 3. Stevens' Power Law (1957)

**Stanley Smith Stevens**, using magnitude estimation, argued in "On the Psychophysical Law" (1957) that the perceived magnitude is better described by a power function of stimulus intensity:

**S = k · I^a**

The exponent *a* depends on the continuum. Typical values reported by Stevens include roughly 0.33 for the brightness of a 5° target seen in the dark and about 0.6 for loudness (as a function of sound pressure), 1.0 for apparent length and about 3.5 for electric shock to the fingers. Exponents below 1 mean compression and above 1 mean expansion of sensation relative to the stimulus.

## Experimental Method

Classical psychophysics developed three standard methods of measuring thresholds:

1. **Method of Limits:** the stimulus is changed step by step in an ascending or descending series until the observer's report changes.
2. **Method of Constant Stimuli:** a fixed set of stimulus values is presented repeatedly in random order.
3. **Method of Adjustment:** the observer adjusts the stimulus until it matches a standard or reaches threshold.

Signal detection theory (developed in the 1950s and 1960s; Green and Swets, 1966) later separated an observer's sensitivity from their response criterion, and so replaced the idea of a fixed sharp "threshold" in many applications.

## Impact on Psychology and Philosophy

### 1. Experimental psychology

Psychophysics provided the methods and the model of quantitative measurement for experimental psychology, including sensory physiology and perception research, and its methods remain in use in vision and hearing science.

### 2. Philosophy of mind

It raised in a concrete form the question whether subjective experience can be measured at all. Critics doubted that sensations could be measured by units (a worry that goes back to Kant's remarks on the impossibility of quantifying inner sense, and to the 1930s British Association committee that debated whether sensation was measurable). It also relates to the discussion of **qualia**, though psychophysics measures discriminations and reports and not the qualitative character of experience itself.

### 3. Applied psychology

- **Audiometry and visual acuity testing** rely on threshold measurement.
- **Human factors**: design of displays, alarms and controls takes account of sensory thresholds.

## Clinical Applications beyond Sensory Testing

**Diagnostic judgment.** Signal detection theory moved into medicine as a way of evaluating decisions. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve plots hit rate against false-alarm rate across decision criteria, so that a test's or clinician's ability to discriminate can be assessed apart from how readily a positive verdict is given. John Swets (1988) reviewed such uses in diagnostic systems, and ROC analysis is now routine for screening instruments, including psychological ones.

**Pain.** Rating and matching procedures, such as visual analogue scales and magnitude matching, are used to track pain and the effects of treatment. The numbers remain reports, shaped by expectation, mood and response habits.

**Anxiety.** Generalization gradients, measured by presenting stimuli that differ in graded steps from one previously paired with an aversive event, allow comparison between groups. Lissek and colleagues (2010) reported broader generalization of conditioned fear in people with panic disorder than in healthy controls.

**Bodily perception.** Heartbeat counting and detection tasks, for example Schandry's (1981), have been used to study how perception of bodily signals relates to anxiety and emotion, although the validity of the counting task has been seriously questioned. These are mainly research tools, not stand-alone diagnostic tests.

## Philosophical Debate

### 1. Is psychophysics reductive?

Phenomenologists such as Husserl criticized naturalistic psychology, including the program of reducing consciousness to measurable functions of physical stimuli (see Husserl's "Philosophy as Rigorous Science," 1911, and later the *Crisis*, 1936), holding that conscious experience cannot be understood merely as a dependent variable of physical processes. Fechner's own view was not a simple reductionism: he regarded the physical and the psychical as two sides of one reality.

### 2. Fechner or Stevens?

Fechner's logarithmic law and Stevens's power law have been compared for many decades, and the choice depends on what is measured (discrimination versus direct magnitude estimation) and on assumptions about what the observers' numerical judgments mean. Stevens's power law is widely used as a descriptive fit for magnitude estimates, but no consensus has declared one law universally correct.

## The Place of the Concept in the Bridge

Psychophysics connects a philosophical question (how matter and experience are related) with an experimental method that measures the dependence of reports and discriminations on physical stimuli. It connects physics, physiology and psychology, but measuring such dependence does not by itself resolve the philosophical question.

## Legacy

- Fechner is regarded as a principal founder of **psychophysics** and a forerunner of experimental psychology; Wundt's laboratory (1879) institutionalized the experimental program.
- Psychophysical methods and scaling influenced later psychological measurement, including the study of attitudes and preferences (for example Thurstone's scaling work in the 1920s).
- Weber's and Fechner's laws are still used as first approximations in sensory science.
