# Compassion Ethics

Compassion ethics is a current in moral philosophy that makes compassion (being affected by the suffering of others and the actual desire to relieve it) the basis of virtue and a source of moral judgment. It is not a passing emotion that reason should refine or exclude. It thus differs from approaches that confine ethics to a cold rational calculation of utility or duty. It holds that compassionate emotion, when refined by wisdom, is itself a kind of correct moral perception.

## In the Islamic Heritage

Compassion occupies a central place in Islamic ethics. The Qur'an opens with the description of God as "the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful." A hadith states: "The merciful will be shown mercy by the Most Merciful. Show mercy to those on earth, and He who is in heaven will show mercy to you." Al-Ghazali, in *Revival of the Religious Sciences*, made compassion one of the fruits of divine love reflected in the believer's treatment of God's creation, and linked it to the virtue of tenderness toward the weak, animals, and children. Muslim moral philosophers such as Miskawayh, in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, worked within an Aristotelian framework in which emotions are to be governed by reason and moderated between excess and deficiency; on that framework, compassion would be a disposition to be disciplined by wisdom and justice rather than indulged to the point of weakness.

## In Western Philosophy

In Greek philosophy, Aristotle, in the *Rhetoric* (II.8), defined pity (*eleos*) as pain at apparent undeserved suffering that one might expect to befall oneself or those close to one. In modern philosophy, Arthur Schopenhauer developed in *On the Basis of Morality* a radical view that makes compassion (*Mitleid*) the sole basis of genuine moral action. He held that the compassionate individual pierces the illusion of individuality and perceives the unity of the will underlying all suffering creatures, so that he acts toward the pain of others as if it were his own. In contemporary ethics, Martha Nussbaum developed a cognitive analysis of compassion that makes it a complex judgment. Following Aristotle, she analyzes it as involving a judgment that the suffering is serious, a judgment that the sufferer does not deserve it, and a judgment that the sufferer is among the things that matter for one's own flourishing.

## Objections and Illustrative Example

Compassion as the foundation of ethics is objected to as selective and limited in scope. People tend to feel compassion for those who resemble them or whom they know, more than for distant strangers, which may lead to an unjust moral bias. Compassion may also at times conflict with justice, as in leniency toward a criminal out of compassion at the expense of the victim's right. An illustrative example is a physician who sees a patient in pain and immediately moves to relieve it, driven by a direct emotional response rather than by a prior utilitarian calculation. This is the essence of what compassion ethics describes as genuine virtue.
