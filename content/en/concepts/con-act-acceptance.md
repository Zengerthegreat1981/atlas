# Acceptance & Commitment Therapy

## Definition

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT, pronounced as one word, "act") is a behavioral psychotherapy of the "third wave," developed by Steven C. Hayes from the 1980s onward (first at the University of North Carolina at Greensboro, later at the University of Nevada, Reno). Hayes presented it, together with Kirk Strosahl and Kelly Wilson, in a book published by Guilford Press in 1999. Its aim is not to remove painful thoughts and feelings, but to build "psychological flexibility": the capacity to remain in the present moment and act in line with chosen values even in the presence of pain.

## Origins and Philosophical Foundations

The therapy grew out of "comprehensive distancing," an early 1980s technique developed by Hayes and Zettle and applied to depression. The theory rests on the philosophy of "functional contextualism" and on relational frame theory (RFT), formulated by Hayes, Barnes-Holmes, and Roche in a book published by Kluwer in 2001, which explains how language gives thoughts the capacity to appear as reality itself. From this comes the hypothesis of "experiential avoidance," presented by Hayes and colleagues in the *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* in 1996: the attempt to suppress internal experience amplifies it and narrows the patient's life.

## The Six Processes

Practice rests on six interrelated processes, usually depicted as a hexagon: acceptance instead of struggling with experience; cognitive defusion (viewing a thought as a thought rather than as a truth); contact with the present moment; "self as context," meaning the vantage point from which experiences are observed; clarification of values; and committed action. The therapist uses metaphors and experiential exercises more than logical debate, which distinguishes it from Aaron Beck's cognitive therapy, which tests the validity of thoughts and modifies them.

## Clinical Evidence

Hayes and colleagues published in *Behaviour Research and Therapy* in 2006 a review of the therapy's model, processes, and outcomes. In a meta-analysis conducted by A-Tjak and colleagues in *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics* in 2015, the therapy outperformed waiting-list, psychological-placebo, and treatment-as-usual conditions across a range of mental and physical health problems, and did not differ clearly from cognitive behavioral therapy. It has also been studied in psychosis (for example, Bach and Hayes, 2002, with hospitalized patients), obsessive-compulsive disorder, substance use, and chronic pain; the quality and size of these trials vary.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The theory does not historically derive from the Islamic heritage, but the comparison can enrich understanding if it is treated as an analytical comparison rather than an intellectual lineage. Contentment with divine decree (*al-ridha bi-al-qada'*) and patience in the face of affliction, both present in the moral literature of al-Ghazali in *Ihya' 'ulum al-din*, resemble "acceptance" in that both require steadfastness in the face of pain without denying it. However, they rest on a teleological metaphysics, whereas acceptance in ACT is presented as a neutral behavioral tool with respect to belief. In this context one may consider the concept of tawakkul in psychology (see the entry on tawakkul), with the same caution.

## Limits of the Model

Critics argue that some of its concepts, such as "self as context," are difficult to define operationally, that its superiority over other active therapies is modest, and that the quality of some early trials was weak. The school is also criticized for overstating its novelty by drawing an excessively sharp line between itself and traditional cognitive behavioral therapy. In practice, "acceptance" may be misunderstood as resignation to unjust circumstances; in fact it concerns inner experience rather than social conditions that can be changed.
