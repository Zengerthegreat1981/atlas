# Gemeinschaft and Gesellschaft

## Definition

A conceptual dichotomy formulated by the German sociologist Ferdinand Tönnies to distinguish two opposed types of social bonds, which became one of the most influential dichotomies in the history of classical sociology.

## Historical Context

Tönnies formulated this dichotomy in his book published in 1887, in the context of late nineteenth-century Germany, which was undergoing a rapid transformation from a rural agrarian society to an industrial, rapidly urbanizing one. This provoked wide intellectual anxiety about the fate of traditional social bonds under this transformation, an anxiety shared by contemporary thinkers such as Émile Durkheim in France and, later, Georg Simmel in Germany itself.

## Key Ideas or Method

Tönnies distinguished between: (1) **Gemeinschaft** ("community"), intimate organic bonds based on kinship, neighborhood, friendship and shared religion, in which spontaneous, uncalculated "organic will" (Wesenwille) prevails, as in the traditional village and the extended family; and (2) **Gesellschaft** ("society"), calculated contractual bonds based on mutual individual interest, in which rational, calculating "rational will" (Kürwille) prevails, as in the capitalist market, the modern city and the bureaucratic state. Tönnies saw modernization as a gradual historical transition from the first type to the second, although this transition was not inevitably linear, complete or final in every context.

## Impact and Critique

This dichotomy intersected with the "mechanical/organic solidarity" dichotomy formulated by Émile Durkheim in different terms at roughly the same time (1893), despite differences in naming and emphasis: Durkheim stressed the solidarity resulting from the division of labor (his "organic" corresponds to modern complexity and not to simplicity, the opposite of the connotation of Tönnies's "Gemeinschaft"), while Tönnies focused on the type of will and psychological motive behind the bond. Together the two dichotomies became a basic analytical tool in the sociology of modernization and urban life throughout the twentieth century. Some sociologists later criticized the simplistic character of this dichotomy when it is used as an inevitable linear narrative of modernization, pointing out that traditional intimate bonds do not necessarily disappear in modern urban societies but change form and coexist with contractual bonds instead of being wholly replaced by them, as later studies in the sociology of urban social networks showed, documenting the persistence of kinship and neighborhood networks even within the largest modern cities.
