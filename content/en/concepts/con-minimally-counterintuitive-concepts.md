# Minimally Counterintuitive Concepts

A central concept in the cognitive science of religion, formulated by Pascal Boyer to explain why some religious concepts are retained and transmitted orally with greater success than others across generations and cultures.

## The Idea

The human mind classifies things into innate ontological categories (persons, animals, tools, plants...), each of which carries a bundle of implicit expectations (a person has a body, dies, does not pass through walls). A concept that violates **only one** expectation from this bundle (a person who has a mind but no body; a statue that hears prayer) is "minimally counterintuitive": surprising enough to attract attention and be easily retained in memory, but not so chaotic that it cannot be remembered or transmitted coherently. A concept that violates many expectations at once becomes "maximally counterintuitive" and is hard to remember and transmit accurately, so it disappears culturally.

## The Experimental Evidence

The hypothesis was developed by Boyer in *The Naturalness of Religious Ideas* (1994) and tested experimentally by others. Justin Barrett and Melanie Nyhof (2001) used serial retelling, in which stories containing counterintuitive elements are passed along a chain of people, and found that minimally counterintuitive concepts were transmitted better than ordinary ones (`stu-spreading-nonnatural-concepts-barrett-nyhof-2001`). Boyer and Charles Ramble (2001) tested recall of ordinary, minimally counterintuitive and more strongly counterintuitive items among participants in France, Gabon and Nepal, and reported that the memory advantage for minimally counterintuitive items appeared across these cultural settings (`stu-cognitive-templates-boyer-ramble-2001`). Later work, including Norenzayan and colleagues' (2006) analysis of Grimm folktales, supported a cultural-selection reading, though subsequent studies have found that the advantage depends on context and method, and the theory remains debated.

## Relation to the Explanation of the Spread of Religion

Boyer uses this principle to explain why a similar cognitive structure recurs in religious beings across culturally distant traditions (gods with a mind but no body, ancestors who see everything despite their death). This structure exploits a general cognitive bias in human memory that is not in itself specifically religious, which makes this hypothesis an explanation of religion as a "by-product" rather than as a direct evolutionary adaptation of religion itself.
