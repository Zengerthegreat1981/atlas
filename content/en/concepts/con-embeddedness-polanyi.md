# Embeddedness & Fictitious Commodities

## Definition

The idea that markets were historically "embedded" in social relations and political institutions, and that the "self-regulating free market" is a deliberate political project, not an eternal natural given that precedes society.

## Historical Context

The economic historian and anthropologist Karl Polanyi put forward this concept in his book "The Great Transformation," published in 1944, a work he wrote in exile during the Second World War, influenced by his witnessing of the collapse of the nineteenth-century European liberal order and the rise of fascism, which he interpreted as a violent social reaction to the attempt to uproot the economy forcibly from its traditional social context.

## Main Ideas or Method

In *The Great Transformation* (1944), Karl Polanyi proposed that markets, throughout human history, were always "embedded" in social relations and in political, religious and kinship institutions: economic activity was not a separate sphere governed solely by its own logic, but part of a broader social fabric. He argued that the "self-regulating free market" promoted by nineteenth-century liberal capitalism was a deliberate political project to "uproot" the economy forcibly from its traditional social context and to turn land, labor and money into "Fictitious Commodities" bought and sold like any other commodity, although they were never produced in the first place for sale. He predicted that this forced uprooting would inevitably generate a social "counter-movement" (Double Movement) demanding that the economy be re-embedded through protective political regulation.

## Major Works or Representatives

His main reference work is The Great Transformation: The Political and Economic Origins of Our Time (1944). Mark Granovetter revived the concept of embeddedness sociologically in his influential article "Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness" in 1985, moving it from Polanyi's macro-historical analysis to a micro-level analysis of the actual networks of personal relations in which economic actors embed their economic decisions, and helping to found the contemporary field of the "new economic sociology." Granovetter argued against both an "undersocialized" view (the rational individual of neoclassical economics) and an "oversocialized" view (actors who simply follow internalized norms), and he disagreed with Polanyi on one point: he held that embeddedness is a feature of modern markets as well, rather than something largely lost under market society.

## Impact and Critique

This concept became the cornerstone that Granovetter revived sociologically, and it deeply influenced the critique of economic neoliberalism and the study of how actual markets are built on networks of trust and personal relations and not on abstract competition alone. Some of Polanyi's interpretation has been criticized as exaggerating the historical determinism of the idea of the "counter-movement," since not every attempt at economic liberalization necessarily led to a protective reaction of the same force in every historical context. Yet his analysis of the three fictitious commodities, land, labor and money, remains among the most used concepts in the critique of the contemporary market economy.
