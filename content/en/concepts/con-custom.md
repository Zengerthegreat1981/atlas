# Custom: The Binding Force of What Is Done

**Custom** is a practice that is followed because it has been followed — because "that is how things are done" — and that carries a sense of obligation: to break it is not merely unusual but wrong. Custom is the most everyday form of social order. It governs how people greet one another, how they eat, marry, mourn, divide land, and settle debts. It is distinct from tradition in emphasis: tradition is the handing down of the past, while custom is the binding force of established practice in the present — the expectation that what has been done will continue to be done, and that those who depart from it will be corrected. Anthropologists study custom as law without legislators, as morality without preachers, and as the quiet machinery that keeps social life predictable.

## Definition and Development

The classical study of custom as law comes from Henry Maine, whose *Ancient Law* (1861) argued that early societies were governed not by legislation but by custom — by usages that hardened into rules and were enforced by the community. Maine's famous formula was that progressive societies move "from status to contract": from a world in which a person's rights and duties are fixed by their position in family and caste, to one in which they are fixed by agreements freely made. Custom, in this account, belongs to the earlier world, but Maine also saw that it never disappears: even modern law rests on a bed of custom, and judges and legislators constantly draw on established practice.

Bronislaw Malinowski, in *Crime and Custom in Savage Society* (1926), gave the concept its ethnographic foundation. Working among the Trobriand Islanders of Melanesia, Malinowski showed that custom is not blind habit but a system of mutual obligations. People follow custom because it is embedded in reciprocity: the fisherman gives fish to his sister's husband, the gardener gives yams to his chief, and each gift creates a claim to a return. Breach of custom is punished not by a court but by the withdrawal of cooperation, by public shaming, and by the slow accumulation of bad feeling — sanctions that are often more effective than fines or imprisonment. Custom, Malinowski argued, is law in the real sense: a body of binding obligations, maintained by social mechanisms rather than by a state.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

E. P. Thompson, in *Customs in Common* (1991), showed how custom works as a language of protest. Studying eighteenth-century England, Thompson found that plebeian crowds defended a "moral economy" — a set of customary rights to fair prices, to gleaning, to common land — against the market and the law. Custom, for Thompson, was not a conservative residue of the past but a resource for resistance: people appealed to what had always been done in order to challenge what was being done now. His work made custom central to the study of class, showing that the defence of customary rights was one of the main forms of popular politics before the age of trade unions and political parties.

Pierre Bourdieu, in *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1972, English edition 1977), offered a different account of custom's power: through habitus, the embodied dispositions learned in childhood, custom becomes second nature. People do not follow custom because they have decided to obey a rule; they follow it because their bodies know how — and because alternatives do not occur to them.

## Ethnographic Examples

Ethnography shows custom at work in every domain of life. Among the Trobrianders, the elaborate exchanges of the kula ring — armshells and necklaces travelling in opposite directions around a circle of islands — are governed entirely by custom: no one is forced to participate, yet a man who fails to pass on a valuable loses his reputation and his partners. In rural England, customary rights to common land, to the collection of firewood, and to the gleaning of fields after harvest survived for centuries alongside formal law, and their enclosure in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries was experienced as theft.

Custom also governs the most intimate matters. Rules about who may marry whom, how the dead are mourned, and how children are named are customary in most societies: they are learned by doing, enforced by the expectations of kin and neighbours, and felt as moral obligations rather than as legal requirements.

## Contemporary Debates

Custom is now at the centre of legal and political debates. In many post-colonial states, customary law is formally recognised alongside state law, and courts must decide which custom applies, who speaks for it, and how it relates to constitutional rights — above all, to the rights of women, who are often disadvantaged by customary rules on marriage, inheritance, and land. Some argue that customary law should be codified and reformed; others argue that codification freezes a living practice and hands power to those who claim to represent it. A second debate concerns custom and development: international agencies often treat customary tenure as an obstacle to progress, while anthropologists argue that customary systems of landholding protect the poor and the environment more effectively than individual title.

A third debate concerns the boundary between custom and habit. If custom is simply what people do, how do we distinguish it from mere routine? The answer anthropologists give is normative: a custom is a practice that people feel ought to be followed, and whose breach calls for a response — a rebuke, a payment, a ritual repair.
