# Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy

## Definition

Psychedelic-assisted therapy is a therapeutic format in which the patient is given a defined dose of a consciousness-altering substance, such as psilocybin, LSD, or MDMA (which is sometimes classified as an entactogen rather than a psychedelic in the strict sense), within a structured psychological framework that includes preparation before the session, supportive presence during it, and integration afterward. The substance is not the complete treatment but a tool within psychological therapy. Other entries cover psilocybin in depression, the psychedelic experience, and integration. This entry focuses on the development of the field, its evidence, and its limits.

## Early History

Albert Hofmann synthesized LSD in 1938 in Switzerland and discovered its effect in 1943 through accidental exposure. Humphry Osmond coined the term "psychedelic" ("mind-manifesting") in 1956-1957, and worked with Abram Hoffer in Saskatchewan, Canada, on treating alcohol addiction. Stanislav Grof, who began clinical LSD research in Czechoslovakia in the 1950s, later wrote *LSD Psychotherapy* (1980). Walter Pahnke, in the "Good Friday Experiment" of 1962, tested the role of the religious setting in the experience. The United States Controlled Substances Act of 1970 then classified these substances in Schedule I, and research stopped for roughly three decades.

## The Modern Revival

Griffiths and colleagues reported in *Psychopharmacology* in 2006 that a high dose of psilocybin given to healthy volunteers produced "mystical-type experiences" that many rated as having substantial personal meaning and spiritual significance. A 2008 follow-up of that sample found that many participants still described the experience that way at fourteen months. Carhart-Harris and colleagues presented open-label results in *Lancet Psychiatry* in 2016 for twelve patients with treatment-resistant depression. Davis and colleagues published in *JAMA Psychiatry* in 2021 a small randomized trial (24 participants) in major depression that showed a large effect compared with a delayed-treatment waitlist. Carhart-Harris and colleagues compared psilocybin with escitalopram in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2021, in a trial of 59 patients, and no significant difference was found on the primary depression scale. In posttraumatic stress disorder, Mitchell and colleagues published in *Nature Medicine* in 2021 a phase 3 trial of MDMA-assisted therapy that reported benefit over placebo with therapy. However, the US Food and Drug Administration rejected the application in August 2024, requesting additional study and pointing to questions about blinding, bias, and documentation of safety.

## Methodological Problems

Trials lose their blinding because participants know whether they received the substance, which inflates expectations of benefit. It is difficult to separate the effect of the drug from the effect of psychological support, and studies are mostly conducted on small and non-diverse samples. Risks of psychosis, panic, and misuse remain, and people with psychosis or bipolar disorder are usually excluded from trials. Ethical boundaries between therapist and patient in cases of severe vulnerability are also a concern, and reports of unprofessional behavior have emerged.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Arabic medical heritage did not know a similar substance used within a therapeutic framework, but it did know a long debate about hashish. Franz Rosenthal studied it in *The Herb: Hashish versus Medieval Muslim Society* (Brill, 1971) as a discussion among jurists, physicians, and men of letters, and Badr al-Din al-Zarkashi wrote his treatise on the prohibition of hashish (see the entry on al-Zarkashi). Sufism offers a parallel discussion of the "states" (ahwal) and experiences of annihilation (fana') that are attained through remembrance and striving rather than through substances. The comparison with the psychedelic experience remains analytical rather than a claim of equivalence (see the entry on Annihilation and Subsistence in Sufism).

## Limits

The evidence is still in its early stages. The benefits reported in trials may be greater than those in routine practice, and as of the mid-2020s these treatments remain largely investigational in most jurisdictions, with limited regulated access in a few (for example, authorized prescribing of psilocybin and MDMA in Australia since 2023). The questions of their regulation, cost, and training of therapists remain open (see the entries on Psilocybin for Depression and the Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy School). This entry is educational and does not constitute clinical advice; these substances carry psychological and legal risks.
