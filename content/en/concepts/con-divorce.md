# Divorce

Divorce is the formal dissolution of a marriage, ending the legal, social and economic bond between spouses and freeing them to remarry. It is distinct from separation, desertion and annulment: separation leaves the marriage intact, desertion ends cohabitation without legal process, and annulment declares that a marriage never validly existed. Divorce takes many forms — by mutual consent, by the unilateral repudiation of one spouse, by court decree on grounds of fault such as adultery or cruelty, or under no-fault law where no blame need be assigned — and its availability, its procedure and its consequences vary enormously across societies and across history. For anthropology and sociology, divorce is a window onto marriage itself: what a society thinks marriage is, who may end it, and what happens to property, children and status when it ends.

## Origins and Development

Divorce is as old as marriage. In ancient Rome, divorce by mutual consent was available to citizens; in classical Islamic law, a husband could repudiate his wife by *talaq* while a wife could seek dissolution (*khul'*) by returning her dower; in many small-scale societies, marriage could be ended by the simple return of bridewealth and the departure of one spouse. The modern Western history of divorce is a history of gradual liberalisation. For centuries the Catholic Church held marriage indissoluble, allowing annulment but not divorce; Protestant states permitted it on limited grounds, and in England before 1857 divorce with remarriage generally required an act of Parliament. The nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw fault-based divorce statutes spread, and then, from the late 1960s, no-fault divorce: California's Family Law Act of 1969 was the first no-fault statute in the United States, and many Western countries liberalised their laws in the following decades.

The sociological study of divorce began in earnest with William Goode's *After Divorce* (1956), based on interviews with divorced mothers in Detroit. Goode found that divorce was not a single event but a process — of emotional separation, legal dissolution and social readjustment — and that its effects depended less on divorce itself than on the resources, networks and meanings people brought to it. His work established the field's central insight: divorce must be studied as a process unfolding over time, not as a momentary rupture.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns the causes of rising divorce rates. Demographers point to structural changes: women's economic independence, the decline of the male-breadwinner family, urbanisation, later marriage and the spread of individualist values. Andrew Cherlin, in *Marriage, Divorce, Remarriage* (1981, revised 1992) and later in "The Deinstitutionalization of American Marriage" (2004), argued that the rise of divorce reflects a transformation in what marriage is for — from a practical partnership organised around production and child-rearing to a companionship based on emotional fulfilment. When love and personal growth become the purpose of marriage, marriages that fail to deliver them are more likely to end. Critics note that this account fits the middle classes better than the poor, for whom economic strain rather than unmet self-realisation drives marital breakdown.

A second debate concerns consequences, above all for children. Judith Wallerstein and Joan Kelly, in *Surviving the Breakup* (1980), followed sixty families for years after divorce and found that many children experienced lasting distress, divided loyalties and difficulties in their own relationships. Paul Amato, reviewing the evidence in 2000, reached a more qualified conclusion: on average, children of divorced parents fare somewhat worse on measures of well-being, achievement and relationship stability, but the differences are modest, many children adapt well, and the effects depend heavily on the level of parental conflict before and after divorce. A high-conflict marriage that ends may benefit children; a low-conflict marriage that ends may harm them. The debate has shifted from whether divorce harms children to under what conditions, and through what mechanisms — economic decline, reduced parental time, inter-parental conflict.

A third debate concerns gender and economics. Divorce typically impoverishes women more than men: women more often retain custody of children while losing access to a husband's income, and their earning capacity has often been reduced by years of unpaid domestic work. Feminist scholarship has therefore treated divorce law — the division of property, maintenance, custody — as a central site of gender inequality, and reforms such as the recognition of homemaking as an economic contribution as partial remedies.

A fourth dimension is comparative and cross-cultural. In some societies divorce is common and unremarkable; in others it is rare and stigmatised; in still others it is effectively available only to men. Anthropologists have shown that the ease of divorce correlates with women's access to independent resources — land, wages, kin support — and with the strength of the conjugal bond relative to the ties between spouses and their own kin. Where marriage is primarily an alliance between families, divorce is a matter for the families; where it is primarily a bond between individuals, divorce is a matter for the couple and the courts.

## Contemporary Relevance

Divorce rates have stabilised or fallen in many Western countries since their late-twentieth-century peak, but divorce remains a mass experience: a substantial share of marriages in the United States and much of Europe still end in divorce. Its consequences ripple through housing, pensions, child welfare and kinship: the rise of stepfamilies, shared custody and "living apart together" arrangements has remade the family landscape. New questions have emerged — the division of digital assets, the status of divorce in same-sex marriage, the growth of "grey divorce" among older couples, and the use of mediation and collaborative law as alternatives to adversarial courts. Divorce is no longer treated as a social pathology but as a normal, if painful, feature of contemporary family life — and its study continues to reveal what marriage, kinship and gender mean in a given society.
