# Totemism: The Animal, the Plant, and the Social Order

**Totemism** is a system in which a human group — a clan, a moiety, a lineage — is identified with a species of animal or plant, or with a natural phenomenon, called its totem. Members of the group regard the totem as a kindred being and often as an ancestor; they may be forbidden to eat it, to kill it, or to harm it, and they may paint, carve, or dance its likeness in ceremony. Totemism links three things that seem at first unrelated: the classification of nature, the organisation of society, and the sacred. For more than a century it has been one of anthropology's most argued-over concepts, a testing ground for theories of religion, kinship, and the human mind.

## Definition and Development

The word "totem" comes from the Ojibwa *ototeman*, meaning "my kin" or "my brother-sister kin," recorded by fur traders and missionaries in North America. Nineteenth-century writers, among them John Ferguson McLennan in essays of 1869–70, seized on the idea and treated totemism as an early stage in the development of religion and society. James Frazer assembled the comparative evidence in *Totemism and Exogamy* (1910), where he traced totemism to mistaken ideas about conception and nature and treated it as distinct in origin from exogamy, the rule of marrying outside one's group.

Émile Durkheim, in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912, English edition 1915), turned such intellectualist explanations on their head. Studying Australian Aboriginal totemism, Durkheim argued that the totem was not really an animal at all: it was the clan itself, worshipped in the form of an emblem. The totemic emblem — painted on bodies, carved on sacred boards, danced in ceremony — was the visible sign of the group, and the reverence paid to it was reverence for society itself. Religion, Durkheim concluded, was born when people gathered together and felt the force of the collective, a force they experienced as sacred and projected onto the totem. Totemism was thus not a primitive confusion but the elementary form of all religion.

## Key Theoretical Contributions

The decisive rethinking of totemism came from Claude Lévi-Strauss in *Totemism* (1962, English edition 1963). Lévi-Strauss argued that earlier theorists had invented a false problem: there is no single thing called totemism, only a variety of practices that had been lumped together under one label. What totemic systems really do, he showed, is use natural species as a language for thinking about social difference. Just as species differ from one another in nature — the eagle from the bear, the kangaroo from the emu — so clans differ from one another in society; the differences between species provide a ready-made model for the differences between groups. Totemic classifications are therefore not primitive biology but a form of thought, a way of ordering the social world by analogy with the natural world.

A. A. Goldenweiser, in "Totemism, an Analytical Study" (1910), had already pointed out that the features attributed to totemism — clan organisation, belief in descent from the totem, taboos on eating it — do not regularly occur together, and that no single explanation could cover them all. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, in *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952), added that totemic animals are chosen not arbitrarily but because they are significant in the group's life: dangerous, useful, conspicuous, or ritually important. Together, these critiques dissolved totemism as a unitary stage of evolution and remade it as a problem of classification and symbolism.

## Ethnographic Examples

Among Aboriginal peoples of central Australia, totemism is inseparable from the Dreaming, the ancestral time when totemic beings shaped the land. Each person belongs to one or more totemic groups, inherits rights in sacred sites and songs, and is forbidden to eat their totem except in strictly ritual contexts. Increase ceremonies are performed to ensure the abundance of the totemic species, and the great cycles of song and dance re-enact the journeys of the ancestors across country. Here totemism is not a belief about animals; it is a system of land tenure, kinship, law, and identity, written into the landscape itself.

In North America, the totem poles of the peoples of the Northwest Coast — Haida, Tlingit, Kwakwaka'wakw — carve clan crests into monumental cedar, proclaiming lineage, rank, and rights at potlatch feasts. In each case, the totem does double work: it binds the members of a group to one another and to their ancestors, and it marks them off from neighbouring groups who carry different emblems.

## Contemporary Debates

Totemism remains alive in anthropology less as a label than as a set of questions. One debate concerns the relationship between totemism and environmental thought: do totemic taboos protect species and habitats, functioning as an indigenous conservation ethic? Some anthropologists argue that totemic prohibitions have real ecological effects; others warn against romanticising them, noting that totems are protected for social and ritual reasons, not for ecological ones, and that the protection is often partial and selective. A second debate concerns identity: in Australia, totemic affiliation has become a basis for claims to land and recognition under native title law, so that an old anthropological category now carries legal and political weight.

A third strand of debate follows Lévi-Strauss in treating totemism as a mode of thought rather than a stage of religion. If totemic classification is a way of thinking with nature, then it has counterparts everywhere: in heraldry, in sports mascots, in national animals, in the team names and logos through which modern groups mark their identities.
