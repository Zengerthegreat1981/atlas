# Diglossia

A sociolinguistic concept formulated by Charles Ferguson in his seminal 1959 article. It describes a linguistic situation in which a single speech community uses **two clearly distinct varieties** of the same language, distributed functionally across separate social contexts.

## Descriptive Structure

Ferguson distinguished between:

- **High variety (H)**: used in formal writing, oratory, education and religion. It is usually not acquired as a native mother tongue but learned at school.
- **Low variety (L)**: used in informal everyday conversation, and acquired naturally as a mother tongue.

In his original article, Ferguson cited four model cases: Arabic (standard versus spoken dialects), Modern Greek (Katharevousa versus Demotic), Swiss German (versus standard German), and Haitian Creole (versus French).

## Descriptive Status of the Concept

Ferguson's concept is descriptive. Contemporary debates over Arabic language policy (such as calls to simplify the standard language, official recognition of the dialects, or preservation of the classical standard) are **contested political and educational positions**, not results imposed by descriptive linguistics itself.

## Ferguson's Nine Criteria for Distinguishing H and L

Ferguson identified nine descriptive dimensions on which the high and low varieties differ: function (strict complementary distribution with no overlap), prestige (H is usually viewed as the socially and sometimes religiously superior variety), literary heritage (usually associated with H alone), acquisition (H is learned at school, L is acquired naturally from childhood), standardization (H is usually described in dictionaries and more precise codified grammars), historical stability (diglossia may last for centuries), grammar (the two differ structurally, not only phonologically), lexicon (the two share most of their vocabulary, but H has technical and learned terms with no ordinary L equivalent, and L has everyday expressions with no H equivalent), and phonology (the sound systems of H and L form a single phonological structure, in which L is the basic system and H is in some respects a subsystem of it).

## Distinction from Ordinary Bilingualism

Ferguson stressed that diglossia is a stable, continuing phenomenon that may last for generations or centuries, not a transitional state leading to one language replacing another. It differs from ordinary individual bilingualism in that the functional distribution is shared socially and is strict, not merely a matter of an individual's command of two languages without a stable functional allocation. This distinction later led Joshua Fishman to extend the concept and to separate the dimensions of diglossia and bilingualism analytically.
