# Bipolar Spectrum Disorders

A diagnostic category within the **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR)**, under the chapter "Bipolar and Related Disorders." It covers disorders marked by fluctuation of mood between **manic or hypomanic** episodes and **depressive** episodes, with varying degrees of severity that form a "spectrum" rather than a single homogeneous category.

## Main Diagnostic Categories

**Bipolar I Disorder**: requires at least one **manic episode**, a period of abnormally elevated or irritable mood accompanied by symptoms such as increased activity, excessive self-confidence, racing thoughts, reduced need for sleep, and dangerous impulsivity. The episode lasts at least one week or requires hospitalization. A major depressive episode is not required for the diagnosis, although it is clinically common.

**Bipolar II Disorder**: requires at least one **hypomanic episode** (symptoms of the same general kind as mania, but not severe enough to cause marked impairment in functioning or to require hospitalization, and without psychosis, lasting at least four consecutive days) and at least one **major depressive episode**, with no full manic episode ever having occurred.

**Cyclothymic Disorder**: chronic fluctuation (at least two years in adults, one year in children and adolescents) between hypomanic symptoms and depressive symptoms that do not reach the threshold of a full episode, with no symptom-free period lasting longer than two consecutive months.

The chapter also includes **substance/medication-induced bipolar and related disorder**, **bipolar and related disorder due to another medical condition**, and the categories of "other specified" and "unspecified" for cases below the full diagnostic threshold. These residual categories accommodate presentations below the full threshold; the broader term "spectrum" is used more widely in research, as discussed below. The concept itself descends from Kraepelin's "manic-depressive insanity" (1899), and the separation of unipolar and bipolar illness was proposed in the 1950s and 1960s (Leonhard, Angst, and Perris).

## The Debate over the Concept of "Spectrum"

Researchers, most notably Hagop Akiskal, have proposed the idea of an extended **bipolar spectrum** that includes cases below the formal diagnostic threshold (such as mild hyperthymic temperament or short-lived mood swings). Their argument is that the sharp separation between unipolar and bipolar depression ignores a real clinical gradient. However, DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 maintain clear classification criteria (episodes of defined duration and severity) to avoid excessive expansion of the diagnosis, which could bring ordinary mood fluctuations within the pathological framework.

## Prevalence

In the US National Comorbidity Survey Replication, Merikangas and colleagues (2007) estimated lifetime prevalence at about 1.0% for bipolar I and about 1.1% for bipolar II, with a further group of subthreshold bipolar spectrum cases. Twelve-month rates were lower. Figures differ by country, instrument, and by how broadly "spectrum" is drawn, so these percentages should not be treated as a worldwide constant. Bipolar disorder often begins in late adolescence or early adulthood, and depressive episodes account for more of the time ill than manic episodes do.

## Clinical Significance

Distinguishing bipolar from unipolar depression bears on treatment decisions. Mood stabilizers (such as lithium and valproate) and some second-generation antipsychotics are core medications in the treatment of bipolar disorder, particularly for mania. Antidepressant monotherapy is generally regarded with caution in bipolar disorder, since it may trigger a switch toward mania or hypomania or accelerate mood cycles; the evidence is less clear for Bipolar II than for Bipolar I. Treatment decisions are individual and belong to a clinician.
