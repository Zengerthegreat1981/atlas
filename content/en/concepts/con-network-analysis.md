# Network Analysis

Social network analysis is the study of social life through the pattern of relationships between actors rather than through their individual attributes. Instead of asking what kind of people do a certain thing, it asks how people are connected to one another, and how the shape of those connections — who is central, who bridges separate groups, where the gaps lie — shapes what happens. A network is made of nodes, which may be individuals, organisations, or states, and ties, which may be friendship, kinship, trade, advice, or conflict. By mapping these ties, network analysis reveals structures that are invisible to methods that treat each person or organisation in isolation.

## Definition

The core premise of network analysis is that social structure is a pattern of relationships. Two actors who occupy similar positions in a network face similar opportunities and constraints, even if they differ in age, income, or beliefs. The method provides a vocabulary for describing that structure. Density measures how many of the possible ties in a network actually exist. Centrality measures how prominent a node is, whether by the number of its direct connections, its position on the paths between others, or its closeness to everyone else. A structural hole is a gap between two clusters of densely connected actors, and the actor who bridges that gap can control the flow of information between them. A weak tie is a casual acquaintance rather than a close friend, and weak ties are often more valuable for finding new information precisely because they reach into social circles the actor does not already inhabit. These concepts are formalised with the tools of graph theory and matrix algebra, which allow researchers to compare networks of very different sizes and kinds on a common footing.

## Origins and Development

Network thinking has several independent roots. In psychology, Jacob Moreno invented sociometry in the 1930s, mapping the attractions and rejections within small groups in his book *Who Shall Survive?* (1934) and developing the sociogram, a diagram of who chooses whom. In anthropology, John Barnes's study of a Norwegian island parish, published in 1954, introduced the term "social network" to describe the web of ties that cut across formal institutions such as the church and the workplace. In sociology, the Harvard school of the 1970s, led by Harrison White and his students, turned these ideas into a rigorous structural programme, arguing that social roles and categories emerge from patterns of ties rather than from the attributes of individuals. Mark Granovetter's "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) became one of the most cited articles in sociology by showing that people who found jobs through personal contacts more often learned of them from people they saw only occasionally or rarely than from those they saw often. Stanley Wasserman and Katherine Faust's *Social Network Analysis* (1994) then systematised the mathematics, and Ronald Burt's *Structural Holes* (1992) connected network position to competitive advantage in organisations.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One central debate concerns the relationship between structure and agency. Critics charge that network analysis can become a purely formal exercise, describing the shape of ties while ignoring what flows through them — money, meaning, emotion, and power — and why people form ties in the first place. Defenders reply that structure and content are complementary, and that the best network research combines formal mapping with ethnographic or historical understanding of the relationships involved. A second debate concerns the boundary problem: a network is never complete, and the researcher's decision about where to draw its edges — a classroom, a firm, a city — shapes the results. A third concerns dynamics. Most early network studies were snapshots, but relationships form, dissolve, and change, and recent work uses longitudinal data and statistical models to explain how networks evolve. A fourth dimension is the level of analysis. Network methods have been applied to friendship in schools, to alliances among firms, to trade between nations, and to the spread of diseases and rumours, and the same structural concepts operate across these scales, though their interpretation differs.

## Contemporary Relevance

Network analysis has become one of the most influential approaches in the social sciences, and its reach now extends far beyond sociology. Public health researchers map sexual and needle-sharing networks to predict the spread of epidemics and to target interventions at the most connected individuals. Organisational scholars use network data to identify informal leaders, bottlenecks in communication, and the brokers who connect otherwise separate departments. Political scientists study networks of donors, lobbyists, and legislators to understand influence. The rise of online social media has produced networks of unprecedented scale, and the analysis of digital trace data has made network methods central to the study of misinformation, political polarisation, and social movements. At the same time, the availability of such data raises ethical questions about privacy and consent, since network data implicates not only the people who provide it but also their contacts. The method's enduring insight is that who you know, and where you sit in the web of relationships, often matters more than who you are.
