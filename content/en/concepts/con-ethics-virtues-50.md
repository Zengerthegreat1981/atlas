# Love Ethics

Love ethics is a current in moral philosophy that sees love, in its broad sense encompassing affection, self-giving, and genuine concern for the good of the other, as the foundation of right moral action, not merely a particular emotion confined to the circle of family and friends. It usually distinguishes several kinds of love: sensual love (*eros*), the love of friendship (*philia*), and unconditional self-giving love (*agape*). The last of these is the centerpiece of religious and philosophical love ethics.

## In the Islamic Tradition

In Islamic thought, and especially in Sufism, love is both a moral and a cognitive station. Ibn Hazm al-Andalusi, in *Tawq al-Hamama fi al-Ulfa wa-l-Ulaf* (The Ring of the Dove), offered a delicate analysis of the natures, causes, and signs of human love, linking it to noble qualities such as fidelity, truthfulness, and chastity. Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' ʿUlum al-Din* (The Revival of the Religious Sciences), distinguished between love of God, which is the highest love and its ultimate goal, and love of creation, which should be a branch of it: whoever loves God loves His creation for His sake. In later Sufism, as in the thought of Ibn Arabi, love became a universal existential principle that explains even the act of divine creation. The saying "I was a hidden treasure and loved to be known" is widely quoted in Sufi literature as a divine saying, but hadith scholars generally regard it as having no sound chain of transmission.

## In Western Philosophy

In Greek philosophy, Plato in the *Symposium* proposed a ladder of love that begins with individual physical attraction and gradually rises to love of absolute, abstract beauty, a conception later known as "Platonic love." In Christian theology, the Swedish theologian Anders Nygren, in *Agape and Eros*, distinguished between two opposing kinds of love: eros, an ascending love that seeks perfection for its own sake, and agape, a descending divine love that is given unconditionally, without the lover having merited it. In contemporary care ethics, as developed by Nel Noddings, love and relational concern are restored as a source of moral knowledge no less important than the abstract principles of justice.

## Objections and an Example

It is objected that making love the foundation of ethics is problematic, since love is a changeable emotion that cannot serve as a stable criterion for right action, and it may lead to favoring those whom we love at the expense of justice toward strangers. Proponents of this current reply that mature love is not blind passion but a refined volitional stance that includes willing the good of the beloved for the beloved's own sake, a formulation that goes back to Aristotle's account of loving a friend (*Rhetoric* II.4) and is echoed in al-Ghazali's account of love. An example: a mother who keeps watch through the nights caring for a sick child, without expecting any return, is an act usually cited as a model of self-giving love (*agape*), in contrast to love based on mutual benefit.
