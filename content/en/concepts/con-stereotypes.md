# Stereotypes

Stereotypes are widely shared, oversimplified beliefs about the characteristics of members of a social group. To stereotype is to attribute traits, motives, or behaviours to a person not on the basis of what is known about that individual, but on the basis of their membership in a category — a nationality, ethnicity, gender, class, age group, or profession. Stereotypes simplify a complex social world into manageable categories, but that simplification carries costs: it distorts perception, justifies inequality, and shapes how groups treat one another.

## Origins and Development

The term entered the social sciences through the American journalist Walter Lippmann, who in *Public Opinion* (1922) borrowed the printer's word "stereotype" — a metal plate cast from a mould, used to reproduce the same image again and again — to describe the "pictures in our heads" through which people make sense of a world too large to experience directly. For Lippmann, stereotypes were not simply errors; they were necessary cognitive shortcuts, but ones that also defended existing habits of thought and social order.

The concept became central to the study of prejudice with Gordon Allport's *The Nature of Prejudice* (1954), which treated stereotyping as a normal outcome of categorisation: the mind groups people just as it groups objects, and the categories then guide expectation and judgement. Henri Tajfel deepened this cognitive account in the 1970s with his minimal group experiments, showing that merely assigning people to arbitrary groups was enough to produce favouritism toward one's own group and bias against the other. Stereotyping, in this tradition, is not a symptom of ignorance or a disordered personality, but a routine feature of how minds organise social information — one that becomes dangerous when it is linked to power.

Later research distinguished the content of stereotypes from their use. Susan Fiske and her colleagues' stereotype content model (2002) showed that groups are commonly judged along two dimensions — warmth and competence — producing distinct emotional responses: envied groups are seen as competent but cold, pitied groups as warm but incompetent, admired groups as both, and contemptible groups as neither. Patricia Devine (1989) demonstrated that stereotypes can be activated automatically, even in people who consciously reject prejudice, so that controlling them requires deliberate effort rather than goodwill alone.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One long-running debate concerns whether stereotypes contain a "kernel of truth." Some researchers argue that many stereotypes reflect real, if exaggerated, group differences in roles or circumstances; others reply that even statistically grounded generalisations mislead when applied to individuals, and that stereotypes typically reverse cause and effect — attributing to a group's nature what is in fact produced by its social position. A related question is whether stereotypes are primarily cognitive or primarily ideological: tools the mind uses to save effort, or tools dominant groups use to legitimise their dominance. Most contemporary scholarship treats them as both at once.

A second dimension is the distinction between descriptive stereotypes (claims about what a group is like) and prescriptive stereotypes (claims about how its members ought to behave). Prescriptive stereotypes police behaviour, punishing women who are assertive, men who are nurturing, or older workers who resist retirement. A third dimension concerns change: stereotypes are remarkably persistent, but they do shift with social roles. As women entered paid work in large numbers, for instance, the stereotype of women as less competent weakened — evidence that stereotypes track the division of labour as much as they distort it.

The phenomenon of stereotype threat, identified by Claude Steele and Joshua Aronson (1995), added a further dimension. When members of a negatively stereotyped group are reminded of the stereotype in a testing situation, anxiety about confirming it can depress their performance, creating a self-fulfilling effect. Stereotypes, in other words, do not merely describe the social world; they help to make it.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Stereotypes remain central to debates about discrimination, media representation, and artificial intelligence. In hiring, policing, and medicine, researchers document how stereotyped expectations shape decisions even when decision-makers deny bias, motivating interventions from structured interviews to blinded review. In media studies, the concept frames criticism of stock characters and the politics of representation, while in technology, machine-learning systems trained on human data have been shown to reproduce and amplify existing stereotypes, raising the question of whether automated decisions inherit human prejudice in a less visible form.

Critics caution against treating stereotyping as a purely individual cognitive flaw, which can obscure the institutions that produce and profit from it. Others warn that the language of stereotyping can be stretched so broadly that any generalisation about a group counts as one, blurring the difference between harmless shorthand and harmful prejudice. The concept's enduring value lies in connecting these levels: it names a mental process that is ordinary and universal, while showing how that process, embedded in unequal societies, sustains discrimination.
