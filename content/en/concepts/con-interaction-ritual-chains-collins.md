# Interaction Ritual Chains and Emotional Energy

The idea that the energy and motives that drive large-scale social conflict arise from the accumulation of everyday micro-moments of interaction, successful or failed, that produce or drain "emotional energy".

## The Central Idea

In *Interaction Ritual Chains* (2004, building on earlier work such as *Conflict Sociology*, 1975), Randall Collins proposed that every face-to-face interaction is an "interaction ritual" (inspired by Goffman and Durkheim) that produces or drains "emotional energy": a feeling of confidence and enthusiasm that the individual carries into later interactions. Individuals seek, without clear awareness, to maximize this energy by selecting their interactions, so "interaction ritual markets" arise in which emotional energy is distributed with sharp class inequality: those with higher social and cultural capital obtain cumulatively more successful interactions, and this micro-level accumulation generates the very structure of overall class conflict.

## Components of a Successful Interaction Ritual

Collins, drawing on Durkheim's analysis of collective religious rituals in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, identified four components necessary for any interaction ritual to succeed in generating emotional energy and collective solidarity: the actual physical assembly of the participants in one place; the existence of clear boundaries separating "insider" participants from "outsiders" to the interaction; a shared focus of attention on a single object or action; and the sharing of a mutual mood or emotional state that is amplified through bodily and vocal feedback among those present (as in collective chanting or synchronized rhythm). When these conditions are met, the ritual produces "sacred symbols" that later symbolize the collective solidarity itself (such as a flag, a slogan or a song), and gives participants emotional energy that they carry with them into their following interactions.

## Interaction Ritual Markets and Class Inequality

Collins extended the "market" metaphor to describe how individuals choose their everyday interactions: people tend, according to an implicit and often unconscious calculation, to prefer interactions that promise a higher return of emotional energy and to avoid those that drain it (such as humiliating or boring interactions). However, the ability to reach emotionally "profitable" interactions is not socially equal: those with a higher social and economic position usually have a wider network of relations and greater opportunities to take part in successful interaction rituals (professional meetings, refined cultural gatherings), while marginalized groups are often denied even the chance to take part in such rituals, so less emotional energy and lower self-confidence accumulate in them, reproducing class inequality psychologically and behaviourally through a repeated everyday micro mechanism and not through a single large structural decision.

## Application to Violence

In *Violence: A Micro-Sociological Theory* (2008), Collins applied the framework to confrontations. He argued that most people find violence difficult because of confrontational tension and fear, which disrupts rhythmic mutual entrainment, and that violence typically occurs when participants find ways around this barrier, for example by attacking a victim who is weak or in a situation of emotional dominance, or through the surge of a "forward panic". His analysis draws on photographs, video recordings and observational accounts of fights, and it has been criticised for relying on selected situations and for downplaying the role of individual and background factors.

## Impact

This framework offered an influential theoretical bridge between micro and macro sociology, and influenced the contemporary sociology of emotion and violence.
