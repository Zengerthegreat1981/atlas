# Animism (Tylorian)

A concept formulated by Edward Tylor in *Primitive Culture* (1871) as a theory of the origin of religion. Belief in spiritual beings (spirits, ghosts, and immaterial forces inhabiting objects and creatures) is, in his account, the simplest and oldest form of religion in human history.

## Central Idea

Tylor argued that animism arose from early humans' attempts to explain puzzling everyday phenomena. Why does a sleeper see events in a dream while the body remains still? Why does the body of the dead become motionless and unconscious? According to Tylor, the earliest humans inferred the existence of a "soul" or "spirit" separate from the body, capable of leaving it (during sleep or at death). They then generalized this idea to include all of nature, with spirits inhabiting trees, rivers, and mountains. Religion arose from this primitive explanatory foundation and developed through more complex stages (polytheism, then monotheism) according to the evolutionary model Tylor adopted.

## Critique

This rationalist-psychological explanation of the origin of religion was later criticized by anthropologists who considered it simplistic. It fails to explain the social and ritual dimension of religion, which Émile Durkheim later emphasized. The theory was also part of the general charge that classical evolutionism was Eurocentric, in assuming an inevitable sequence from the "primitive" to the "civilized."

## Impact

Despite this criticism, the concept of animism has remained in use as a descriptive term in the anthropology of religion to the present day. The contemporary ontological turn (Philippe Descola especially) has revived animism, not as a primitive evolutionary stage, but as one of several modes of relating humans and non-humans, widespread in the cosmologies of Indigenous peoples around the world.

## Tylor's Broader Theory

Animism is part of Tylor's broader evolutionary project, set out in *Primitive Culture*: a conception of the development of human culture through broad stages from "savage" through "barbarous" to "civilized" life. In explaining the origin of religion, it challenged the mythological theory of Friedrich Max Müller, who traced myth to a "disease of language" and solar imagery. Within this scheme, religious beliefs also run parallel to the sequence, from simple animism to organized polytheism and then monotheism. Tylor's work on this theme is an example of what was later called "armchair anthropology", which relied on comparing the reports of missionaries and travelers about geographically distant peoples to derive general evolutionary laws, largely without systematic fieldwork by the researcher.

## The Concept of "Survivals"

Alongside animism, Tylor developed the concept of cultural "survivals": practices or beliefs from earlier evolutionary stages that persist in later, "civilized" societies without a clear practical function. Such survivals are like archaeological remains within a living culture, for example popular astrology, which continues despite the spread of modern science. He used them as additional evidence for his supposed evolutionary sequence.
