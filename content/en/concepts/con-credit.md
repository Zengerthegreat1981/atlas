# Credit Systems

Credit systems are the social arrangements through which people lend, borrow, and defer payment, creating obligations that stretch across time. Credit is not only a financial instrument: it is a social relationship. To extend credit is to trust that the borrower will repay, and to accept credit is to enter a relationship of obligation that may be expressed in money, goods, labour, or loyalty. Anthropologists study credit systems in markets and villages, in rotating savings associations and pawnshops, in kinship networks and in the formal institutions of banks and states, asking how trust is built, how debt is enforced, and what happens when repayment fails.

## Origins and Development

Classical economics imagined that money came first and credit followed, with barter giving way to coin and coin to lending. Anthropology reversed this story. Marcel Mauss argued in *The Gift*, published in 1925, that in many societies exchange was organised not by buying and selling but by giving, receiving, and reciprocating, and that the obligation to repay a gift was the earliest form of credit. A gift created a debt that had to be honoured, and honour depended on repaying it. Credit, in this view, is older than money and more fundamental than markets.

Karl Polanyi developed the argument in *The Great Transformation*, published in 1944. He showed that markets in land, labour, and money were historically recent creations, and that for most of human history economic life was embedded in social relations — in kinship, religion, and political obligation. Credit in such societies was personal: it depended on knowing the borrower, on reputation, and on the sanctions of the community. The rise of impersonal banking and state-backed money did not abolish these personal forms of credit; it layered new ones on top of them.

Ethnographers have documented the extraordinary variety of credit systems. Chris Gregory, writing about Papua New Guinea in *Gifts and Commodities* in 1982, distinguished between gift exchange, in which things carry the identity of the giver and create lasting relationships, and commodity exchange, in which things are alienated and the transaction ends. Credit sits between the two: a loan is a commodity-like transfer of value, but it creates a continuing relationship like a gift. Rotating savings and credit associations, found from West Africa to Southeast Asia, formalise this logic: members contribute regularly to a common fund and take turns receiving the whole sum, so that each member is in turn a creditor and a debtor of the group.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns the morality of debt. David Graeber argued in *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, published in 2011, that debt has been a moral and political question for millennia, not merely an economic one. He showed how states, temples, and empires have repeatedly used debt to organise labour and obedience, and how debt crises have provoked revolts, reforms, and jubilees — the periodic cancellation of debts. For Graeber, the question is never only whether a debt can be repaid, but whether it should be, and who has the power to decide.

A second debate concerns trust and enforcement. In formal banking, credit is secured by collateral, contracts, and courts. In informal systems, it is secured by reputation, social pressure, and the threat of exclusion. Anthropologists have shown that informal enforcement can be highly effective: a borrower who defaults may lose standing, marriage prospects, or access to future help. But it can also be harsh, with debt bondage and the seizure of children or labour as penalties. The comparison raises a question that runs through the whole anthropology of credit: whether impersonal institutions make credit fairer, or merely move its coercion out of sight.

A third debate concerns the boundary between credit and gift, and between debt and obligation. When a relative lends money without interest or a deadline, is it credit or help? When a patron advances seed to a farmer, is it a loan or a claim on loyalty? Anthropologists argue that these ambiguities are not confusions to be cleared up but the point itself: credit works precisely because it can be framed in different ways, as generosity, as business, or as duty, depending on who is speaking and what is at stake.

## Contemporary Relevance

Credit systems are at the centre of contemporary economic life. Microcredit programmes promise to extend credit to the poor, yet ethnographers have documented how such loans can deepen debt and discipline borrowers, particularly women, through group pressure. Mobile money and digital lending have brought formal credit to places banks never reached, while also creating new forms of surveillance and exclusion. Household debt, student debt, and sovereign debt are now political issues in every country, and the anthropology of credit offers a distinctive perspective: that debt is never only a number on a balance sheet, but a relationship between people, with a history, a morality, and a politics. Understanding credit systems means understanding how societies organise trust across time, and what happens when that trust breaks down.
