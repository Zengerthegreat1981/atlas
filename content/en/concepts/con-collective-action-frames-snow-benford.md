# Collective Action Frames

## Definition

A concept formulated by the American sociologists David Snow and Robert Benford, borrowing the concept of the "frame" from the sociologist Erving Goffman, to analyze how social movements persuade individuals to participate by building convincing collective interpretations of reality.

## Historical Context

This concept arose in the context of the development of American social-movement sociology in the 1980s, when many researchers felt that the dominant models of the time (such as resource mobilization theory, which focuses on organizational and material factors, and the political process model, which focuses on structural political opportunities) neglected a decisive dimension of any social movement's success: how the movement persuades ordinary individuals in the first place that a given situation is a "problem" worth protesting, that change is possible, and that their personal participation is necessary and worthwhile. Snow and Benford borrowed from the interactionist sociologist Erving Goffman the concept of the "frame," which he developed in his book *Frame Analysis* (1974) to describe how individuals understand everyday social situations through implicit interpretive schemas, and applied it to the context of organized collective action.

## Key Ideas or Method

Snow and Benford argue that social movements, in order to achieve actual mobilization, need active "framing" of social reality through three interrelated interpretive tasks: "diagnostic framing," which is identifying the problem precisely and naming who is responsible for it; "prognostic framing," which is proposing a specific solution and an executable collective plan of action; and "motivational framing," which is formulating a persuasive rationale that actually motivates individuals to participate actively and not merely sympathize passively. The 1986 article by Snow, Rochford, Worden and Benford identified four "frame alignment" processes by which movements link their interpretations to those of potential participants: frame bridging, frame amplification, frame extension and frame transformation. The diagnostic/prognostic/motivational typology was elaborated in Snow and Benford's 1988 chapter on ideology and frame resonance. The two researchers hold that the success of any collective frame depends on its cultural "resonance" (shaped by a frame's credibility and by its salience to the target audience's values and everyday experiences) and on its ability to compete with and defeat counter-frames put forward by opponents or authorities to interpret the same reality in a different way that discourages mobilization.

## Impact and Critique

This concept became one of the major complementary frameworks in contemporary social-movement sociology (alongside resource mobilization and the political process model), focusing attention on the cultural-interpretive dimension of collective mobilization that other structural theories relatively neglected. Some critics objected that some applied studies using this framework tend toward a static description of the content of frames instead of analyzing the dynamic process of their formation and actual competition in real time. Benford himself published an "insider's critique" in 1997, noting among other things that the literature tended to treat frames as static things rather than as outcomes of ongoing negotiation. It was also objected that cultural "resonance" is hard to operationalize independently of judging the movement's success retrospectively (a potential circularity problem). Nevertheless this framework became one of the most widely used tools for analyzing protest discourse across diverse social movements around the world, from the American civil rights movement to contemporary environmental and social-justice movements.
