# Domestication

Domestication is the process by which humans bring other species — plants and animals — into sustained, managed relationships, transforming both the domesticated organism and the human societies that depend on it. Wheat, rice, maize, dogs, sheep, goats, and cattle are the products of thousands of years of selection, intentional and unintentional, and their domestication made agriculture, settled life, and eventually cities possible. Anthropology treats domestication not as a single event or a simple act of human mastery, but as a long, mutual entanglement: as humans reshaped plants and animals, those species reshaped human bodies, diets, social organisation, and landscapes.

## Origins and Development

Charles Darwin devoted two volumes to the subject in *The Variation of Animals and Plants under Domestication* (1868), arguing that domestication was a form of selection — humans preserving the variations they valued, generation after generation. Darwin's insight made domestication central to evolutionary theory, but it also framed the process as something humans did to other species. Later scholars complicated that picture. V. Gordon Childe, in *Man Makes Himself* (1936), placed domestication at the heart of the "Neolithic Revolution": the shift from foraging to farming that transformed human history, first in the Near East around 10,000 years ago and independently in China, Mesoamerica, the Andes, and elsewhere.

Archaeology has since replaced the idea of a single revolution with a mosaic of local processes. Melinda Zeder's research, synthesised in her 2008 article "Domestication and Early Agriculture in the Mediterranean Basin," showed that domestication was often slow, reversible, and geographically dispersed — a matter of intensifying relationships with particular species over millennia rather than a sudden invention. Genetic evidence has confirmed this picture: the origins of dogs, for example, remain debated, with some studies suggesting that dogs were domesticated from wolves in more than one place.

Tim Ingold, in his essay "From Trust to Domination" (1994), offered an anthropological reframing. Hunter-gatherers, he argued, relate to animals through trust — as persons with whom one negotiates — while pastoralists relate to them through domination, as property to be controlled. Domestication, on this view, is not only a biological process but a transformation of the human-animal relationship itself, from reciprocity to ownership. Jared Diamond's *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (1997) gave the subject a global, popular frame, arguing that the uneven distribution of domesticable species helps explain why some societies developed agriculture, states, and empires while others did not — a thesis that remains influential and contested.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The first debate concerns agency: who domesticated whom? Recent work stresses that domestication was often mutual. Dogs may have domesticated themselves, in part, by hanging around human camps and being selected for tameness; wheat and barley spread because they thrived in human-disturbed soils. Some scholars speak of "self-domestication" and of humans as a domesticated species too — shaped by the demands of living with animals and plants, from lactose tolerance to the microbes of the gut.

A second dimension is the boundary between domestication and other forms of management. Many societies manage wild species without fully domesticating them — through burning, transplanting, or selective harvesting — and the line between foraging and farming is often blurred. Anthropologists therefore speak of a spectrum of human-plant and human-animal relationships rather than a sharp divide between "wild" and "domestic."

A third dimension is the cost. Domestication brought food surpluses, but also new diseases, harder labour, worse diets, and greater inequality. The archaeological record shows that early farmers were often shorter and less healthy than their foraging neighbours. Anthropology insists on holding both sides of this ledger in view: domestication made civilisation possible, but it was not a simple story of progress.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Domestication is a live issue today. Industrial agriculture, genetic modification, and the loss of crop diversity have made the history of domestication a matter of urgent practical concern: the wild relatives of wheat, rice, and maize carry genes that may be needed to breed crops for a warming climate. Conservationists and Indigenous communities argue that traditional varieties and breeds — developed over centuries of careful selection — are a form of biocultural heritage that deserves protection.

Critics of the classic narrative warn against treating domestication as a ladder from wild to civilised, a framing that has long been used to rank societies. Contemporary anthropology instead emphasises the diversity of human relationships with other species — including relationships of care, kinship, and respect that do not fit the model of control. The study of domestication thus opens onto larger questions: what kinds of relationships with the non-human world are possible, and what kind of world do we want to make?
