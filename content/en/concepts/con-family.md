# Family

The family is one of anthropology's oldest and most contested concepts: a social group bound by ties of kinship, marriage, descent, or shared residence, within which children are raised, resources are pooled, and care is organised. Yet no single definition fits every society. The nuclear family of two parents and their children, long treated in Euro-American thought as the natural unit, is only one of many forms. Extended families spanning several generations, joint families sharing a single household economy, single-parent households, and families formed through adoption, fostering, or same-sex partnership all perform the work that anthropologists associate with family life. What unites them is less a fixed structure than a set of relationships recognised as intimate, enduring, and morally binding.

## Origins and Development

Nineteenth-century evolutionists made the family a central problem of the new science of anthropology. Lewis Henry Morgan argued in *Ancient Society* (1877) that human kinship had passed through successive stages, from promiscuous hordes through group marriage to the monogamous family, tying family form to the development of private property. His scheme was speculative and soon challenged, but it fixed the family as a key to social evolution. Bronislaw Malinowski countered with intensive fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands, arguing in *Sex and Repression in Savage Society* (1927) that the family was a universal institution rooted in the biological needs of child-rearing, though its form varied. George Peter Murdock's cross-cultural survey *Social Structure* (1949) then claimed the nuclear family was universal, a conclusion that shaped a generation of teaching even as later scholars showed his sample and definitions smuggled in Western assumptions.

Claude Levi-Strauss reframed the debate by treating the family not as a natural unit but as an alliance system: marriage creates bonds between groups, and the prohibition of incest is the rule that forces those bonds outward. In his essay "The Family" (1956), he insisted that the family is a social institution rather than a biological given, held together by rules of residence, descent, and exchange. Jack Goody's historical work, notably *The Development of the Family and Marriage in Europe* (1983), showed how inheritance, dowry, and the Church's marriage rules reshaped European households over centuries, demonstrating that family forms respond to property, law, and religion as much as to biology.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

The most durable debate concerns whether "the family" names a universal institution or a Western folk category imposed on other societies. David Schneider's *A Critique of the Study of Kinship* (1984) argued that anthropologists had projected Euro-American ideas of blood and love onto societies whose own categories worked differently, and that kinship itself might be an artefact of the discipline. Jane Collier, Michelle Rosaldo, and Sylvia Yanagisako pressed the point in "Is There a Family?" (1997), showing that the very idea of the family as a private, loving refuge set apart from the economy and the state is a historically specific ideology, not a cross-cultural fact. Feminist anthropology added that the family is also a site of inequality: the unpaid domestic labour of women, the control of sexuality, and the transmission of property have all been organised through family roles.

A second axis of debate concerns function versus meaning. Functionalist accounts stress what families do: reproduce the labour force, socialise children, provide welfare, and care for the old. Interpretive accounts ask what families mean to their members: belonging, obligation, honour, and identity. Ethnography shows these are inseparable. Among the Tallensi of Ghana, for example, family membership carries ancestral obligations that structure farming, ritual, and dispute settlement, so that the household is simultaneously an economic unit and a moral community.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Today the family is studied amid rapid change. Migration stretches families across continents, with remittances and video calls sustaining ties that once depended on co-residence; anthropologists call these transnational families. Assisted reproduction, surrogacy, and adoption by same-sex couples have multiplied the routes to parenthood, challenging definitions built on blood and heterosexual marriage. Meanwhile, the state's role has grown: welfare systems, family law, and child-protection regimes now regulate intimate life in ways that earlier generations of anthropologists rarely examined. The COVID-19 pandemic made visible how much care work families still absorb, and how unevenly it is shared.

The concept endures because it names something real in every society: the small, dense web of relationships in which people are fed, raised, and remembered. Its analytical value now lies precisely in its instability. Rather than assuming what a family is, anthropologists ask how people in a given place make and mark kin, what obligations those bonds carry, and how they change when economies, laws, and beliefs shift. The family remains, in that sense, both a universal human experience and a category that must be rebuilt in every ethnographic encounter.
