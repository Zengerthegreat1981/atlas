# The Hard Problem of Consciousness and Qualia

"The Hard Problem of Consciousness" is a name coined by the Australian philosopher David Chalmers in his article "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness", published in the *Journal of Consciousness Studies* in 1995, and then elaborated in his book *The Conscious Mind* (1996). The problem is a question about why the physical processing of information in the brain is accompanied by "subjective experience": why is there "something it is like" to be this being, a phrase taken from Thomas Nagel?

## The Easy Problems and the Hard Problem

Chalmers distinguishes between the "easy problems", which are the explanation of capacities and functions: discrimination of stimuli, informational integration, verbal report of internal states, focusing of attention, and control of behavior. They are "easy" in the sense that they are solved by the ordinary scientific method: identifying the neural or computational mechanism that performs the function. The "hard problem" remains after all of them are solved: why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience? A functional explanation does not appear to address this question, because any description of a function remains logically compatible with the absence of experience.

## Supporting Arguments

Chalmers raises arguments, the most famous being the "zombie argument": one can conceive of a being that matches a human physically and behaviorally in every respect without having experience; if this conception is logically possible, then consciousness is not entailed by physics. Among them is the "explanatory gap" argument formulated by Joseph Levine (1983), and Frank Jackson's "Mary" argument in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982): a neuroscientist who knows all the physical facts about colors while seeing in black and white, then leaves and sees red and learns something new. Before that Thomas Nagel, in his article "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), showed that the first-person perspective of experience is not captured by objective description.

## Responses

Responses diverge in several directions: (1) reductionism and functionalism, which hold that the problem will be solved by the progress of science (Patricia Churchland); (2) "illusionism" in Dennett and then Keith Frankish (2016), which holds that the belief in a mysterious subjective experience is an illusion to be explained; (3) biological naturalism in John Searle (*The Rediscovery of the Mind*, 1992): consciousness is a real biological phenomenon produced by the brain just as the stomach produces digestion; (4) property dualism in Chalmers himself, which makes consciousness a fundamental principle in the world; (5) panpsychism in Galen Strawson and Philip Goff; (6) the "integrated information theory" of Tononi (2004) and the "global workspace" of Bernard Baars. Colin McGinn held that the problem may exceed the capacity of our minds (new mysterianism).

## Connection to the Islamic Heritage

Researchers point to intersections with questions in Arabic philosophy: the "floating man" of Ibn Sina in *al-Shifa': al-Nafs* (the first treatise), where he imagines a human being created all at once suspended in the void who does not sense his body yet perceives his self, as evidence that self-awareness precedes sensation. And al-Suhrawardi presents "knowledge by presence" in *Hikmat al-Ishraq* (1186), a direct knowledge of the soul of itself, not through a form. These are comparisons in meaning and do not imply that these thinkers posed Chalmers's problem in its formulation.

## Limits

Disagreement remains over the source of the gap intuition: is it evidence of a metaphysical reality or a shortcoming in our conceptions? It is noted that testing for consciousness in animals and machines depends on one's position on the problem.
