# Exchange

Exchange is the transfer of things, services, or obligations between people, and it is one of the central concepts of anthropology. Every society has ways of moving goods from hand to hand — through gifts, trade, tribute, wages, or theft — and each of these ways carries its own rules, meanings, and social consequences. To exchange something is never simply to move an object: it is to create, confirm, or break a relationship. The study of exchange is therefore the study of how societies are made and held together, and of how people use things to act upon one another.

## Definition

Anthropologists distinguish three broad forms of exchange. Reciprocity is exchange between parties of roughly equal standing, in which a gift or service calls for a return: it may be immediate, as in a swap, or delayed for months or years, as when a wedding gift is repaid at the next wedding. Redistribution is exchange through a centre: people give goods to a chief, a temple, or a state, which then gives them back in feasts, rations, or public works. Market exchange is exchange mediated by prices, in which goods are bought and sold for money. Most societies combine all three, and the same object may pass through each form in turn.

Marcel Mauss, in *The Gift* (1925), founded the modern anthropology of exchange with a simple observation: in many societies, gifts are never free. A gift creates an obligation to receive it and an obligation to return it, and this triple obligation — to give, to receive, to repay — binds people together in a continuous cycle of debt and generosity. To refuse a gift is to refuse a relationship; to fail to repay is to lose standing. Exchange, in this view, is the social contract in its most concrete form.

## Origins and Development

The comparative study of exchange grew out of ethnography in the early twentieth century. Bronislaw Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) described the kula, a ceremonial exchange of shell valuables that linked the islands off Papua New Guinea in a great ring of partnerships, voyages, and obligations. Alongside the kula ran ordinary trade in food, pots, and tools, and Malinowski showed that the two were inseparable: ceremonial exchange made trade possible, and trade made ceremonial exchange worthwhile.

Claude Levi-Strauss, in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949), extended the logic of exchange to marriage itself, arguing that the exchange of women between groups — through rules of exogamy and alliance — was the foundation of social life, and that the prohibition of incest was at bottom a rule about exchange. Marshall Sahlins, in *Stone Age Economics* (1972), systematised the field, arguing that the form of reciprocity — generous, balanced, or exploitative — depends on the social distance between the parties, from the open sharing of the household to the hard bargaining of trade with strangers.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One major debate concerns the distinction between gifts and commodities. Chris Gregory, in *Gifts and Commodities* (1982), argued that in a gift economy what circulates is a part of the giver — a thing that carries the giver's identity and creates a lasting bond — while in a commodity economy what circulates is alienable, stripped of personal history, and valued by price. Critics replied that the distinction is never absolute: commodities can be personalised, gifts can be calculated, and people move between the two logics constantly, as when a family heirloom is sold or a purchased present is given with love.

A second debate concerns the role of calculation. Is exchange driven by self-interest, as economists assume, or by the desire for relationship and prestige, as Mauss argued? Ethnography suggests both: people haggle hard and give generously, sometimes in the same transaction, and the same exchange can be experienced as generosity by one party and as a debt by the other. The ambiguity is part of the point — exchange works precisely because its meanings are never fully fixed.

A third dimension is power. Exchange can create equality, as when partners trade as equals, or hierarchy, as when a big man distributes wealth to win followers, or a state extracts tribute from its subjects. The potlatch of the Pacific Northwest, in which chiefs gave away or destroyed vast wealth to shame their rivals, showed that exchange could be a form of competition as fierce as war. Exchange is thus not only about solidarity; it is also about domination.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Exchange remains central to contemporary life, from the gift economies of open-source software and blood donation to the global trade in goods, labour, and data. The rise of the sharing economy, crowdfunding, and digital platforms has created new forms of reciprocity, while also raising old questions: who gives, who receives, and who profits from the exchange? Anthropologists have shown that even the most impersonal markets depend on trust, reputation, and personal relationships, and that gift-giving persists at the heart of capitalist societies — in Christmas presents, in wedding lists, and in the unpaid labour of care.

The critique of exchange theory has also been productive. Feminist anthropologists have shown that classic accounts of exchange often ignored women's labour and women's gifts, and that the celebrated exchange of women in alliance theory was written from the perspective of men. The study of exchange continues to evolve, but its central insight endures: to understand a society, look at what its people give, to whom, and what they expect in return.
