# Say's Law of Markets

**Say's law of markets** is an economic principle formulated by **Jean-Baptiste Say** in his book *Traité d'économie politique* (1803). It is usually summarized in the phrase "supply creates its own demand", a shortened formulation that Say himself did not use in this form; the name "Say's law" was popularized later, notably by Keynes.

## The basic logic

The principle rests on a simple observation: when an individual produces a good and sells it on the market, he receives monetary income in return, and this income is usually not hoarded but used in turn to buy other goods produced by others. In this sense, **the act of production itself** is what generates the purchasing power needed to absorb that output in the market. Aggregate supply and aggregate demand therefore tend toward balance across the economy as a whole, even if temporary partial imbalances occur in particular sectors.

## The political conclusion: the impossibility of a persistent "general glut"

Say and the classical economists who followed him (including David Ricardo) drew a far-reaching political conclusion: a persistent **general glut** of production cannot occur across the economy as a whole, because any increase in aggregate supply necessarily generates an equivalent increase in aggregate demand through the incomes that producers receive. This means that economic crises, if they occur, cannot arise from a general shortage of demand, but only from partial sectoral imbalances that the market corrects automatically over time, without the need for government intervention to stimulate aggregate demand.

## The debate with Malthus

This law faced an early objection from **Thomas Robert Malthus**, who warned of the possibility of an actual "general glut" if the owners of high incomes saved a large part of their income without spending it immediately on consumption or investment. This could create a real shortfall in aggregate demand. Say rejected this possibility, insisting that saving itself is necessarily transformed into investment through capital markets, and so does not actually disappear from the economic cycle.

## The Keynesian challenge

John Stuart Mill restated the doctrine in *Principles of Political Economy* (1848): commodities are paid for with commodities, so a general excess supply of all goods at once is not the ordinary case. Mill still allowed that a temporary rise in the demand for money can coincide with unsold goods and commercial distress.

**John Maynard Keynes**, in *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), treated the rejection of Say's law, in the sense that supply creates its own demand, as a starting point of his theory of employment. He argued that decisions to save and decisions to invest are made by different people, and that they need not match at a level of output that fully employs the labour force. The economy can then settle where aggregate demand is short of what full employment would require. The phrase "Say's law" itself became standard largely through Keynes's usage.
