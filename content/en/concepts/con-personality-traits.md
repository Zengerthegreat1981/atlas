# Personality Traits and the Big Five

Personality traits are relatively enduring patterns of thinking, feeling and behaving that differ between people and show some consistency across situations and time. Trait theory is the most widely used approach to describing personality in research psychology. Rather than sorting people into types, it places them along continuous dimensions such as sociability or conscientiousness, with most people near the middle.

## The trait approach

Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert noted in 1936 that the English language contains thousands of words describing personal characteristics. Allport argued that traits are real dispositions that guide behaviour. Raymond Cattell reduced such descriptors with factor analysis, producing a model with sixteen source traits. Hans Eysenck, working from a different tradition, proposed a much smaller set of superfactors, including extraversion and neuroticism, and linked them to biological differences.

## The Five-Factor Model

From the 1960s onward, analyses of trait vocabulary and of questionnaires converged on five broad dimensions. The lexical tradition, associated with Lewis Goldberg and others, relies on the idea that important individual differences become encoded in language. The questionnaire tradition, developed by Paul Costa and Robert McCrae, produced the NEO inventories. The five factors are commonly remembered with the acronym OCEAN:

- **Openness to experience**: curiosity, imagination, preference for variety.
- **Conscientiousness**: organisation, self-discipline, dependability.
- **Extraversion**: sociability, assertiveness, positive emotionality.
- **Agreeableness**: cooperativeness, trust, compassion.
- **Neuroticism**: tendency toward negative emotions such as anxiety and low mood; the opposite pole is often called emotional stability.

Each factor is a broad domain composed of narrower facets. The labels are descriptive, not evaluative, and each dimension has both advantages and costs contingent on context.

## Stability and change

Personality traits are fairly stable, especially in adulthood, in the sense that rank order among people changes little over years. Stability is not absolute. Longitudinal studies show mean-level change across the lifespan: on average, people tend to become somewhat more conscientious, agreeable and emotionally stable from young adulthood into middle age, a pattern sometimes called the maturity principle. Major life events and deliberate effort can also shift traits modestly.

## Origins and uses

Twin and adoption studies suggest that genetic differences account for a substantial portion of trait variation, and that most of the remainder comes from non-shared environment, meaning experiences that differ even between siblings, rather than from shared family environment. These estimates describe populations and not individuals. Trait scores have predictive value: conscientiousness is associated with job performance and health-related behaviour, and neuroticism with vulnerability to emotional disorders. Effects are usually modest in size but can be consistent across studies.

## Criticisms and debates

- **The person-situation debate.** Walter Mischel argued in 1968 that behaviour varies across situations much more than trait theory implies, and that correlations between traits and specific behaviours are low. The debate led to the recognition that traits predict aggregated behaviour better than single acts, and that both person and situation matter, often interacting.
- **Description versus explanation.** Critics note that traits describe regularities without explaining them. Saying someone helps because they are agreeable is circular unless underlying processes are specified.
- **Number of factors.** Some researchers favour six dimensions, adding honesty-humility, as in the HEXACO model. Others argue that higher-order factors exist, or that five is not a natural boundary.
- **Self-report limits.** Most trait research relies on questionnaires, which are vulnerable to social desirability, limited self-insight and response styles. Ratings by acquaintances add useful information.
- **Cross-cultural generality.** The structure has been replicated in many countries, but the fit is weaker in some samples, and most research has drawn on Western, educated populations.
- **Popular tests.** Type-based tools, such as the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator, categorise people into types, and psychometric reviews generally find them less reliable and valid than trait measures.
- **Neglect of context and meaning.** Critics, including narrative and cultural psychologists, argue that trait scores omit goals, values and life stories, which also shape who a person is.

## Why it matters

The Big Five provides a common vocabulary that allows research on personality, health, work and relationships to be compared across studies. It is best used as a descriptive framework with real but limited predictive power.
