# Ethnopsychiatry

## Definition

Ethnopsychiatry is a branch that studies mental disorder in societies as a cultural and ethnic category. It divides into two opposing orientations: a colonial one that produced "knowledge" about the mentality of colonized peoples, and a critical, humanistic one (Devereux, Nathan, and Fanon) that sought to understand disorder from within local systems of meaning. It predates contemporary cultural psychiatry and overlaps with it (see the entry on the Ethnopsychiatry and Culture-Bound Syndromes school).

## The Colonial Dimension

Kraepelin treated his 1904 visit to Java as an occasion for what he called comparative psychiatry. Antoine Porot, later associated with the "Algiers school," published "Notes de psychiatrie musulmane" in the *Annales médico-psychologiques* in 1918. That article described the Muslim population of North Africa as primitive, highly suggestible, and governed by rudimentary instincts, a racialized psychology that later writing from the same school extended. Carothers, in his study for the World Health Organization, *The African Mind in Health and Disease* (1953), did something similar in Africa, comparing the African to a European who had undergone frontal lobe ablation. Richard Keller, in *Colonial Madness: Psychiatry in French North Africa* (University of Chicago Press, 2007), detailed how this psychiatry served the colonial administration and provided a scientific justification for discrimination.

## The Fanonian Critique

Frantz Fanon, who worked as a physician at the Blida-Joinville hospital in Algeria between 1953 and 1956, offered a systematic critique. In *The Wretched of the Earth* (Maspero, 1961; English translation Grove Press, 1963), in the chapter "Colonial War and Mental Disorders," he described how war, torture, and colonial violence produce psychological disorders. With Jacques Azoulay he wrote the article "Social Therapy in a Ward of Muslim Men: Methodological Difficulties" (*L'Information psychiatrique*, 1954), in which he described the difficulties of adapting institutional social therapy to the patients' cultural environment. In this way he turned ethnopsychiatry from a classification of races into a critique of the social conditions of illness (see the entries on Fanon and the Fanonian sociology of liberation).

## Contemporary Ethnopsychiatry

Georges Devereux (1908–1985) developed, in *Essais d'ethnopsychiatrie générale* (Gallimard, 1970), an approach combining psychoanalysis and anthropology (the complementarism he set out further in *Ethnopsychanalyse complémentariste*, 1972). He held that each culture offers its members characteristic patterns of defense and "typical" or ethnic disorders, while the underlying psychic functioning is universal and expressed in culturally shaped forms. Tobie Nathan, who worked with Devereux's legacy, developed a clinic for migrant families in France and explains his approach in *L'influence qui guérit* (Odile Jacob, 1994). It takes seriously the representations of spirits, ancestors, and traditional healing from the patient's culture of origin within the therapeutic setting. Nathan has been criticized for adopting "cultural" classifications that may freeze the patient in an original identity.

## What the Concept Means for the Arab World

The concept points to a paradox: the medieval Arab-Islamic heritage (the hospitals, or bimaristans, and medical writing on melancholia) built an early medical understanding of madness, which later became the object of colonial studies. One task of modern Arab psychiatry is to recover this heritage without idealizing it, and to study the local expressions of distress with a critical method (see the entry on mental illness in non-Western contexts).

## Limits

There is a persistent risk that cultural differences will be turned into racial determinism, and that "the Other" will become a permanent object of examination. The most valuable work remains that which couples cultural description with an analysis of power.
