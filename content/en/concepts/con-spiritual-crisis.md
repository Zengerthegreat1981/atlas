# Spiritual Crisis

A spiritual crisis is the collapse of meaning-making structures: religious faith, ideological commitments or existential narratives that once organized and justified a life. It is often described as a philosophical confrontation with meaninglessness rather than a purely medical condition, though it can overlap with depression and other clinical states. For existential thinkers it is both a painful ordeal and a possible turning point.

## Definition

The term covers experiences in which a person's basic framework for understanding why life matters gives way. The trigger may be a loss, an illness, a betrayal by a community or leader, a loss of faith, or a gradual erosion. It differs from clinical depression in focus: its center is questions of meaning, truth and value, though in practice the two can occur together and cannot always be separated by the person experiencing them. In diagnostic classification, DSM-IV (1994) introduced, under Other Conditions That May Be a Focus of Clinical Attention, the category "Religious or Spiritual Problem" (code V62.89). DSM-5-TR (2022) retained it (code Z65.8), and an APA update of September 2025 renamed it "Moral, Religious, or Spiritual Problem". The category recognizes that such struggles can be a focus of clinical attention without being mental disorders in themselves.

## Origins

The theme is old and appears across traditions. The Christian mystic John of the Cross wrote of the "dark night of the soul," a period of felt abandonment seen as part of spiritual maturation. Kierkegaard described despair as the failure to become a self before God. Paul Tillich's *The Courage to Be* (1952) analyzed the anxiety of meaninglessness and doubt in the modern age. In psychology, Carl Jung's *Modern Man in Search of a Soul* (1933) argued that many patients in the second half of life suffered from loss of a meaningful outlook. James Fowler's *Stages of Faith* (1981) described how faith develops and how transitions between stages can involve crisis. Stanislav and Christina Grof, in *Spiritual Emergency* (1989), proposed that some intense crises are better understood as transformation than as illness, a view that remains contested.

## The Function of Crisis

A spiritual crisis is not purely pathological. It may represent a necessary dissolution of immature or inauthentic meaning-systems in service of building sturdier ones. Someone whose faith collapses may be in real pain, yet the dissolution may make more honest engagement with meaning possible. Frankl's logotherapy treats the search for meaning as a basic human concern and the frustration of it as a source of distress, and Yalom regards meaninglessness as one of the ultimate concerns every person must face.

## Therapeutic Stance

The existential therapist does not try to restore the abandoned meaning-system, nor to persuade the person to abandon or keep their beliefs. The aim is to help them stay with the void, grieve the lost certainty, and discover what meaning can be found or created. This may involve gradual reconstruction of values and coherence. Kenneth Pargament's research on religious coping distinguishes spiritual struggles from healthy religious coping, and suggests that such struggles are common and can be worked through.

## Criticisms and Limits

Critics caution against romanticizing crisis. Severe distress, hopelessness, thoughts of self-harm, or confusion and perceptual disturbances need professional assessment and care, and should not be assumed to be "spiritual growth." Distinguishing spiritual crisis from depression or psychosis is a clinical task that cannot be settled by the person's or the therapist's philosophical preference. The Grofs' concept of spiritual emergency in particular has been criticized for lacking strong empirical support and for the risk of delaying treatment. The existential reading is also culturally situated: in communities where faith and identity are inseparable, loss of faith may also be a loss of belonging, which therapy has to take seriously.
