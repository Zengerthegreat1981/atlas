# Freedom & Choice

The question of freedom and choice in ethics raises a central issue: does a human being have a real capacity to act otherwise than they did, at the same moment and under the same circumstances, or is every choice predetermined by a chain of prior causes (natural, psychological, social) that renders freedom an epistemic illusion? The answer is closely tied to whether moral responsibility is possible, since there is no sense in praising or blaming an act that the agent was not free to choose otherwise.

Muslim theologians conducted this debate with remarkable depth. The determinists (*Jabriyya*, such as Jahm ibn Safwan) denied that the servant has any genuine act; everything occurs solely by God's decree and predestination. The Mu'tazila opposed them, holding that the servant creates their voluntary acts through a capacity God grants them, since otherwise obligation, reward, and punishment would be void. The Ash'ari school took a middle position through the theory of "acquisition" (*kasb*): God is the creator of the act, both as action and as capacity, while the servant is the "acquirer" of it through their concurrent will, without being its creator. This was an attempt to reconcile absolute divine unity with bearing responsibility. Ibn Rushd, in works such as *al-Kashf 'an Manahij al-Adilla*, was critical of the theologians' competing formulas, holding that human actions occur through causes within a divinely ordered causal system while humans retain some capacity for choice.

In Western philosophy, the problem took shape under the names "compatibilism" and "incompatibilism." Compatibilists, from Hume through Harry Frankfurt to Daniel Dennett, hold that freedom does not mean the absence of causality but the absence of external coercion: an agent is free if they act in accordance with their own desires without compulsion, even if those desires are themselves the product of a causal chain. Incompatibilists, such as Peter van Inwagen, hold that real freedom requires the genuine possibility of acting otherwise, which is not wholly determined, and that if determinism is true it cancels moral responsibility. Robert Kane developed a "libertarian" theory that links freedom to decisive moments of choice (Self-Forming Actions) that are not governed by deterministic causes.

The most prominent objection to compatibilism is the "consequence argument" formulated by van Inwagen. Its claim is that if our actions are the necessary result of the laws of nature and of events prior to our existence, then we have no control over them in any real sense. Compatibilists reply that the concept of "control" itself must be redefined to fit the fact that we are natural beings.

*Example:* A person who resists the temptation of a bribe despite severe economic pressure displays the exercise of moral freedom, regardless of the metaphysical debate about whether their motives are determined.

The issue remains strongly present in contemporary criminal law, where the notions of punishment and reduced sentences for coercion or psychological disorder are built on the implicit assumption that there are degrees of freedom, rather than absolute freedom or a complete absence of it. The discussion also intersects with modern neuroscience. Benjamin Libet's experiments in the 1980s suggested that a measurable brain signal (the readiness potential) begins preparing an action before the agent becomes conscious of their decision, which provoked wide debate about whether the "free will" is merely a subsequent feeling that accompanies a decision already taken at an unconscious level. This reopens the ancient question with new scientific tools, although many philosophers doubt that such experiments, with their limited scope, can settle a philosophical question of this depth.
