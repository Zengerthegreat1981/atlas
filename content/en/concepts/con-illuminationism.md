# Illuminationism (Hikmat al-Ishraq)

## Definition

**Illuminationism** (*hikmat al-ishraq*, the Philosophy of Illumination) is the philosophical system set out by Shihab al-Din Yahya al-Suhrawardi (549–587 AH / 1154–1191 CE), called *Shaykh al-Ishraq* and, after his death, *al-Shaykh al-Maqtul*. Its principal statement is *Hikmat al-Ishraq*, which he completed in 582 AH / 1186 CE. Light, rather than the abstract notion of being used by the Peripatetics, is the basic category of both reality and knowledge. From the Light of Lights (*nur al-anwar*), identified with God, reality descends by degrees of intensity to bodies that are dark or only partly lit.

## Origins

Al-Suhrawardi had studied the logic and metaphysics of Ibn Sina and did not discard argument. He took up Ibn Sina's unfinished reference to an "Eastern philosophy" (*al-hikma al-mashriqiyya*) and turned the phrase toward a philosophy of illumination. He also claimed a lineage of ancient sages running through Plato and the Persian kings and priests of light. Specialists treat that lineage as a constructed genealogy, not as a documented transmission of Zoroastrian scriptures. Shorter symbolic tales, among them the Persian *Aql-i Surkh* (The Red Intellect) and the Arabic *Qissat al-Ghurba al-Gharbiyya* (The Tale of the Occidental Exile), carry the same cosmology in narrative form.

## The Book *Hikmat al-Ishraq*

The book has a preface and two parts. The preface ranks knowers and distinguishes discursive philosophy (*hikma bahthiyya*) from philosophy by immediate tasting (*hikma dhawqiyya*). The top rank on his scale is the sage who joins both, *al-hakim al-muta'allih*.

The first part revises logic. A simple light, he argues, is not captured by an Aristotelian definition through genus and differentia, and the Peripatetic categories are not a map of extramental reality. "Existence," said of many things, is a mental consideration (*i'tibar dhihni*). What is real outside the mind, on his account, are concrete lights and dark barriers, differentiated by intensity.

The second part sets out the metaphysics of light, which he presents as grounded in tasting as well as in discourse. Incorporeal lights stand in two orders. A longitudinal order (*tabaqat tuliyya*) of dominating lights descends from the Light of Lights. A latitudinal order (*tabaqat 'ardiyya*) comprises coordinating lights, the "lords of the species" (*arbab al-anwa'*), which he relates to Platonic forms. Managing lights (*anwar mudabbira*) govern particular bodies. Bodies are barriers (*barazikh*): dark, if they hold no light of their own, or dusky, if they receive it. Between intellect and body he places the suspended images (*al-suwar al-mu'allaqa*), an imaginal realm that is neither a material world nor a mere fiction in the mind, and that he uses to account for veridical vision.

Knowledge, in this scheme, is not only a form impressed on the mind (*'ilm husuli*). An incorporeal light is present to itself. That self-presence is presential knowledge (*al-'ilm al-huduri*), and it is his model for the direct witnessing (*mushahada*) that argument alone does not produce.

## Later Reception

Shams al-Din al-Shahrazuri and Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi wrote the commentaries that kept the book in use. In Safavid Iran, Mulla Sadra drew on Illuminationist accounts of presence and of gradation, and rejected the priority of quiddity in favor of the primacy of existence (*asalat al-wujud*). His "transcendent philosophy" (*al-hikma al-muta'aliya*) joins Peripatetic argument, Illuminationist metaphysics, and gnosis (*'irfan*). It is not an unchanged continuation of al-Suhrawardi. The texts are still read in Iranian seminaries.

## His Death and the Limits of Transmission

Al-Suhrawardi was put to death in Aleppo in 587/1191, under al-Malik al-Zahir Ghazi, Saladin's son. Chroniclers report a charge of corrupting the faith and an order from Saladin. They do not agree on one legal formula. The later tradition lived mainly in Persian philosophical writing, and less in Arabic Sunni curricula than the Peripatetic books did.
