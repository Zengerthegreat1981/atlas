# Courage Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Courage is a practical virtue consisting in the capacity to confront danger and fear in pursuit of a right end. It is a mean between two vices: recklessness, in which a person advances without regard for the danger, and cowardice, in which a person shrinks from a required action.

## Islamic Roots

In Islamic ethics, courage was counted among the four cardinal virtues of Muslim philosophers (wisdom, chastity, courage and justice). Ibn Miskawayh elaborated on it in *The Refinement of Character* as the moderation of the irascible power of the soul: its excess is recklessness and its deficiency is cowardice. The Quran linked courage to steadfastness against falsehood rather than to combat alone, as in the story of the People of the Cave, who "stood up and said: Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and the earth." Their courage was therefore a courage of doctrinal stance before it was a martial courage. Islamic moral literature also distinguished physical courage (confronting bodily danger) from moral courage (speaking the truth before an unjust ruler), often treating the latter as the higher degree, citing the Prophetic tradition that the best jihad is a word of truth before an unjust ruler (reported by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi).

## The Western Context

In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle defined courage as the virtue concerned with fear and confidence, and as a mean between recklessness and cowardice, emphasizing that it does not mean the absence of fear but its regulation by reason. In the *Republic*, Plato (429c-430b) described courage as the preservation, even under the pressure of pleasure and pain, of a right conviction about what is to be feared and what is not, rather than mere momentary boldness.

## Example

A soldier who withdraws tactically in order to preserve his forces may show more courage, in the Aristotelian sense, than one who charges without a plan and destroys himself and his men, because courage is guided by reason rather than blind impulse.

## Kinds of Courage

Muslim moralists commonly distinguished between martial, social and intellectual forms of courage. A scholar who openly states an opinion that contradicts the prevailing view, if it is true, or a poor man who endures hardship without asking people for help, exhibits a form of courage no less valuable than facing the enemy on the battlefield. Muslim philosophers also linked courage to justice, since boldness that serves falsehood has no value. Courage is therefore a virtue conditioned on the rightness of its end, not mere daring in itself. The Quran linked steadfastness in battle to patience rather than emotional agitation, saying "O you who believe, when you meet a force, stand firm" (8:45); the verse is often cited to show that courage in battle is tied to steadfastness and remembrance of God rather than rashness.

## Courage in the Face of Strife

The conduct of some Companions during the early civil strife (*fitna*) suggests that genuine courage may sometimes mean wise silence as much as open declaration. Several prominent Companions, such as Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas and 'Abd Allah ibn 'Umar, are reported to have held back from the fighting that followed the death of 'Uthman, out of reluctance to shed Muslim blood. Scholars regarded this as a form of wise courage rather than cowardice, distinguishing between silence born of cowardice and silence born of wisdom that weighs the public interest.
