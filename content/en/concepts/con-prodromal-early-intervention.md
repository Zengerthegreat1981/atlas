# Prodromal States & Early Intervention

## Definition

The concept refers to the **prodromal period** that precedes the first explicit psychotic episode (as in schizophrenia), a phase in which mild or subthreshold symptoms appear (such as odd ideas, social withdrawal, and minor perceptual disturbances) without rising to the level of full psychosis. It also refers to the set of clinical practices aimed at detecting this period and intervening early to prevent the disorder from developing or to reduce its severity.

## Theoretical Framework

Systematic focus on the prodromal phase developed in Melbourne, Australia, in the early 1990s. Patrick McGorry founded the Early Psychosis Prevention and Intervention Centre (EPPIC) in 1992, and McGorry and Alison Yung established the PACE clinic (1994) and formulated the "at-risk mental state" or "ultra-high risk" (UHR) concept. Thomas McGlashan and colleagues at Yale developed a parallel research program in the United States and assessment instruments, most notably the **Structured Interview for Prodromal Syndromes (SIPS)** and the **Scale of Prodromal Symptoms (SOPS)**. McGlashan also took part in the Scandinavian TIPS (Early Treatment and Intervention in Psychosis) study, in which an early-detection program in Norway and Denmark reduced the **duration of untreated psychosis (DUP)** and was associated with less severe symptoms at presentation.

The theoretical framework rests on the hypothesis that schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders develop through gradual clinical stages (the clinical "staging model" associated with McGorry), and that intervening in the prodromal phase, before full psychosis crystallizes, may limit the long-term functional decline associated with recurrent psychotic episodes.

## Applications

- Specialized clinics for assessing "clinical high risk" (CHR) among at-risk adolescents and young adults, using standardized instruments (SIPS/SOPS, or the Australian CAARMS).
- Staged psychosocial and pharmacological intervention protocols, usually beginning with cognitive behavioral therapy and intensive monitoring before resorting to antipsychotics.
- Community awareness campaigns and the shortening of the duration of untreated psychosis as a preventive strategy at the public health level.

## Challenges

The greatest challenge for this model is that most people who meet "high risk" criteria do not develop full psychosis within a few years: a meta-analysis by Fusar-Poli and colleagues (2012) estimated transition rates of roughly 20-35% over one to four years, and rates appear to have declined in more recent cohorts. DSM-5 lists "attenuated psychosis syndrome" as a condition for further study rather than an official diagnosis. This raises questions about the accuracy of prediction and the risks of stigmatizing individuals or treating them with medication without a clear need. This debate remains open in the clinical literature. This entry is educational and does not constitute clinical advice.
