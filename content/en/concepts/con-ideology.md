# Ideology

## Definition

Ideology is a system of ideas, beliefs, and values that explains the social world and, in doing so, legitimates or challenges existing arrangements of power. The term carries two broad senses that are often in tension. In a neutral sense, ideology means any coherent worldview, whether liberalism, socialism, feminism, or nationalism. In a critical sense, it means ideas that serve the interests of a dominant group by presenting a particular social order as natural, inevitable, or just, thereby concealing the conflicts and inequalities on which it rests. Much of the concept's power comes from this ambiguity: calling something an ideology can be a neutral description or an accusation that the ideas in question are distorted by power. Sociology uses the concept to ask how beliefs become widely shared, whose interests they serve, and how they are produced and sustained through institutions such as schools, media, and religion.

## Origins and Development

The word was coined during the French Revolution by Antoine Destutt de Tracy, who meant by it a science of ideas. It acquired its critical edge with Marx and Engels, who in *The German Ideology* argued that the ruling ideas of any age are the ideas of the ruling class, and that ideology inverts reality, presenting the interests of a particular class as the common interest of all. Antonio Gramsci developed this insight through the concept of hegemony: dominant groups rule not only by coercion but by securing the consent of the governed, winning acceptance for their view of the world as common sense. Karl Mannheim, in *Ideology and Utopia*, broadened the concept by arguing that all thought, including the thought of critics, is socially situated, and he distinguished ideology, which defends the existing order, from utopia, which imagines its transcendence. Louis Althusser reformulated the concept in structuralist terms, arguing that ideology is not a set of false beliefs held in people's heads but a material practice, operating through ideological state apparatuses such as schools, churches, and the family, which interpellate individuals as subjects. Terry Eagleton later surveyed these traditions and argued that ideology should be understood not as false consciousness alone but as the discursive means by which power is legitimated and contested.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One long-running debate concerns whether ideology means false belief. If ideology is defined as distortion, then the task of critique is to unmask it and reveal the truth beneath. But if all social thought is shaped by social position, as Mannheim suggested, then there is no ideology-free standpoint from which to judge, and the concept must be redefined in terms of function rather than truth. A second debate concerns agency: Althusser's account was criticised for making subjects mere effects of ideological structures, leaving little room for resistance, while Gramsci's hegemony and later cultural studies emphasised that consent must be continually won and can be withdrawn. A third debate concerns the scope of the concept in contemporary societies. Daniel Bell famously announced the end of ideology, arguing that in affluent post-industrial societies the grand political visions of the nineteenth century had exhausted themselves. Critics replied that ideology had not ended but changed form, becoming less explicit and more embedded in technical expertise, consumer culture, and the unquestioned assumption that there is no alternative to market society. A fourth dimension concerns the relationship between ideology and discourse: in the wake of Foucault, many sociologists analyse ideology less as a doctrine than as the taken-for-granted categories through which people think, speak, and act.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Ideology remains indispensable for understanding contemporary politics. The spread of neoliberal ideas since the 1980s, presenting market competition as the natural and efficient way to organise every sphere of life, is widely analysed as an ideological project with identifiable advocates, think tanks, and institutional carriers. Digital platforms raise new questions: algorithmic recommendation systems shape what billions of people see and believe, while claims about misinformation and disinformation revive old questions about false belief in a new technological setting. Climate politics illustrates the concept's continuing force: denial and delay are sustained not only by ignorance but by organised interests and by worldviews in which economic growth and fossil-fuel dependence appear as common sense. Critics of the concept argue that it is too blunt an instrument, that it tempts analysts to dismiss views they disagree with as mere ideology, and that it underestimates the rationality of ordinary people, who often hold contradictory beliefs and recognise the interests at stake. Its defenders reply that without a concept of ideology, sociology cannot explain why inequalities that harm the majority so often win their consent, or why alternatives appear unimaginable even when the present order is widely judged to be failing.
