# Redistribution

Redistribution is the movement of goods, food, and wealth through a centre: resources are collected from many hands, pooled under the control of a chief, a king, a temple, or a state, and then given out again. A farmer pays tribute in grain to the chief's storehouse; the chief feasts the village, feeds the craftsmen, and sends gifts to allies. A taxpayer pays income tax; the state pays pensions, builds roads, and funds schools. In both cases, the flow is not a direct exchange between two parties, but a two-step movement — in to the centre, and out again — that creates and sustains a relationship of dependence between the centre and those it serves. Redistribution is one of the fundamental ways human societies organise their economies, and understanding it is essential to understanding chiefdoms, states, and the modern welfare state. It is distinct from reciprocity, the direct exchange of gifts between equals, and from market exchange, the buying and selling of goods at a price; this entry treats redistribution on its own terms.

## Definition

The classic account comes from Karl Polanyi. In *The Great Transformation* (1944), Polanyi argued that economies are organised by three forms of integration: reciprocity, redistribution, and market exchange. Redistribution, for Polanyi, requires a centre — a chief, a temple, a palace — toward which goods move and from which they are dispensed. It is the characteristic economic form of chiefdoms and early states, where the pooling of resources makes possible large-scale projects: irrigation works, temples, armies, and craft production. In his 1957 essay "The Economy as Instituted Process," Polanyi stressed that redistribution is an instituted pattern, embedded in social and political relationships. Redistribution can take many forms. It may be a regular tribute, collected at harvest and stored against famine. It may be a feast, in which a chief displays generosity and binds followers to him. It may be a state welfare system, in which taxes are collected and benefits paid. What unites these cases is the centrality of the centre: goods do not move directly between producers and consumers, but pass through an authority that decides who gives and who receives. This gives the centre power — the power to reward and to withhold — and makes redistribution a political as much as an economic process.

## Origins and Development

The anthropology of redistribution grew out of the study of chiefdoms. Elman Service, in *Primitive Social Organization* (1962), argued that redistribution was the defining feature of the chiefdom: the chief's role was to collect surplus and redistribute it, and this function justified his authority. Marshall Sahlins, in *Stone Age Economics* (1972), placed redistribution in a broader typology of exchange, distinguishing it from generalised reciprocity — the open-ended giving among kin — and balanced reciprocity — the direct exchange of equivalents — and showing how each form corresponded to a different kind of social relationship. Timothy Earle, in *How Chiefs Come to Power* (1997), showed through comparative archaeology in Denmark, the Andes, and Hawaii how chiefs built power by controlling the flow of goods: staple finance, the collection and redistribution of food, and wealth finance, the control of prestige goods such as ornaments and weapons.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns whether redistribution benefits the community or the chief. Service and Sahlins tended to see it as a public service: the chief pooled resources, stored them against famine, and funded communal projects. Critics, drawing on Marxist anthropology, argued that redistribution was a mechanism of exploitation: the chief took more than he gave, and the ideology of generosity disguised the extraction of surplus. Ethnographic evidence supports both views: redistribution can be a genuine form of insurance against scarcity, and it can be a way for elites to accumulate wealth and power. A second debate concerns the relationship between redistribution and the state. Is the modern welfare state a continuation of chiefly redistribution, or something new? Some argue that taxation and public spending are redistribution on a vast scale, governed by democratic politics rather than by chiefly authority; others argue that the logic is the same — collection at the centre, allocation by authority — and that the welfare state is best understood as the latest form of an ancient pattern. A third debate concerns the conditions under which redistribution breaks down: when the centre takes too much, gives too little, or distributes unfairly, people may withdraw, rebel, or turn to markets and reciprocity instead. The legitimacy of the centre depends on the perceived fairness of the flow.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Redistribution is at the centre of contemporary political debate. Taxation, welfare benefits, foreign aid, and development assistance are all forms of redistribution, and arguments about them — who should pay, who should receive, and how much — are arguments about the legitimacy of the centre. The same logic appears in debates about global inequality: should wealthy countries redistribute resources to poor ones, and through what institutions? Critics of the classical account note that it focused on chiefdoms and states, and paid less attention to redistribution within households, between generations, and in non-state settings such as religious communities. The enduring insight is Polanyi's: redistribution is not a technical device for moving goods, but a social relationship, and the question of who controls the centre — and for whose benefit — is one of the oldest questions in politics.
