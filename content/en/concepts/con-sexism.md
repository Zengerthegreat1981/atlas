# Sexism

Sexism is the ideology and practice that treats one sex — overwhelmingly women — as inferior, and that sustains unequal treatment on the basis of sex. It operates at several levels at once: in beliefs that naturalise gender difference, in the unequal distribution of pay, power, and safety, and in everyday interactions that belittle, sexualise, or silence women. Sociology treats sexism not as a collection of individual prejudices but as a social system: a set of arrangements that reproduces male advantage even when no individual intends harm.

## Definition

The term "sexism" was coined in the 1960s, modelled on racism, and it quickly became a central analytic concept. Sociologists distinguish several forms. Hostile sexism is open antipathy: the belief that women are incompetent, manipulative, or properly subordinate. Benevolent sexism is more subtle: women are idealised as pure, nurturing, and in need of male protection — a flattering image that nonetheless confines them to dependent roles. Institutional sexism is embedded in rules and routines: hiring practices, pay scales, promotion criteria, and legal frameworks that systematically disadvantage women without any explicit statement of prejudice. And everyday or "subtle" sexism appears in interruptions, the attribution of women's ideas to men, sexualised jokes, and the expectation that women perform the emotional and domestic labour of workplaces and homes.

Sexism is closely related to patriarchy, the broader system of male dominance, but the two are not identical. Patriarchy names the structure; sexism names the ideology and practices that maintain it. It also intersects with other inequalities: a Black woman or a working-class woman faces forms of sexism shaped by racism and class, a point developed by intersectional theory.

## Origins and Development

Feminist thought has analysed male dominance for centuries, and a key source for the modern sociology of sexism is Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949). Her claim that "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman" reframed femininity as a social construction: women are made into the Other — defined in relation to men, who occupy the position of the universal subject. The women's liberation movement of the 1960s and 1970s turned this insight into a political programme, naming sexism as a system comparable to racism and demanding equality in law, work, and the family.

Kate Millett's *Sexual Politics* (1970) gave the analysis a sociological vocabulary, arguing that sex is a status category with political implications, and that patriarchy operates through socialisation, ideology, and institutions rather than brute force alone. Sylvia Walby's *Theorizing Patriarchy* (1990) systematised the theory, identifying six interlocking structures — paid work, housework, sexuality, culture, violence, and the state — through which male dominance is reproduced. Social psychology added precision: Peter Glick and Susan Fiske's theory of ambivalent sexism (1996) showed that hostile and benevolent attitudes coexist and reinforce each other, while Bernice Sandler and later researchers documented the "chilly climate" of small slights that accumulate into exclusion in classrooms and workplaces.

## Key Debates

A first debate concerns causes. Biological explanations attribute gender inequality to innate differences in strength, aggression, or maternal instinct. Sociologists counter that the size and shape of gender gaps vary enormously across societies and over time, and that biology cannot explain why the same traits are rewarded in men and punished in women. Socialisation, institutions, and power are the stronger explanations.

A second debate concerns how sexism changes form. As overt discrimination became illegal and socially unacceptable in many countries, sexism did not disappear but went underground: it appears in the gender pay gap, in the undervaluation of care work and feminised occupations, in the "glass ceiling" and the "glass cliff," and in the unequal burden of unpaid domestic labour. Some argue that this subtlety makes sexism harder to challenge, since each incident can be explained away; others warn against seeing sexism everywhere, which can trivialise the concept.

A third debate is about men and masculinity. Research on masculinities argues that sexism harms men too, by imposing rigid norms of toughness, emotional restraint, and breadwinning — and that dismantling sexism requires changing what it means to be a man, not only improving women's position.

## Contemporary Relevance

Sexism remains a live political issue. The #MeToo movement, a phrase first used by Tarana Burke in 2006 and spread globally from 2017, exposed the scale of sexual harassment and assault and forced institutions to reckon with cultures of silence. Debates over pay transparency, parental leave, reproductive rights, and the representation of women in politics and media all turn on the recognition of sexism as a structural problem. Online, sexism has found new venues: harassment, coordinated abuse, and algorithmic amplification target women in public life. At the same time, feminist movements have made gains that would have seemed implausible a generation ago, and the concept of sexism itself has been extended to analyse discrimination against people who do not conform to binary gender norms. Understanding sexism sociologically means refusing to reduce it to bad attitudes: it is a pattern woven into institutions, interactions, and ideas, and it can only be undone at all three levels together.
