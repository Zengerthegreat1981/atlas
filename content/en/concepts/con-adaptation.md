# Adaptation

Adaptation is the process by which living beings — and the societies they build — adjust to changing conditions in order to survive and function. In anthropology, the term covers two linked stories: the biological story of how human bodies and behaviours were shaped by natural selection, and the cultural and economic story of how societies invent new ways of making a living when their environment, technology, or neighbours change. A people that moves from hunting to farming, or from a stable climate to a warming one, is adapting, just as surely as a species that evolves thicker fur in a colder climate.

## The Biological Roots

The concept begins with Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859), which showed that the fit between an organism and its environment is produced by natural selection acting on variation over generations. Traits that help an organism survive and reproduce spread; traits that do not, fade. Human beings are the products of this process: bipedalism, large brains, and the long period of childhood dependence are all adaptations to particular ecological pressures. But much of human adaptation is cultural rather than genetic: we do not wait for evolution to give us thicker skin against the cold; we invent clothing, shelter, and fire.

Jean Piaget gave the concept a psychological form. In *The Origins of Intelligence in Children* (1936), he argued that intelligence itself is adaptation, built from two complementary processes: assimilation, fitting new experience into existing mental schemas, and accommodation, changing those schemas when experience will not fit. The child's mind, like a species, adapts by constant negotiation between what it already knows and what the world demands. The parallel with culture is direct: a society assimilates a new crop, tool, or idea into its existing way of life, and accommodates — reorganises itself — when the novelty cannot be absorbed.

## Cultural Adaptation

Cultural ecology, developed by Julian Steward and set out in *Theory of Culture Change* (1955), made adaptation the central question of anthropology. Steward argued that a society's "cultural core" — its subsistence technology, work organisation, and settlement pattern — is shaped by its environment, and that similar environments produce similar adaptations in unrelated societies. He compared early irrigation-based societies, including those of coastal Peru and Mesopotamia, and argued that parallel developments toward centralised authority reflected similar ecological and technological conditions rather than a shared history, a thesis later much debated.

Roy Rappaport's *Pigs for the Ancestors* (1968) gave this argument one of its best-known ethnographic illustrations. Among the Tsembaga Maring of New Guinea, the ritual cycle of pig feasts — which looked purely religious — regulated the pig population, distributed protein, restored garden land, and managed relations between warring groups. The ritual was an adaptation: a cultural mechanism that kept the human population and its environment in a workable balance, operating through beliefs and ceremonies rather than through conscious ecological planning.

## Economic Adaptation

Adaptation is also an economic story. Societies adapt their livelihoods when the conditions of making a living change: pastoralists become traders when grazing land shrinks, farmers take up wage labour when land is enclosed, fishing communities turn to tourism when stocks collapse. Such shifts are rarely smooth. They require new skills, new social arrangements, and the abandonment of knowledge accumulated over generations — decisions that are as much about identity and dignity as about income.

The psychological side of this adjustment is described by the stress and coping tradition. Richard Lazarus and Susan Folkman (1984) showed that adaptation to pressure depends on appraisal: whether a person judges a situation as a threat or a challenge, and whether they believe they have the resources to meet it. The same framework scales up to communities: a village facing a failed harvest copes differently depending on whether it has stored grain, kin in the city, and access to credit.

## Climate Adaptation and Technological Change

Climate change has made adaptation an urgent practical question. The strategies now documented by anthropologists — changing planting calendars, reviving drought-resistant crops, migrating seasonally, rebuilding houses on stilts, restoring mangroves — are often old knowledge applied to new conditions. But adaptation has limits: there are thresholds beyond which no adjustment suffices, and the people who must adapt most are usually those who contributed least to the problem. Adaptation is a question of justice as well as of technique.

Rapid technological change poses a parallel challenge. The arrival of the mobile phone, the internet, and now artificial intelligence is forcing adaptations as profound as any climatic shift: new forms of work, new kinds of community, new threats to privacy and attention. Here the psychological dimension reappears in the form of hedonic adaptation — the tendency to return to a baseline level of satisfaction after both good and bad events — which helps explain why each new technology quickly stops feeling new.

## Limits and Critiques

The concept has been criticised for "adaptationism": the assumption that every trait and practice must serve a purpose. Stephen Jay Gould and Richard Lewontin (1979) warned against this Panglossian habit, arguing that many features are by-products of structure — like the triangular spaces between the arches of a cathedral, which exist because of the architecture, not because they were designed. Not every custom is an adaptation; some practices persist because they are locked in by history rather than because they work.

There is also an ethical warning. In clinical and social contexts, telling people to "adapt" can blame the victim, demanding that individuals adjust to harmful conditions rather than changing the conditions themselves. Adaptation is a real and powerful process, but it is not always the right response: sometimes the environment, the economy, or the institution is what needs to change.
