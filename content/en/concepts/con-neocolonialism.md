# Neocolonialism

## Definition

Neocolonialism is the continuation of colonial domination after formal political independence, through economic, financial, cultural, and military means rather than direct territorial rule. The term captures a central paradox of decolonisation: countries that won sovereignty over their flags, anthems, and parliaments often remained dependent on former colonial powers and on international institutions for markets, credit, technology, and security. Where colonialism ruled by occupation and administration, neocolonialism rules through unequal trade, debt, foreign investment, aid conditionality, and the cultural authority of Western knowledge and media. The concept is therefore both an analytical tool for studying postcolonial power and a political accusation, naming a structure of domination that presents itself as partnership, development, or globalisation.

## Origins and Development

The term was popularised by Kwame Nkrumah, Ghana's first prime minister, in *Neo-Colonialism: The Last Stage of Imperialism*, published in 1965. Nkrumah argued that independence without economic independence was a sham, and that the new states of Africa and Asia were being held in a web of financial and commercial relationships that transferred wealth outward and constrained political choice. His concerns paralleled the work of the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch, who argued that the terms of trade tended to favour industrial exporters over primary producers (a thesis that fed into later dependency theory), and Frantz Fanon's warning, in *The Wretched of the Earth*, that national elites could become intermediaries for foreign interests rather than agents of liberation. Walter Rodney's *How Europe Underdeveloped Africa* extended the argument historically, documenting how centuries of extraction, from the slave trade to colonial taxation, had structured African underdevelopment. In the 1970s and 1980s the concept was taken up by world-systems theorists, notably Immanuel Wallerstein, who placed neocolonial relations within a global division of labour between core, semi-peripheral, and peripheral zones, and by scholars of international institutions who analysed the role of the World Bank, the International Monetary Fund, and structural adjustment programmes in reshaping postcolonial economies.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

A central debate concerns the concept's scope. For some, neocolonialism describes a specific set of mechanisms, such as debt dependence and the control of commodity markets; for others, it names a general condition of postcolonial life, including cultural and epistemic domination, in which Western science, education, and media define what counts as knowledge and progress. A second debate concerns agency: critics argue that the term can reduce postcolonial states to passive victims, ignoring how elites use foreign alliances for their own purposes, how popular movements resist, and how countries such as China, India, and Brazil have renegotiated their positions in the world economy. A third debate concerns the role of new actors. The rise of China as a lender and investor in Africa and Asia has prompted arguments about whether South-South relations reproduce neocolonial patterns or offer an alternative to them, and about whether the concept should be reserved for relations with former colonial powers or extended to any asymmetric relation between states. A fourth dimension concerns the distinction between neocolonialism and imperialism more generally: David Harvey's account of the new imperialism argues that contemporary domination operates through the dispossession of public assets and the opening of new markets, a process that affects societies in the global North as well as the South.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

Neocolonialism remains a live framework for analysing global inequality. The debt crises that have repeatedly struck countries in Africa, Latin America, and Asia, and the conditions attached to emergency lending, are routinely described in neocolonial terms. The control of digital infrastructure by a small number of corporations headquartered in the United States and China has given rise to the concept of digital colonialism, in which data extraction, platform dependence, and the governance of the internet reproduce older patterns of extraction in a new medium. Cultural debates over museum collections, the restitution of looted artefacts, and the dominance of Western universities in global rankings keep the cultural dimensions of the concept in view. Critics argue that the term is now used so broadly that it explains everything and therefore nothing, and that globalisation has produced a world too multipolar for a framework built on a simple coloniser-colonised binary. Its defenders reply that the binary was always an oversimplification, and that the concept's value lies precisely in tracing how domination adapts: direct rule gives way to debt, debt to data, but the underlying asymmetry of power persists, and naming it is the first step towards challenging it.
