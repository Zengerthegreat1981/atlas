# Nirvana, Extinction, and Buddhist Liberation

Nirvana (in Pali: nibbana) is the goal of the Buddhist path: the extinguishing of the fires of desire, hatred, and ignorance, and with it the cutting off of the cycle of birth and death (samsara). The word comes from a root meaning "to blow", so it is a metaphor of a flame that goes out when the fuel is gone. The word was not the Buddha's invention; it was also in use in other Indian religious currents, but he gave it a particular content.

## In the Pali Sources

- **The fire of the passions:** in the "Fire Sermon" (Samyutta Nikaya 35:28) the Buddha says that everything is burning with the fire of desire, hatred, and delusion, and that the disciple who sees this becomes disenchanted and is liberated.
- **The unconditioned:** the *Udana* (8:3) says that there is an "unborn, unmade, uncompounded", and that were it not for it, there would be no escape from the born, made, and compounded.
- **The question of the state after death:** in the *Aggivacchagotta* (Majjhima Nikaya 72) the Buddha refuses to say that the perfected one exists after death or does not exist, and compares this to asking where the fire went when it went out.
- **Nirvana with remainder and without remainder:** nirvana is attained in life through the cessation of the passions while the body remains, and is completed at death (parinibbana), when there is no new birth.

## The Readings

European scholars have disagreed since the nineteenth century: is it absolute annihilation (a common understanding that many have rejected) or a transcendent reality? Steven Collins (1998) showed that the metaphors are multiple, and that nirvana is depicted in a wide Buddhist literature in images of peace and security and is not merely nothingness. In the Mahayana, Nagarjuna says in the *Fundamental Verses of the Middle Way* (25:19–20) that there is no difference between samsara and nirvana because both are empty of intrinsic nature (svabhava), and the Mahayana adds the example of the bodhisattva who postpones entering it out of compassion for beings.

## Comparison

Hindu moksha appears close, but in Vedanta it is understood as a real self that is Brahman, whereas Buddhism denies a fixed self (anatta), so nirvana becomes a causal extinction and not a union with an absolute. Nor is it a conception of the afterlife in the monotheistic sense.

## Presence in the Arab Heritage

Arab writers of the medieval period knew reports about Buddhism, such as al-Biruni in his description of India, but their presentation remained limited and did not address nirvana as the texts know it, so they cannot be relied upon as a source for it.

## Limits

It is noted that speaking about nirvana, positively or negatively, goes beyond language, and the texts acknowledge this. Critics hold that modern psychological interpretations (inner comfort) diminish the metaphysical content. The debate remains over the meaning of the "remainder" after extinction, and whether what is called "deathlessness" does not contradict anatta.
