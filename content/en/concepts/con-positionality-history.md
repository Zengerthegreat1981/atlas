# Positionality in History

Positionality is a contemporary methodological concept which holds that the social position and identity of the historian (class, race, gender, nationality, institutional affiliation) inevitably affect the choice of subject, sources and interpretive framing of the past, and that critical awareness of this position (not denying it) is a condition for writing history with greater methodological integrity.

## Definition and Theoretical Roots

The concept of "positionality" derives from **standpoint theory**, developed mainly in feminist philosophy and critical sociology, according to which all knowledge is produced from a specific position of power and from particular lived experiences, so that no entirely neutral "view from above" exists that a researcher can adopt. The concept has been applied specifically to the practice of historical writing as an extension of the broader debate over the limits of historical objectivity. However, it goes further than merely acknowledging a general bias: it asks the researcher to state their own position clearly and to examine its effect on their particular research choices.

## Application in Historical Research

The concept operates in practice at several levels: "theoretical positionality," which comprises the historian's academic training, intellectual school and prior assumptions about what is worth studying in the first place; and "researcher positionality," which comprises personal identity and the researcher's relationship to the subject of their research (for example, a historian belonging to a formerly colonised society writing the history of colonialism from a different position than a historian from the colonising state). This awareness applies especially to **oral history**, where the question of "insider status" arises, and its effect on the relationship between the researcher and interviewees and on the interpretation of the testimonies collected.

## The Archive as Non-Neutral

The concept is linked to a parallel critique of the nature of the archive itself. Archives are not neutral, complete repositories of the past. They are the product of historical processes of selection, preservation and exclusion carried out by institutions with particular interests and positions of power (colonial states, churches, ruling families). The historian's awareness of their own position thus becomes part of a broader awareness of the positionality of the archive they work on, and not merely a personal question isolated from the nature of the sources themselves.

## Value and Limits

This approach offers an alternative between two poles: the Rankean claim to a complete mirror-like objectivity on the one hand, and absolute relativism that levels all accounts regardless of evidence on the other. Acknowledging positionality does not mean abandoning standards of verification and evidence. Rather, it clarifies the framework within which such verification is practised. Some critics, however, warn that formal "positionality statements" may become an empty ritual procedure that does not actually change research methods, or may paradoxically be used as a tool to absolve the researcher from deeper scrutiny of the power structures within which they work.
