# Child Fostering

Child fostering is the practice of raising a child in a household other than that of its birth parents, without severing the child's ties to its natal kin. Unlike legal adoption in Euro-American law, which typically transfers parental rights permanently and exclusively, fostering in many societies is a flexible, often reversible arrangement in which a child may move between households, maintain claims on more than one set of parents, and return home as circumstances change. Anthropologists treat fostering as a window onto how kinship is actually made: not only through birth, but through feeding, care, residence, and the daily work of bringing up children.

## Definition

Fostering takes many forms. A child may be sent to live with grandparents, a mother's brother, a childless relative, or a wealthier household that can offer schooling or an apprenticeship. The arrangement may last a few months or an entire childhood, and it may be initiated by the birth parents, requested by the foster parents, or arranged by the wider kin group. What distinguishes fostering from adoption is that the birth parents generally retain recognised rights and responsibilities, and the child retains membership in its natal lineage. Esther Goody, whose comparative work on West Africa shaped the field, distinguished fostering from adoption precisely on this point: fostering adds parents rather than replacing them. The child acquires additional caregivers, additional claims to support, and additional obligations, while the birth parents keep their place in the child's life.

## Origins and Development

Anthropological interest in fostering grew out of the study of kinship in the mid-twentieth century, when ethnographers working in Africa, Oceania, and the Caribbean noticed that children routinely circulated between households in ways that Euro-American categories could not capture. Early accounts often treated such circulation as a response to crisis — the death of a parent, divorce, or poverty. Later research showed that fostering is frequently a normal, even valued, part of family life rather than an emergency measure. In West Africa, for example, sending a child to live with kin is a way of strengthening ties between households, distributing the costs and rewards of child-rearing, and giving children access to education, trade skills, or urban opportunities. In the Pacific, fostering has long been bound up with rank, and land rights. Jessaca Leinaweaver's study of Andean Peru showed how the circulation of children creates moral bonds between households and how children themselves experience these moves as both opportunity and loss.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns whether fostering should be understood primarily as a strategy or as a relationship. The strategic view emphasises how families use fostering to manage labour, secure alliances, and invest in children's futures. The relational view stresses that fostering creates kinship: a foster parent becomes a real parent through care, and a fostered child becomes a real member of the household. These views are not mutually exclusive, but they point to different questions. Another debate concerns the child's own agency. Early ethnography often described fostering from the perspective of adults; more recent work asks how children negotiate belonging in two households, how they weigh loyalty to birth and foster parents, and how they remember moves made on their behalf. A third debate concerns inequality. Fostering can redistribute resources and opportunities, but it can also concentrate them: children sent to wealthier urban kin may gain schooling, while children kept at home do not, and girls fostered as domestic helpers may work long hours with little reward.

## Contemporary Relevance

Fostering remains widespread, and it has taken on new forms under migration, urbanisation, and formal state welfare systems. Migrant parents in West Africa, the Caribbean, and the Pacific often leave children with kin at home while they work abroad, creating transnational fostering arrangements sustained by remittances and phone calls. State child-welfare systems have also borrowed the language of fostering: in Britain and the United States, foster care is a formal, state-regulated arrangement for children removed from their birth families, usually intended as temporary. The resemblance is only partial, however, since state foster care is governed by law, licensing, and the possibility of permanent adoption, whereas informal fostering rests on kinship norms and negotiation. Anthropologists also study how international adoption debates draw, often uncritically, on Euro-American assumptions that a child can have only one true set of parents — an assumption that fostering societies have long challenged. Understanding fostering therefore matters for policy: interventions that treat all child circulation as abandonment or trafficking risk misunderstanding arrangements that families themselves value, while a romantic view of fostering risks overlooking the exploitation that can occur within it.
