# Out-group

An out-group is a social group to which a person does not belong, and against which their own group — the in-group — is defined and often judged. The distinction is relational rather than fixed: the same group can be an in-group in one context and an out-group in another, and every in-group creates its out-groups by drawing the boundary of "us" against "them." Out-groups range from rival teams and neighbouring villages to ethnic minorities, immigrants, and religious communities, and attitudes toward them run from indifference and curiosity to suspicion, contempt, and open hostility.

## Origins and Development

The paired terms in-group and out-group were coined by the American sociologist William Graham Sumner in *Folkways* (1906). Sumner observed that loyalty to one's own group and hostility toward outsiders seemed to grow together: the cohesion of the in-group was nourished by contrast with, and often conflict against, the out-group. For Sumner this was a near-universal feature of human societies, rooted in the struggle for survival, and he popularised the name for the attitude, ethnocentrism (a term used earlier by Ludwig Gumplowicz), which remains in use today.

The concept was given experimental footing in the twentieth century by social psychology. Muzafer Sherif's Robbers Cave experiment (conducted in 1954 and reported in full in 1961) showed that competition between groups of boys at a summer camp rapidly produced hostility, stereotyping, and aggression, and that only shared goals requiring cooperation could undo it. Henri Tajfel's minimal group experiments (1970) went further: simply sorting people into groups on an arbitrary basis — by a supposed preference for one painter over another, or, in a later variant by Michael Billig and Tajfel (1973), openly at random — was enough to make them favour their own group and discriminate against the other. Group membership alone, without history, conflict, or self-interest, could generate out-group bias. Tajfel and John Turner formalised this in social identity theory (1979), arguing that people derive part of their self-esteem from their group memberships, and so are motivated to see their in-group as superior.

## Key Debates and Dimensions

One debate concerns how inevitable out-group hostility is. Sumner's view suggests that in-group love and out-group hate are two sides of one coin, but Marilynn Brewer's review of minimal-group research (1979) indicated that in-group favouritism does not automatically entail out-group derogation: people often simply prefer their own group without actively disliking the other. Hostility, on this view, requires additional ingredients — perceived threat, competition over resources, or ideologies that cast the out-group as dangerous or inferior. Gordon Allport's contact hypothesis (1954) offered a practical corollary: under the right conditions — equal status, shared goals, institutional support — contact between groups can reduce prejudice rather than inflame it.

A second dimension is the distinction between realistic and symbolic threat. Realistic group conflict theory holds that hostility grows from competition over jobs, land, or political power; symbolic accounts stress threats to values, identity, and way of life. Immigration debates often blend the two, with economic anxieties fused to cultural ones. A third dimension concerns the fluidity of boundaries: out-groups can be redefined, absorbed, or newly invented. Groups once treated as threatening outsiders — Irish and Italian immigrants in the United States, for instance — were gradually reclassified as insiders, while new out-groups were constructed in their place.

## Contemporary Relevance and Critique

The out-group concept remains central to understanding prejudice, nationalism, and populism. It frames analyses of how political leaders mobilise support by naming enemies, how media coverage casts some groups as perpetual outsiders, and how online echo chambers sharpen the boundary between "us" and "them." It also informs research on dehumanisation, in which out-group members are denied full humanity — a process that research links to support for exclusionary policies and, in extreme cases, violence.

Critics warn that the in-group/out-group language can naturalise divisions that are in fact made by institutions, laws, and histories — borders, citizenship rules, and colonial categories — rather than by human nature. The concept can also obscure the internal diversity of groups and the many people who belong to several groups at once, moving across the boundary. Its value, however, lies in its precision: it identifies the mechanism by which solidarity within a group so easily turns into exclusion of those outside it, and it points to the conditions — shared goals, equal contact, common identities — under which the boundary can be softened or redrawn.
