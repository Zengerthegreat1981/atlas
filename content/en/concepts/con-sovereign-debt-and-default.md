# Sovereign Debt and Default

**Sovereign debt** is debt owed by a national government, whether issued at home in domestic currency or borrowed abroad, often in a foreign currency. **Sovereign default** is a failure to meet the contractual payments, by delay, by a reduction in the amount or interest due, or by an outright repudiation. Defaults of this kind long predate modern theory: Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff document repeated sovereign defaults over several centuries. The 1981 paper discussed below is a landmark in the recent economics of why such lending occurs at all.

## The peculiarity of sovereign debt: the absence of coercive enforcement

Sovereign debt differs fundamentally from the debt of companies and individuals, because there is no international judicial mechanism capable of forcing a government that refuses to pay to comply, or of seizing its assets as happens in corporate bankruptcy. **Jonathan Eaton** and **Mark Gersovitz** analysed this peculiarity theoretically in their article "Debt with Potential Repudiation" (1981). They showed that creditors nevertheless continue lending to sovereigns, because a defaulting state loses something else of importance: its creditworthiness and its future ability to access international financial markets. This is an implicit deterrent that partly replaces direct legal enforcement. Models of this kind explain why lending and default can coexist, but their ability to predict the timing of actual crises is limited.

## Patterns of historical crises

Sovereign debt crises in the modern era have followed recurring patterns. The **Latin American debt crisis (1982)** arose when sharply higher US interest rates and a global recession raised the burden of servicing accumulated dollar-denominated debt that had been borrowed in an era of low real interest rates. Mexico announced it could not service its debt in August 1982, and many other countries followed into what became known as the "lost decade". The **Argentine sovereign default (2001)** came after years of a strict currency peg to the dollar had exhausted the competitiveness of the economy. The **eurozone sovereign debt crisis (from 2010)**, beginning with Greece, revealed the fragility of countries that had lost the independent exchange rate instrument within a monetary union, without a sufficient federal budget to absorb the shock.

## Restructuring tools

When full repayment becomes impossible, governments and creditors turn to debt restructuring. This includes extending maturities, reducing the nominal value of the debt (a "haircut"), and lowering the interest rate on existing debt. This is often mediated by the International Monetary Fund, within structural adjustment programmes conditional on fiscal and monetary reforms.

## An Arab context

Several Arab countries have experienced sovereign debt crises of varying severity. These include the large Paris Club debt relief granted to Egypt in 1991 and the financial collapse of Lebanon from 2019, which was accompanied by a default on its Eurobonds in March 2020, the first in the country's history. These cases illustrate how weak public revenues, a deteriorating exchange rate and the loss of creditor confidence interact to accelerate the spiral of default.
