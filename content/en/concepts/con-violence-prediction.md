# Violence Prediction

A subfield of forensic psychiatry concerned with estimating the probability that a particular person will commit violent behavior in the future, based on documented psychological, behavioral, and social factors. Violence risk assessment is used in sensitive judicial and clinical decisions, such as parole, involuntary commitment, and, in some jurisdictions, preventive detention orders. Legal standards differ between countries and states.

## Historical Development

Early studies (in the 1970s) were highly skeptical of psychiatrists' ability to predict violence at all, with reviews such as Monahan's (1981) concluding that unstructured clinical predictions of violence were wrong more often than right. This criticism prompted the development of more systematic tools from the 1980s onward, moving from a binary, categorical "prediction" (will commit violence / will not commit violence) to probabilistic "risk assessment" based on multiple weighted factors.

## Main Approaches

- **Actuarial tools**: these use fixed statistical formulas into which predefined risk factors are entered (such as criminal history, age at first offense, and substance use) to produce a numerical risk score. An example is the Violence Risk Appraisal Guide (VRAG).
- **Structured professional judgment (SPJ)**: this combines a standardized list of systematic risk factors with a final clinical judgment that takes into account the particulars of the individual case. Its best-known tool is the Historical-Clinical-Risk Management-20 (HCR-20), one of the most widely used violence risk assessment instruments internationally. It divides its factors into ten historical, five clinical (current), and five risk management factors.
- **Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R)**: a tool for assessing psychopathic personality traits, developed by Robert Hare. It is sometimes used as an additional predictive factor within broader risk assessments, although it was originally a personality diagnosis tool rather than a direct predictive instrument.

## Scientific Evidence

Systematic reviews find that instruments such as the HCR-20 predict later violence better than chance, with modest accuracy, and that structured methods generally outperform unaided clinical impression. Comparisons between instruments are not uniform: a 2010 meta-analysis found nine commonly used tools broadly interchangeable, with moderate accuracy (Yang, Wong and Coid, 2010), whereas a 2011 review reported differences between tools, with the youth instrument SAVRY performing best and the LSI-R and PCL-R least well (Singh, Grann and Fazel, 2011). Any score remains probabilistic. Tools produce false positives (rating as higher risk a person who does not later act violently) and false negatives.

## Applications and Debate

These tools are used in parole decisions, pretrial assessment, and the management of mentally ill patients in therapeutic or judicial settings. Their use, however, raises sharp ethical debate: the possibility that individuals may be deprived of their liberty on the basis of a probabilistic estimate rather than an act they have actually committed, and the risk that some risk factors (such as socioeconomic status or ethnic history within the justice system) may be biased in a way that entrenches structural inequalities that already exist.

## Limits

No violence prediction tool approaches complete accuracy. The accuracy of tools varies by the population studied (psychiatric patients, convicted offenders, adolescents), which limits the generalizability of a tool tested in one context to a different context without empirical re-validation.

These tools are aids to structured decision-making, not substitutes for clinical or legal judgment; this entry is educational only.
