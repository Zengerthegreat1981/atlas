# Form Criticism (Formgeschichte)

A critical method in the study of the Bible, pioneered by Hermann Gunkel at the beginning of the twentieth century. It extends the documentary question of "Higher Criticism" (who wrote this text, and when?) with a new question: **how was this material formed orally before it was written down?**

## The Literary Form (Gattung) and the Life Setting (Sitz im Leben)

In *Genesis* (1901) Gunkel treated the patriarchal narratives as collections of originally oral legends (*Sagen*). He assumed more generally that the biblical texts, even after their analysis into the four documentary sources of Wellhausen, contain within them older layers of inherited oral traditions, each with a regular "literary form" (*Gattung*) recurring across the text (a creation myth, a victory hymn, an individual lament, a wisdom saying). Gunkel held that each of these forms originally arose in a specific social "life setting" (*Sitz im Leben*, for example a collective cultic performance of a victory hymn, or a mourning assembly for a lament) before being later gathered into the continuous written text we know today. The task of the literary critic is to isolate these individual forms and to link each one with its original social-ritual context.

## Its Relationship to Wellhausen's Documentary Criticism

Gunkel's method does not overturn Wellhausen's documentary hypothesis but operates **beneath** it: it assumes the multiplicity of documentary sources (J/E/D/P) as a necessary first step, and then asks what happened *before* each of these sources was written down, that is, the history of the older oral forms that were gathered later within each document. This method therefore represents a deepening of the Higher Criticism project rather than an alternative to it. It later became a standard tool in the field, and its scope extended beyond the Old Testament to the study of the Gospels by later critics.

## Extension to the New Testament

The German theologian Rudolf Bultmann later applied Gunkel's method to the Gospels in *The History of the Synoptic Tradition* (*Die Geschichte der synoptischen Tradition*, 1921), analysing the sayings, miracles and parables of Jesus into regular recurrent forms (parable, healing miracle, legal dispute) that each arose in a specific life setting within the early Christian community before the Gospels were written. Martin Dibelius's *Die Formgeschichte des Evangeliums* (1919) developed the same approach independently. This opened a research branch in New Testament studies known as form criticism or tradition criticism.

## Criticism

Later scholars questioned whether the oral stages and original "life settings" can really be reconstructed from written texts, and whether forms can be neatly tied to single social settings. Such objections led to newer methods such as redaction criticism, rhetorical criticism and canonical approaches, while form-critical vocabulary remains in wide use.

## Applied Example: Psalms of Lament

Gunkel illustrated his method by applying it to the Book of Psalms: he classified many psalms under the form of the "Individual Lament", with a fixed recurrent structure (a cry for help, a description of the distress, a plea, an expression of trust, and a closing vow of praise), concluding that this recurrent structure reflects typical situations of worship (for example, a person in distress seeking help in a cultic setting) rather than the free creation of each poet individually. He developed this in his *Introduction to the Psalms* (*Einleitung in die Psalmen*), completed by Joachim Begrich in 1933.
