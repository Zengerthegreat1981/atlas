# Homeland: Territory, Memory, and Belonging

A **homeland** is a place — real, remembered, or imagined — that a people regard as their own: the territory of origin to which they belong and which belongs to them. It is never merely a location on a map. A homeland is a territory saturated with memory, myth, and moral claim. It anchors identity, organises longing, and legitimates political demands, from the right of return to the defence of borders. Anthropology studies the homeland not as a natural fact but as a cultural construction: a place made meaningful through stories, rituals, graves, songs, and law.

## Definition and Development

The concept entered anthropological discussion through the study of nationalism and of diaspora. Benedict Anderson, in *Imagined Communities* (1983), argued that nations are imagined political communities: members who will never meet nonetheless picture themselves as sharing a common past and a common territory. The homeland is the spatial anchor of that imagination — the land where the nation's ancestors lived, fought, and are buried, and whose soil is therefore treated as sacred rather than merely useful. Anderson showed that print media, maps, censuses, and museums made it possible for people to imagine a bounded national territory as their homeland even when they had never seen most of it.

Anthony D. Smith, in *The Ethnic Origins of Nations* (1986), added a historical dimension. He argued that modern nations draw on older ethnic cores — *ethnies* — with their own myths of descent and sacred territories. The homeland, in this account, is the land where the ethnic community's myths are set: its mountains, rivers, and cities carry the story of the people. This is why disputes over territory so often turn on archaeology, place names, and ancient texts: each side seeks to prove that the land is its homeland by right of first presence.

## Homeland and Diaspora

The homeland is most intensely felt by those who are away from it. William Safran's influential account of diaspora, in "Diasporas in Modern Societies: Myths of Homeland and Return" (1991), placed the homeland at the centre of diasporic identity: dispersed communities retain a collective memory of their original territory, regard it as their true home, believe they should eventually return, and remain committed to its safety and prosperity. The homeland thus functions as a myth even for people who have never lived there: second- and third-generation emigrants may feel a powerful attachment to a village they have only seen in photographs.

Arjun Appadurai, in *Modernity at Large* (1996), argued that globalisation has loosened the bond between people and place without destroying it. Migrants sustain their homelands through remittances, phone calls, satellite television, and social media, creating deterritorialised attachments — a homeland that is lived at a distance. At the same time, states cultivate diasporic loyalty, offering dual citizenship and voting rights to emigrants precisely because the homeland remains a source of identity and resources.

## Examples

The Arabic term *watan* carries the same layered meaning: it denotes both a birthplace and a political homeland, and modern Arab nationalism made the *watan* the object of loyalty and sacrifice. For Palestinians, the homeland is at once a lost territory, a lived reality under occupation, and a symbol of return — a case where the homeland is simultaneously present and absent, which is why it figures so centrally in poetry, political discourse, and everyday conversation. Jewish attachment to the land of Israel, Armenian memory of a homeland lost in 1915, and Kurdish claims to a homeland without a state all show how the concept organises collective identity and political struggle.

Indigenous peoples offer a different model: for many Aboriginal Australian, Native American, and Amazigh communities, the homeland is not a nation-state's territory but a lived landscape of ancestral presence, where particular places hold stories, obligations, and spiritual power. Here the homeland is inseparable from stewardship: belonging to a place means caring for it, not merely owning it.

## Critique and Contemporary Questions

Critics warn that the idea of the homeland can become exclusionary. When a homeland is defined as belonging to one people by blood or ancient right, those who also live there — minorities, migrants, later arrivals — can be cast as strangers or intruders. Nationalist movements have used homeland myths to justify expulsion and ethnic cleansing. Feminist and postcolonial scholars add that homeland narratives often privilege male heroes, warriors, and founding fathers, marginalising women's experiences of home and displacement.

Contemporary anthropology therefore treats the homeland as a contested claim rather than a given fact. Whose homeland is a place? Who gets to define its boundaries and its story? And what happens when homelands multiply — when a person feels at home in two countries, or in none? These questions keep the concept at the centre of debates about migration, citizenship, and belonging.
