# Gender-Kinship Intersection

The idea that the study of kinship and the study of gender are not separate fields that occasionally intersect, but a single analytical system in which neither side can be understood without the other. Kinship terms, rules of marriage and inheritance shape what it means to be "female" or "male" in a given society, and presumed gender differences in turn shape the rules that govern who marries whom and who inherits what.

## Central Idea

Before the 1970s, classical anthropology (particularly Lévi-Strauss's structuralism and British descent theory) treated kinship as a near-formal structure of rules of descent and alliance, and treated "gender" (when it was mentioned at all) as a secondary biological given on which social organization was built. Feminist critique in the 1970s (Gayle Rubin, Michelle Rosaldo) reversed this order. The rules of kinship themselves, such as the exchange of women in marriage between groups of men, as Lévi-Strauss analyzed in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship*, are the mechanism that produces gender hierarchy, rather than a neutral consequence of it. Then came the book edited by Jane Collier and Sylvia Yanagisako, *Gender and Kinship: Essays Toward a Unified Analysis* (1987), which explicitly called for dissolving the methodological boundary between the two fields and treating them as a single integrated analysis.

## How the Intersection Works

- **Kinship terms make gender as much as they describe it**: kinship categories ("wife," "brother-in-law," "aunt") are not neutral descriptions of biological ties. They carry culturally specific gender expectations about the work, authority and sexuality permitted to the holder of each category.
- **Organizing inheritance and property through gender**: rules of who inherits (the eldest son? only male sons? daughters in the absence of sons?) directly determine economic inequality between the sexes within the family, and these rules change historically in response to broader economic transformations, rather than being fixed "traditional" constants.
- **Marriage as a transaction between groups, not only between two individuals**: in many systems (as in analyses of bridewealth and dowry), marriage is understood as an economic and social exchange between two descent groups, in which the woman becomes, in critical feminist analysis, a "sign" of the exchange more than an active party to it. Gayle Rubin drew attention to this in her concept of the "traffic in women."
- **Contemporary extension toward reproductive technologies and non-binary kinship**: with the spread of artificial insemination, transnational adoption and same-sex marriage, the intersection has deepened to question the gender assumptions embedded in the very definition of "mother" and "father" (Marilyn Strathern, Janet Carsten).

## Impact

This framework reshaped the entire field of kinship studies, which had been regarded in the 1960s as a dry "technical" branch of anthropology. It also influenced social history and family sociology, by highlighting how definitions of the family and of gender both change historically, instead of treating the "traditional" family as a natural constant.

## Limitations

Some early theorizing (especially Gayle Rubin's borrowings from Lacanian psychoanalysis) was criticized for being difficult to test ethnographically with precision. Moreover, the early focus on Western societies and on binary male–female marriage was slow to take account of the diversity of kinship systems in some non-Western societies that do not rest on the strict male/female binary.
