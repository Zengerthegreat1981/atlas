# Risk Assessment & Dangerousness

## Definition

The concept refers to the approaches and tools used by forensic psychiatrists and criminal justice specialists to estimate the probability that a person will commit violent or dangerous behavior in the future. These estimates serve clinical decisions (such as involuntary commitment) or judicial ones (such as parole decisions or pretrial assessment).

## Theoretical Framework

The field developed through three successive methodological waves. The first, dominant until about the 1970s and 1980s, relied on **unstructured clinical judgment**, in which the physician depends on personal experience alone. Later studies showed that the accuracy of unaided clinical prediction was modest. The cohort released after *Baxstrom v. Herold* (1966) was followed principally by Henry J. Steadman; John Monahan's reviews in the 1980s drew on that work and on other first-generation studies. This led to the second wave: **actuarial tools**, which rely on statistical algorithms built on risk factors established empirically. The third wave, the most widely used today, is **Structured Professional Judgment** (SPJ). It combines a scientifically standardized list of risk factors with a flexible final clinical judgment that takes the specifics of the case into account. The leading tool of this wave is the **HCR-20** (Historical-Clinical-Risk Management), developed by Christopher Webster, Kevin Douglas, Derek Eaves, and Stephen Hart (version 2 published in 1997; version 3 in 2013). It contains 20 items distributed across three scales: Historical (10 items), Clinical (5), and Risk Management (5).

## Applications

- Assessment of patients in forensic psychiatric hospitals before decisions to transfer them to lower security levels.
- Advice to courts in cases of parole, preventive detention, and "dangerousness to others" decisions that warrant involuntary commitment.
- Risk management in prisons and forensic mental health facilities, through treatment plans that target modifiable risk factors (such as relapse into substance use or treatment non-adherence).

## Legal Context

The legal use of "dangerousness" depends on jurisdiction. In the United States, civil commitment statutes generally require mental illness together with dangerousness to self or others (the Supreme Court addressed the constitutional limits in *O'Connor v. Donaldson*, 1975, and *Addington v. Texas*, 1979), and many states recognize a clinician's duty to protect identifiable third parties following *Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California* (California, 1976). Other jurisdictions use different tests, and risk assessment tools inform but do not determine legal outcomes.

## Ethical and Methodological Challenges

These tools generate continuing debate about the limits of their predictive capacity: even structured tools achieve only moderate accuracy, and their evidence on fair application across demographic groups is mixed. They also raise concern about the risk of bias when applied across different ethnic or socio-economic groups, and about the ethical tension between protecting society and the rights of an individual who has not yet committed any actual act.
