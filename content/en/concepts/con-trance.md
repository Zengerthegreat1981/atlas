# Trance

## Definition

Trance is a state of altered consciousness, entered in ritual and other culturally patterned settings, in which a person's ordinary awareness, sense of self, and control over the body are temporarily transformed. The person in trance may tremble, dance, speak in voices not their own, become insensitive to pain, or report visions and journeys. Trance is the broadest term in the anthropology of altered states: it covers the shaman's soul journey, the medium's possession by a spirit, the revivalist's collapse under the power of the Holy Spirit, and the dancer's absorption in rhythm. What unites these states is not a single experience but a family resemblance — a culturally recognised departure from ordinary consciousness that is understood to open contact with powers beyond the everyday.

## Origins and Development

The comparative study of trance began with the observation of its sheer ubiquity. Erika Bourguignon's cross-cultural surveys found institutionalised altered states of consciousness in the great majority of the world's societies, and her edited volume *Religion, Altered States of Consciousness, and Social Change* (1973) established trance as a central topic rather than an exotic curiosity. Her later work, *Possession* (1976), distinguished possession trance, in which a spirit is believed to take over the person, from trance without possession, such as visionary states and soul journeys, each with its own social logic.

I. M. Lewis's *Ecstatic Religion* (1971) gave trance a sociological interpretation. He argued that trance is not random or merely pathological: it is patterned by social position. In the possession cults he studied, trance afflicted those on the margins of power — often women in patriarchal societies — and gave them a language in which to make claims. The spirit, speaking through the afflicted person, could demand gifts, criticise a husband, or challenge an authority in ways the person herself could not. Trance, in this account, is a social idiom: a way of saying what cannot be said in ordinary speech.

Gilbert Rouget's *Music and Trance* (1985, English translation of the 1980 French original) investigated how trance is produced. He showed that music is the most common trigger worldwide — drumming, chanting, and song — but that the connection is learned rather than mechanical: the same rhythm induces trance in one society and leaves people unmoved in another, because what matters is the cultural association between a sound and a state. Felicitas Goodman, in *Ecstasy, Ritual, and Alternate Reality* (1988), took the opposite tack, arguing for a universal physiology of trance and proposing that particular body postures, combined with rhythmic stimulation, reliably produce visionary experience across cultures. The debate between these positions — learned association versus shared biology — continues to shape the field.

## Key Debates

The first debate concerns the relationship between trance and pathology. Western psychiatry has often treated trance-like states as symptoms of dissociation or hysteria. Anthropologists have countered that trance is usually a controlled, valued, and socially useful capacity: the medium enters trance at the appointed time and place, performs a recognised role, and returns to ordinary life. The distinction between a religious virtuoso and a psychiatric patient, they argue, is not in the state itself but in its cultural framing and its consequences. Tanya Luhrmann's *When God Talks Back* (2012), a study of American evangelicals who train themselves to hear God's voice, indicated that even in a modern, literate society, trance-like absorption can be learned, practised, and experienced as real — and that the boundary between religious experience and mental illness is negotiated, not given.

A second debate concerns agency: who is acting in trance? In possession, the answer is the spirit; in the shaman's journey, it is the shaman's soul. But anthropologists increasingly attend to the social work trance does: it redistributes authority, heals affliction, resolves conflict, and gives voice to the otherwise voiceless. Whether the spirit is "real" is, for the anthropologist, less important than what the state accomplishes in the community.

A third debate concerns the body. Trance is a bodily phenomenon — shaking, sweating, dancing, falling — and recent work stresses that it is learned through the body: through repeated participation, imitation, and the discipline of ritual. The body is not a passive vehicle for the spirit; it is where trance is trained and remembered.

## Contemporary Relevance

Trance is far from a relic. Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, among the fastest-growing religious movements of recent decades, centres on trance-like states: speaking in tongues, being "slain in the Spirit", prophecy, and healing. Spirit possession continues to be a major idiom of affliction and healing across Africa, Asia, and the Americas, and it increasingly appears in migration contexts, where migrants carry their spirits with them and negotiate them in new settings. Medical anthropology studies how trance states are recognised, treated, or suppressed in clinics and hospitals, and how diagnostic categories travel across cultures.

For anthropology, trance remains a test case for the study of consciousness. It shows that awareness is not a fixed human given but something cultures shape, train, and value differently — and that the line between the ordinary and the extraordinary is itself a cultural achievement.
