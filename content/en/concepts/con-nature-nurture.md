# Nature and Nurture

The nature-nurture question asks how much of the differences between people, and of human development generally, is due to inherited biology (nature) and how much to environment and experience (nurture). Francis Galton popularised the pairing in the 1870s. For much of the twentieth century it was framed as a contest; contemporary science treats it as a question about how the two work together.

## Heritability

The central quantitative idea is **heritability**: the proportion of variation in a trait, within a particular population and environment, that is statistically associated with genetic differences between individuals. Three points are routinely misunderstood. Heritability describes populations, not individuals: a heritability of 50 percent does not mean that half of one person's trait comes from genes. It is specific to the population and environment studied, and can change if the environment changes. And a high heritability does not imply that a trait is unchangeable. Height is highly heritable in well-nourished populations, yet average height rose substantially across the twentieth century with better nutrition.

## Research designs

**Twin studies** compare identical (monozygotic) twins, who share nearly all segregating genes, with fraternal (dizygotic) twins, who share about half on average. If identical twins resemble each other more than fraternal twins, genetic influence is inferred. **Adoption studies** compare adopted children with their biological and adoptive relatives, separating genetic from family-environment resemblance. Studies of twins reared apart, such as the Minnesota study led by Thomas Bouchard, have been prominent but small and debated. More recently, **molecular genetic methods**, including genome-wide association studies and polygenic scores, estimate effects of many common variants directly. Heritability estimates from molecular data have generally been lower than twin estimates, a discrepancy often called the "missing heritability" problem, which remains an area of active research.

Behavioural geneticists summarise one broad pattern, sometimes called the first law of behavioural genetics, that virtually all psychological traits show some heritability. Another pattern is that shared family environment, the part of the home that makes siblings alike, often explains less variation in adult traits than people expect, while non-shared environment, the experiences that make siblings different, explains more.

## Gene-environment interplay

Treating genes and environment as separate additive ingredients is misleading. **Gene-environment correlation** refers to the way genotype shapes the environments people encounter: children may receive environments correlated with their parents' genes (passive), evoke particular responses (evocative), or seek out niches that suit them (active). **Gene-environment interaction** means that the effect of a genetic variant depends on environment, or vice versa. A well-known example was a 2003 study by Caspi and colleagues linking a serotonin transporter variant, stressful life events and depression; later large analyses and meta-analyses failed to confirm it consistently, which shows the danger of relying on early candidate-gene findings. **Epigenetics** studies chemical modifications that regulate gene activity in response to experience; it is an important research area, but claims about transgenerational inheritance of psychological traits in humans are often stronger in popular accounts than in the evidence.

## Misconceptions

- That a trait is either genetic or environmental. Both are involved in every trait, and the proportions are not fixed.
- That heritable means fixed. Medical and educational interventions can alter heritable outcomes.
- That heritability within groups explains differences between groups. Richard Lewontin's analogy of seeds planted in two different soils illustrates why the inference fails: high within-group heritability is compatible with entirely environmental differences between groups.
- That environment means only parenting. Peers, schooling, nutrition, culture and chance developmental events all contribute.
- That identical twins share identical environments, or that twin studies are free of assumptions. The equal-environments assumption has been criticised, though tests of it have generally supported it.

## Debates and limits

Critics point to the difficulty of separating genes from environment in observational designs, to the dependence of estimates on measurement quality, and to the way early claims of "genes for" behaviours have fallen away. The field has a troubled history, including the eugenics movement, which misused hereditarian ideas; this history is one reason claims about race and genetics attract intense scrutiny and why careful, modest wording matters. Developmental systems theorists argue that the nature-nurture dichotomy itself should be abandoned in favour of a view of development as the product of constant interaction. Most researchers accept a middle position while still debating specific magnitudes.
