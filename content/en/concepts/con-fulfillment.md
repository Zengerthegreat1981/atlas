# Fulfillment

Fulfillment, existentially, is not satisfaction of desires or achievement of goals, but alignment of lived life with acknowledged values. A wealthy, successful person can be unfulfilled; someone facing constraint may experience deep fulfillment.

## Definition and Origins

The idea has roots in Aristotle's *eudaimonia*, usually translated as flourishing: a life well lived in accordance with virtue, as opposed to a life of mere pleasure. In twentieth-century psychology it reappears in Maslow's notion of self-actualization (*Motivation and Personality*, 1954), in Carol Ryff's model of psychological well-being (1989), which includes purpose in life, autonomy, and personal growth, and in the distinction drawn by Ryan and Deci (2001) between hedonic and eudaimonic well-being. Existential thinkers add an emphasis on responsibility, finitude, and meaning.

## Fulfillment vs. Satisfaction

Satisfaction is the gratification of desire; fulfillment is coherence between who one is, what one values, and how one lives. A person may be satisfied by accumulating possessions yet existentially unfulfilled because their life does not reflect their actual values. Research on the "hedonic treadmill" (Brickman and Campbell, 1971) is often cited to show how satisfaction from acquisitions tends to fade, though it does not directly test the existential concept.

Frankl's observations clarify the point. In *Man's Search for Meaning* he argued that the primary human drive is a "will to meaning," and that meaning can be found through creative work, through experience such as love, and through the attitude taken toward unavoidable suffering. Fulfillment arises from the sense that one's life has meaning, that one lives in accord with values, and that one's choices are one's own. It is less common than satisfaction and, on this view, more durable and less dependent on external circumstances.

## The Role of Constraints

Paradoxically, constraints can facilitate fulfillment. Someone living within limitations (poverty, illness, obligation) may be more fulfilled than someone with unlimited options if they have consciously chosen engagement within constraints and found meaning. Mortality gives choices their weight: if time were unlimited, nothing would need to be chosen.

Van Deurzen notes that fulfillment requires a realistic appraisal of what is possible and commitment to making meaning within those possibilities. It is an active practice, not a destination. Csikszentmihalyi's work on absorption in challenging activity ("flow," 1990) is sometimes linked to this practice of engaged commitment.

## In Practice

Existential therapists may help clients identify their actual values, compare them with how they spend their time, and consider what changes, large or small, would bring the two closer. Questions such as "What would make this life feel like mine?" are more common than prescriptions about what a good life contains.

## Criticisms and Limits

Critics note that the concept is hard to define and measure, and that "alignment with one's values" gives no way to judge the values themselves, since a person may feel fulfilled by harmful commitments. The emphasis on meaning through hardship can be misused to justify suffering that should be relieved, and accounts of fulfillment may reflect culturally specific ideals of individual self-realization that are less central in more communal cultures.
