# Emergence: Properties Not Reducible to Their Parts

In the philosophy of mind and the philosophy of science, emergence is the idea that a whole may have properties that do not exist in its parts and cannot be predicted from the properties of the parts alone. Liquidity is a property that cannot be attributed to a single water molecule, and consciousness, in the emergentist view, is a mental state that arises from neural activity without dissolving into it. This bears on the mind–body problem: is consciousness something other than the brain, or an emergent property of it?

## Ancient Roots and Their Arabic Echo

The Arabic heritage did not know the term "emergence," but it knew the idea in the question of temperament (*mizaj*). It inherited from Aristotle the view that a body composed of elements is not merely the sum of its parts, and that its composition has a new form and new powers. Ibn Sina, in the *Physics* of *al-Shifa'* and in *al-Qanun*, examined how from the mixing of elements arises a "temperament" carrying a new intermediate quality, which is the principle of the powers of plants and animals. This reading comes close to emergence without matching it, because it remains framed in terms of form and matter. In the Western tradition, John Stuart Mill, in *A System of Logic* (1843), prepared the ground by distinguishing "homopathic" laws, whose results can be calculated by addition, from "heteropathic" laws, which produce a new effect through combination.

## Modern Formulation

George Henry Lewes took the term "emergent" in *Problems of Life and Mind* (1875) to distinguish it from the "resultant," which can be calculated. Samuel Alexander, in *Space, Time, and Deity* (1920), added that the philosopher must accept emergent qualities with "natural piety" as raw empirical facts. Lloyd Morgan wrote *Emergent Evolution* (1923). Then C. D. Broad, in *The Mind and Its Place in Nature* (1925), offered the most precise philosophical analysis of the question: he distinguished "pure mechanism," which calculates everything from the parts, from "emergentism," which admits laws that cannot be derived from the laws of the lower level. He illustrated this with the properties of silver chloride, which cannot be extracted from the properties of silver and chlorine. In psychology, the Gestalt school (Koffka, Köhler and Wertheimer, building on Christian von Ehrenfels) argued that wholes have properties not found in their parts.

## Contemporary Debate

Philosophers today distinguish between "weak" emergence, which in principle can be simulated and explained computationally, as Bedau (1997) set out, and "strong" emergence, in which higher-level phenomena are not deducible even in principle from lower-level facts and may have novel causal powers, as Chalmers (2006) analyzed. Jaegwon Kim, in *Mind in a Physical World* (1998), showed that strong emergence faces the "argument from causal exclusion": if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, there is no room for mental causation. Philip Anderson wrote "More Is Different" (1972), defending the view that each level of science has its own independent character. In the enactivist tradition, Evan Thompson, in *Mind in Life* (2007), develops the view that mind arises from the self-organizing dynamics of living organisms in coupling with their environments, using the idea of emergence in this non-reductive sense.

In complexity science, emergence names collective patterns in systems of many interacting components (for example flocking, or the behaviour of ant colonies), as discussed in John Holland's *Emergence: From Chaos to Order* (1998); most of these cases are treated as weak emergence, explainable in principle by simulation, and not as evidence of strong emergence.

## Distinction from Adjacent Concepts

Emergence differs from **reductionism**, which reduces the whole to its parts; from **holism**, which affirms the unity of the whole without analyzing the relation between levels; and from **functionalism**, which defines a mental state by its causal role rather than its quality. Its relation to **qualia** is also close: those who regard conscious qualities as emergent from physical processes usually hold a form of strong emergence, though many reject this and prefer reductive or eliminative views.

## Limits of the Concept

Critics argue that the word "emergent" may be a name for our ignorance rather than an explanation: if we say consciousness emerges without showing the mechanism, we are only postponing the question. Moreover, strong emergence may be rejected because it undermines the causal closure of the physical world.
