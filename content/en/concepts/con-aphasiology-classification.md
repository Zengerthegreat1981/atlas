# Aphasiology & Aphasia Classification

## Definition

Aphasia classification is the clinical-linguistic system that classifies disorders of acquired language caused by brain damage (stroke, trauma, tumour) according to the location of the damage and the features of linguistic performance: fluency, comprehension, repetition, and naming. The classification emerged from the nineteenth-century legacy of Broca, Wernicke and Lichtheim. It crystallized in the twentieth century through Norman Geschwind and the Boston School (Harold Goodglass and Edith Kaplan), via the Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination.

## Classificatory Principle

The classical classification rests on the intersection of three performance axes: spoken fluency (intact or impaired), auditory comprehension (intact or impaired), and verbal repetition (intact or impaired). From the intersection of these axes, specific syndromes are derived. Broca's aphasia involves impaired fluency, relatively intact comprehension, and impaired repetition. Wernicke's aphasia involves apparently fluent speech with verbal jargon, impaired comprehension, and impaired repetition. Conduction aphasia involves relatively intact fluency and comprehension with a severe deficit in direct repetition. Global aphasia disrupts all axes. The transcortical aphasias (motor, sensory, and mixed), in which repetition is relatively preserved, are further categories in the Boston-style scheme. Anomic or nominal aphasia is a selective deficit in naming, with fluency, comprehension, and repetition relatively spared. In clinical use these labels are profiles, not pure types. Broca's aphasia often includes agrammatism: short utterances with few function words and simplified verb inflection, alongside effortful articulation. Wernicke's aphasia often includes empty circumlocution, semantic or phonemic paraphasias, and poor awareness of the deficit. Conduction aphasia is marked by phonemic paraphasias that worsen on repetition, with relatively spared comprehension of ordinary conversation. Global aphasia combines severely limited output with severely limited comprehension. Transcortical motor aphasia resembles Broca's aphasia but repetition is spared; transcortical sensory aphasia resembles Wernicke's aphasia but repetition, including of sentences the person does not understand, is relatively spared. Anomia may occur alone or remain after other syndromes have improved.

## Anatomical Basis and Contemporary Critique

The classical model linked each syndrome to a specific cortical location: Broca's area (the third inferior frontal gyrus) for Broca's aphasia, Wernicke's area (the posterior superior temporal gyrus) for Wernicke's aphasia, and the arcuate fasciculus connecting them for conduction aphasia. However, modern lesion-mapping and imaging studies, including the work of Nina Dronkers, have shown that the relation between anatomical location and syndrome is weaker and more complex than the classical model assumed. They also suggest that lesion size and extension into subcortical white-matter structures contribute substantially to the aphasic pattern alongside lesion location. This gave rise to alternative models, such as the dual-stream model of Hickok and Poeppel, which replaces the anatomical dichotomy with two functional streams: a ventral stream for understanding meaning and a dorsal stream for translating sound into articulation.

## Contemporary Clinical Use

The classical classification remains a standard communicative tool among physicians and speech-language pathologists despite its theoretical shortcomings. It provides a quick shared vocabulary for describing the symptom pattern at the bedside. More precise assessment batteries (such as the Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Examination or the Western Aphasia Battery) are used by trained clinicians for a finer quantitative description beyond the simple binary classification, and for tracking change over time; classification is a descriptive aid, not a substitute for clinical assessment.
