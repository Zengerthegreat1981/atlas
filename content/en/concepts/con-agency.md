# Agency

Agency is the capacity of people, individually and collectively, to act with some purpose and to make a difference to what happens, including the possibility of acting otherwise. Sociologists use the term chiefly in relation to structure: the rules, resources, positions, and institutions that enable action and limit it.

## The Problem

The issue is how to explain conduct without treating people either as free choosers detached from circumstances or as carriers of social forces. Marx's observation in *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852) that people make history, but not in circumstances of their own choosing, is a common point of departure. Durkheim stressed the external and constraining character of social facts, whereas Weber made the subjective meaning of action central to explanation.

## Main Approaches

Anthony Giddens, in *The Constitution of Society* (1984), described a duality of structure: structures are both the medium and the outcome of the practices they organise, and agency refers to the capability to act differently. Pierre Bourdieu's concept of habitus links durable, socially acquired dispositions to practical improvisation. Margaret Archer, in *Realist Social Theory* (1995), objected that merging structure and agency hides their interplay over time, and later examined reflexive internal conversation as the way people mediate structural conditions. William Sewell Jr. (1992) tied agency to the ability to transpose cultural schemas and mobilise resources in new settings. Mustafa Emirbayer and Ann Mische (1998) distinguished three temporal elements: iteration (habit), projectivity (imagining futures), and practical evaluation (judgment in the present).

## Empirical Studies

Paul Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977) showed working-class boys actively rejecting school culture in ways that helped lead them into manual jobs, a case in which agency contributes to reproducing structure. Glen Elder's life-course research, beginning with *Children of the Great Depression* (1974), examined how families and individuals responded to economic hardship, and agency became one of the principles of the life-course perspective. Saba Mahmood's *Politics of Piety* (2005) studied women in Egypt's mosque movement and argued that agency includes the deliberate cultivation of religious norms, not only resistance to them.

## Applications across Disciplines

Psychology, notably Albert Bandura's social cognitive theory, connects agency to intention, self-regulation, and beliefs about one's own efficacy. Anthropology discusses it through practice theory, as in the work of Sherry Ortner. Economics models choice by agents under constraints. Science and technology studies, particularly actor-network theory, attributes agency to nonhuman actors as well, a position that remains disputed.

## Measurement and Critiques

Agency is difficult to observe directly. Researchers infer it from choices, strategies, life histories, and reported sense of control, and these indicators do not measure the same thing: a feeling of control may exist where real options are few. Critics argue that the term is used vaguely for intention, choice, resistance, or any causal effect; that stressing it can understate constraint and shift responsibility onto disadvantaged people; and that some versions assume a liberal, autonomous individual that does not fit all social settings.
