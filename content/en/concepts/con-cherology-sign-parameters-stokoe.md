# Stokoe's Cherology and Sign Parameters

An analytical system formulated by William Stokoe (1960) to describe the sub-morphemic, sensorimotor structure of signs in American Sign Language, in structural parallel with the analysis of phonemes in spoken language. He named it "Cherology" (from the Greek *kheir*, "hand").

## The Three Basic Parameters

Stokoe analyzed each sign into three simultaneous minimal parameters that make up a single sign: "Handshape" (the configuration of the fingers of the hand), "Movement" (the path of the hand's motion through space), and "Location" (the place where the sign is produced relative to the body or face). Later researchers (notably Robbin Battison) treated "Orientation" (the direction of the palm) as a separate fourth parameter.

## The Structural Parallel with the Phoneme

This analysis directly parallels the analysis of the phoneme into distinctive features in spoken language. Just as the phonemes /b/ and /p/ are distinguished by a single minimal sound feature (voicing), two signs in sign language are sometimes distinguished by only one small parameter (such as changing the handshape alone while keeping the movement and location constant). This is decisive evidence that sign language has a real, structurally organized phonological level, not merely free, unconstrained gestures.

## Impact

This analysis founded the field of sign phonology as an independent branch parallel to spoken phonology, and paved the way for applying more advanced theoretical tools later (such as Liddell and Johnson's Movement-Hold model and Brentari's Prosodic Model) to the analysis of the internal structure of signs in sign languages around the world.

## The Written Notation System

Stokoe also developed an original written notation system (known today by his name, Stokoe Notation) for representing these parameters symbolically on paper. This made it possible for the first time to write down American Sign Language signs with structural precision, rather than only rough kinesthetic description. This achievement paved the way for the creation of the first systematic linguistic dictionary of American Sign Language (*A Dictionary of American Sign Language on Linguistic Principles*, 1965, co-authored with Dorothy Casterline and Carl Croneberg).

## Academic Resistance and Later Recognition

Stokoe's thesis was initially met with wide skepticism and rejection (in the late 1950s and early 1960s) from the prevailing linguistic and educational circles, which regarded sign language at the time as merely a primitive system of visual gestures dependent on spoken language, rather than a complete natural language in its own right. Broad academic recognition of his thesis came only gradually during the 1970s and 1980s, as additional linguistic evidence accumulated (from researchers such as Ursula Bellugi and Edward Klima) confirming that sign languages possess complex grammatical and phonological structures fully parallel to spoken languages.
