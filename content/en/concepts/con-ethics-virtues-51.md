# Altruism Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Altruism (*ithar*) is the willing preference of another's need over one's own need, even when one's own need exists. It is higher than mere generosity or almsgiving, because it presupposes that the one who prefers others is himself in need and nonetheless gives precedence to the other.

## Islamic Roots

The concept takes its name from the verse of Surat al-Hashr, "They prefer [others] over themselves, even though they themselves are in need." The verse was revealed concerning the Ansar, who gave their wealth to their brethren, the emigrants, despite their own need. This verse is regarded in the Islamic ethical tradition as a classical model of voluntary sacrifice that is not conditioned on any return. Later scholars generally treated *ithar* as praiseworthy in worldly matters such as money and food, while cautioning that it should not lead to neglecting one's own religious obligations or to forbidden harm, including harm to oneself. Ibn Miskawayh discusses friendship and love at length in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* as part of the perfection of social virtue.

## The Western Context

In Western philosophy, *ithar* is compared with "altruism," a term (French *altruisme*) coined by Auguste Comte in the nineteenth century as the opposite of egoism. A biological and philosophical question arose about whether there can be "pure" altruism that is not driven by some hidden interest, such as the satisfaction of the self. Thomas Hobbes is traditionally read as holding that all voluntary human action is ultimately driven by the agent's own interest (psychological egoism), though this interpretation is debated. Joseph Butler, by contrast, defended the existence of genuine benevolent inclinations in human nature that cannot be reduced to self-interest.

## An Example

A person who gives up his only seat in a lifeboat to another person, despite the danger to his own life, is a standard illustration of extreme self-sacrifice, which religious and philosophical traditions often admire, though philosophers disagree about whether such acts are required or merely supererogatory.

## Altruism Between Nature and Acquisition

Muslim moral scholars discuss a precise question: is altruism a natural inclination or a virtue acquired through upbringing? Many of them treat it as a combination of the two. The inclination to love and compassion is innate in the social human being by nature, but raising it to the level of preferring others despite one's own need requires training and purification of the soul that breaks the dominance of instinctive egoism. Some jurists also warned against altruism that leads to the loss of one's obligatory right to oneself, such as giving up one's food for the day when one is hungry and there is no one in greater need. Praiseworthy altruism is thus governed by the scale of legal priorities, not by unconsidered emotional impulse.

## The Social Impact of Altruism

Some writers argue that communities that cultivate norms of mutual aid, through charitable associations and cooperatives, tend to enjoy greater social cohesion, and they see this as a practical extension of the Qur'anic value of *ithar*. Such empirical claims are debated and vary across contexts.
