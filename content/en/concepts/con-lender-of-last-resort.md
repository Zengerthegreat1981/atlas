# Lender of Last Resort

**The lender of last resort** is the role played by the central bank in supplying emergency liquidity to the banking system during acute financial crises. This occurs when the ordinary sources of funding between banks themselves dry up due to panic and loss of mutual trust. The purpose is to prevent a fundamentally sound bank that is experiencing a temporary liquidity crisis from turning into an actual bankruptcy that threatens the entire financial system.

## Bagehot's Classical Principle

The idea was earlier discussed by Henry Thornton (*An Enquiry into the Nature and Effects of the Paper Credit of Great Britain*, 1802). The financial journalist **Walter Bagehot**, editor of *The Economist*, laid the classic principles of this role in his book *Lombard Street* (1873), formulating a famous rule. The central bank must lend generously in times of crisis, but at a high penalty interest rate and against good collateral (sound assets that would have been accepted as collateral in normal times). Later readers summarize the rule as aiming assistance at illiquid but solvent institutions rather than insolvent ones, although Bagehot's own test was the quality of the collateral offered. The high rate was meant to ensure that banks would not rely on the central bank as a routine source of funds or take excessive risks in expectation of rescue.

## Its Role in Minsky's Financial Instability Hypothesis

**Hyman Minsky** gave this role a central place in his financial instability hypothesis. He held that capitalist economies tend structurally, through successive phases of credit boom, toward growing fragility (speculative finance and then Ponzi finance). This can end in a moment of panic (later dubbed the "Minsky moment"). He argued that central bank intervention as lender of last resort at that moment is necessary to halt the contraction of debt and prevent a comprehensive banking collapse that would turn into a deep depression.

## Tension with Moral Hazard

This role remains a permanent source of tension between its necessity for preventing comprehensive banking crises and the risk of encouraging moral hazard. If large banks become convinced that they will always be rescued ("too big to fail"), they may take greater risks than they would if they faced the real possibility of bankruptcy. This dilemma has not been resolved by a decisive rule, even after the emergency lending facilities of the Federal Reserve and other central banks in 2007-2009 and the reconsideration of banking regulation that followed.

## The International Dimension

The International Monetary Fund is often described as playing a role resembling an international lender of last resort, for example during the Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s. Because no global central bank exists, this raises the same debate about conditionality and moral hazard.
