# Will to Power (Wille zur Macht)

The "will to power" (German: *Wille zur Macht*) is a central concept in the philosophy of Friedrich Nietzsche. It names a basic drive in living beings and in human psychology: not mainly a desire for political domination, but a striving to expand, to overcome resistance, to shape, to create and to evaluate, including mastery over oneself.

## Definition and Texts

The idea first appears in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1883-85), notably in the chapter "On Self-Overcoming", and is developed in *Beyond Good and Evil* (*Jenseits von Gut und Böse*, 1886), *On the Genealogy of Morals* (*Zur Genealogie der Moral*, 1887) and *Twilight of the Idols* (*Götzen-Dämmerung*, written 1888, published 1889). In *Beyond Good and Evil* (section 36) Nietzsche offers a thought experiment: if all organic functions could be traced back to a single basic will, then the world seen from within would be "will to power" and nothing else. He presents it there as a hypothesis to be tried out. Life itself, he says elsewhere, is to be understood as will to power, and self-preservation is only one of its indirect consequences (*The Gay Science*, section 349).

## The Posthumous Compilation

A book titled *Der Wille zur Macht* was assembled from Nietzsche's notebooks after his death, by his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche and Peter Gast (Heinrich Köselitz), and published in 1901 and in an enlarged edition in 1906. Nietzsche himself did not publish it as a book. The critical edition of Giorgio Colli and Mazzino Montinari showed that the arrangement is not Nietzsche's, so scholars now treat his notebook material (the Nachlass) with caution and give priority to his published works.

## Difference from the Darwinian "Struggle for Survival"

- Darwin's theory is often summarized as the survival of the fittest through the struggle for existence.
- Nietzsche objected that the striving of living things is not mainly for bare survival: they seek to discharge and increase their strength, and the strong are not necessarily the ones who survive.
- He is thus critical of Darwin and of Herbert Spencer's evolutionary ethics.

## Philosophical Significance

### 1. Metaphysics or psychology?

Interpreters disagree about the status of the doctrine. Martin Heidegger, in his lectures on Nietzsche (delivered 1936-40, published 1961), read it as the culmination of Western metaphysics. Walter Kaufmann (*Nietzsche: Philosopher, Psychologist, Antichrist*, 1950) stressed its psychological side, and Alexander Nehamas (*Nietzsche: Life as Literature*, 1985) questioned whether Nietzsche offered it as a literal metaphysical thesis at all.

### 2. Ethics

- **Master morality** (Herrenmoral) and **slave morality** (Sklavenmoral): in *Beyond Good and Evil* (section 260) and the *Genealogy*, Nietzsche contrasts a morality that starts from the self-affirmation of the noble with one that arises from the resentment (ressentiment) of the weak and defines "good" as the opposite of what threatens them. He associates the latter with Judaeo-Christian morality, a view that is controversial.
- **The Overman** (Übermensch): introduced in *Zarathustra* as a figure who creates new values. It is not simply a corollary of the will to power, though the two are often linked.

### 3. Politics and reception

- In the twentieth century his writings were appropriated by Fascists and Nazis, helped by the editorial activity of his sister, although Nietzsche criticized German nationalism and anti-Semitism and the herd mentality.
- Gilles Deleuze (*Nietzsche and Philosophy*, 1962) read the will to power as a play of differential forces, and Michel Foucault drew on Nietzsche's genealogy in his analyses of power and knowledge, though Foucault's account of power is his own.

## Critique of the Will to Power

- **Schopenhauer**: the will (Wille) is a blind and irrational striving that ought to be denied (the will to live). Nietzsche was at first deeply influenced by him and later rejected his pessimism, turning the will toward affirmation and self-overcoming.
- **Heidegger**: sees in it the final form of the modern will to will, a critique of Nietzsche as the last metaphysician.
- **Critics of the empirical status**: if it is meant as a universal explanation of all behaviour, it risks being unfalsifiable.
