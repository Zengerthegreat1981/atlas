# Mortality

Mortality is the human condition of being finite: each of us will die, at a time we do not know, and the knowledge shapes how we live. In existential philosophy and psychotherapy it is treated less as a topic to be understood than as a condition to be lived. Awareness of death moves from an abstract background fact into conscious life, and how a person answers it is closely bound up with authenticity.

## Definition

In everyday speech "mortality" simply means being subject to death. The existential tradition gives it a sharper sense: not death as a future event that happens to the body, but the awareness, present throughout life, that our time is limited and that every choice is made against that limit. It is distinct from fear of dying as a medical event; it concerns what finitude means for how a life is spent.

## Origins

Reflection on death is ancient. Philosophers from Plato ("philosophy as preparation for death") to Epicurus (who argued death is nothing to us) and the Stoics made it a central theme. Kierkegaard linked the confrontation with finitude to anxiety and to the "sickness unto death" of despair. Tolstoy's novella *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* (1886) remains a classic literary portrait of a life revealed as inauthentic by approaching death. In the twentieth century Heidegger gave the theme its most influential philosophical form in *Being and Time* (1927).

## Being-Toward-Death

Heidegger described human existence (Dasein) as "being-toward-death" (*Sein-zum-Tode*). Death, for him, is not merely the last event but a constant horizon and a possibility that is always one's own and cannot be handed to anyone else. Authentic existence faces this possibility rather than covering it over with the anonymous talk of "one dies." Taken in this way, the awareness of death is not morbid: it clarifies what matters and distinguishes it from mere distraction.

## Denial and Authenticity

Much inauthentic living can be read as a way of denying mortality. We speak as though we had unlimited time, postpone what matters, and treat life as a rehearsal. Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death* (1973) argued that many human projects are, at root, defenses against death-awareness; later "terror management" research in social psychology (Solomon, Greenberg and Pyszczynski) developed this idea experimentally, though its findings and interpretations remain debated.

## In Practice

Irvin Yalom made death one of the four "ultimate concerns" of existential psychotherapy, alongside freedom, isolation and meaninglessness (*Existential Psychotherapy*, 1980). In *Staring at the Sun* (2008) he describes "awakening experiences", such as a bereavement, a serious illness or a milestone birthday, in which a person's awareness of death becomes felt rather than merely intellectual. Such moments can prompt a reordering of priorities, more honest relationships and clearer values. Existential therapists typically do not force this confrontation; they make room for it when it arises and help clients ask what they would do differently if they fully accepted limited time. It is also used in work with people facing serious illness or bereavement, where it is approached gently and at the person's own pace.

## Criticisms and Limits

Sartre objected that death is not "my ownmost possibility" but an absurd event that removes all possibility, so it cannot be the source of meaning Heidegger suggests. Later thinkers, including Levinas and Hannah Arendt, argued that the emphasis on one's own death neglects the death of others and the fact of birth ("natality"). Clinically, death awareness can be overwhelming, and for people with severe anxiety or recent trauma it calls for caution and appropriate professional support rather than deliberate exposure. Finally, claims about the transformative effect of death awareness rest largely on clinical observation and case accounts rather than on controlled evidence.
