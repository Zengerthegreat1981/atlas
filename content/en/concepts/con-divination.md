# Divination

Divination is the practice of seeking knowledge of hidden things — the future, the causes of misfortune, the guilt or innocence of a suspect, the right course of action — by interpreting signs, omens, or the outcome of a random procedure. It takes many forms: casting lots, reading the entrails of animals, throwing bones or shells, interpreting dreams, watching the flight of birds, consulting the I Ching, or reading tarot cards. What unites these practices is the belief that the world contains messages that can be read by those with the right technique, and that uncertainty can be resolved by appealing to a source of knowledge beyond ordinary observation. Divination is distinct from an oracle, which is a particular institutional form: a specific shrine, priesthood, or medium through which divine answers are given. Divination is the broader practice; oracles are one of its settings.

## Origins and Development

The anthropological study of divination began with E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande*, published in 1937. Evans-Pritchard showed that Zande divination was not irrational superstition but a rational system: the Azande consulted the poison oracle — feeding poison to a chicken and reading whether it lived or died — to answer questions about witchcraft, illness, and the future, and they had elaborate rules for checking, cross-checking, and explaining away contradictory results. Divination, he argued, was a way of making decisions under uncertainty, and its logic was internally consistent even if its premises differed from those of Western science.

Later work extended this insight. Victor Turner, in *Ndembu Divination: Its Symbolism and Techniques* (1961), studied divination among the Ndembu of Zambia, where diviners used baskets of objects — each symbolising an ancestor, a spirit, or a social relationship — to diagnose illness and misfortune. The diviner's task was not only to reveal a hidden cause but to bring the patient's social conflicts into the open, so that divination became a form of therapy and social mediation. Philip Peek's edited volume *African Divination Systems* (1991) documented the variety of African practices — Ifa among the Yoruba, bone-throwing in Southern Africa, and many others — and argued that divination is a mode of knowing, a way of thinking about causality, responsibility, and the future that deserves to be studied on its own terms rather than as a failed science.

The comparative study of divination has also drawn on history. In ancient China, oracle bones were used to consult ancestors; in ancient Greece, oracles at Delphi gave answers to cities and individuals; in Rome, augurs read the flight of birds before public decisions. These cases show that divination has been practised at the highest levels of state power as well as in village life.

## Key Debates

One central debate concerns rationality. Early writers, following Frazer and Tylor, treated divination as a primitive error, a confusion of correlation with causation. Evans-Pritchard's work challenged this view, showing that diviners and their clients reason carefully within their own premises. The debate now turns on whether rationality should be judged by the truth of the premises or by the coherence of the reasoning, and on whether divination should be compared with science at all. Some anthropologists argue that divination is better understood as a practice of decision-making than as a theory of the world: it does not claim to predict the future in the way a weather forecast does, but to help people act in the face of uncertainty.

A second debate concerns the social function of divination. Functionalist accounts stress that divination resolves conflicts, allocates blame, and legitimates decisions: by attributing a misfortune to witchcraft or an ancestor, the diviner gives the community a course of action. Critics argue that this reduces divination to its effects and ignores what it means to its practitioners — the experience of being in contact with the sacred and the artistry of interpretation. Recent work combines both perspectives, treating divination as simultaneously a social institution and a lived experience.

A third debate concerns the relationship between divination and power. Diviners can be powerful figures, consulted by kings and commoners alike, and their answers can make or break reputations, marriages, and political careers. But their power is also fragile: a diviner whose predictions fail loses clients, and rulers have often sought to control divination, licensing some practitioners and suppressing others. The debate asks how divinatory authority is made, maintained, and challenged, and what happens when it competes with other sources of knowledge, such as medicine, law, or science.

## Contemporary Relevance

Divination remains widespread. Astrology columns, tarot readings, and fortune-telling apps are a global industry, and in many parts of Africa, Asia, and Latin America diviners continue to be consulted about health, business, and family matters. Anthropologists argue that its persistence is not a survival of the past but a response to the present: in a world of economic insecurity, illness, and political uncertainty, divination offers a way of making sense of misfortune and deciding what to do next. Its study illuminates a broader question about knowledge itself — how people know what they know, whom they trust, and what counts as evidence — and it challenges the assumption that modernity has replaced divination with science. In practice, the two coexist, and many people move between them without seeing any contradiction.
