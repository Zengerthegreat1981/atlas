# Financial Instability Hypothesis

The **financial instability hypothesis** is an economic theory developed by the American economist **Hyman Minsky** from the mid-1970s (first set out in *John Maynard Keynes*, 1975), reaching its most mature formulation in his book *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986). It offers an alternative explanation for the origin of financial crises: they do not result from accidental external shocks striking a financial system that is stable by nature, but arise from the internal dynamics of how financial capitalism operates over time.

## Three escalating stages of financing

Minsky classifies the financing structure of economic units (firms and households) into three categories of increasing danger. **Hedge finance** is where expected cash flows are sufficient to repay both the principal and the interest of the debt safely. **Speculative finance** is where cash flows are sufficient only to pay the interest, while the principal is continually refinanced through new borrowing. **Ponzi finance** is where cash flows are not even sufficient to pay the interest, and borrowers depend entirely on the continued rise in the value of their assets (such as property) to cover their accumulating obligations.

## Stability is destabilizing

Minsky formulates the central principle of this hypothesis: long periods of economic stability and calm growth generate, by themselves, a rising financial fragility. As the apparent calm lengthens, borrowers and lenders gradually come to accept higher levels of risk and leverage. This pushes the overall financing structure of the economy to slide gradually from safe hedge finance toward speculative finance and then toward Ponzi finance, until the system becomes fragile enough to collapse from a relatively simple shock that exposes the hidden accumulated fragility.

## A necessary role for public policy

From this analysis Minsky concludes that an active and continuous role is needed, not only intermittent during crises, for both the central bank as the "lender of last resort" that halts the sequence of collapse, and for what he called "Big Government" and "Big Bank" (a large government budget that stabilises profits and a central bank acting as lender of last resort), together with careful ongoing government regulation that limits the accumulation of excessive leverage before a crisis occurs at all. He argues that the stability of financial capitalism requires a permanent institutional structure, not merely periodic management of aggregate demand in the simple traditional Keynesian sense, in which the role of the financial sector was neglected.

## Revival after the 2008 crisis

For decades after its formulation this hypothesis remained relatively marginal in mainstream academic economics, which was dominated by the assumption that financial markets are efficient. The global financial crisis of 2008, which arose from a mortgage bubble driven by credit expansion and rising financial fragility over several years, appeared to be an almost literal embodiment of Minsky's scenario. This revived broad academic and media interest in the hypothesis, and the term "Minsky moment", coined by the investment manager Paul McCulley in 1998 (in the context of the Russian crisis), came into wide use to describe the sudden collapse of a financial system that had appeared stable for a long time. Minsky himself, who died in 1996, did not use the phrase.
