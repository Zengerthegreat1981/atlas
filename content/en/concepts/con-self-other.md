# Self and Other

The fundamental existential reality is that I am a subject and the other is also a subject, not an object. Martin Buber called this I-Thou relating, as opposed to I-It. Yet in everyday life we habitually treat others as objects, as means to our ends, and defend against the vulnerability of genuine otherness. The relation between self and other is among the oldest problems in philosophy and a daily concern of psychotherapy.

## Definition

"Self and other" names the double fact that a person exists as a distinct center of experience and that this existence is bound up with other centers of experience. Others are the source of recognition, love, conflict and meaning, but they are never fully accessible: I cannot live their experience from within. Existential thought treats this as a permanent tension to be lived with rather than solved.

## Origins

The theme runs through several traditions. Hegel's account of the struggle for recognition in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) argued that self-consciousness depends on being recognized by another. Husserl explored how other subjects are given to us in experience. Buber's *I and Thou* (1923) described two basic attitudes, I-It and I-Thou, the latter a meeting in which the other is addressed as a whole being. Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) described "the look": being seen by another turns me into an object in their world, which is why others can be experienced as threatening. In psychiatry, R. D. Laing's *The Divided Self* (1960) and *Self and Others* (1961) drew on these ideas to describe how people protect themselves against the danger of being engulfed or exposed by others.

## The Other as Radically Other

Emmanuel Levinas (*Totality and Infinity*, 1961; English 1969) emphasized that the Other cannot be assimilated to my categories or brought under my control. However well I know someone, there is an irreducible alterity: they are not fully mine to understand or possess. This is both a loss, since we remain separate, and the basis of ethics, since the other's face makes a claim on me that I cannot reduce to my own purposes. For Levinas ethics, the responsibility for the other, comes before knowledge.

## In Practice

In therapy the self-other distinction is crucial. Many people suffer from enmeshment, losing the boundary between self and other, while others suffer from isolation, treating other people as entirely separate and unreachable. Family-systems thinkers such as Murray Bowen described a related balance in terms of "differentiation of self," the capacity to remain connected to others while holding one's own position. Existential therapists such as Yalom and May see isolation, both from others and from oneself, as one of the basic givens of existence, and treat the therapeutic relationship itself as a place where genuine encounter can be practiced. Buber's idea of dialogue influenced humanistic therapy, and Buber and Carl Rogers held a well-known public dialogue in 1957 on the limits and possibilities of the helping relationship. Authentic relating requires maintaining both connection and separateness.

## Criticisms and Limits

Some critics find Levinas's ethics overly asymmetrical, since it seems to demand unlimited responsibility without reciprocity, and ask how it can guide ordinary justice between equals. Buber's sharp contrast between I-Thou and I-It has been challenged as unrealistic, because everyday life necessarily involves instrumental relations and I-Thou moments are rare and cannot be willed. Feminist and relational thinkers have argued that starting from a separate self may misdescribe human development, in which connection comes first. Clinically, the abstract vocabulary of subject and object needs translation into concrete work, and different cultures place the balance between independence and interdependence differently, so any one ideal of "healthy separateness" should be held cautiously.
