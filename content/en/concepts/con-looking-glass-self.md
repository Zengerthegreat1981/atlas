# Looking-Glass Self (Cooley)

A concept formulated by Charles Horton Cooley to describe the mechanism by which an individual's perception of his social self is formed, through an imaginative interactive process with others and not through isolated individual introspection.

## Content

In *Human Nature and the Social Order* (1902), Cooley formulated the idea that the "self" is not an inner individual given prior to society, but is formed through a three-step interactive process he calls the "looking-glass self." (1) The individual imagines how his appearance and behavior look in the eyes of the other. (2) He imagines the judgment that other passes on what he sees. (3) He forms a subjective feeling, pride, shame or embarrassment, in response to this imagined judgment, and not necessarily to the actual judgment (the individual's imagining of the other's judgment may be entirely wrong, yet it is what determines his actual feeling). The central metaphor: others act as a "mirror" in which the individual sees a reflection of himself, not a neutral objective reflection but one filtered through his own imagining of their evaluation.

## Impact

This concept formed a direct basis on which George Herbert Mead later built his more detailed and abstract theory of the emergence of the self through the process of "taking the role of the other" and the formation of the "Generalized Other," and it thereby became one of the direct theoretical foundations of Blumer's symbolic interactionism. The concept remains an analytical tool in use today in social psychology and the sociology of the self and identity.

## Cooley's Intellectual Context

Charles Horton Cooley, the American sociologist (1864–1929) at the University of Michigan, where he spent his entire academic career, developed this concept in the context of his critique of the excessive individualism prevailing in American social thought in the late nineteenth century, which treated the "individual" and "society" as two separate entities interacting from outside. Cooley saw the individual and society as "twins" that cannot be separated, since there is no "individual" already formed prior to social interaction; rather the self itself is a product of this interaction from the moment of birth, beginning with the child's relationship with the members of his immediate family and what Cooley calls "Primary Groups," such as the family and the children's play group and the neighbourhood. The term "primary group" was coined by Cooley (in *Social Organization*, 1909), and it became a firmly established basic concept in American sociology and social psychology alike, influencing the understanding of how an individual's personality is formed through the nearest circles of his direct social relationships before they later widen to the broader society.
