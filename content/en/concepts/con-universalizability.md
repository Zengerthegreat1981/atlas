# Universalizability

## Meaning and Definition

Universalizability is the criterion that Kant proposes for testing whether an act is morally permissible. The agent asks themselves whether they can will the rule on which their act is based (the maxim) to become a universal law binding on all rational beings without contradiction. If generalizing the rule leads to a contradiction, the act is impermissible. For example, the rule "lie whenever it benefits you," if generalized, would cause the very concepts of promise and trust to collapse. This concept is organically linked to the school of Kantian deontological ethics, of which it is the first and best-known formulation of the "categorical imperative" (the formula of universal law).

## An Approach in Islamic Thought

This principle finds a moral counterpart, though with a different logical instrument, in the prophetic hadith, "None of you truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself." It presupposes a similar imaginative test: place yourself in the position of the other, and do not do to others what you would not accept for yourself. Jurists, in the chapter on analogy (*qiyas*) in the principles of jurisprudence, worked with a loosely comparable idea: that a ruling extends to every case in which the same effective cause ('illa) is present, without arbitrary discrimination. This is a distant parallel to Kant's requirement of consistency, although the essential difference remains that for the jurists the source of obligation is the Shari'a, not autonomous reason.

## Philosophical Context

Kant formulated this criterion in *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) as a purely formal test, independent of any empirical content or particular ends. He distinguished two kinds of contradiction: a contradiction in conception (such as a false promise, which would nullify the very concept of promising if generalized) and a contradiction in will (such as refusing to help others, which involves no contradiction in conception if generalized, but which no rational agent can will as a universal law, since they would someday need others' help). Hegel, in the *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (1821) and earlier writings, offered an influential critique, charging the criterion with empty formalism: it presupposes an institution such as property rather than establishing it, and its verdicts can depend on how the maxim is described. On this view, consistency alone is insufficient to determine the actual moral content. Defenders of Kant reply that the test is meant to filter maxims, not generate duties, and that it must be read together with his other formulations.

## Illustrative Example

The rule "take what does not belong to you whenever you can escape punishment" fails the generalization test, because if it became a universal law the concept of ownership on which the rule itself depends for its meaning would collapse.

## Relation to the Golden Rule

Kant's universalizability test is often compared with the "Golden Rule" shared by many religions and cultures ("treat people as you would like them to treat you"). Kant, however, in a footnote to the *Groundwork*, rejected the Golden Rule as a universal law, since it makes duty depend on the agent's own inclinations. He observed, for example, that on its basis a convicted criminal could argue against the judge who sentences him. The universalizability test, by contrast, is meant to rest on the consistency of rational willing independent of individual desire, and it therefore stands, in Kant's view, as a more objective criterion.
