# Universal vs Particular

The tension between the universal and the particular is one of the oldest problems in anthropology: how can human beings be alike in some ways and different in others, and how should the discipline hold both truths at once? The universal refers to what is shared by all humanity — capacities, needs, and patterns found in every society. The particular refers to what is specific to one culture, place, or historical moment — the beliefs, practices, and meanings that make a society distinct. Anthropology has always moved between these poles, seeking general truths about human nature while insisting on the irreducible value of cultural difference. The balance between them has shifted repeatedly, and each shift has remade the discipline.

## Definition

Universal claims take many forms. Some concern human nature: all humans have language, kinship, and some form of religion, politics, and morality. Others concern capacities: all humans can learn any language, reason, and create meaning. Still others concern values: the claim that all humans possess rights or dignity simply by virtue of being human. Particular claims concern the forms these universals take. Language is universal, but each language carves up experience differently. Kinship is universal, but kinship systems vary enormously in who counts as a relative and what relatives owe each other. Morality is universal, but moral codes differ in what they permit and forbid. Franz Boas, in *The Mind of Primitive Man* (1911) and later work, argued that each culture must be understood in its own historical terms before general claims are made, and that premature generalisation produces false universals — the projection of one culture's categories onto all humanity. The universal and the particular are therefore not rivals but partners: universals are empty without particular content, and particulars are unintelligible without some comparative frame.

## Origins and Development

The universal-particular tension shaped anthropology from its beginnings. Nineteenth-century evolutionists sought universal stages through which all societies passed, treating cultural differences as differences of development. Boas and his students reversed this programme, insisting on the historical particularity of each culture and the dangers of ranking societies. In the mid-twentieth century, the debate took the form of the relativism controversy. Melville Herskovits, in *Man and His Works* (1948) and in essays collected posthumously in 1972, argued that moral judgements can only be made within the framework of a particular culture, and that no universal standard exists by which cultures can be ranked. In "Anti Anti-Relativism" (1984), Clifford Geertz declined to champion relativism as a doctrine but attacked its opponents, arguing that the fear of relativism was misplaced and was encouraging a return to simplified universal accounts of human nature and the human mind. The debate continued as anthropologists confronted globalisation, human rights, and the claims of universal science against local knowledge.

## Key Debates

The most consequential debate concerns human rights. Are rights universal, applying to all people regardless of culture, or do they impose Western particulars under the guise of universality? Feminist anthropologists have asked whether universal claims about women's oppression erase the particular ways women experience and resist it. A second debate concerns comparison itself. If every culture is unique, how can anthropologists compare them at all? Marshall Sahlins, in "Goodbye to Tristes Tropes" (1993), insisted that peoples meet world-historical forces through their own cultural categories, so that general processes are always realised in particular local forms. A third debate concerns science. Are the findings of cognitive science and evolutionary psychology universal truths about the human mind, or are they artefacts of studying particular populations — often Western university students — and mistaking them for humanity? Michael Herzfeld argued in 2001 that anthropology's distinctive contribution is to hold the universal and the particular together, using ethnographic detail to test and complicate general theory.

## Contemporary Relevance

The universal-particular tension is more urgent than ever. Global challenges — climate change, pandemics, migration — demand universal responses, yet they are experienced in particular ways shaped by history, inequality, and culture. Debates about cultural appropriation, religious freedom, and the rights of Indigenous peoples all turn on where the line between universal principle and particular practice should be drawn. In global health, the question is whether medical interventions can be standardised across contexts or must be adapted to local understandings of the body and illness. In development, it is whether universal models of progress serve the particular communities they claim to help. Anthropology offers no final resolution to this tension, and that is its strength: by insisting on both the unity of humanity and the diversity of cultures, it refuses both the false universalism that erases difference and the false particularism that denies common ground.
