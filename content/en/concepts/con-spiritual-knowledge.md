# Spiritual Knowledge in Sufism

The Sufis call the knowledge of God *ma'rifa* (gnosis), distinguishing it from *'ilm* (knowledge), which is knowledge through proof and reasoned inquiry. Al-Junayd made gnosis a state through which secrets become present. Dhu al-Nun al-Misri (d. 245 AH/860 CE), to whom the first organized speech on the subject is attributed, held that gnosis has three kinds: the gnosis of unity for the common people, the gnosis of proof and exposition for the scholars, and the gnosis of the attributes of unity for the saints (*awliya'*), who see God with their hearts.

## Taste, Unveiling and Insight

For the Sufis, *dhawq* (taste) is the direct experience of meaning. *Kashf* (unveiling) is the lifting of the veil from what lies beyond the senses. *Firasa* (perspicacity) is an intuition that God casts into the heart. al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi (d. c. 295 AH/908 CE) discussed sainthood (*wilaya*) and the saint's God-given knowledge in *Khatm al-Awliya'* (also known as *Sirat al-Awliya'*), distinguishing it from the knowledge of the prophets. Uthman Yahya edited it in 1965. Gerhard Böwering, in *The Mystical Vision of Existence in Classical Islam* (1980), studied the Qur'anic interpretation and mystical thought of Sahl al-Tustari (d. 283 AH/896 CE).

## Al-Ghazali: Inspired Knowledge

Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH/1111 CE), in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* and *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal*, distinguished between knowledge acquired by instruction and *'ilm ladunni* (inspired knowledge), which falls into the heart without acquisition, as a light that God casts. He based this on the Quranic verse "and We taught him knowledge from Our presence" (Al-Kahf 18:65). In *Mishkat al-Anwar* (The Niche of Lights) he made true knowledge the knowledge that true existence is one, and he arranged the lights in order. David Buchman translated the work as *The Niche of Lights* (1998).

## Ibn Arabi: Imagination and Manifestation

This line of thought reached its peak with Ibn 'Arabi (d. 638 AH/1240 CE), who placed unveiling above reason, considered reason limited by its own confinement, and held that the heart turns with the manifestation of the Real in forms without end. William Chittick devoted *The Sufi Path of Knowledge* (1989) to explaining this theory through the texts of Ibn 'Arabi.

## Legal Control

The Sufis did not accept unveiling without a criterion. A saying attributed to al-Junayd holds that this knowledge is bound by the Book and the Sunna. Al-Qushayri required that whatever arrives be presented to the Sharia, and the sources warn against deception (*istidraj*) and satanic inspiration. The meaning is that, for them, unveiling does not establish a new legal ruling.

## Limits of Mystical Knowledge

Jurists object that it has no criterion by which to distinguish among those who claim it, and philosophers object that it lacks a transmissible proof. Ibn Sina sought to justify it rationally in *al-Isharat wa-al-Tanbihat*. Some contemporary scholars compare this knowledge with mystical experience in other traditions, and they disagree over whether the experience is one even when its languages differ.
