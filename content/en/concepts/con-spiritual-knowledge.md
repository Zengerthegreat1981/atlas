# Spiritual Knowledge in Sufism

The Sufis call direct knowledge of God *ma'rifa* (gnosis) and distinguish it from *'ilm*, knowledge argued from proof. Al-Junayd (d. 298 AH/910 CE) treated ma'rifa as a realized state, not as information stored from study. Dhu al-Nun al-Misri (d. 245 AH/860 CE), to whom later Sufi writers attribute the first sustained discourse on ma'rifa, is reported to have distinguished three kinds: the gnosis of unity among ordinary believers, the gnosis of proof and exposition among the elect, and the gnosis of the attributes of unity among the saints (*awliya'*), who see God with their hearts.

## Taste, Unveiling and Insight

For the Sufis, *dhawq* (taste) is the direct experience of meaning. *Kashf* (unveiling) is the lifting of the veil from what lies beyond the senses. *Firasa* (perspicacity) is an intuition that God casts into the heart. al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi (his death is not securely dated; reports place it from the late third century AH into the early fourth) discussed sainthood (*wilaya*) and the saint's God-given knowledge in *Khatm al-Awliya'* (also known as *Sirat al-Awliya'*), and he distinguished it from the knowledge of the prophets. Uthman Yahya edited the work in 1965. Gerhard Böwering, in *The Mystical Vision of Existence in Classical Islam* (1980), studied the Qur'anic interpretation of Sahl al-Tustari (d. 283 AH/896 CE).

## Al-Ghazali: Inspired Knowledge

Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH/1111 CE), in the *Ihya' ulum al-din*, distinguishes knowledge acquired by learning from a knowledge that God casts into the heart, and he connects the latter with the Quranic words "We taught him knowledge from Our presence" (18:65), later called *'ilm ladunni*. In *al-Munqidh min al-dalal* he describes such a light as the turning point of his own path. In *Mishkat al-Anwar* (The Niche of Lights) he ranks lights and treats God as the true light from which other lights derive. Readers have disagreed about how far the metaphysical portion of the book goes. David Buchman translated it as *The Niche of Lights* (1998).

## Ibn Arabi: Imagination and Manifestation

Ibn 'Arabi (d. 638 AH/1240 CE) ranked unveiling above discursive reason in the knowledge of God, held that reason is bounded by its own premises, and described the heart as turning with each manifestation of the Real. William Chittick's *The Sufi Path of Knowledge* (1989) expounds this account from Ibn 'Arabi's own texts.

## Legal Control

The Sufis did not accept unveiling without a criterion. A saying attributed to al-Junayd holds that this knowledge is bound by the Book and the Sunna. Al-Qushayri required that whatever arrives be presented to the Sharia, and the sources warn against deception (*istidraj*) and satanic inspiration. The meaning is that, for them, unveiling does not establish a new legal ruling.

## Limits of Mystical Knowledge

Jurists object that it offers no public test for distinguishing those who claim it, and philosophers object that it lacks a proof that can be transmitted. Ibn Sina gave a philosophical account of the knower and the stages of the path in the later sections of *al-Isharat wa-al-tanbihat*. Some contemporary scholars compare this knowledge with mystical experience in other traditions, and they disagree over whether the experience is one when the languages differ.
