# Conversational Implicature

## Definition

Conversational implicature is a meaning that the speaker implies implicitly without stating it explicitly, which the hearer infers on the basis of the assumption that the speaker is committed to the cooperative principle and its four conversational maxims as formulated by Paul Grice (1975): quantity, quality, relation, and manner. Implicatures arise either when a speaker apparently flouts a maxim (and the hearer, assuming the speaker is still cooperating, looks for an additional meaning that restores consistency) or when the hearer reasons from the assumption that the maxims are being observed. This additional meaning is the conversational implicature.

## The Mechanism of Derivation

For instance, a speaker who says "some of the students passed" is assumed to be observing the maxim of quantity (being as informative as required), so the hearer infers that the speaker is not in a position to say more; the utterance implicates that not all the students passed, since otherwise the speaker would have said "all of them." An answer that appears off-topic (a violation of the maxim of relation) implicates a hidden meaning that connects it to the question. Saying "she got married and had a baby" implicates, under the maxim of manner ("be orderly"), that the events occurred in that order.

## Scalar Implicature

Among the best-known types of generalized conversational implicature is the **scalar implicature**, based on semantic scales ordered by strength (some < all, possible < necessary, or < and). Using the weaker item on the scale ("some") implicates the denial of the stronger item ("all"), because if the speaker had meant "all," they would have used it directly in compliance with the maxim of quantity.

## Generalized and Particularized Implicature

Grice distinguishes between generalized conversational implicature, which arises by default in ordinary contexts without depending on special features of the situation (such as scalar implicature), and particularized conversational implicature, which depends entirely on the particularities of the specific communicative situation and does not recur in the same pattern in other contexts.

## The Cancellability Test

The decisive property that distinguishes conversational implicature from logical entailment is its **cancellability**: the speaker can add "in fact, all of them passed" without logical contradiction after saying "some of the students passed," because the implicature is a defeasible inference that can be withdrawn, unlike a logical entailment, whose negation would constitute an explicit contradiction.

## Impact

The framework was developed further along two lines. Neo-Gricean pragmatics reduces the maxims to fewer principles: Laurence Horn's Q and R principles (Horn 1984), and Stephen Levinson's Q, I, and M principles, set out fully in *Presumptive Meanings* (2000). Relevance theory (Sperber and Wilson 1986; 2nd ed. 1995) replaces the four maxims with a communicative principle of relevance, grounded in a cognitive principle: an interpretation is relevant to the extent that it yields cognitive effects for the processing effort it requires.
