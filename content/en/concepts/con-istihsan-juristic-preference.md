# Istihsan (Juristic Preference)

**Istihsan** is a usuli source of evidence relied on primarily by the Hanafi school, and to a lesser degree by the Malikis. It is defined roughly as **departing from the requirement of an apparent analogy in favor of a different ruling, on the basis of a stronger proof** (a text, a consensus, a necessity, or a prevailing public interest) that calls for this departure. In other words, it is the exception of a particular issue from a general rule, or from a universal analogy, on the basis of a stronger specific proof.

## The Dispute over Its Nature: An Independent Source or Not?

- **The Hanafis** regarded it as an independent and valid source of juristic reasoning, and set out controls for it (istihsan based on a text, on consensus, on necessity, on custom, and on hidden analogy).
- **Al-Shafi'i** is reported to have rejected it in strong terms, in a saying attributed to him that whoever practices istihsan has legislated for himself. **Al-Ghazali** (in *al-Mustasfa*) likewise rejected istihsan as an independent source of law, criticizing what he saw as discretion without methodological controls, and argued that those examples of it that are sound can be justified by other recognized proofs.

## Its Importance in Comparative Legal Theory

Istihsan is sometimes compared, in a careful analytical manner rather than as a literal equivalence, with the concept of **equity** in Western common law traditions. Both are mechanisms for correcting the rigidity of a general rule when its literal application would produce a result contrary to the purpose of the legal system itself. The essential difference is that istihsan remains internal to the system of juristic reasoning and does not presuppose a dual court system (as the courts of equity historically arose in England).

## The Hanafi and Maliki Elaboration

Hanafi jurists answered such criticism by arguing that istihsan is not arbitrary inclination without controls. It is in reality the preference of a stronger proof, such as a hidden analogy (*qiyas khafi*) over a weaker evident analogy, or a text, consensus or necessity over analogy. **Shams al-A'imma al-Sarakhsi** (d. c. 483 AH / 1090 CE), in his *Usul*, presents istihsan in this way and treats it as a form of reasoning grounded in proof, not whim. The Maliki school, for its part, applies istihsan largely as the preference of a particular interest or custom over a general analogy, and is often described as having made wider use of it.

## The Completion of the Framework in al-Shatibi: Interest and Objectives

**Abu Ishaq al-Shatibi** (d. 790 AH / 1388 CE), a Maliki jurist, discussed istihsan in *al-Muwafaqat* within his theory of the **objectives of Sharia**. In his account, istihsan expresses regard for the overarching interest of Sharia (the preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny and property) when the application of literal analogy would produce a result that contradicts these highest objectives, the very objectives for which the particular rulings were enacted to serve. On this view istihsan is not an obscure exception but an application of weighing the particular against the universal within an objectives-based system. This line of thought is closely related to the Maliki doctrine of **public interest** (*al-masalih al-mursala*), which is treated in a separate entry.

## An Applied Example

A classic juristic example of istihsan is the following. The apparent analogy holds that a contract of *istisna'* (ordering a good to be manufactured, such as ordering a shoe not yet made at the time of the contract) is invalid, because selling a non-existent item is void by analogy with the prohibition of selling what does not exist or is uncertain (*gharar*). The Hanafis, however, held it permissible on the basis of istihsan, given the general and recurring need for this type of commercial dealing and the people's customary practice of it without objection. They therefore preferred the proof of istihsan (necessity and custom) over the apparent analogy.

## Comparing the Hanafi and Maliki definitions

Muhammad Abu Zahra's survey of Maliki method, drawing on al-Shatibi, al-Qarafi and Ibn al-Arabi, treats the two schools as agreeing on the result and differing on the route. For the Malikis, istihsan checks an analogy that has been pressed too far by appealing to prevailing custom, a predominant benefit, or the removal of hardship and pressing necessity. For the Hanafis, the usual case is a clash of two analogies: an apparent analogy whose effect is weak, and a hidden analogy whose effect is stronger. The stronger hidden analogy is called istihsan. Both schools also set analogy aside for necessity and custom. They part company on labels and on what counts as a ground. Hanafi writers fold the preference of a text, including a solitary report, or of consensus over analogy into the secondary kinds of istihsan. Maliki writers generally do not call that move istihsan. They do, unlike the Hanafis in Abu Zahra's account, set a particular benefit against a general analogy. Ibn al-Arabi's shorter formula brings the schools together: istihsan, for Malikis and Hanafis, is acting on the stronger of two proofs. Al-Shatibi then marks istihsan off from unrestricted public interest: the former is an exception to a rule that already applies, while masalih mursala is used where no other proof covers the case.
