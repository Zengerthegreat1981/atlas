# Labour Process Theory and Deskilling

Adam Smith, in **The Division of Labour**, describes the breakdown of the production process into specialized tasks as a source of efficiency and productivity growth, citing his famous example of the pin factory, where output multiplies many times over when the work is divided among ten specialized workers rather than each worker producing a complete pin alone. This economic-technical description appears neutral in principle, since it is presented as a purely engineering benefit. But Harry Braverman, a Marxist writer and former metalworker, re-read this same process in *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974), from the angle of power relations within the workplace rather than from engineering efficiency alone.

## Braverman's Thesis: Deskilling as a Tool of Control

In his book *Labor and Monopoly Capital*, Braverman argues that dividing work into small repetitive tasks, which appear from Smith's perspective as pure "specialization," is in its social essence a systematic process of **deskilling** serving a dual aim for the owner of capital. On one hand, the skills required of each individual worker reduce his bargaining power (because an unskilled worker is easy to replace with another, unlike a skilled craftsman who is hard to replace). On the other hand, control over "know-how" passes from the worker himself to the management and engineers who design and document the process. Braverman called this the "separation of conception from execution," drawing on the principles of "scientific management" developed by Frederick Taylor in the early twentieth century to standardize the worker's movement and time precisely.

## The Bridge with the Political Economy of Productivity

This sociological analysis offers an important correction to the standard reading of economic growth. When economists measure productivity growth resulting from the division of labour and specialization, they often do not distinguish between two different sources of this growth, which may coincide in practice but are analytically distinct: a purely technical-engineering improvement in organizing the workflow, and a redistribution of power and bargaining relations between capital and labour in favour of the former, by weakening workers' mutual dependence on individual skill. This distinction is one way, among several, of thinking about why productivity growth does not necessarily translate into parallel growth in real wages, a pattern some studies describe as a "decoupling" of productivity and typical pay in the United States and some other economies after the 1970s; the causes of that decoupling are debated.

## Later Debates and the Limits of the Thesis

Braverman's thesis later faced criticism from within the sociological field itself. Researchers such as Michael Burawoy (*Manufacturing Consent*, 1979) argued, on the basis of ethnographic work in a machine shop, that workers do not simply experience domination: shop-floor games and practices lead them to participate actively in and consent to the organization of work. Andrew Friedman and Richard Edwards likewise stressed that managers combine direct control with strategies of "responsible autonomy" and that the labour process is a contested terrain. Critics also noted that Braverman paid little attention to workers' subjectivity and resistance. Moreover, some contemporary sectors (such as knowledge services) have shown a partial opposite trend toward "reskilling" rather than pure deskilling.

## Contemporary and Global Reading

Braverman's thesis has regained renewed relevance with the rise of the digital platform economy, where algorithms managing work (such as delivery apps and ride-sharing drivers) break down the labour process into atomized tasks measured and monitored digitally with considerable precision. In such settings, the "knowledge" needed to organize work is shifted entirely into a central algorithm whose internal logic the individual worker has no access to. This renews debate about the power relations concealed behind what appears to be "flexibility" in platform-based freelance work.

## Algorithmic Management in Arab Platform Work

Studies of app-based work in Arab labour markets describe software that assigns, monitors and rates tasks. They do not measure a decline in occupational skill of the kind Braverman traced in factory craft. Rania Magdi Fawzy's reading of the Uber driver interface in Cairo (*Journal of Labor and Society*, 2025) treats the app as labour governance: reminders, status gauges, ratings and game-like rewards direct drivers while presenting compliance as chosen flexibility. She argues that this blurs autonomy and control where informal work and economic insecurity are already widespread. A survey of 378 couriers on delivery platforms in Saudi Arabia (Elsayed, Saleh and Mahgoub, *SAGE Open*, 2026) reports that monitoring, goal setting, scheduling, ratings and pay rules are associated with lower job autonomy, and that this loss of autonomy is linked with lower proactivity, adaptability and resilience. The design of the task sits in the software rather than in the courier's discretion. That is the setting in which Braverman's separation of conception from execution is now discussed for these markets, without a direct estimate of deskilling.
