# DSM-5 Diagnostic Criteria

## Definition

The **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition** (DSM-5) is the classification manual issued by the **American Psychiatric Association** (APA). It is the main diagnostic reference in United States clinical practice and in much English-language research; most countries use the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases for official health statistics. DSM-5 was published in 2013. A research-planning process was underway by the early 2000s (Kupfer, First, and Regier, 2002), and an official task force, chaired by David J. Kupfer, oversaw thirteen work groups. Its text revision, **DSM-5-TR**, was issued in 2022 with updated descriptive text, some revised criteria, and new diagnoses, and involved a large group of expert reviewers.

## Historical Context

The manual's history goes back to its first edition in 1952. But **DSM-III** (1980) marked a radical turning point by adopting explicit, operational, observable diagnostic criteria in place of the theoretical explanations (especially psychoanalytic ones) that dominated the earlier editions. DSM-5 kept this operational logic but reorganized the disorder categories to reflect developmental change across the lifespan, from neurodevelopmental disorders in childhood to neurocognitive disorders in old age. It merged some categories, such as autism and Asperger's syndrome into a single "autism spectrum disorder," and added new categories, such as hoarding disorder, binge-eating disorder, and disruptive mood dysregulation disorder. It also abandoned the multiaxial system used since DSM-III. Field trials of reliability reported that agreement between clinicians (kappa) for some common diagnoses, such as major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder, was lower than hoped (Regier et al., 2013).

## Key Dimensions

- **Operational structure of the criteria**: Each disorder in DSM-5 is defined through a list of specific behavioral and temporal criteria, including the number of required symptoms, the minimum duration, and the functional impairment requirement. The aim is to ensure the highest possible **diagnostic reliability** among different clinicians.
- **Structure of Section II**: the diagnostic chapters of DSM-5-TR run from neurodevelopmental disorders through schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders, bipolar and related disorders, depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive and related disorders, trauma- and stressor-related disorders, dissociative disorders, somatic symptom and related disorders, feeding and eating disorders, elimination disorders, sleep-wake disorders, sexual dysfunctions, gender dysphoria, disruptive, impulse-control, and conduct disorders, substance-related and addictive disorders, neurocognitive disorders, personality disorders, and paraphilic disorders, to a final chapter of other mental disorders (twenty diagnostic chapters in all, followed by chapters on medication-induced movement disorders and other conditions that may be a focus of clinical attention).
- **Addition of "prolonged grief disorder"**: Among the most notable additions in the revised DSM-5-TR (2022) is formal recognition of prolonged grief disorder as a separate diagnostic category, along with new supplementary codes for documenting suicidal behavior and non-suicidal self-injury.
- **Section III: alternative models under study**: The manual includes a third section that presents emerging measures and models for further study, such as the alternative model for personality disorders (AMPD). This acknowledges that strict categorical classification does not always reflect the continuous clinical reality.
- **Relationship to ICD**: the two systems are intended to be broadly compatible, but they differ in structure and detail. DSM-5-TR assigns the ICD-10-CM codes used for administrative purposes in the United States, whereas the World Health Organization's **International Classification of Diseases** has since moved to ICD-11, which among other differences takes a dimensional approach to personality disorders.
- **Global use and critique**: Although DSM remains a dominant research and clinical reference worldwide, especially in research published in English, the **ICD** is the officially adopted standard for health reporting in most countries outside the United States.
