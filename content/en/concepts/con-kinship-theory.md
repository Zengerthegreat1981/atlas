# Kinship Theory

## Definition and Historical Development

Kinship theory is the set of theoretical frameworks developed by anthropologists to understand how human societies are organized through kinship ties. Kinship was not always a subject of systematic anthropological study: in the nineteenth century, European thought was concerned with general "social evolution," not with the details of specific kinship systems.

The starting point was Lewis Henry Morgan (1871), who published a massive book entitled *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family*. Morgan attempted to classify all known kinship systems into a few major categories. He believed in "linear evolution": that societies evolve from "simple" to "complex" kinship systems. Despite the errors of this approach, Morgan laid the foundations for the scientific study of kinship.

## Major Phases

### 1. British Functionalism (1920–1960)

Radcliffe-Brown and Fortes rejected belief in "linear evolution." They argued that kinship systems are not linear but "adaptive": each system solves social and economic problems in its own way. They focused on function: what does the descent group do in society? How does it regulate property and inheritance?

### 2. French Structuralism (1950–1980)

Claude Lévi-Strauss transformed the field entirely with his book *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949). He argued that kinship is not the "result" of economic practices but a symbolic system in its own right. Marriage (who marries whom) is a "social exchange," like language: a language of ties. Lévi-Strauss was fascinated by "reciprocal marriage" and symbolic exchange.

### 3. Critique and Revision (1970–2000)

Researchers began to criticize older assumptions. Leach showed that descent rules are not fixed. Feminists showed that anthropologists' studies focused on men and neglected women. David Schneider (1984) showed that kinship itself may be a "Western concept": other societies may not think about their kinship in the same way.

## Core Concepts

### Key Distinctions

- **Descent:** Who descends from whom? How do we assign an individual to a group?
- **Affinity:** Who marries whom? How are alliances formed through marriage?
- **Filiation:** Who is a person's parent? This may differ from "descent" in some systems.
- **Gender:** What roles do women and men play in the kinship system?

### Hierarchy

Some systems classify relatives into hierarchical levels (females ranked below males), while other systems are more horizontal.

## Contemporary Critiques

### Feminist Critique

Feminist anthropologists such as Michelle Rosaldo, Leela Dube and Marilyn Strathern argued that classical studies were built largely on men's models and neglected women's perspectives, and that women may reckon and experience kinship differently from men.

### Relativist Critique

Fredrik Barth, in *Models of Social Organization* (1966), criticized functionalist accounts for treating social structure as given. He argued that people use kinship strategically, making choices that serve their own interests and that, in aggregate, shape the social forms observed.

### Postmodern Critique

Janet Carsten, in *After Kinship* (2004), proposed the notion of "relatedness": people create kinship through everyday practices such as feeding, living together, and sharing substance, so that fixed, bounded "systems" are a poor guide to how kinship is lived. People draw on several idioms of relatedness according to context.

## Recent Developments

### Kinship and Technology

With assisted reproductive technologies (surrogacy, artificial insemination, nuclear transfer), the definition of "biological parents" has become ambiguous. Who counts as a parent: the one who donated the sperm, the one who carried the child, or the one who raised it?

### Kinship and Migration

In diasporas and migrant communities, people have redefined their kinship systems. Communication technologies help sustain kin ties across distance.
