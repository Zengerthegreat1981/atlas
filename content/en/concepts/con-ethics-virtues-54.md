# Peace Ethics

Peace ethics is a branch of moral and political philosophy that examines the ethical value of peace as a desirable goal and state in relations among individuals, groups, and states, and the moral conditions that either justify resort to war or require its avoidance. This field intersects with the theory of "just war" and with currents of absolute pacifism that reject violence outright.

## In the Islamic Heritage

The Islamic conception of peace rests on the principle that the default in relations among people is peace, not war. The Qur'an states: "And if they incline toward peace, then incline toward it [too]," and God is named among His beautiful names as "al-Salam" (The Peace). Muslim jurists, however, also developed a jurisprudence of jihad that regulates resort to fighting by strict conditions of justice, defense, and the lifting of oppression. Fighting is thus not an end in itself, but a restricted means bound by ethical controls, such as the protection of non-combatants and the fulfilment of covenants. Al-Farabi, in *Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila* (The Views of the People of the Virtuous City), affirmed that the aim of human association is cooperation toward happiness; in his political writings, war is treated as justified only in service of virtuous ends rather than for domination.

## In Western Philosophy

In his essay *Perpetual Peace* (1795), the German philosopher Immanuel Kant set out a philosophical and political project for establishing a lasting global peace based on republican constitutions, a federation of states that respects one another's sovereignty, and a universal right of hospitality. He regarded peace as a moral duty, not merely a temporary truce. Among absolute pacifist currents, Leo Tolstoy, and Mahatma Gandhi, who corresponded with Tolstoy, defended the principle of nonviolence (*ahimsa*) as a comprehensive moral position, practicing it as active, disciplined resistance to injustice (*satyagraha*) rather than passivity. In contemporary ethics of war, Michael Walzer, in *Just and Unjust Wars*, developed a framework that distinguishes between the justice of entering war (*jus ad bellum*) and the justice of conduct during it (*jus in bello*), a framework later extended by others, and in Walzer's own later writing, to the justice of the peace settlement that follows war (*jus post bellum*).

## Objections and Example

Absolute pacifism is objected to on the ground that it may leave victims defenseless before aggression that is deterred only by force. This led most ethical traditions, including the Islamic and the Kantian, to accept a limited, just defensive war in place of absolute peace. Example: a state that is subjected to external aggression resorts to legitimate defense with the least force necessary to repel the attack, then, once the danger has passed, seeks a peaceful settlement that preserves the rights of the parties. This is a model of the balance between realism and idealism that peace ethics calls for.
