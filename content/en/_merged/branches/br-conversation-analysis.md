# Conversation Analysis

Conversation analysis (CA) studies how people organise talk and other conduct in interaction. Its premise is that ordinary conversation is orderly in fine detail: participants coordinate who speaks, what an utterance is doing, and how troubles are fixed. Analysts work from recordings of naturally occurring interaction and ask how each contribution displays the speaker's understanding of what came before.

## Origins and Key Figures

CA emerged in the 1960s from the work of Harvey Sacks, in close collaboration with Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson. It drew on Erving Goffman's attention to face-to-face conduct and Harold Garfinkel's ethnomethodology. Sacks's lectures, delivered between 1964 and 1972 and published posthumously, began partly from recorded calls to a suicide prevention centre. Jefferson developed the transcription system, widely used in the field, that records overlap, pauses, stress, and other features of delivery. Anita Pomerantz, John Heritage, Paul Drew, and many others extended the programme.

## Core Findings

Turn-taking: Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson's 1974 article in *Language* described how speakers allocate turns locally, so that mostly one person speaks at a time and transitions occur with little gap or overlap.

Sequence organisation: many actions come in adjacency pairs, such as question and answer or invitation and response. A first action makes a particular kind of second relevant, and its absence is noticeable.

Preference organisation: acceptances and agreements are typically delivered promptly and plainly, whereas refusals and disagreements are often delayed, hedged, or accounted for.

Repair: participants have systematic ways of dealing with problems of speaking, hearing, and understanding, with a strong tendency for speakers to correct their own talk (Schegloff, Jefferson, and Sacks, 1977).

Openings and closings are also jointly managed, as studies of telephone calls showed.

## Empirical Examples

Research on institutional talk examines how general practices are adapted to particular settings. Paul Drew and John Heritage's *Talk at Work* (1992) collected studies of courts, medical consultations, and other workplaces. Heritage and Steven Clayman's work on news interviews showed how interviewers pursue challenging questions while maintaining a neutral stance. Studies of medical visits examine how symptoms are presented and how treatment recommendations are negotiated, and research on emergency calls traces how requests for help are processed. A comparative study led by Tanya Stivers (2009) examined the timing of answers to questions in ten languages and found broadly similar tendencies alongside local differences.

## Debates

CA insists that categories such as gender, power, or institution should enter analysis when participants' conduct shows that they are relevant. Critics argue that this restricts attention to wider structures; defenders reply that it prevents analysts from imposing explanations. Other discussions concern the use of statistics and coding, the integration of gesture and gaze, and the study of technologically mediated interaction.
