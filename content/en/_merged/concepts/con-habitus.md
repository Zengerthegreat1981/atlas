# Habitus

Habitus is the set of durable, largely unreflective dispositions through which people perceive, judge and act. It is acquired through upbringing and social experience, is expressed in taste, speech and bodily bearing as much as in opinions, and tends to incline people toward practices that fit the conditions in which it was formed. The concept is best known from the work of Pierre Bourdieu.

## Background

The Latin term *habitus* translates the Greek *hexis*, used by Aristotle for an acquired disposition, and it passed into scholastic philosophy. In modern social thought it appears in Marcel Mauss's "Techniques of the Body" (1935), which showed that ways of walking, swimming or eating are learned and socially variable, and in Norbert Elias's account of the long-term formation of self-restraint. The art historian Erwin Panofsky used the idea of a mental habit in relating Gothic architecture to scholastic thought, a study that Bourdieu translated into French.

## Bourdieu's Account

Bourdieu developed the concept in *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (French 1972; English 1977) and *The Logic of Practice* (French 1980; English 1990). Habitus consists of durable and transposable dispositions: structured by past conditions, they in turn structure present practice. Because dispositions learned in one setting are carried into others, a person can improvise appropriately without following explicit rules, with what Bourdieu called a practical sense or feel for the game. The aim was to avoid both an objectivism in which people merely execute social structures and a subjectivism in which they choose freely from nowhere.

## Habitus, Field and Capital

Habitus operates in relation to fields, relatively autonomous arenas such as education or art, and to the economic, cultural and social capital that people bring to them. In *Distinction* (1979) Bourdieu used survey and interview material from France to show that preferences in food, art and leisure vary systematically with class position. Where habitus matches a field, conduct seems natural; where conditions change faster than dispositions, a mismatch results, which Bourdieu called hysteresis.

## Criticism and Use

Critics, among them Richard Jenkins, have argued that the concept leans toward determinism, explains change poorly and is hard to observe independently of the practices it is meant to explain. Defenders reply that habitus is generative rather than mechanical and can be revised by new experience and reflection. It is widely used in research on education, class mobility, sport, the body and professional training; Loïc Wacquant's study of a Chicago boxing gym, *Body and Soul* (English 2004), examines how a habitus is built through apprenticeship.
