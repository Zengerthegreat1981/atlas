# Bilateral Kinship

Bilateral kinship is a way of reckoning relatives through both parents at once. A person counts mother and father, and the kin of each, as members of one personal network rather than as members of a single line that excludes the other parent. The arrangement is familiar in much of Europe, the Americas, and many urban societies, where grandparents on both sides, aunts, uncles, and cousins of both sexes are kin. Anthropologists contrast it with unilineal descent, in which group membership passes through fathers only or through mothers only. Bilateral reckoning does not by itself create a bounded corporate group. It creates an ego-centred field of relatives, often called a kindred, whose membership shifts from person to person.

## Reckoning and the kindred

In a bilateral system each individual stands at the centre of a slightly different set of kin. Siblings share most relatives, but cousins do not: my mother's sister's children are close kin to me but are usually no relation by descent to my father's brother's children. George Peter Murdock, in *Social Structure* (1949), used "kindred" for this overlapping network and distinguished it from a lineage or clan, whose members share a common ancestor and a common name. Because kindreds overlap, they rarely hold land or political office as a single body. People activate particular ties — for care, loans, funerals, or inheritance — rather than belonging automatically to one exclusive kin corporation. Inheritance is often divided among children of both sexes, or follows rules of law that treat both parental lines as relevant. Marriage links two kindreds without merging them into one descent group.

## How the category entered anthropology

Nineteenth-century kinship study, from Lewis Henry Morgan onward, paid close attention to unilineal clans because many of the societies then described in Africa, Native North America, and Australia organised politics and ritual through lineages. Bilateral arrangements, close to the family patterns of the ethnographers' own societies, were easy to treat as a residual case. Claude Lévi-Strauss's *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949) shifted emphasis toward marriage alliance, a frame that applies whether or not a society has lineages. Murdock's comparative survey showed how widespread bilateral groupings were. David Schneider's *American Kinship* (1968) then argued that, in the United States, kinship was a cultural code joining shared substance, often glossed as "blood," with a code of conduct he described as diffuse, enduring solidarity. Robin Fox's *Kinship and Marriage* (1967) set bilateral systems beside unilineal ones as a coherent alternative, not as the absence of structure.

## Debates

One argument is whether bilateral kinship is "weaker" than lineage organisation. Earlier writers sometimes treated the lack of a corporate descent group as a lack of kinship structure. Later work treats flexibility as the point: ties can be emphasised or allowed to lapse as people move, marry, and change households. A second argument concerns terminology. "Bilateral" describes tracing kin through both parents; "cognatic" is often used for the same broad class, while "ambilineal" names a different practice in which a person chooses one parental group, as in parts of Polynesia. Collapsing these terms hides real differences in land rights and political allegiance. A third argument, pressed by feminist and later kinship studies, asks whose labour the kindred depends on. Care, child-rearing, and the work of keeping in touch often fall unevenly, so a formally symmetrical system can still privilege one line in practice. New reproductive technologies, adoption, and remarriage further separate genetic, legal, and everyday parenthood, which bilateral folk models that equate kinship with "blood" do not automatically resolve.

## Why it matters

Most readers of an atlas entry live inside some version of bilateral reckoning: two sets of grandparents, inheritance law that looks to both parents, and family obligations that people negotiate rather than inherit as members of a clan. Migration stretches kindreds across countries while remittances, visits, and calls keep selected ties alive. States register births, citizenship, and property through rules that assume bilateral parentage even when they do not name the anthropological term. Studying bilateral kinship is a way of seeing how familiar families are organised, and how that organisation differs from lineage systems without being a failed version of them.
