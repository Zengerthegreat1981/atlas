# Shamanism

Shamanism is a comparative term for religious practices in which a specialist is held to communicate with spirits, often in an altered state, on behalf of a community. The word entered European languages from Tungusic languages of Siberia, where ethnographers described ritual specialists who journeyed, in their own account, to other realms in order to heal, divine, or escort the dead. Anthropologists later applied the term far beyond Siberia, to the Arctic, the Americas, Central and Inner Asia, and parts of Southeast Asia. The breadth of that use is itself a problem: practices grouped as shamanism differ in cosmology, training, and social role, and many specialists reject the label or use a local title.

## The Siberian starting point

Early accounts, including those associated with the Russian ethnographer Sergei Shirokogoroff among Tungus-speaking peoples, stressed a few recurring features. The specialist is called to the role, sometimes after illness; learns songs, costumes, and spirit relationships through apprenticeship; and performs in public, often with drumming. The séance is a social event. Clients bring sickness, lost souls, bad hunting, or conflict, and the specialist's authority depends on a reputation for effective dealing with spirits. Shamanism in this narrow sense is a technique and an office, not a church and not a doctrine written in a canon.

## Comparative theory

Mircea Eliade's *Shamanism: Archaic Techniques of Ecstasy* (French 1951; English 1964) made the term a general category. Eliade treated the shaman's ecstatic journey, the cosmic tree or axis, and the recovery of souls as an archaic pattern surviving in many religions. The book organized a vast literature and shaped both scholarship and later popular usage. Anthropologists have since argued that the pattern is too broad. It flattens differences between Siberian séance, Amazonian tobacco shamanism, Korean *mudang* practice, and Nepalese spirit mediumship, and it leans on a history-of-religions search for origins rather than on what people do in a particular place.

A more ethnographic approach asks who may become a specialist, how the role is gendered, whether the specialist is feared as well as sought, and how the performance is judged. Healing is often the public test. Success is not only a private trance; it is a diagnosis that kin accept, a payment, and a change in how the sick person is treated. Where biomedicine is also available, people commonly use both, assigning different illnesses to different kinds of cause.

## Power, scepticism, and the modern career of the term

Shamans are political figures as well as healers. Control of spirits can support a leader, accuse a rival of witchcraft, or give a marginal person a voice that ordinary speech does not. Colonial governments and mission churches often banned the practices as superstition or as competition. In the twentieth century the same practices were sometimes revived as ethnic heritage or as tourist performance. Michael Taussig's work on colonialism and healing in Colombia is one influential account of how shamanic imagery is entangled with terror, commerce, and the state, rather than standing outside history.

From the 1960s a separate, popular "shamanism" grew in Europe and North America: workshops, neo-shamanic drumming, and claims of a universal technique detachable from any community. Anthropologists generally distinguish this movement from the offices studied in ethnography. The popular form borrows the Siberian word and Eliade's journey motif; it does not reproduce the kinship obligations, spirit pacts, or local scepticism of the settings from which the term was taken.

## What the comparison can still do

Used carefully, the term marks a family of resemblances: a specialist, a spirit idiom, a publicly scrutinized performance, and a claim to act for others. It is a weak claim if it is treated as one religion, and a useful one if each case is described in its own ritual language first. Debates continue over whether "shaman" should be reserved for Siberia and the circumpolar north, and over how to write about efficacy without either dismissing the practice as illusion or treating spirit claims as facts the ethnographer has confirmed.
