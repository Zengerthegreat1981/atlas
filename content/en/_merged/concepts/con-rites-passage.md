# Rites of Passage

A rite of passage is a ritual that marks a change of social status. Birth, initiation, marriage, installation in office, and death are the classic cases. The person (or the group) leaves one recognised place in society and enters another. Anthropology uses the term for a pattern that appears, with very different contents, in many societies: the change is not left to private feeling. It is staged, witnessed, and made binding.

## Van Gennep's scheme

Arnold van Gennep set out the scheme in *Les rites de passage* (1909). He argued that such rituals tend to fall into three phases. Separation removes the person from the old status: hair may be cut, ordinary clothes put aside, the novice taken out of the village. Transition, which he called the margin, is an in-between condition in which ordinary rules are suspended or reversed. Incorporation returns the person to social life under a new name, role, or set of duties. Van Gennep treated the pattern as a formal property of ritual sequences, useful for comparing ceremonies that look unlike one another on the surface. He drew on ethnographic and folkloric reports from many regions rather than on a single field study.

## Liminality and later theory

Victor Turner took up the middle phase, which van Gennep had also called liminal, from the Latin for a threshold, and developed it as liminality, notably in *The Ritual Process* (1969). In that interval, he argued, initiates are often stripped of rank and may form an intense, relatively egalitarian bond with others passing through the same ordeal, which he called communitas. The bond is temporary. The rite ends by placing people back into a structured order, often with a clearer rank than before. Turner's account made the passage model a theory of how ritual both loosens and restores social structure. Later writers have used it for pilgrimage, theatre, political ceremony, and some forms of illness and healing, where a person is set apart and then returned.

## What the rites do

The ceremonies do more than announce a change that has already happened. In many cases they produce the change. An age grade, a married status, or a title may not exist for practical purposes until the rite is completed. Instruction, ordeal, seclusion, gift exchange, and public witness fix the new obligations in memory. Funerals are passages for the dead and for the living: they move the deceased toward an ancestral or other post-mortem status and reorganise the household that remains. Marriage rites create ties between groups as well as between spouses. The same three-phase outline can therefore describe both an individual life course and a rearrangement of kinship and politics.

## Limits of the model

Not every ritual is a passage. Seasonal festivals, protective rites, and routines of daily worship may renew a world that is meant to stay the same. Some transitions are gradual, legal, or bureaucratic, with little ceremony. Critics have also noted that classic accounts often took male initiation as the type and gave less attention to women's rites, to people excluded from the passage, and to the violence that some initiations involve. The scheme is a comparison tool. It does not show that all societies share one symbolism, and it does not rank rites as more or less developed. Secular institutions still stage passages — graduation, naturalisation, enlistment, retirement — and the old vocabulary remains useful where a status change is made public and irreversible.
