# Development Anthropology

Development anthropology applies ethnographic research to projects and policies intended to improve livelihoods, health, infrastructure, or governance. It is often distinguished from the anthropology of development, which studies development institutions and ideas themselves, usually from a critical distance. Many researchers move between the two positions.

## Formation

Anthropologists had advised colonial administrations and postwar technical assistance programmes, but the field expanded in the 1970s when aid agencies, including the United States Agency for International Development and the World Bank, paid more attention to rural poverty and the social effects of projects. The Institute for Development Anthropology was founded in 1976 by David Brokensha, Michael Horowitz, and Thayer Scudder. Michael Cernea, a sociologist, argued within the World Bank for putting people first in project design and for safeguards in cases of involuntary resettlement.

## What practitioners do

Researchers study land tenure, household labour, gender divisions, local institutions, and risk. They may appraise a proposal, monitor implementation, or evaluate outcomes. Their findings often show that a "community" contains competing interests, that households do not pool all resources, and that apparently irrational refusal may protect food security or customary rights. Work on dams and resettlement, notably by Scudder and Elizabeth Colson among Gwembe Tonga displaced by the Kariba Dam, documented hardship over long periods.

## Critiques

Arturo Escobar's *Encountering Development* (1995) argued that postwar development discourse constructed much of the world as underdeveloped and thereby authorised expert intervention. James Ferguson's *The Anti-Politics Machine* (1990), a study of a project in Lesotho, showed how a technical account could omit political and economic relations, while the project, even in failing at its stated aims, extended state bureaucratic power. These works are sometimes grouped as post-development or postcolonial critique, although their arguments differ. David Mosse's *Cultivating Development* (2005), drawing on his own involvement in a project in India, argued that policy may serve to interpret and legitimate events more than to direct them. Tania Murray Li examined how improvement schemes in Indonesia define problems in technical terms.

## Ethics and politics

An anthropologist employed by an agency may face limits on publication, short timetables, and conflicts between funders' aims and residents' wishes. Informed consent for research is not the same as consent to a project. Participation can transfer real decisions, or it can ask people to endorse choices already made. Critics question whether reform from inside is possible; some practitioners reply that detailed knowledge can prevent harm or strengthen claims for compensation. Newer research follows cash transfers, microfinance, humanitarian programmes, climate adaptation, and Chinese and other South–South aid, where older divisions between donor and recipient need reconsideration. Evaluation should ask who defined success and over what period it was measured.
