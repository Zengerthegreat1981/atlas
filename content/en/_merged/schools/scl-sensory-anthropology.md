# Sensory Anthropology

Sensory anthropology studies how people learn to perceive, value, and act through the senses. It treats seeing, hearing, touch, taste, smell, movement, and bodily orientation as shaped by training and social life, without assuming that biology is irrelevant. It also questions the habit of making ethnography mainly a matter of words and sight.

## Origins

Earlier anthropologists recorded perception in various ways, from nineteenth-century tests of sensory acuity to Claude Lévi-Strauss's analysis of sensible qualities in myth. A more focused programme developed in the 1980s and 1990s. Paul Stoller's *The Taste of Ethnographic Things* (1989), drawing on fieldwork among the Songhay of Niger, urged ethnographers to attend to taste, smell, and sound. Steven Feld's *Sound and Sentiment* (1982) showed how Kaluli people in Papua New Guinea connected birds, weeping, poetry, and song; Feld later used the term acoustemology for knowing through sound. David Howes, Constance Classen, and colleagues in Montreal developed comparative research on sensory orders, including the volume *The Varieties of Sensory Experience* (1991). Nadia Seremetakis wrote on sensory memory in Greece.

## Sensory orders

Comparative work asks which distinctions a community elaborates, which senses it links to knowledge or morality, and how perception is distributed by class, gender, age, or ritual status. Classen's work showed that the familiar list of five senses and the high rank often given to vision have histories. Ethnographies of smell, heat, sound, or balance trace how these qualities help to classify persons and places. Research on skill examines how apprentices, cooks, healers, craft workers, and clinicians learn to notice differences that novices miss.

## Debate over method

Howes has emphasised cultural models of the senses and comparison among them. Tim Ingold criticised this emphasis, arguing that it divides perception into separate channels and treats experience as the expression of cultural representations; he favoured the study of movement and engagement with an environment in which the senses work together. Sarah Pink's *Doing Sensory Ethnography* (2009) describes research as an emplaced practice and discusses walking, eating, participating, and audiovisual recording. These approaches overlap while disagreeing about what comparison should compare.

## Limits and uses

A researcher's own sensation can prompt questions but cannot show that others experience the same thing. Careful accounts connect bodily participation with local vocabulary, instruction, disagreement, and observation of conduct. Recording equipment selects and transforms what it captures. Disability research adds that blind, Deaf, and other differently perceiving people cannot be treated as lacking a single normal sensorium. The approach has been used in studies of cities, museums, food, medicine, religion, and design. It also raises historical questions about how noise regulation, sanitation, colonial rule, and markets have judged some sounds and smells as improper and others as refined.
