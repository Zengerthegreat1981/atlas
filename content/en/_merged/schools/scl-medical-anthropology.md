# Medical Anthropology

Medical anthropology studies health, illness, healing, and bodily suffering in their social and cultural settings. It treats biomedicine as well as other healing traditions as subjects of inquiry. Its methods range from long-term ethnography to collaboration in public health, and its questions run from the meaning of a symptom to the global distribution of disease and drugs.

## Foundations

W. H. R. Rivers examined the relation of medicine, magic, and religion in work published in 1924. Evans-Pritchard's study of Azande witchcraft (1937) showed how people could accept practical causes while asking why misfortune struck a particular person. After the Second World War, anthropologists joined international health programmes, and the subfield gained organisations and journals in the United States from the 1960s and 1970s. George Foster and Barbara Anderson's *Medical Anthropology* (1978) was an early textbook.

## Meaning and experience

Arthur Kleinman distinguished disease, as framed by practitioners, from illness, as lived by patients and families, and proposed asking about explanatory models of cause, course, and treatment. Byron Good analysed how medical knowledge and illness narratives construct their objects. Nancy Scheper-Hughes and Margaret Lock's "The Mindful Body" (1987) distinguished the individual, social, and political body. These approaches warn against treating culture as a fixed list of beliefs that explains why patients fail to follow advice.

## Mental health beyond one model

Research on distress asks how categories travel. Kleinman's work in China examined the clinical label neurasthenia and the bodily expression of distress, and questioned the assumption that Western diagnostic categories are universal templates. Local idioms of distress, possession, prayer, and family intervention may organise suffering and recovery in ways that psychiatric manuals do not capture. Comparative studies also caution against romanticising local treatment, since restraint, neglect, and stigma occur in many systems, and people often move among several kinds of help.

## Power and inequality

Critical medical anthropology links sickness to political economy. Paul Farmer's work in Haiti used the idea of structural violence to show how poverty and racism affect exposure to infection and access to care. Scheper-Hughes's *Death Without Weeping* (1992) examined child death and mothering amid hunger in northeast Brazil, a study that provoked debate over its account of maternal feeling.

## Pharmaceuticals and technologies

The anthropology of pharmaceuticals follows medicines through research, manufacture, marketing, prescription, informal sale, and domestic use. Sjaak van der Geest, Susan Reynolds Whyte, and Anita Hardon proposed tracing their social life. João Biehl studied the Brazilian response to AIDS and the part played by access to antiretroviral drugs, while Adriana Petryna examined the global movement of clinical trials. Reproductive technologies, genetics, organ transfer, and epidemic response raise related questions about consent, value, and who benefits. Applied researchers may improve programmes, but critics ask whether cultural explanation can distract from shortages of staff, medicines, and income.
