# Linguistic Anthropology

Linguistic anthropology studies language as part of social life. It examines how speaking, signing, and writing create relationships, mark identities, transmit knowledge, and distribute authority. In the United States it has long been counted as one of the four fields of anthropology. Its characteristic evidence is recorded and transcribed interaction, interpreted with knowledge gained through ethnographic fieldwork.

## Documentation and relativity

Franz Boas made language description central to American anthropology. His introduction to the *Handbook of American Indian Languages* (1911) argued that each language should be analysed through its own categories rather than those of Latin or English. Edward Sapir developed this work and wrote on the relation between language, culture, and personality. Benjamin Lee Whorf argued that habitual grammatical patterns guide habitual thought. The label "Sapir–Whorf hypothesis" was applied later, and neither author set it out as a single testable proposition. Research from the 1990s, including work by John Lucy and Stephen Levinson, reopened questions of linguistic relativity with narrower comparative claims.

## Communication and context

In the 1960s Dell Hymes and John Gumperz promoted the ethnography of communication. Hymes argued that knowing a language includes knowing when, how, and to whom to speak, which he called communicative competence, in contrast with Noam Chomsky's narrower grammatical competence. Gumperz studied how contextual cues, such as intonation or a switch of language, shape inference, and how mismatched expectations can disadvantage speakers in interviews and institutions. Keith Basso's work with Western Apache speakers showed the meanings of silence and of place names in moral instruction.

## Practice, socialization, and ideology

Elinor Ochs and Bambi Schieffelin studied language socialization, showing that children learn to speak and learn to be members of a community together. Alessandro Duranti treated language as a cultural practice and a form of action. Michael Silverstein developed an account of indexicality, the way signs point to speakers, settings, and social types, and drew attention to people's ideas about language. Studies of language ideology examine why some varieties are heard as correct, modern, or authentic, and how those judgments support claims about class, race, gender, and nation.

## Multilingualism, media, and endangerment

Code-switching, the movement between languages or varieties within an exchange, is analysed as patterned conduct that can signal solidarity, stance, or a change of topic, not as a failure to command either language. Digital communication adds new combinations of writing, image, and voice and new norms of address. Work on endangered languages documents speech and supports revitalization, while asking who controls recordings and whether the language of "loss" matches speakers' own views. Jane Hill and others have warned that expert rhetoric about endangerment can treat languages as objects detached from their communities.
