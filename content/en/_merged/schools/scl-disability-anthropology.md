# Disability Anthropology

Disability anthropology studies how bodily, sensory, cognitive, and psychiatric differences are experienced, classified, and lived in particular social settings. It asks when a difference becomes a disability, how institutions and built environments include or exclude, and how disabled people create knowledge, relationships, and politics. It draws on medical anthropology and on disability studies while remaining distinct from clinical accounts of impairment.

## Background

Robert Edgerton's *The Cloak of Competence* (1967) followed people discharged from a state institution who had been labelled mentally retarded, in the usage of the time, and examined how they managed stigma. The anthropologist Robert Murphy's *The Body Silent* (1987) analysed his own progressive paralysis, describing disabled people as occupying a liminal social position. In the 1970s and 1980s, disability activists and scholars, particularly in Britain, advanced the social model, which separates impairment from the barriers that disable people. Nora Groce's *Everyone Here Spoke Sign Language* (1985) reconstructed a Martha's Vineyard community where hereditary deafness was common and signing was widely shared among hearing residents.

## Local worlds and comparison

Benedicte Ingstad and Susan Reynolds Whyte's edited volume *Disability and Culture* (1995) placed disability in the context of ideas about personhood in different societies. A person's standing may depend less on independent function than on kinship, marriage, work, or the capacity to take part in exchange. The volume cautioned against assuming that "disability" is a ready-made category everywhere. Faye Ginsburg and Rayna Rapp argued in a 2013 review that disability deserves a central place in anthropology, partly because it can enter any life and reshapes families and public culture.

## Themes

Research examines schooling, prosthetics, care, rehabilitation, welfare assessment, war injury, prenatal testing, and the global spread of diagnostic and rights categories. Studies of Deaf communities describe sign languages and shared institutions, and many Deaf people identify as members of a linguistic minority rather than primarily as disabled. Work on autism has examined self-advocacy and different forms of communication and sociality. Ethnography of care shows dependence running in several directions, as people who receive assistance also give support.

## Method and politics

Participant observation and interviews often need adapting: interpretation, alternative forms of consent, slower pacing, or recording of nonverbal conduct. Collaboration with disabled researchers and organisations affects which questions are asked. Debates concern the limits of the social model when pain, fatigue, or illness matter to people's lives; the tension between claiming rights through diagnostic categories and rejecting medical definition; and the applicability of rights frameworks developed mainly in wealthy states to places where survival rests on kin and informal work. Julie Livingston's research in Botswana, for example, related disability and debility to labour migration, illness, and moral expectations of care.
