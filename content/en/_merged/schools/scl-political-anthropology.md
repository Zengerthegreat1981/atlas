# Political Anthropology

Political anthropology studies authority, order, conflict, and collective decision-making through comparison and ethnography. It includes societies with centralised rulers and societies without them, as well as parliaments, bureaucracies, movements, and informal networks. Its recurring concern is that political life may be carried by kinship, ritual, exchange, or everyday relations and not only by institutions labelled governmental.

## Structure and order

*African Political Systems* (1940), edited by Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard, is usually treated as a founding work. It contrasted societies with centralised authority and judicial institutions with those lacking them, giving prominence to segmentary lineages. Evans-Pritchard's account of the Nuer described groups that oppose or combine according to the level of a dispute. The approach clarified how order could exist without a state, but tended to bracket colonial rule and historical change.

## Process and action

Edmund Leach's *Political Systems of Highland Burma* (1954) showed communities moving between competing political models. Max Gluckman and researchers associated with Manchester studied conflict, ritual, and situations produced by colonial and industrial change. F. G. Bailey and Fredrik Barth analysed strategy, factions, and leadership; Barth's study of Swat Pathans (1959) examined how followers choose patrons. The volume *Political Anthropology* (1966), edited by Marc Swartz, Victor Turner, and Arthur Tuden, emphasised processes, goals, and public contests. Georges Balandier published an influential French synthesis in 1967.

## Evolution and political economy

In the United States, Morton Fried and Elman Service proposed typologies of political evolution, such as band, tribe, chiefdom, and state. Critics questioned fixed stages and showed that mobile or decentralised peoples often developed in relation to states. Pierre Clastres argued that some Amazonian societies had institutions that worked against the concentration of coercive power. Eric Wolf's *Europe and the People without History* (1982) placed local societies within expanding systems of trade, production, and conquest.

## States, resistance, and governance

James C. Scott described everyday forms of peasant resistance and later the ways states simplify society to make it manageable. Drawing partly on Michel Foucault, ethnographers examined discipline, expertise, and governmentality. Akhil Gupta studied how ideas of the state are formed through encounters with officials and talk about corruption in India. Research on nationalism, violence, democracy, humanitarianism, borders, and sovereignty now treats the state as an uneven assemblage of people, documents, and practices.

## Questions of method

Fieldwork can show how formal rules are used and evaded, but access to powerful institutions may be limited. Researchers in situations of conflict must consider surveillance, partisanship, and the safety of those who speak. Debates continue over the definition of politics: a very narrow definition misses important forms of authority, while one that includes every relation of influence loses analytic precision.
