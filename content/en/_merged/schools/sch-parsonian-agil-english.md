# Parsonian Structural Functionalism

Parsonian structural functionalism is the general theory of action and social systems developed mainly by Talcott Parsons (1902-1979) at Harvard. It asks how stable social order is possible and answers by examining how shared norms and values, socialisation and institutions coordinate action, and which functions a social system must meet in order to persist.

## Background

Parsons studied at Amherst, the London School of Economics and Heidelberg, and taught at Harvard from 1927. *The Structure of Social Action* (1937) read Alfred Marshall, Vilfredo Pareto, Émile Durkheim and Max Weber as converging on a "voluntaristic" theory of action: actors choose means and ends, but within conditions and under norms. In 1946 Harvard's Department of Social Relations, which Parsons chaired, brought together sociology, social anthropology and social and clinical psychology.

## Action and Systems

*Toward a General Theory of Action* (1951), edited with Edward Shils, and *The Social System* (1951) distinguish personality, social and cultural systems. A social system consists of interaction organised through roles and shared expectations. Order depends on the internalisation of values and on mechanisms of social control. The **pattern variables**, such as universalism versus particularism and achievement versus ascription, describe choices of orientation that distinguish roles and types of society.

## The AGIL Scheme

*Working Papers in the Theory of Action* (1953), written with Robert F. Bales and Shils, introduced the four-function scheme: **adaptation** to the environment and the securing of resources; **goal attainment**; **integration** of the parts; and **latency** or pattern maintenance. In *Economy and Society* (1956), Parsons and Neil Smelser treated the economy as the adaptive subsystem of society and analysed its exchanges with the other subsystems. Later work described generalised media of interchange, including money, power, influence and value commitments. In the 1960s Parsons also returned to long-term social change, using ideas of differentiation and evolution.

## Applications

The analysis of the "sick role" in *The Social System* became a reference point in medical sociology. Parsonian ideas were applied to the family, education, professions and modernisation.

## Criticism and Legacy

C. Wright Mills attacked Parsons's "grand theory" in *The Sociological Imagination* (1959). Other critics argued that the framework gave too little weight to conflict, power and change, that its functional explanations were weak or circular, and that its account of gender roles in the family treated a particular arrangement as a functional requirement. Its dominance declined in the 1960s. Niklas Luhmann developed a different systems theory partly in response to Parsons, and Jeffrey Alexander and others proposed a "neofunctionalism" in the 1980s.
