# Urban Anthropology

Urban anthropology studies social life in cities and the processes through which urban places are made. It includes research on neighbourhoods, work, housing, migration, infrastructure, public space, and government. A central distinction is between anthropology in the city, which happens to be set there, and anthropology of the city, which treats urban organisation itself as the problem.

## Precursors

Sociologists of the Chicago School used observation and life histories to study immigrant districts and street life, and Louis Wirth's "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938) linked size, density, and diversity to impersonal relations. Robert Redfield's folk–urban continuum, drawn from research in Mexico, invited criticism for treating rural and urban life as poles. In central Africa, researchers associated with the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute and Manchester studied mining towns. J. Clyde Mitchell's *The Kalela Dance* (1956) showed how ethnic categories took on new uses in urban settings.

## Consolidation

The subfield gained a distinct identity in the 1960s and 1970s. Oscar Lewis proposed a "culture of poverty", which critics argued could blame poor people for conditions produced by economic and political exclusion. Janice Perlman's *The Myth of Marginality* (1976) showed that residents of Rio de Janeiro's favelas were integrated into city life on unequal terms. Network analysis traced ties that crossed neighbourhoods, and Ulf Hannerz's *Exploring the City* (1980) assessed ways of building an urban anthropology.

## Inequality and space

Later ethnographies connected daily life with political economy. Philippe Bourgois studied drug sellers in East Harlem. Teresa Caldeira's *City of Walls* (2000) examined crime talk, fear, and fortified enclaves in São Paulo. Setha Low analysed plazas and gated communities. AbdouMaliq Simone described how residents of African cities combine relationships and improvised arrangements to sustain livelihoods, proposing that people themselves can act as infrastructure.

## Megacities

Very large urban regions challenge the assumption that a neighbourhood study represents a city. Researchers follow commuting, supply chains, water, electricity, waste, finance, and policy across districts and jurisdictions. Work on cities of the global South has criticised theory that treats European and North American cities as the normal case. Informal settlement and informal labour are examined as relations to law and the state, which may tolerate, tax, demolish, or regularise them, not as zones outside government.

## Digital urban space

Phones, mapping services, delivery platforms, ride hailing, and surveillance systems alter how streets are navigated and who becomes visible. Ethnography can compare the claims made for "smart cities" with maintenance work, breakdown, and unequal access. Online neighbourhood groups also affect reputation, safety, and mobilisation. These topics require attention both to the physical setting and to companies and data systems located elsewhere.
