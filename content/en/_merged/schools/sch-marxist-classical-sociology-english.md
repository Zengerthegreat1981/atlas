# Marxist Classical Sociology

Marxist classical sociology refers to the analysis of society that stems from Karl Marx (1818-1883) and Friedrich Engels (1820-1895) and to its development by their successors. Marx did not describe himself as a sociologist, but his account of class, capitalism and historical change became one of the main reference points of the discipline.

## Historical Setting

Marx and Engels wrote amid industrialisation and political upheaval in Europe. Engels's *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (German edition 1845) drew on observation in Manchester and on official reports. *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) presented history as shaped by class struggle. Marx, living in London from 1849, published the first volume of *Capital* in 1867; Engels edited the second and third volumes after Marx's death.

## Core Concepts

**Historical materialism** holds that the way people produce their means of life conditions social, political and intellectual life. The **forces of production** (labour, tools, knowledge) develop within **relations of production** (relations of ownership and control), and tension between them is a source of change. The 1859 preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* describes an economic base on which a legal and political superstructure arises, an image whose interpretation is much disputed.

**Class** is defined chiefly by position in the relations of production. Under capitalism, wage workers sell their labour power to owners of capital; Marx argued that they produce more value than they receive as wages, and that this **surplus value** is the source of profit. The early manuscripts of 1844 analyse **alienation** from the product, the activity of work, human capacities and other people. **Ideology** and, in *Capital*, **commodity fetishism** concern the ways social relations come to appear natural or to take the form of relations between things.

## Later Development

Within the Marxist tradition, Karl Kautsky systematised the doctrine in the Second International, Georg Lukács developed the theory of reification in *History and Class Consciousness* (1923), and Antonio Gramsci analysed hegemony in his prison notebooks. In academic sociology, Marxist ideas shaped conflict theory, labour process studies such as Harry Braverman's *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974), and the class analysis of Erik Olin Wright.

## Criticism

Max Weber argued that stratification also involves status and party and that religious ideas can influence economic conduct. Critics question economic determinism, the labour theory of value, and predictions of class polarisation and revolution. Feminist authors have argued that the analysis neglects unpaid domestic and reproductive work. Many Marxists reject deterministic readings and treat the theory as a method for analysing historically specific relations.
