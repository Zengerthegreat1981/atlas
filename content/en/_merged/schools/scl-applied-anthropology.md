# Applied Anthropology

Applied anthropology is the use of anthropological methods and knowledge to address practical problems for governments, communities, agencies, firms, and advocacy groups. It covers work in health, development, education, disaster response, heritage, law, and design. It is less a single theory than a relation between research and use, and the central questions concern who defines the problem, who receives the findings, and who bears the consequences.

## Colonial and wartime origins

Ethnology was offered to colonial administration early in its history. In British Africa, advocates of indirect rule argued that officials needed knowledge of local law, land, and authority, and Bronisław Malinowski wrote in favour of a "practical anthropology" in 1929. In the United States, anthropologists worked with the Office of Indian Affairs during the reforms of the 1930s. The Society for Applied Anthropology was founded in 1941. During the Second World War, Margaret Mead, Ruth Benedict, and others studied morale, food habits, and enemy societies for government bodies; Benedict's *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946) grew out of this research. Anthropologists were also employed in the camps where Japanese Americans were confined, an episode later examined as a test of professional responsibility.

## Intervention and action research

After 1945, applied work expanded into technical assistance and public health. The Vicos project in Peru, begun in 1952 under Allan Holmberg of Cornell University, took on the lease of a highland hacienda and sought to transfer control to its residents. Admirers presented it as participatory change; critics asked how far outsiders had set its goals. Sol Tax proposed "action anthropology" from work with the Meskwaki, in which research would help a community clarify and pursue its own choices. Later approaches, including collaborative, participatory, and advocacy anthropology, share the demand that the people studied have a say in questions and outputs.

## The ethics of intervention

Controversies have shaped the field. Project Camelot, a United States Army-sponsored social research programme on insurgency, was cancelled in 1965 after it became public in Chile. Disputes over counterinsurgency research in Thailand followed during the Vietnam War. The Human Terrain System, which embedded social scientists with military units from the 2000s, was criticised by the American Anthropological Association's executive board in 2007. Ethics codes stress informed consent, openness about sponsorship, and the avoidance of harm. These principles are hard to apply when a client owns the data, when community interests conflict, or when refusing to take part leaves decisions to people with less local knowledge.

## Postcolonial and internal critiques

Critics argue that applied work can translate political conflicts into technical problems and lend authority to plans already made. Talal Asad's edited volume *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973) pressed the discipline to examine its relation to imperial power, while Vine Deloria Jr.'s *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969) attacked researchers who took knowledge from Native communities without serving them. Some practitioners answer that engagement under imperfect conditions is preferable to detachment. Others distinguish applied anthropology, which serves a defined client, from engaged anthropology, which openly takes a side, although the boundary between them is not fixed.
