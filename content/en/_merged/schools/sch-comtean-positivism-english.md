# Comtean Positivism

Comtean positivism is the philosophy of science and programme for a science of society developed by Auguste Comte (1798-1857) in France. It held that reliable knowledge concerns observable phenomena and the regular relations between them, and that society could be studied in this way just as nature is. Comte introduced the word "sociology" for the new science, which he had earlier called "social physics".

## Historical Setting

Comte worked in the decades after the French Revolution. He studied at the Ecole Polytechnique and from 1817 served as secretary to Henri de Saint-Simon, from whom he broke in 1824. He saw post-revolutionary Europe as intellectually and politically disordered and argued that agreement based on science could end the crisis. His main early statement is the *Course of Positive Philosophy*, published in six volumes between 1830 and 1842.

## Core Ideas

The **law of three stages** holds that each branch of knowledge passes through a theological stage, which explains events by supernatural wills; a metaphysical stage, which appeals to abstract essences and forces; and a positive stage, which gives up the search for ultimate causes and seeks laws connecting observed facts.

The **classification of the sciences** orders them from the most general and simple to the most particular and complex: mathematics, astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology and sociology. Each depends on those before it, and sociology, the last to become positive, was to crown the series.

Comte divided sociology into **social statics**, the study of the conditions of order, such as the family, language, the division of labour, government and shared beliefs, and **social dynamics**, the study of the laws of development, in which intellectual change plays the leading role. His methods were observation, experiment (in a limited sense), comparison and, above all, historical analysis. His slogan that science yields foresight, and foresight yields action, expresses the practical aim of the programme.

## The Later Work

In the *System of Positive Polity* (1851-1854) Comte gave greater weight to feeling and morality, coined or popularised the term "altruism", and set out a Religion of Humanity with its own rites, calendar and priesthood. Positivist societies formed in several countries, and the movement had particular influence in Brazil, whose flag carries the words "Order and Progress".

## Influence and Criticism

Harriet Martineau's condensed English translation of the *Course* appeared in 1853. John Stuart Mill drew on Comte's account of method but sharply criticised the later political and religious system in *Auguste Comte and Positivism* (1865). Émile Durkheim retained the ambition of a science of social facts while rejecting much of Comte's philosophy of history.

Critics object that the law of three stages is a speculative scheme and not a tested law, that it treats European history as the model for humanity, and that the political programme gave great authority to a scientific elite. Interpretive sociologists argue that explaining social action requires attention to meaning. "Positivism" in later debates, including twentieth-century logical positivism, often means something different from Comte's doctrine, so the uses should be kept apart.
