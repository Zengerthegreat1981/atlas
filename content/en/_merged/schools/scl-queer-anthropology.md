# Queer Anthropology

Queer anthropology studies sexuality and gender ethnographically while questioning the categories that mark some desires, bodies, and families as normal. It includes research on lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender lives, but also examines heterosexual norms, state regulation, kinship, and the translation of identity terms between societies.

## Antecedents

Anthropologists reported sexual and gender variation well before the field had a name, often briefly or in the language of deviance. Esther Newton's *Mother Camp* (1972), a study of female impersonators in the United States, treated drag as a social world and an analysis of gender as performance. Gayle Rubin's essay "Thinking Sex" (1984) argued that sexuality needs its own political analysis and described hierarchies that rank sexual practices. In the 1980s, research on HIV and AIDS brought both funding and urgency, along with disputes over how behaviour and identity were classified. The organisation of lesbian and gay anthropologists within the American Anthropological Association, now the Association for Queer Anthropology, gave the work an institutional base.

## Kinship and identity

Kath Weston's *Families We Choose* (1991) showed gay men and lesbians in the San Francisco Bay Area building kinship through friendship, former partnerships, and care, and questioned the assumption that biology is the self-evident basis of family. Ellen Lewin studied lesbian mothers. Ethnographies from other regions showed that categories do not travel unchanged. Don Kulick's *Travesti* (1998) examined Brazilian transgendered prostitutes and local ideas that link gender to sexual practice. Martin Manalansan's *Global Divas* (2003) followed Filipino gay men in New York. Tom Boellstorff's *The Gay Archipelago* (2005) analysed how Indonesians take up the terms *gay* and *lesbi* in relation to national belonging.

## Theory and method

Queer theory, associated with writers such as Judith Butler and Michael Warner, encouraged attention to norms rather than only to minority identities. Ethnographers have tested its claims against particular settings, where people may seek recognition and respectability as much as disruption. David Valentine's *Imagining Transgender* (2007) showed how an institutional category could include people who did not use it for themselves. Fieldwork raises questions about the researcher's own sexuality, disclosure, erotic relations in the field, and the safety of participants where exposure can bring violence or prosecution.

## Debates

Critics warn against exporting a Western model of identity and coming out, and equally against treating non-Western gender systems as timeless proof of tolerance. Roles often cited as "third genders" differ in religious meaning, obligation, and stigma. Scholars also debate whether rights-based politics narrows the range of intimate life that is recognised, and how race, class, migration, and religion alter what a sexual norm demands. The relation to feminist anthropology is close, although the two do not always give gender and sexuality the same analytic place.
