# Suicide: A Study in Sociology

*Suicide: A Study in Sociology* (1897) by Émile Durkheim is a founding work of empirical sociology and the book that argued, against the common sense of its time, that suicide — the most private and individual of acts — varies with social conditions. Published in Paris as *Le Suicide: Étude de sociologie*, it was the third of Durkheim's major books, following *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) and *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), and it was designed as the proof that sociology could be a science: that it could explain a social fact by other social facts, without reducing it to individual psychology.

## The Method: Suicide as a Social Fact

Durkheim's method is the book's first argument. He begins by defining suicide precisely — every case of death resulting directly or indirectly from a positive or negative act of the victim himself, which he knows will produce this result — in order to exclude accidents, homicides, and deaths whose intention cannot be established. He then assembles official suicide statistics from France, Prussia, England, and other European countries and shows that the rates are strikingly stable from year to year within each society, while differing sharply between societies. Stability, he argues, cannot be an accident of individual motives: it indicates a social current, a *suicide rate* characteristic of the society itself, which constrains individuals from without. He proceeds by elimination, ruling out the explanations offered by his contemporaries: insanity, alcoholism, imitation, heredity, race, climate, and season each fail to account for the observed variations. What remains is social: the rate varies with the degree of integration into social groups and the degree of regulation by social norms.

## Four Types of Suicide

From the two dimensions of integration and regulation Durkheim derives four types. *Egoistic suicide* results from insufficient integration: the individual is detached from the groups that give life meaning, and the suicide rate rises among the unmarried, the childless, and Protestants, whose religion, Durkheim argues, integrates its members less tightly than Catholicism. *Altruistic suicide* results from excessive integration: the individual is so absorbed in the group that self-destruction becomes a duty — the soldier who dies for his regiment, or the self-sacrifice that some societies expected of widows, the elderly or the sick. *Anomic suicide* results from insufficient regulation: in times of rapid change — economic crisis, sudden prosperity, divorce — norms lose their hold, desires become limitless, and the suicide rate rises. *Fatalistic suicide*, from excessive regulation, is barely developed in the book; Durkheim mentions it in a footnote, with the example of the slave or the childless married woman, and it has remained the least theorised of the four. The typology's power is that it treats suicide as an indicator of the state of the social bond, not as a symptom of individual disorder.

## Reception, Criticism, and Legacy

The book helped establish the sociological monograph as a form and strengthened Durkheim's reputation as a founder of the discipline. Its influence runs through the whole of twentieth-century sociology: the use of official statistics, the comparative method, and the concepts of integration and regulation became standard equipment. Its criticisms are equally canonical. Jack Douglas argued in *The Social Meanings of Suicide* (1967) that official statistics are not neutral records but the products of coroners' and officials' own judgments, so that Durkheim's rates measure the process of classification as much as the act itself. Maurice Halbwachs, a younger member of the Durkheimian school, reworked the argument in *The Causes of Suicide* (1930), stressing urbanisation and the complexity of modern life over the simple opposition of integration and regulation. Others have noted that the book's data were unreliable — nineteenth-century suicide statistics undercounted the phenomenon and varied with the zeal of the recording authorities — and that its claims about religion, above all the Protestant–Catholic difference, have been seriously challenged by later research. Feminist critics have pointed out that the book's account of marriage and family reflects the patriarchal assumptions of its time. Yet the book's central claim has endured: suicide rates vary systematically with social conditions, and no account of suicide that ignores the social bond is complete. The book also supplied later sociology with its model of the monograph as a form of argument: a single phenomenon, pursued through definition, comparison, and elimination until only a social explanation remains. That structure — still imitated a century later — is as much Durkheim's legacy as any of the four types. Later sociological and psychological work on suicide, for example Jean Baechler's *Les suicides* (1975), has typically defined itself in dialogue with Durkheim.
