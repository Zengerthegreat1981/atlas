# The Social System

*The Social System* (Free Press, 1951) is a theoretical treatise by the American sociologist Talcott Parsons (1902-1979) of Harvard University. It develops a general conceptual scheme for analysing how patterns of social interaction are organised and maintained.

## Background

In *The Structure of Social Action* (1937) Parsons had built a theory of action from readings of Alfred Marshall, Vilfredo Pareto, Émile Durkheim and Max Weber. In the years around 1951 he worked within Harvard's Department of Social Relations and collaborated with Edward Shils and others on *Toward a General Theory of Action* (1951). *The Social System* applies that programme to social relations.

## The Framework

Parsons distinguishes three analytically separate but interdependent systems of action: the social system, the personality system and the cultural system. A social system consists of interaction among actors in situations. Its basic structural unit is the status-role, the position an actor occupies and the expected conduct attached to it. Order depends on shared value standards that are institutionalised in role expectations and internalised through socialisation, so that conforming can also satisfy the actor's own dispositions.

## Pattern Variables

Parsons classifies orientations by five pairs of alternatives: affectivity and affective neutrality, self-orientation and collectivity-orientation, universalism and particularism, achievement and ascription, and specificity and diffuseness. They allow relationships, such as those of kinship and of professional practice, to be compared.

## Socialisation, Deviance and Medicine

Substantial parts of the book treat how social systems reproduce themselves through socialisation and how they respond to deviance through mechanisms of social control. A chapter on modern medical practice presents the "sick role": the sick person is excused from some ordinary duties and not held responsible for the condition, but is expected to want to recover and to seek competent help. The physician's role is analysed as technically specific, affectively neutral, universalistic and oriented to the patient's welfare.

## Influence and Criticism

The book became a major statement of structural functionalism in American sociology and a reference point for medical sociology and the study of professions. The four-function AGIL scheme often associated with Parsons was developed mainly in subsequent collaborative work, including *Economy and Society* (1956) with Neil Smelser. C. Wright Mills attacked *The Social System* in *The Sociological Imagination* as an example of "grand theory". Other critics, including conflict theorists and Alvin Gouldner, argued that it gives too little weight to conflict, power and change and tends to favour the existing order. Defenders reply that the book discusses sources of strain and change and offers analytical categories, not an endorsement of any particular society.
