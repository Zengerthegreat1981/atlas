# Street Corner Society

*Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum* (University of Chicago Press, 1943) is a study by William Foote Whyte (1914-2000) of young men in a poor Italian American district of Boston, the North End, which he called "Cornerville". It is a classic of participant observation in urban sociology.

## Fieldwork

Whyte began the research in the later 1930s as a junior fellow of Harvard's Society of Fellows. He moved into the district, lived for a time with a local family, learned some Italian and spent several years with the people he studied. His principal guide and collaborator was a corner-group leader known in the book as "Doc", who introduced him to others and discussed the findings with him. Whyte later submitted the work for a doctorate at the University of Chicago.

## The Argument

The book contrasts two kinds of young men: the "corner boys", whose lives centred on a street corner and its nearby shops and eating places, and the "college boys", who sought advancement through education. Doc's group, the Nortons, is set beside an Italian community club associated with a college man called Chick. Corner loyalty and mutual obligation held a group together but could limit individual mobility, whereas upward movement often meant loosening those ties.

Whyte showed that rank within a group shaped everyday conduct. His best-known illustration concerns bowling, where members' performance tended to correspond to their standing in the group. He then traced links outward from the corner to racketeering, the police, local politics and the settlement house. His central conclusion was that Cornerville was not simply disorganised, as outsiders often assumed. It had an elaborate social organisation, one that was poorly connected to the institutions of the wider society.

## Method

The second edition (1955) added a long methodological appendix describing how Whyte entered the community, built relationships, made mistakes and recorded observations. This appendix became widely used in teaching field methods because it presents the researcher's own position and conduct as part of the research process.

## Reception and Debate

The book influenced the study of small groups, urban communities and informal social structure, and later community ethnographies such as Herbert Gans's *The Urban Villagers* (1962). In the early 1990s it became the subject of a methodological and ethical controversy after W. A. Marianne Boelen, who had revisited the district, challenged Whyte's account. The *Journal of Contemporary Ethnography* devoted an issue to the dispute in 1992, including Whyte's defence. The debate raised wider questions about restudies, the rights of research subjects and the verification of ethnographic claims. Other readers note that the book gives far more attention to men than to women.
