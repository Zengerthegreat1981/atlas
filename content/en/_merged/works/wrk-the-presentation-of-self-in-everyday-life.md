# The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life

*The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* is a book by the Canadian-born sociologist Erving Goffman (1922-1982). A first version was published as a monograph by the University of Edinburgh's Social Sciences Research Centre in 1956, and a revised and expanded edition appeared with Anchor Books in 1959. It analyses face-to-face interaction through the language of theatre.

## Background

Goffman trained at the University of Chicago. The book draws partly on his doctoral fieldwork in a small community in the Shetland Islands, alongside etiquette books, memoirs, novels and other researchers' studies of workplaces and institutions.

## The Dramaturgical Perspective

When people enter one another's presence, they seek information about each other and try to influence the definition of the situation. Goffman calls the activity by which a participant influences others on a given occasion a performance. He distinguishes the expression a person "gives", mainly deliberate verbal communication, from the expression a person "gives off", which others read from conduct.

## Key Concepts

A performance relies on a "front": the setting together with personal front, including appearance and manner. Performers may be sincere, believing in the part they play, or cynical about it. Performances tend to idealise, stressing officially valued standards and concealing what is inconsistent with them. Many are staged by "teams" whose members cooperate to maintain a shared definition. Goffman separates the "front region", where a performance is given, from the "back region" or backstage, where performers prepare, relax and drop their front, as with a restaurant dining room and kitchen. Further chapters examine discrepant roles, such as informers and go-betweens, communication out of character, and the arts of impression management. Tact by the audience helps performers recover from disruptions that cause embarrassment.

## Significance

The book shows that ordinary encounters have an order that participants work continuously to sustain. It shaped symbolic interactionism, microsociology and studies of work, identity and, more recently, self-presentation online. Goffman extended related themes in *Asylums* (1961), *Stigma* (1963) and *Interaction Ritual* (1967). He presented the theatrical vocabulary as a limited analytical device, not a claim that social life simply is theatre.

## Criticism

Critics argue that the approach can depict people as calculating managers of appearances, leaving little room for a self behind the performances, and that it pays limited attention to institutions, power and historical change. Alvin Gouldner read it as reflecting a middle-class world concerned with appearances. Others reply that Goffman's subject is the moral order of interaction itself, in which participants are obliged to protect one another's claims.
