# Political Systems of Highland Burma: A Study of Kachin Social Structure

*Political Systems of Highland Burma: A Study of Kachin Social Structure* (1954) is Edmund Leach's study of political organisation in the Kachin Hills. It rejects the assumption that a society is a stable, bounded system in equilibrium. Leach argues that people in the region lived among competing and inconsistent ideas of political order, and that choices made in pursuit of power could move local communities from one form toward another.

## Research and setting

Leach began fieldwork in the Kachin village of Hpalang in 1939. The war interrupted it, and he served in the army in Burma, gaining wider knowledge of the highlands; his field notes were lost. The book, written afterwards as an elaboration of his doctoral research and published with a foreword by Raymond Firth, draws on memory, wartime experience, and historical and administrative records. The people grouped as Kachin spoke several languages and lived near Shan populations of the valleys, who had states and irrigated rice cultivation. Leach argued that language, culture, and political organisation did not mark off one clear tribal unit.

## Shan, gumsa, and gumlao

The analysis uses two contrasting models. Shan polities were hierarchical states. The *gumlao* ideal of the hills rejected hereditary chiefs and asserted equality among lineages. Between them stood *gumsa* organisation, with ranked lineages and chiefs, which Leach treated as an unstable compromise. Ambitious *gumsa* chiefs might model themselves on Shan princes, straining kinship obligations to their followers and provoking a turn toward *gumlao* rejection of rank. *Gumlao* communities could in turn develop inequalities that led back toward *gumsa* forms. He envisaged oscillation over long periods, not a daily choice between two options.

## Marriage and rank

Among the Kachin, a pattern of marriage with the mother's brother's daughter links lineages as wife-givers (*mayu*) and wife-takers (*dama*). Leach showed how bridewealth, debt, and feasting connect this asymmetric alliance to rank: in the *gumsa* system wife-givers tend to stand above wife-takers. The study engages critically with Claude Lévi-Strauss's theory of generalised exchange, especially the claim that its internal tendencies must produce instability for particular reasons.

## Models, myth, and ritual

Leach distinguishes the anthropologist's models, and the ideals people state, from the untidy distribution of power on the ground. Models of equilibrium are useful fictions. Myth and ritual do not express one agreed structure: different versions of a story support rival claims to rank and land, so myth is a language of argument as much as of harmony. Ritual, in his account, makes statements about social status. He also suggests that people act to gain access to power and esteem, a general assumption that gives the analysis its attention to strategy.

## Criticism and later research

The book challenged the structural functionalism associated with Radcliffe-Brown and was later valued for its treatment of process and individual choice. Critics questioned the universal motive of seeking power and the adequacy of historical evidence for a cycle. Jonathan Friedman reanalysed the variation in terms of production, ecology, and expanding exchange. David Nugent argued that the opium trade and its collapse, along with wider regional political changes, better explained the nineteenth-century movements. The Kachin anthropologist Maran La Raw challenged parts of the account, including its translation of key political terms and its portrayal of the Kachin as passive subjects of history. A volume edited by François Robinne and Mandy Sadan in 2007 reconsidered the study against later research on ethnicity, history, and Kachin viewpoints. Leach wrote from a colonial setting, and the book's limited attention to the effects of British rule has been discussed. Women appear mainly as links in marriage alliances.

## Significance

The work influenced studies of ethnicity, upland Southeast Asia, kinship, and political process. Fredrik Barth's work on ethnic boundaries and later accounts of highland societies avoiding states, including James C. Scott's, took up questions it raised.
