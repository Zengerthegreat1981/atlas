# Coming of Age in Samoa: A Psychological Study of Primitive Youth for Western Civilisation

*Coming of Age in Samoa: A Psychological Study of Primitive Youth for Western Civilisation* (1928) is Margaret Mead's first book. It asks whether the stress then widely attributed to adolescence in the United States follows from puberty itself or from the conditions under which young people grow up. Mead answered, from her study of Samoan girls, that culture makes a great difference. The book reached a wide public and later became the focus of one of anthropology's best-known controversies.

## Research

A student of Franz Boas at Columbia University, Mead went to American Samoa in 1925, at the age of twenty-three, on a National Research Council fellowship. Her main work was carried out on the island of Ta'u in the Manu'a group, over several months of 1925 and 1926. She studied several dozen girls and young women from three neighbouring villages, using interviews, observation, household information, and some simple tests. She lodged with an American naval family rather than in a Samoan household and worked in a language she had only recently begun to learn.

## The argument

The book describes childhood, care of younger siblings, household and village organisation, work, dance, sexual relations, and conflict. Mead argues that for most of the girls adolescence was not a distinct period of crisis. Large households allowed a child in difficulty to move among relatives, attachments were comparatively diffuse, expected paths were few and familiar, and knowledge of birth, death, and sex was not hidden. Premarital sexual relations were, in her account, commonly tolerated for girls of ordinary rank, while the ceremonial village virgin, the *taupou*, was held to a strict standard. She also described girls who deviated or came into conflict with their families.

The final chapters turn to the United States. Mead linked adolescent difficulty there to conflicting standards and to the number of choices pressed on young people, and argued that education should prepare them to choose. She did not present Samoan life as a model to be copied whole; she regarded its ease as connected with a low intensity of personal feeling and limited scope for individuality.

## Reception and style

Written for general readers and published by William Morrow with a foreword by Boas, the book offered anthropology as evidence in debates about education, sexuality, and human nature. It answered theories, associated with G. Stanley Hall, that made adolescent storm and stress largely a matter of development. Its vivid generalisations helped its popularity and left readers uncertain how many cases supported particular statements.

## The Mead–Freeman controversy

Derek Freeman's *Margaret Mead and Samoa* (1983), published after Mead's death, argued that she was seriously wrong: Samoan society valued virginity strongly, showed rivalry, violence, and sexual aggression, and did not give adolescents the easy passage she described. Freeman charged that her expectations, shaped by Boasian opposition to biological explanation, misled her, and later argued that two Samoan companions had deceived her with joking stories. Many anthropologists replied that Freeman's evidence came largely from other places, periods, and social positions, including his own work in western Samoa years later, and that he misrepresented the Boasians as denying biology. Lowell Holmes, who restudied Ta'u, disputed parts of Mead's account while supporting its broad contrast. Paul Shankman's analysis of the field records rejected the hoaxing thesis. Disagreement about some descriptions remains, but the controversy does not reduce to a choice between culture and biology.

## Other critiques

Feminist readers have valued the attention to girls' lives while asking how far Mead's American concerns about sexuality framed her questions. Samoan critics and postcolonial scholars have objected to the image of a permissive South Seas society and to research conducted under United States naval administration, in which those described had little control over their representation. The term "primitive" in the subtitle reflects an evolutionary ranking that the book's own comparative argument did not escape. The work remains important for the question it posed and as a case study in evidence, restudy, and ethnographic authority.
