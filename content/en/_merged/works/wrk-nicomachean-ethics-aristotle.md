# Nicomachean Ethics

## About the Work

The *Nicomachean Ethics* (Ēthika Nikomacheia) is the best-known ethical work of Aristotle (384–322 BCE). It is probably a collection of lectures he delivered at his school, the Lyceum, in Athens. These were later edited, and the title is traditionally explained by reference to his son Nicomachus (or his father of the same name), though the origin of the name is uncertain. It consists of ten books. Although Aristotle also left the *Eudemian Ethics* as a parallel text, the *Nicomachean Ethics* has remained the most famous reference text in the history of Western moral philosophy. It is the cornerstone of what is known today as virtue ethics.

## Central Theses: Happiness as the Highest Good

Aristotle opens the work with a methodological observation: every art, inquiry, and action aims at some good, and some ends are means to other ends (such as wealth as a means to comfort). There must therefore be a supreme end sought for its own sake and not for the sake of anything else. This end is happiness (*eudaimonia*), a Greek term sometimes translated as "flourishing" or "the good life," to avoid confusion with happiness as a pleasant fleeting feeling in everyday usage. Aristotle means by it an objective state of complete human flourishing over the course of a life, not mere momentary enjoyment. He rejects defining happiness as pleasure (the life of bodily gratification), as honor (because honor depends on the esteem of others rather than on the worth of the person himself), or as wealth (because wealth is a means, not an end). He concludes that happiness is activity of the soul in accordance with virtue (and, if there are several virtues, the best and most complete). That is, the excellent exercise, throughout a complete life, of the characteristic function (*ergon*) of the human being, which is reason.

## The Doctrine of the Mean and the Moral Virtues

Aristotle distinguishes two kinds of virtue. The intellectual virtues (dianoetic), such as theoretical wisdom (*sophia*) and practical wisdom (*phronesis*), are acquired through teaching. The moral virtues (*ethike*), such as courage, temperance, and justice, are acquired through habit and repeated practice, not through theoretical instruction alone. "We become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts" (Ross's translation, II.1). Aristotle formulates his famous theory of the mean (*mesotes*): each moral virtue is a state between two vices, one of excess and the other of deficiency. Courage lies between rashness and cowardice, generosity between prodigality and meanness, and temperance between self-indulgence and insensibility. Aristotle clarifies that this mean is not a fixed arithmetic point for everyone, but relative to each individual in his circumstances ("the mean relative to us"). Determining it requires practical wisdom (*phronesis*), which the "wise man" (*phronimos*) possesses. He is able to perceive the right act in the particular situation, rather than mechanically applying a general rule.

## Friendship and the Contemplative Life

Aristotle devotes two complete books (the eighth and ninth) to friendship (*philia*), which he considers necessary for the happy life. He distinguishes three kinds: friendship of utility, friendship of pleasure, and friendship of virtue. The last alone is complete and perfect, grounded in love of the friend for his virtuous character rather than for the benefit or pleasure he brings. In the concluding tenth book, Aristotle returns to the question of the highest happiness. He concludes that the highest form of happiness is the contemplative life (*bios theoretikos*), that is, pure philosophical contemplation. This is because it is the human activity closest to the activity of the divine intellect, which contemplates itself eternally. He nonetheless acknowledges that most people live a "second-order" happiness through practical virtue in civic life.

## Reception and Historical Interpretations

The text reached the Islamic world through the translation movement in Baghdad. It was discussed by al-Farabi and by Ibn Rushd (Averroes), who wrote a middle commentary on the *Nicomachean Ethics* (preserved in Hebrew and Latin translations). Miskawayh engaged with it in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, combining the Aristotelian doctrine of the mean with the inheritance of Persian and Islamic wisdom, and producing an original synthesis specific to the Arabic tradition. In the Latin West, Thomas Aquinas wrote a detailed commentary (*Sententia libri Ethicorum*, 1270s) on the text and integrated it into his theological system through the theory of natural law. He regarded the Aristotelian virtues as a preparatory step, completed by the theological virtues of faith, hope, and charity. In the twentieth century, Elizabeth Anscombe revived interest in the text in her article "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958). She called for a return to the Aristotelian concept of virtue in place of the modern concept of "duty," which she saw as lacking a metaphysical foundation after the decline of the religious framework that had produced it. Alasdair MacIntyre developed this direction further in *After Virtue* (1981).

## Objections and Replies

Aristotle is objected to on the grounds that the theory of the mean is procedurally empty: it tells us to be "moderate" without a predetermined criterion for locating moderation. Its interpreters reply that this is not a flaw but an intended feature of the Aristotelian project. In his view, ethics is not an exact science capable of formulating rigid rules in advance, as mathematics is. It is a practical domain that requires a flexible perceptual judgment (*phronesis*), acquired through experience rather than by deduction. A further objection concerns the elitism of Aristotle's conception of happiness. The complete contemplative life seems available only to a limited class of free men with leisure, excluding slaves, women, and laborers from the possibility of complete happiness. This reflects a clear historical class and gender bias in Aristotle's political thought.

## Applied Example: Courage on the Battlefield

Aristotle illustrates his theory with the example of the soldier in battle. The coward flees at the slightest danger, which is a deficiency in facing fear. The rash man charges without assessing the real dangers, and destroys himself and his comrades pointlessly, which is an excess. The courageous man occupies the middle position. He feels the natural fear appropriate to the real danger, but faces it steadfastly because a noble end, such as defending the city, is worth the risk. This requires a precise judgment through practical wisdom to determine when steadfastness is courage and when it is pure rashness. That judgment cannot be reduced to a rigid, predetermined arithmetic rule.

## Contemporary Influence

The text has enjoyed an enormous revival in Anglo-American moral philosophy since the mid-twentieth century, through what is known as the virtue ethics revival. Contributors include Philippa Foot, Martha Nussbaum, and Rosalind Hursthouse. Virtue ethics has been established as a third alternative to the Kantian and utilitarian frameworks that dominate contemporary ethics. Its concepts, especially "human flourishing" and "capabilities," also influenced the Capability Approach developed by Martha Nussbaum and Amartya Sen in development economics.
