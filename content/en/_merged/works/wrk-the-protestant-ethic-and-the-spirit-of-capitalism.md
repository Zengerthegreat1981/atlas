# The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism

*The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* is a study by the German sociologist Max Weber (1864-1920). It first appeared as two essays in the *Archiv für Sozialwissenschaft und Sozialpolitik* in 1904-1905. Weber published a revised version in 1920 within his collected essays on the sociology of religion, and Talcott Parsons translated it into English in 1930.

## The Question

Weber asks how a particular attitude to economic life arose: the methodical pursuit of profit as a duty, combined with restraint in enjoying it. He calls this the "spirit" of modern capitalism and illustrates it with maxims of Benjamin Franklin. The desire for gain, he argues, is found in many societies; what needed explanation was its disciplined, rational form.

## The Argument

Weber begins with Martin Luther's idea of the calling (*Beruf*), which gave religious value to worldly work. He then turns to ascetic Protestantism, especially Calvinism, and also Pietism, Methodism and the Baptist sects. The Calvinist doctrine of predestination held that salvation was decided by God and could not be earned. Weber argues that this produced intense anxiety among believers, and that pastoral advice encouraged them to seek assurance through tireless, systematic work in a calling. Such "inner-worldly asceticism" discouraged luxury and idleness, so that earnings tended to be saved and reinvested.

Weber describes the link as one of affinity and historical contribution. He does not claim that Protestantism alone caused capitalism, and he explicitly declines to replace a one-sided materialist explanation with an equally one-sided religious one. He concludes that modern capitalism no longer needs its religious supports. Its order confines people regardless of belief, in the image that Parsons rendered as the "iron cage".

## Method

The study connects the meanings that actors attach to conduct with large historical outcomes. It constructs ideal types, such as the spirit of capitalism and ascetic Protestantism, and compares religious teachings, pastoral writings and patterns of conduct. Weber later pursued the comparative question in studies of the religions of China and India and of ancient Judaism.

## Debate

Criticism began in Weber's lifetime, and he replied to early reviewers. R. H. Tawney's *Religion and the Rise of Capitalism* (1926) gave greater weight to economic change in shaping religious teaching. Historians have pointed to capitalist development in Catholic regions before the Reformation, questioned Weber's reading of Puritan sources and disputed the evidence connecting belief with economic behaviour. Defenders answer that many objections attribute to Weber a simple causal thesis he did not hold. The book remains central to economic sociology, the sociology of religion and discussions of rationalisation.
