# Gang Leader for a Day: A Rogue Sociologist Takes to the Streets

A long-term ethnographic study carried out by the sociologist Sudhir Venkatesh, then a graduate student at the University of Chicago, through years of direct involvement in the life of a poor housing complex on Chicago's South Side controlled by the economy of drug gangs.

## Method and Content

Venkatesh began by trying to conduct a traditional survey questionnaire on urban poverty inside the "Robert Taylor Homes" housing complex. A local gang leader ("JT") detained him on suspicion, and later allowed him to accompany him and observe directly for years, including one day on which Venkatesh actually ran the gang's daily affairs under the leader's direction (the source of the book's title). The study documented in unusual detail the economic structure of the informal drug economy: the hierarchy of ranks and wages, the gang's complex relationship with the complex's residents (protection and extortion at once), and the continuous negotiation with the complex's residents and its official management and the police authority.

## Context

Venkatesh, the son of middle-class Indian immigrants, began his research in the late 1980s as a graduate student in sociology at the University of Chicago, influenced by the same long-established ethnographic tradition of the school that had produced studies such as William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* decades earlier. His field involvement in the Robert Taylor Homes lasted nearly seven years, a period in which Chicago's public housing was deteriorating; the Robert Taylor Homes were later demolished, between the late 1990s and 2007, under the Chicago Housing Authority's Plan for Transformation.

## Impact and Critique

Through prolonged field immersion, the study offered an ethnographic window on the economic structure of the informal economy in poor American neighborhoods. It also provoked a methodological and ethical debate about the limits of the researcher's immersion in activities that may be illegal for research purposes, echoing earlier debates raised by Laud Humphreys's *Tearoom Trade* (1970). Some academic colleagues criticized Venkatesh for getting so close to his subject that analytical neutrality may be threatened, and for not reporting to official authorities criminal activities he witnessed directly. Venkatesh has argued that deep involvement was what made this documentation possible.
