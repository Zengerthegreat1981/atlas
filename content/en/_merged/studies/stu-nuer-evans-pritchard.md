# The Nuer: Evans-Pritchard on Pastoral Kinship and Time

E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer: A Description of the Modes of Livelihood and Political Institutions of a Nilotic People* (Clarendon Press, 1940) is a classic ethnography of the Nuer, a cattle-keeping people of the Nile basin in what is now South Sudan. This entry gives particular attention to the first half of the book, on cattle, ecology and the Nuer ideas of time and space, and to the kinship and marriage practices he later described in *Kinship and Marriage Among the Nuer* (1951). The political and segmentary lineage dimension of the book is treated in a companion entry.

## Context

Evans-Pritchard (1902-1973) worked among the Nuer in several short visits between 1930 and 1936, amounting to about a year in all, with support from the Sudan government. The Nuer had been subject to British military campaigns ("pacification") in the 1920s and early 1930s, and he was at first treated with suspicion; his well-known descriptions of difficult fieldwork emphasize how little the Nuer volunteered and how dependent his data was on what he could observe and on what he asked. He became professor of social anthropology at Oxford in 1946, after the book was published.

## Cattle, Ecology and Social Life

The Nuer combine cattle herding with millet cultivation and fishing, moving between villages on higher ground in the rains and cattle camps near rivers in the dry season. Evans-Pritchard described a "cattle-centred" culture in which cattle are central to livelihood, to ritual (sacrifice), to bridewealth and compensation, and to personal names and speech. Many of the Nuer's ways of talking about people and relations draw on a cattle vocabulary, and personal identity is closely linked to a man's cattle and his ox-names.

## Time and Space

A well-known part of the book analyzes Nuer ideas of time and space. Evans-Pritchard distinguished **oecological time**, the cycle of the year as it is experienced in the shift between rains and dry season and in the pastoral round, and **structural time**, which is time reckoned through the relations among social groups, such as the depth of lineages and the succession of age-sets. He argued that the Nuer have no abstract concept of time as an independent entity and instead organize time through activities and social relationships, and he likewise distinguished ecological space from structural space (the social distance between groups). This was an influential demonstration that time and space are partly social categories, in the Durkheimian tradition, though it has been debated since.

## Kinship and Marriage

Kinship is closely tied to cattle through bridewealth. Marriage involves transfers of cattle from the groom's kin to the bride's kin, which establish the groom's rights in the woman's children and create bonds between their families. In *Kinship and Marriage Among the Nuer* (1951) Evans-Pritchard described unusual forms in which social paternity is distinct from biological paternity. In "ghost marriage," when a man dies without heirs, a kinsman marries a wife in the dead man's name and acts as the biological father (the genitor) while the dead man is recognized as the social father (the *pater*). In "woman-to-woman marriage," a woman who has no children may marry another woman by paying bridewealth; she becomes the social father of the children, whose biological father is a man engaged to provide this service. These cases showed that kinship statuses are defined by social and legal rights rather than by biological parentage alone, and became standard examples in discussions of "social paternity."

## Reception and Critique

The book remains one of the best-known ethnographies, but critics note that it presents a timeless picture of a society undergoing violent change under British rule, that its account of segmentary politics is idealized, and that women's views and activities are underrepresented. Later scholars, including Raymond Kelly (*The Nuer Conquest*, 1985), explained Nuer expansion into Dinka territory in the nineteenth century through features of their social and economic organization, notably the demands of bridewealth, and Sharon Hutchinson (*Nuer Dilemmas*, 1996) analyzed changes in the relation between cattle, money and the state. Even so, *The Nuer* is valued for its detailed attention to cattle, environment and language.
