# Howard S. Becker

Howard Saul Becker (1928-2023) was an American sociologist in the Chicago interactionist tradition, known for his work on deviance, occupations, art and the craft of research.

## Life and Context

Becker was born in Chicago in 1928 and played piano professionally as a young man. He studied at the University of Chicago, where Everett C. Hughes was an important teacher, and received his doctorate in 1951. After research appointments he became a professor at Northwestern University in the 1960s and moved to the University of Washington in the 1990s. He later lived in San Francisco, spent time regularly in France, where his work found a wide readership, and died in 2023.

## Main Ideas

- **Collective action:** Becker asked how people do things together, and treated institutions as ongoing coordinated activity.
- **Deviance as a social process:** groups create deviance by making rules and applying them to particular people, who may then be labelled outsiders.
- **Careers:** involvement in an activity develops in stages, in which people learn skills, meanings and identities.
- **Moral enterprise:** rules result from the initiative of people who campaign for them and agencies that enforce them.
- **Art worlds:** artworks depend on networks of cooperation among artists, suppliers, distributors, critics and audiences, linked by shared conventions.
- **Hierarchy of credibility:** members of organizations tend to grant those at the top the right to define how things are, so research that takes subordinates seriously appears biased.

## Case Studies

His article "Becoming a Marihuana User" (1953) traced how a person learns to smoke, to recognize the effects and to enjoy them. His studies of dance musicians showed how an occupational group distinguished itself from the audiences it served. *Boys in White* described how medical students at the University of Kansas collectively worked out what to study and how to meet faculty demands.

## Method

Becker relied on fieldwork and interviews and emphasized building explanations step by step from cases, including the search for evidence that challenges an emerging idea. He wrote plainly and urged others to do the same, treating methods as practical problems of work.

## Influence and Criticism

His work was influential in the sociology of deviance, work, education and culture. Critics of labelling theory argued that it says too little about why rules are broken in the first place and gives limited attention to power at the level of social structure; Becker did not regard *Outsiders* as a complete causal theory. Alvin Gouldner criticized the form of partisanship he associated with Becker's position. Some scholars of the arts contrast Becker's cooperative "worlds" with Pierre Bourdieu's competitive "fields".
