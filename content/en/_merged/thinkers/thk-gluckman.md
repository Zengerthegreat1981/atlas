# Max Gluckman: Ritual, Conflict, and Social Drama

Max Gluckman (1911–1975) was a South African-born social anthropologist whose work centred on conflict, law, ritual, and social change. He founded the Department of Social Anthropology and Sociology at the University of Manchester and led the research circle known as the Manchester School. His best-known argument is that open conflict, including ritualised rebellion against those in authority, can in certain political systems help to reaffirm the order it appears to challenge.

## Life and career

Gluckman was born in Johannesburg to Jewish parents who had come from the Russian Empire. At the University of the Witwatersrand he turned from law to social anthropology, studying with Winifred Hoernlé among others, and went to Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, where he completed a doctorate in 1936. The work of A. R. Radcliffe-Brown and Evans-Pritchard strongly influenced him, and he also read Marx and Freud closely.

He did field research in Zululand from 1936 to 1938. In 1939 he joined the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute in Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia), which he later directed, and he studied the Lozi, or Barotse, of the upper Zambezi. After a lectureship at Oxford he moved in 1949 to the chair at Manchester, which he held for most of the rest of his life. He maintained close ties with the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute, whose researchers often passed through Manchester. He died in 1975.

## The social situation

*Analysis of a Social Situation in Modern Zululand*, published in parts from 1940, begins with the ceremonial opening of a bridge attended by officials, a Zulu regent, missionaries, and local people. From the conduct of those present Gluckman traced relations of cooperation and division between Black and white South Africans. He insisted that both belonged to a single social system marked by deep cleavage, instead of describing the Zulu as an isolated tribal society disturbed by outside contact. The use of an observed event to open up wider structure became a model for later situational analysis.

## Conflict and cohesion

*Custom and Conflict in Africa* (1955), based on radio lectures, argues that people are divided by some loyalties and joined by others. In the feud, ties of marriage, residence, and kinship that cut across opposed groups create pressure for settlement. Cross-cutting allegiances can thus contain disputes. Gluckman also separated rebellion, which seeks to replace those who hold office, from revolution, which seeks to change the offices and the social order itself. He argued that some African kingdoms experienced repeated rebellions that left the institution of kingship in place.

## Rituals of rebellion

His Frazer Lecture, published in 1954 as *Rituals of Rebellion in South-East Africa*, examined ceremonies in which tensions are openly expressed. One case was a Zulu agricultural rite in which women adopted men's dress and tasks and behaved with licence. Another was the Swazi *incwala*, a first-fruits and kingship ceremony known to him largely through Hilda Kuper's research, which he read as dramatising hostility toward the king before affirming his position. Gluckman held that such rites were possible where people disputed the holders of authority without questioning the system, and that in this setting protest could renew social unity.

## Law and reasoning

*The Judicial Process among the Barotse of Northern Rhodesia* (1955) analyses cases heard in Lozi courts. Gluckman showed judges weighing conduct against the standard of a reasonable person in a given social position and seeking to repair relationships among parties who would continue to live together. Flexible concepts allowed general rules to be applied to varied cases. *The Ideas in Barotse Jurisprudence* (1965) examined concepts of property, obligation, and liability. His claim that Lozi legal reasoning could be compared with Western jurisprudence led to a long debate with Paul Bohannan, who warned that translating local concepts into European legal categories would distort them.

## The Manchester School

Colleagues and students associated with Manchester and the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute included J. Clyde Mitchell, Elizabeth Colson, John Barnes, Victor Turner, A. L. Epstein, and Jaap van Velsen. They studied villages, mines, towns, courts, and factories, using detailed cases followed over time. Van Velsen later described this as the extended-case method and situational analysis. Turner's concept of social drama developed in this setting.

## Assessment

Critics argue that explaining rebellion as a source of cohesion retains a functionalist bias toward equilibrium and cannot easily show when ritual protest fails or turns into real change. Later historians and anthropologists questioned his reading of the *incwala* as an expression of hostility. Others note limited attention to women's own interpretations. His treatment of colonial Africa as one divided society and his attention to process remain influential in political and legal anthropology.
