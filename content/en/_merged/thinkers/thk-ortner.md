# Sherry Ortner: Practice Theory and Ritual Agency

Sherry B. Ortner (born 1941) is an American cultural anthropologist known for work in practice theory, feminist anthropology, symbolic analysis, Sherpa ethnography, and the study of class and public culture in the United States. A recurring question in her writing is how people formed by relations of power and cultural meaning nonetheless act, with intentions of their own, in ways that may reproduce or change those relations.

## Life and career

Born in Newark, New Jersey, Ortner studied at Bryn Mawr College and received her doctorate in anthropology from the University of Chicago in 1970, where Clifford Geertz was among her teachers. She carried out her first extended fieldwork among Sherpas in northeastern Nepal in the later 1960s and returned for further research in later decades. She taught at Sarah Lawrence College, the University of Michigan, the University of California, Berkeley, Columbia University, and the University of California, Los Angeles. She received a MacArthur Fellowship in 1990.

## Gender and symbolism

"Is Female to Male as Nature Is to Culture?" (1972; revised in *Woman, Culture, and Society*, 1974) asked why women's subordination seemed so widespread. Ortner proposed that women are culturally seen as closer to nature than men, because of bodily processes, social roles linked to child-rearing, and ideas about psychic structure, while culture is valued as transcending nature. The argument was about symbolic construction, not natural inferiority. Critics showed that the opposition and its ranking vary or may be absent. Ortner revisited the essay in *Making Gender* (1996), conceding some points while defending the importance of looking for broad patterns. With Harriet Whitehead she edited *Sexual Meanings* (1981), which linked gender ideas to systems of prestige.

## Symbols and ritual

"On Key Symbols" (1973) distinguished summarising symbols, which condense a set of attitudes, from elaborating symbols, which provide categories or scenarios for ordering action. *Sherpas through Their Rituals* (1978) began from public rites, including hospitality, temple festivals, offerings, and funerals, to uncover tensions in Sherpa social life, among them those between household self-interest and the Buddhist ideals of detachment and compassion, and between laity and clergy.

## Practice theory

"Theory in Anthropology since the Sixties" (1984) surveyed symbolic anthropology, cultural ecology, structuralism, and political economy, and identified practice as an emerging orientation. Drawing on discussions associated with Pierre Bourdieu, Anthony Giddens, and Marshall Sahlins, she described the task as explaining how society produces actors, how actors produce society, and how history enters both. The essay did much to name and organise this approach in anthropology, though Ortner presented it as a convergence of many authors' work.

Her later idea of "serious games" emphasises that actors pursue culturally defined projects within relations of inequality and solidarity. *Anthropology and Social Theory* (2006) develops this discussion and treats subjectivity and agency, arguing against accounts that reduce people to effects of discourse or structure.

## History, mountaineering, and class

*High Religion* (1989) studies the founding of Sherpa monasteries and nunneries in the early twentieth century through status rivalry, new wealth, and cultural scripts. *Life and Death on Mt. Everest* (1999) examines relations between Sherpas and foreign mountaineers across the twentieth century, including the risks borne by paid climbers and the changing ways each side understood the other.

*New Jersey Dreaming* (2003) follows members of Ortner's own high school class of 1958 to examine class, ethnicity, race, and mobility. *Not Hollywood* (2013) studies American independent film. In "Dark Anthropology and Its Others" (2016) she reflected on the discipline's turn to inequality and suffering alongside work on ethics, wellbeing, and resistance.

## Assessment

Ortner's syntheses are widely used to explain how culture, power, and action can be studied together. Critics ask whether an emphasis on intentional projects gives too little weight to unreflective habit, affect, or nonhuman forces, and whether practice theory is a unified theory at all. Her insistence on culture as a source of both constraint and motivation, joined to history and political economy, remains central to the debate.
