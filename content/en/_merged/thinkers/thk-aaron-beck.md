# Aaron Beck

American psychiatrist (born in Providence, Rhode Island, 1921; died 2021), professor emeritus in the Department of Psychiatry at the University of Pennsylvania, widely regarded as the originator of **cognitive therapy**, and a figure who strongly shaped psychotherapy in the second half of the twentieth century. He graduated from Brown University and then from Yale Medical School, and began his training as a psychoanalyst before gradually moving away from Freudian assumptions.

## What He Contributed

### The Cognitive Model

In the 1960s, while treating depressed patients in a psychoanalytic framework, Beck observed that what explains their suffering is not repressed unconscious conflicts, but **automatic negative thoughts** about the self, the world, and the future, which he later called the "cognitive triad." He developed the idea that these thoughts arise from **schemas** formed early in life, and that directly modifying them, rather than exploring the unconscious, is what relieves the symptoms. He set out this framework in *Depression: Clinical, Experimental, and Theoretical Aspects* (1967) and *Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders* (1976), and in the treatment manual *Cognitive Therapy of Depression* (with Rush, Shaw, and Emery, 1979). Earlier, he had tested the psychoanalytic idea that depression reflects inverted hostility by studying the dreams of depressed patients, and reported themes of loss and defeat rather than hostility, which contributed to his turn away from the psychoanalytic view.

### The Beck Depression Inventory

In 1961 he designed the **BDI**, a 21-item questionnaire for assessing the severity of depressive symptoms. It became one of the most widely used psychological measurement tools in clinical practice and research around the world, and revised versions followed later (BDI-II, 1996).

### Establishing Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) as a Therapeutic Movement

Beck did not stop at formulating a theoretical model; he built organized therapeutic protocols that could be tested experimentally. Early trials, including a 1977 comparison with imipramine by Rush and colleagues, helped make cognitive therapy one of the most extensively researched psychotherapies, and it is widely used in mental health services today. It later merged with other behavioral traditions to form what is generally known as "cognitive behavioral therapy" (CBT). In 1994, together with his daughter Judith Beck, he founded the Beck Institute for Cognitive Behavior Therapy to train therapists and disseminate the approach.

### Extending the Model to Other Disorders

Beck later extended the application of his cognitive model to anxiety, personality disorders, schizophrenia, and suicidal tendencies. He developed, with colleagues, complementary measures such as the Beck Hopelessness Scale (1974) and the Scale for Suicide Ideation (1979), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (1988), and, with Emery and Greenberg, a cognitive account of anxiety in *Anxiety Disorders and Phobias* (1985). With Arthur Freeman he applied the model to personality disorders (1990). With Neil Rector he published a 2005 review of cognitive approaches to schizophrenia, and with Rector, Neal Stolar, and Paul Grant the book *Schizophrenia: Cognitive Theory, Research, and Therapy* (2009). The symptom scales are screening and monitoring aids, not diagnostic tests.

## Impact

Aaron Beck, together with Albert Ellis, who developed rational emotive therapy, is a central figure in the cognitive therapy tradition. He received major honours for his work, including the Lasker-DeBakey Clinical Medical Research Award (2006). He died on 1 November 2021, aged 100.

## Debates

Critics have questioned how much of the benefit of cognitive therapy is due to changing thoughts as opposed to behavioural activation or common therapeutic factors, and some meta-analyses suggest that effect sizes in trials have declined over time. The BDI and related scales also depend on self-report. These debates concern the evidence for the approach and do not by themselves settle its value for any individual.
