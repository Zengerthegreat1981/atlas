# Marsha M. Linehan

An American clinical psychologist (1943–2026), professor at the University of Washington, and developer of Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), one of the most empirically documented psychological treatments for borderline personality disorder and chronic suicidal behavior.

## Personal and Professional Path

Linehan earned her doctorate in psychology, with clinical training, from Loyola University Chicago in 1971, worked for several years at the Catholic University of America, and joined the University of Washington in 1977, where she spent decades developing DBT and testing it empirically. In 2011 she publicly disclosed that at the age of seventeen she had suffered a severe psychiatric disorder that required hospitalization, and that she had been given a diagnosis of schizophrenia at the time; she later said her difficulties were more consistent with borderline personality disorder. She told her story at length in her memoir *Building a Life Worth Living* (2020). The disclosure drew wide attention as a rare case of a therapist who developed a treatment for a disorder she had herself experienced. She died in Seattle on 10 November 2026, aged 83.

## Contributions

- **Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)**: She developed it during the 1980s and early 1990s as a structured treatment for patients at chronic risk of suicide who have borderline personality disorder. It combines cognitive-behavioral techniques with mindfulness practices influenced by Zen Buddhist meditation, together with skills for emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness.
- **The central principle of "dialectics"**: The philosophy of the treatment rests on balancing two apparently opposed but complementary poles: **acceptance** (the patient's acceptance of themselves and their experience as it is) and **change** (active work to modify harmful behavioral and emotional patterns). This balance gives the therapy its name.
- **A multi-track treatment structure**: The standard protocol combines individual sessions, skills-training groups, telephone coaching between sessions, and supervision meetings for the therapists themselves. This structure was designed specifically to meet the challenges of treating patients at high risk of suicide.
- **Empirical evidence**: Linehan's first randomized controlled trial (1991) found fewer parasuicidal acts and less treatment dropout than treatment as usual among women with borderline personality disorder, and later trials, including a 2006 comparison with community experts, have generally supported its benefits for self-harm. It is among the best-supported psychological treatments for this population, although effects on some outcomes are modest and trial quality varies.
- **The biosocial theory**: Linehan proposed that borderline personality disorder arises from the transaction between a biologically based vulnerability to intense, rapidly escalating emotion and an *invalidating environment* that dismisses or punishes emotional expression. Therapy therefore combines validation with change-oriented problem solving.
- **The four skills modules**: skills training teaches mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness, usually in a structured group format.

## Legacy

DBT has been adapted for other conditions marked by difficulty regulating emotion, such as eating disorders and substance use disorders, and for adolescents (DBT-A), although the evidence for these adaptations is less extensive than for borderline personality disorder. It is widely taught in psychotherapy training programs.

## Limitations

Full DBT requires an intensive institutional structure (a multidisciplinary treatment team, specialized training, and a long-term time commitment). This limits its availability in health systems with limited resources. Most of the evidence for its effectiveness also comes from Western samples. This entry is educational and not clinical advice.
