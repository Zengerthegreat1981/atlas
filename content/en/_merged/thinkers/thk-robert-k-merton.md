# Robert K. Merton

Robert King Merton (1910-2003) was an American sociologist whose work on theory of the middle range, anomie, functional analysis and the sociology of science became standard reference points in the discipline.

## Life and Context

Born Meyer R. Schkolnick in Philadelphia to Jewish immigrants from eastern Europe, Merton studied at Temple University and took his doctorate at Harvard in 1936, where he worked with Pitirim Sorokin, Talcott Parsons and the historian of science George Sarton. After teaching at Harvard and Tulane, he joined Columbia University in 1941. There he collaborated for many years with Paul Lazarsfeld at the Bureau of Applied Social Research. He served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1957 and received the National Medal of Science in 1994. He died in New York in 2003.

## Main Ideas

- **Theories of the middle range.** Against all-embracing systems, he called for theories limited enough to be tested yet general enough to connect separate findings.
- **Functional analysis.** He distinguished manifest from latent functions, introduced the notion of dysfunction and functional alternatives, and rejected the assumption that every institution is indispensable or good for the whole society.
- **Anomie and strain.** "Social Structure and Anomie" (1938) located deviance in the gap between culturally prized goals and access to legitimate means, with five adaptations: conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism and rebellion.
- **Unintended consequences.** A 1936 article analysed why purposive social action so often produces unanticipated results.
- **Self-fulfilling prophecy.** In 1948 he showed how a false definition of a situation can produce conduct that makes it come true.
- **Roles and reference groups.** He developed the concepts of role-set and reference group behaviour.

## Sociology of Science

His study of seventeenth-century England (1938) linked Puritan values to the rise of experimental science. He later described a normative structure of science, usually summarized as communalism, universalism, disinterestedness and organized skepticism. He also examined priority disputes, multiple discoveries and the "Matthew effect", the tendency for eminent scientists to receive disproportionate credit.

## Method

Merton insisted on a two-way relation between theory and research. His study of a wartime war-bond broadcast, *Mass Persuasion* (1946), and his work on the focused interview showed how close empirical inquiry could refine concepts.

## Influence and Criticism

Strain theory shaped criminology, including work by Albert Cohen, Richard Cloward and Lloyd Ohlin, and later Robert Agnew. His vocabulary spread far beyond sociology. Critics argued that the anomie scheme assumes a broad consensus on goals and explains some forms of deviance poorly, that functional analysis gives limited weight to power and conflict, and that actual scientific practice departs from the stated norms. Constructivist studies of science went further by examining how the content of knowledge itself is socially produced.

## Major Works

- *Social Theory and Social Structure* (1949; enlarged 1957 and 1968)
- *On the Shoulders of Giants* (1965)
- *The Sociology of Science* (1973)
