# Victor Turner: Ritual, Symbol, and Social Drama

Victor Witter Turner (1920–1983) was a British anthropologist, later based in the United States, whose studies of ritual and symbol reached religious studies, literature, history, and performance studies. He moved the analysis of ritual toward process: how conflicts unfold, how symbols act on participants, and how people pass between social positions.

## Life and career

Born in Glasgow, Turner began studying English literature at University College London. The Second World War interrupted his studies; a conscientious objector, he served in a non-combatant role that included bomb disposal. He returned to study anthropology, graduating in 1949, and then worked at Manchester under Max Gluckman. Between 1950 and 1954 he did fieldwork among the Ndembu in Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia) for the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute, together with his wife Edith Turner, a collaborator throughout his life. After completing his doctorate at Manchester and working there, he moved to the United States, teaching at Cornell University, the University of Chicago, and the University of Virginia. He died in 1983.

## Social drama

*Schism and Continuity in an African Society* (1957) examines Ndembu village disputes arising from the tension between matrilineal descent and residence in the husband's village. Turner analysed sequences he called social dramas in four phases: breach of a norm, mounting crisis, redressive action through informal, legal, or ritual means, and either reintegration or recognised schism. Later he applied the model to historical and political events, for example in *Dramas, Fields, and Metaphors* (1974).

## Symbols

*The Forest of Symbols* (1967) treats the symbol as the smallest unit of ritual. Dominant symbols condense many referents and join disparate ideas. Turner described two poles of meaning: one concerning moral and social norms, the other sensory experiences and desires. The milk tree of girls' initiation, which exudes a white latex, evokes breast milk, the bond of mother and child, matrilineal continuity, and womanhood. To interpret such symbols he combined local explanation (exegetical meaning), observation of how they are used (operational meaning), and their relation to other symbols (positional meaning). His discussions with the ritual expert Muchona were an important source. He also analysed boys' circumcision (*mukanda*), girls' puberty rites (*nkang'a*), hunters' cults, and cults of affliction.

## Liminality and communitas

Developing Arnold van Gennep's account of rites of passage, Turner focused on the middle or liminal phase, in which persons are "betwixt and between" recognised positions. Novices may be secluded, stripped of rank, treated as dead or unborn, instructed, and confronted with strange combinations of familiar things. *The Ritual Process* (1969) pairs structure, the differentiated order of positions, with anti-structure. Communitas is a direct sense of fellowship among people temporarily released from those distinctions. He distinguished spontaneous communitas from ideological accounts of it and from normative communitas, in which it is organised into a lasting way of life, as in some religious orders. Society, he argued, involves a movement between structure and communitas.

## Pilgrimage, play, and performance

With Edith Turner he wrote *Image and Pilgrimage in Christian Culture* (1978), treating pilgrimage as a largely voluntary departure from ordinary ties. Turner called many leisure and artistic activities in industrial societies liminoid: they resemble liminal states but are typically chosen, individualised, and open to critique of existing institutions. Collaboration with the theatre director Richard Schechner and essays collected in *From Ritual to Theatre* (1982) connected ritual and stage performance. *The Anthropology of Experience* (1986), edited with Edward Bruner, appeared after his death.

## Assessment

Turner's vocabulary has been applied far beyond its Ndembu origins, sometimes loosely. Critics argue that the four-phase drama and the opposition of structure to anti-structure can impose a single pattern. Studies of pilgrimage, notably *Contesting the Sacred* (1991), edited by John Eade and Michael Sallnow, showed that division and competing interests persist where communitas was expected. Caroline Walker Bynum argued that his model of liminality fitted medieval men's life stories better than women's. His work remains important for joining close ethnography to a theory of how symbolic action changes the people who take part.
