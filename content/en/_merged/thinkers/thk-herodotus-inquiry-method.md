# Herodotus: Inquiry and Oral Sources as Historical Foundation

**Herodotus of Halicarnassus** (c. 484 – c. 425 BCE), designated by the Roman orator Cicero as the "father of history" (*pater historiae*), made *historiē* (inquiry or investigation) the organising idea of an influential new kind of prose narrative. Rather than deriving the human past from epic poetry, divine mythology, or genealogical verse, Herodotus traveled across the Mediterranean and the Near East to conduct field inquiries, cross-examine living witnesses, and compile oral traditions regarding the causes and course of the Greco-Persian Wars (499–449 BCE).

## Historical Context and Travels

Born in the Carian city of Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum, Turkey) during the Persian Empire's hegemony over Ionia, Herodotus was positioned at the crossroads of Greek and Asian civilizations. Following political exile, he undertook extensive journeys across the ancient world:
- **Egypt (mid-fifth century BCE)**: Navigating the Nile as far south as Elephantine (Aswan), consulting Egyptian priests, and recording their chronological records and temple customs.
- **The Near East and the Levant**: Visiting Tyre, Babylon, and Mesopotamia, gathering details on Persian administration and eastern religious rituals.
- **The Black Sea (Pontus) and Scythia**: Observing nomadic steppe societies, trade routes, and funerary customs.
- **Athens and Southern Italy**: Presenting public recitations in Periclean Athens and, according to later tradition, joining the Panhellenic colony of Thurii in Magna Graecia; his *Histories* are divided into nine books.

## The Method of Inquiry (*Historiē*)

In the preface to his *Histories*, Herodotus articulates his dual objective: to preserve the memory of great human deeds from decay, and to explain why Greeks and barbarians fought one another. This investigation was structured around distinct methodological practices:

1. **Autopsy and Oral Interrogation**: Scholars commonly describe three kinds of grounds in his text: *opsis* (autopsy, what he saw with his own eyes), *gnōmē* (judgment or reasoning), and *akoē* (oral report, what he heard from others). He often marks which of these he is relying on and acknowledges when an account comes from informants.
2. **Comparative Sifting of Conflicting Traditions**: When confronted with competing versions of an event, Herodotus recorded them side by side, leaving final judgment to the reader. He states (*Histories* 7.152, paraphrased) that he is obliged to report what is said but not obliged to believe all of it, and that this applies to his whole work.
3. **Integration of Ethnography and Geography**: Herodotus recognized that political events cannot be divorced from geographic and environmental conditions. His accounts of the Nile floods, Persian geography, and Scythian climate served as integral analytical components of his historical explanation.

## Modern Re-evaluation and Archaeological Corroboration

Some ancient critics, notably Plutarch (*On the Malice of Herodotus*), accused Herodotus of bias and of telling sensational tales, and his reliability has remained debated. Modern research, including the decipherment of the Behistun Inscription and the excavation of Scythian burial mounds (kurgans) along the Black Sea coast, has corroborated some of his reports while leaving others doubtful. Modern historiography, influenced by scholars such as Arnaldo Momigliano and François Hartog, tends to view Herodotus not as a naive chronicler but as a sophisticated early practitioner of ethnographic inquiry and comparison.
