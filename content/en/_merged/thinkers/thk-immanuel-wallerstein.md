# Immanuel Wallerstein

Immanuel Wallerstein (1930-2019) was an American historical social scientist who developed world-systems analysis, which studies capitalism as a single economy spanning many states.

## Life and Context

Wallerstein was born in New York City in 1930 and studied at Columbia University, receiving his doctorate in 1959. His early research concerned African politics and decolonization. He taught at Columbia until the early 1970s, then at McGill University, and from 1976 at Binghamton University, where he directed the Fernand Braudel Center. He later became a senior research scholar at Yale. He served as president of the International Sociological Association from 1994 to 1998 and chaired the Gulbenkian Commission on the restructuring of the social sciences. He died in 2019.

## Main Ideas

- **Unit of analysis:** national societies cannot be understood separately from the historical system of which they are parts.
- **World-economy:** the modern world-system emerged in the "long sixteenth century" as a capitalist economy with one division of labor and many polities.
- **Core, semi-periphery and periphery:** unequal exchange between zones with different production processes transfers surplus toward the core; the semi-periphery occupies an intermediate position.
- **Capital accumulation:** the system is driven by the pursuit of endless accumulation and moves through cycles and long-term trends.
- **Hegemony:** at particular periods one state, such as the Dutch Republic, Britain or the United States, gains a temporary preeminence.
- **Structural crisis:** he argued that the capitalist world-system was in a crisis that would lead to a different system whose form was not predetermined.
- **Unidisciplinarity:** he criticized the separation of economics, political science, sociology and history.

## Major Works

- *Africa: The Politics of Independence* (1961)
- *The Modern World-System*, four volumes (1974, 1980, 1989, 2011)
- *Historical Capitalism* (1983)
- *Open the Social Sciences* (1996, report of the Gulbenkian Commission)
- *World-Systems Analysis: An Introduction* (2004)

## Case Study

The first volume of *The Modern World-System* contrasts northwestern Europe, which became a core with relatively strong states and wage and tenant labor, with eastern Europe and Spanish America, where coerced labor produced goods for the wider market.

## Method

He combined comparative history with theory, drawing on Marx, Fernand Braudel and the Annales school, and dependency theory, and relying chiefly on secondary historical scholarship over long periods.

## Influence and Criticism

World-systems analysis shaped historical sociology, development studies and research on globalization. Theda Skocpol argued that it reduced state power and politics to economic position. Robert Brenner criticized it for explaining capitalism through trade and the market more than through class relations of production. Others have questioned its functional explanations, its limited attention to culture and its account of Europe's rise; Andre Gunder Frank argued for a much older and less Europe-centered world system.

## Cross-References

Compare Marx on accumulation, Braudel on long duration, and Frank and Cardoso on dependency.
