# Barbara Myerhoff: Ritual, Aging, and Self-Creation

Barbara Myerhoff (1935–1985) was an American anthropologist known for studies of ritual, pilgrimage, ageing, storytelling, and reflexivity. Her best-known work, on elderly Jews at a community centre in Venice, California, showed how people sustain identity and claim attention through narrative and public performance.

## Life and career

Born in Cleveland, Myerhoff studied at the University of California, Los Angeles, and the University of Chicago, and received a doctorate in anthropology from UCLA in 1968. She taught at the University of Southern California, where she also chaired the anthropology department. She worked with filmmakers and folklorists and wrote for audiences beyond the university. She died of cancer in January 1985.

## The Huichol peyote pilgrimage

Myerhoff's early research concerned the Huichol, or Wixárika, of western Mexico. Working with the anthropologist Peter Furst and the ritual specialist Ramón Medina Silva, she took part in a pilgrimage to Wirikuta, the desert region where peyote is gathered. *Peyote Hunt: The Sacred Journey of the Huichol Indians* (1974) analyses the linked symbols of deer, maize, and peyote. In her interpretation, the pilgrimage temporarily restores a mythic condition in which pilgrims take the identities of ancestral beings and ordinary distinctions are reversed. Later scholars have debated how far the account of a single, closely involved group and its leading narrator represents Huichol practice.

## *Number Our Days*

In the 1970s Myerhoff studied members of the Israel Levin Senior Adult Center, many of whom had emigrated from Eastern Europe as young people and had outlived the world of their childhood, including relatives killed in the Holocaust. Poverty, distance from children, and public inattention marked their old age. A documentary based on the research, *Number Our Days*, directed by Lynne Littman, won an Academy Award, and Myerhoff's book of the same title appeared in 1978.

The book combines participant observation, life histories, accounts of classes, disputes and celebrations, and reflection on her own position as a younger Jewish woman who expected one day to be old. It presents some conversations in reconstructed form, including an imagined continuing dialogue with a member who had died. Quarrels at the centre are treated as part of social life, not as a failure of community.

## Definitional ceremonies and re-membering

Myerhoff used the term definitional ceremony for performances through which people who feel unseen present their own account of who they are before witnesses. Her examples include a public march organised by centre members after one of them was fatally injured, and celebrations in which participants shaped how they wished to be remembered. She wrote of "re-membering" as the purposeful gathering of the figures and episodes that belong to a person's life, distinct from passive recollection. Narrative therapists, notably Michael White, later adopted both ideas.

## Ritual and reflexivity

With Sally Falk Moore she edited *Secular Ritual* (1977), which argued that ritual form can give an appearance of order and continuity to matters that are uncertain and need not involve religion. Her essays drew on Victor Turner's work on liminality and performance. With Jay Ruby she introduced reflexive perspectives in anthropology in the volume *A Crack in the Mirror* (1982), distinguishing deliberate attention to the process of making knowledge from mere self-absorption.

Late research took her to the Fairfax neighbourhood of Los Angeles, where she studied Orthodox Jewish life while ill. The film *In Her Own Time* (1985), made with Littman, records that work and her own search for support. Essays collected by Marc Kaminsky appeared as *Remembered Lives* (1992).

## Assessment

Her work is valued in the anthropology of ageing, performance studies, folklore, Jewish studies, and life-history research. Critics and sympathetic readers alike have asked how editing and reconstruction shape voices presented with great immediacy, and whether an emphasis on creative self-definition gives enough weight to constraint and material hardship. These questions follow from the reflexivity she encouraged.
