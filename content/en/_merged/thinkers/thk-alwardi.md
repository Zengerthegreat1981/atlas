# Ali Al-Wardi

Ali Al-Wardi (1913-1995) was an Iraqi sociologist, widely regarded as the founding figure of modern Iraqi sociology. He combined social psychology, the legacy of the medieval thinker Ibn Khaldun, and the historical study of Iraqi society to explain the tensions of Iraqi culture and politics.

## Life and Context

Al-Wardi was born in Kadhimiya, Baghdad, and studied at the American University of Beirut before completing doctoral work in the United States at the University of Texas. He later taught sociology at the University of Baghdad. His career unfolded during the late Ottoman aftermath, the British Mandate, the monarchy and the republican upheavals, and these political shifts supplied much of the material for his social analysis. He wrote for a broad Arabic-reading public as well as for scholars, and his books were widely read and debated.

## Main Ideas

Al-Wardi drew on Ibn Khaldun's contrast between bedouin life (badawa) and settled urban life (hadara). He argued that Iraqi society was shaped by a long-running clash between tribal, desert-derived values (honor, solidarity, force) and the norms of settled, urban civilization. He suggested that this clash produced a kind of duality in the Iraqi personality, with individuals moving between competing value systems. He also criticized the role of religious preachers and court scholars in legitimating rulers, and argued that social conflict, including sectarian and tribal divisions, had to be explained through history and social structure rather than through abstract doctrine.

## Major Works

- The Personality of the Iraqi Individual (1951)
- Preachers of the Sultans (1954)
- The Logic of Ibn Khaldun (1962)
- A Study in the Nature of Iraqi Society (1965)
- Social Glimpses of Modern Iraqi History (multi-volume, published from 1969)

## Method

Al-Wardi worked as a historical sociologist. He used chronicles, travel accounts and political history alongside sociological concepts, and he treated Ibn Khaldun as a source of analytical tools rather than only a historical figure. His approach was interpretive and synthetic rather than based on surveys or statistics.

## Influence and Criticism

His work shaped later Arabic-language sociology and public debate in Iraq on identity, tribalism and sectarianism. Critics have noted that his generalizations about a national character rest on limited systematic data, and that framing social life through the bedouin and urban contrast can appear schematic. His writings also provoked controversy among religious and political authorities of his day.
