# Erving Goffman

Erving Goffman (1922-1982) was a Canadian-born sociologist who made face-to-face interaction a distinct field of study. He analyzed self-presentation, institutions, stigma and the frameworks through which people understand situations.

## Life and Context

Goffman was born in Mannville, Alberta. He took his first degree at the University of Toronto and his master's degree and doctorate (1953) at the University of Chicago, where he was influenced by Everett Hughes and W. Lloyd Warner, among others, and by the reading of Durkheim and Simmel. He taught at the University of California, Berkeley, from 1958 and at the University of Pennsylvania from 1968. He was president of the American Sociological Association in 1981-1982 and died in 1982; his presidential address, "The Interaction Order," appeared posthumously.

## Main Ideas

- **Dramaturgy.** In *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1956; expanded 1959) people manage impressions through performances, with front regions, backstage areas and teams that cooperate to sustain a definition of the situation.
- **Face and interaction ritual.** Participants work to protect their own and others' "face"; deference and demeanor give the self a ritual value.
- **Total institutions.** Places such as asylums, prisons and monasteries enclose daily life under a single authority and may strip inmates of earlier identities.
- **Stigma.** An attribute becomes discrediting within relationships and expectations; those already discredited face problems different from those who can still conceal it.
- **Civil inattention.** Strangers acknowledge one another briefly and then withdraw attention.
- **Frames.** Frames organize the sense of what is going on, and activity can be transformed into play, rehearsal or deception.

## Case Studies

His doctoral fieldwork in a small community in the Shetland Islands supplied material for *The Presentation of Self*. In the mid-1950s he observed life at St. Elizabeths Hospital, a large psychiatric institution in Washington, D.C. The resulting *Asylums* (1961) described mortification of the self and the "secondary adjustments" by which patients preserved some autonomy.

## Method

Goffman combined participant observation with illustrations from memoirs, news items, etiquette books and fiction, building classifications through metaphor and comparison. He gave little systematic account of his procedures.

## Influence and Criticism

His work shaped the study of deviance, mental health, disability, conversation, gender and the media. He is often grouped with symbolic interactionism, although he did not readily accept labels. Critics have argued that he neglected large-scale structure and history, portrayed a calculating or hollow self, and relied on unsystematic evidence.

## Related Concepts

Interaction order, impression management, labeling; compare Durkheim on ritual, Mead on the self and Harold Garfinkel's ethnomethodology.
