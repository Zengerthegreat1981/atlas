# Zora Neale Hurston

Zora Neale Hurston (1891–1960) was an American writer, folklorist, and anthropologist whose work recorded Black life in the southern United States and the Caribbean. A prominent figure of the Harlem Renaissance, she moved between fiction, theatre, and ethnography, and treated vernacular speech, story, song, and religious practice as creative achievements.

## Formation

Born in Notasulga, Alabama, Hurston grew up in Eatonville, Florida, a self-governing Black town that figures throughout her writing. After studies at Howard University she entered Barnard College, from which she graduated in 1928, and studied anthropology with Franz Boas; she also knew Ruth Benedict. Boasian training gave her methods and institutional support for collecting folklore, while her familiarity with Southern communities gave her a different relation to the material from that of an outside visitor. She wrote of anthropology as a "spy-glass" through which she could look at the culture she had grown up in.

## *Mules and Men*

In the late 1920s Hurston collected tales, songs, sermons, and conjure practices in Florida and Louisiana, supported in part by the patron Charlotte Osgood Mason, whose control over the material constrained her. *Mules and Men* (1935), with a preface by Boas, presents stories inside the occasions of their telling: porch talk, work camps, parties, and quarrels. The narrator is a participant who must win trust and at times faces danger. The second part concerns hoodoo in New Orleans and includes accounts of her initiations. This narrative form was long judged insufficiently scientific and was later praised for showing performance, interaction, and the fieldworker's position.

## Caribbean research and other work

With Guggenheim fellowships, Hurston travelled to Jamaica and Haiti in 1936 and 1937. *Tell My Horse* (1938) combines travel narrative, political commentary, and accounts of Vodou and Jamaican practices. While in Haiti she wrote the novel *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937), which draws on her ear for speech and on Eatonville. In 1927 she had interviewed Cudjo Lewis, one of the last survivors of the transatlantic slave trade to the United States; the manuscript, *Barracoon*, was published in 2018. Scholars have noted that an early article based on this research copied heavily from a previously published account.

## Reputation

Hurston's attention to Black cultural life on its own terms drew criticism from some contemporaries, including Richard Wright, who objected that her fiction did not confront racial oppression in the way he thought necessary. Her later political positions, including criticism of the reasoning behind the 1954 school desegregation decision, added to controversy. She died in poverty in Fort Pierce, Florida. Alice Walker's search for her grave, described in a 1975 essay, helped to renew interest. Hurston is now read in literary studies and anthropology as an early practitioner of native, reflexive, and experimental ethnography, labels that were applied after her time.
