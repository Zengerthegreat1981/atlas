# Legal Anthropology: Law Beyond the Text of the State

A subfield that studies how binding rules are organized and disputes are settled in societies that do not necessarily rely on written law issued by a centralized state, and how multiple legal systems (official, customary, and religious) coexist within a single society, a condition known as "legal pluralism." The subfield starts from a premise opposed to the common popular understanding that confines "law" to written legislation and state courts. Instead, it shows that every human society has mechanisms, whether ritual, kinship-based, or religious, for regulating behavior and settling disagreements, even where the institutions of the modern state are entirely absent. Classifications differ on whether the field is best treated as part of political anthropology, with which it shares many questions about authority and order, or as an independent subfield.

## Bronisław Malinowski and "Primitive Law"

Malinowski's book *Crime and Custom in Savage Society* (1926) is a foundational starting point for this subfield. Drawing on his fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands, he argued that "law" in those societies is not embodied in severe penalties issued by a central authority. It lies instead in a network of mutual obligations based on the principle of reciprocity. Individuals comply with the society's rules because their long-term interests, such as the continuation of exchange, marriage, and alliance relationships, depend on their social reputation, not out of fear of direct and immediate punishment. This was an early challenge to the evolutionist view that "primitive societies" lacked any real legal system.

## Legal Pluralism and the Colonial State

The subfield later developed the concept of "legal pluralism" to describe how colonial and modern states alike impose a formal legal system on top of existing customary and religious systems. This produces a complex competition or coexistence between official courts and local tribal or Sharia councils. This pattern is prominent in parts of the Arab world, where positive law derived from European legal traditions coexists with Islamic law and tribal customs in matters of personal status and in resolving property and blood disputes. As a result, "litigation" is in practice a strategic choice among multiple legal forums, which disputants turn to according to what serves their interests in each case.

## Legal Anthropology and Colonialism: From Documentation to Critique

European colonialism, particularly British colonialism in Africa and French colonialism in North Africa, was interested in documenting local "tribal customs" through administrative surveyors and field anthropologists. This was done under the claim of "respecting" local law, notably within the British policy of indirect rule. However, later researchers, working in the context of the postcolonial critique of the field, showed that this documentation itself often "froze" customs that had originally been flexible and constantly changing. It turned them into fixed texts that served the purposes of colonial administration more than they reflected the actual practice of the societies concerned before colonization.

## From Dispute to Human Rights

In recent decades, the subfield's interest has expanded to include the study of how the global language of "human rights" is translated into locally diverse practices. Researchers show that universal concepts such as "domestic violence" or "women's inheritance rights" are understood and applied in radically different ways depending on the layered local legal context. This makes the subfield an important critical tool for understanding the limits of the "universality" of law when international law meets a local legal reality with multiple sources and authorities.
