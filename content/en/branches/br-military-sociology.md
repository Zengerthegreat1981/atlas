# Military Sociology

Military sociology is the study of the armed forces as a social institution and of war as a social phenomenon. It examines military organisation and culture, the relationship between the military and civilian society, the experience of soldiers, and the social causes and consequences of armed conflict. It studies the military as a workplace, a community, and a political actor — how people are recruited, trained, and socialised into military life, how cohesion and morale are built, how veterans return to civilian life, and how military power shapes states and societies. It differs from the study of war as a concept, which takes war itself — its meanings, its ethics, and its place in human history — as its object: military sociology is the sociology of the institution that wages war, and of the people who serve in it.

## History

Military sociology was born in the Second World War. The American Soldier studies, published as *The American Soldier* (1949) by Samuel Stouffer and colleagues, were a very large wartime social research programme, surveying hundreds of thousands of soldiers on morale, attitudes, and adjustment, and they established the field's empirical tradition. The Cold War gave the field its institutional form. Samuel Huntington's *The Soldier and the State* (1957) argued that military professionalism — expertise, responsibility, and corporateness — required civilian control, and framed the central problem of civil-military relations. Morris Janowitz's *The Professional Soldier* (1960) studied the changing character of the officer corps as warfare became more technical and political. Charles Moskos's *The American Enlisted Man* (1970) analysed the experience of enlisted soldiers, and his later essay "From Institution to Occupation" (1977) distinguished the institutional model of service, based on duty and honour, from the occupational model, based on pay and conditions.

## Key Figures

Samuel Stouffer helped establish the field's empirical tradition with the American Soldier studies, showing that large-scale survey research could illuminate the inner life of an army. Samuel Huntington is the classic theorist of civil-military relations, arguing that a professional military, subordinate to civilian authority, is essential to both military effectiveness and democratic stability. Morris Janowitz was a central figure in the field, studying the officer corps, the changing nature of military organisation, and the convergence of military and civilian institutions. Charles Moskos analysed the transformation of the military from an institution to an occupation, and documented the experiences of enlisted soldiers and the effects of racial integration. Cynthia Enloe brought feminist analysis to the field, showing how militarisation depends on gendered ideas and on women's labour, and how military power reaches far beyond the battlefield. Giuseppe Caforio edited the *Handbook of the Sociology of the Military* (2003), consolidating the field's international scope.

## Key Works

Stouffer and colleagues' *The American Soldier: Adjustment during Army Life* (1949) is the field's founding empirical work. Huntington's *The Soldier and the State: The Theory and Politics of Civil-Military Relations* (1957) remains the classic statement on civilian control of the military. Janowitz's *The Professional Soldier: A Social and Political Portrait* (1960) is the landmark sociological study of the officer corps. Moskos's *The American Enlisted Man* (1970) is the classic analysis of the military as an institution in transition. Enloe's *Does Khaki Become You? The Militarisation of Women's Lives* (1983) opened the field to feminist analysis, showing how militarism shapes women's lives in and beyond the armed forces. Caforio's edited *Handbook of the Sociology of the Military* (2003) is the standard reference, bringing together research from many countries on organisation, culture, and civil-military relations.

## Method

Military sociology uses methods adapted to a closed and hierarchical institution. Surveys of soldiers, veterans, and military families are the field's backbone, measuring morale, cohesion, attitudes toward service, and the effects of deployment and combat. Ethnography — observation in barracks, on bases, and in training — reveals the informal culture of military life: the rituals, jokes, and norms through which soldiers make sense of their world. Interviews with officers, enlisted personnel, and veterans trace careers, the transition to civilian life, and the long-term effects of service. Historical and comparative research studies how different countries organise their armed forces, and how civil-military relations vary across regimes. The field's distinctive methodological challenge is access: the military is a secretive institution, and researchers must negotiate permission, security clearance, and the suspicion of those they study. The best work combines insider knowledge with outsider questions.

## Influence and Debates

Military sociology has had a direct influence on defence policy and on the management of armed forces. Its research on cohesion, morale, and leadership has shaped training and doctrine, and its studies of racial integration, gender, and sexuality in the military informed debates about who should serve. Its work on civil-military relations remains central to discussions of democratic control of the armed forces. The field has also been the site of sharp debate. One argument concerns the military's transformation: has it become an occupation like any other, as Moskos argued, or does it retain a distinctive institutional character? A second concerns civil-military relations: Huntington's model of objective civilian control has been criticised for assuming a separation between military and political spheres that no longer exists, if it ever did. A third concerns gender and militarisation: feminist scholars argue that military sociology has treated war as a male domain and ignored the women who sustain it, while others reply that the integration of women into combat roles has changed the institution fundamentally. The field that took shape through surveys of American soldiers in the Second World War now studies a military world that is more technological, more privatised, and more entangled with civilian life than its founders imagined.
