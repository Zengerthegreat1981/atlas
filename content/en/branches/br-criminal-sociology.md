# Criminal Sociology

Criminal sociology is the sociological study of crime, deviance, and punishment. It treats crime not as a property of individuals — a matter of bad character or broken biology — but as a social phenomenon: defined by law and custom, distributed unevenly across groups, produced by social conditions, and responded to by institutions that themselves shape what crime means. It is distinct from criminology as a whole, which is an interdisciplinary field drawing on psychology, biology, law, and criminal justice. Criminal sociology is criminology's sociological core: it asks how society makes crime, and how crime makes society.

## Overview

The field's starting point is that crime is socially defined. Acts become crimes when laws prohibit them, and laws change: behaviours once punished are decriminalised, while new offences are created. The same act may be treated as crime, illness, or eccentricity depending on who commits it and where. Criminal sociology therefore studies the process of definition as much as the acts themselves — who gets labelled criminal, by whom, and with what consequences.

It also studies distribution. Crime rates vary by age, gender, class, neighbourhood, and historical period, and these patterns demand social explanation: blocked opportunities, peer groups, subcultures, policing practices, and the availability of illicit markets. And it studies reaction: the police, courts, and prisons, and the informal controls of family, school, and community. Punishment, in this view, is not only a response to crime but a social institution with its own history, rituals, and effects — including effects that may increase crime rather than reduce it.

## History

Émile Durkheim founded the sociological approach to crime. In The Division of Labor in Society (1893) he argued that crime is normal — present in every society — and functional: it provokes collective outrage that reaffirms shared values and marks the boundaries of acceptable conduct. A society without crime, he suggested, would be one without moral boundaries at all. Robert Merton's strain theory, first published as "Social Structure and Anomie" (1938), explained high crime rates as a product of the gap between culturally approved goals, such as material success, and the legitimate means available to achieve them, especially for the poor.

Edwin Sutherland's theory of differential association, presented in Principles of Criminology (1939), argued that criminal behaviour is learned in interaction with others, like any other behaviour — a direct challenge to theories of innate criminality. In the 1960s, labelling theory, above all Howard Becker's Outsiders (1963), shifted attention from the causes of deviance to the social reaction that creates deviants: being caught and labelled changes a person's identity, opportunities, and associations, sometimes producing the very behaviour the label claims to describe. Stanley Cohen's Folk Devils and Moral Panics (1972) showed how media and moral entrepreneurs amplify deviance into public panic. Michel Foucault's Discipline and Punish (1975) recast the prison as a technique of power, while David Garland's Punishment and Modern Society (1990) synthesised the classical tradition, and Loïc Wacquant's Punishing the Poor (2009) linked mass incarceration to neoliberal social policy.

## Key Figures and Works

Durkheim established that crime is a social fact with social functions. Merton connected crime to the structure of opportunity and the pressure of cultural goals. Sutherland made learning central and insisted that white-collar crime, committed by respectable people, belongs in the same analysis as street crime. Becker showed that deviance is a process of labelling and that deviant careers, like other careers, develop through stages.

Cohen's concept of moral panic explains how societies periodically fasten on folk devils — mods and rockers, drug users, gangs — and how the reaction often does more social damage than the behaviour itself. Foucault showed that punishment shifted from public spectacle to discipline of the body and soul, and that the prison produces delinquency as much as it contains it. Garland argued that punishment expresses cultural sensibilities as well as controlling crime, and Wacquant demonstrated that the penal state has grown alongside the retrenchment of welfare, managing poverty through incarceration rather than support.

## Methods

Criminal sociologists are sceptical of official statistics, which reflect policing and reporting as much as behaviour, so they combine sources: police and court records, victimisation surveys, self-report studies, and ethnographies of gangs, drug markets, and prisons. Comparative research contrasts punishment systems across countries, showing that similar crime rates can coexist with very different levels of imprisonment. Historical research traces how crimes and punishments are invented and abandoned. Increasingly, the field studies cybercrime, surveillance technologies, and the algorithmic tools now used in policing and sentencing.

## Further Topics

Three areas extend the core tradition. Research on mass incarceration, especially in the United States, documents how imprisonment became concentrated among poor, young, and disproportionately Black men; Bruce Western's *Punishment and Inequality in America* (2006) examines its effects on employment and family life, and Todd Clear's *Imprisoning Communities* (2007) argues that concentrated incarceration can itself weaken the neighbourhoods it targets. Restorative justice, associated with Howard Zehr's *Changing Lenses* (1990) and John Braithwaite's theory of reintegrative shaming in *Crime, Shame and Reintegration* (1989), asks whether responses to harm should centre repair and reintegration rather than punishment, and its practices have been adopted in varied forms across many countries. Studies of cybercrime and online deviance apply established frameworks, such as routine activity theory, to fraud, harassment, and intrusion, while debating whether networked settings require new concepts.

## Influence and Debates

Criminal sociology has shaped criminal justice policy, from the critique of deterrence to the case for restorative justice, and its concepts — anomie, labelling, moral panic, the penal state — are widely used in public debate. It has also been central to documenting how punishment falls unevenly by race and class.

Live debates include whether mass incarceration reduces crime or reproduces it; how to explain persistent racial disparities in policing and sentencing; whether cybercrime requires new theory or fits existing accounts; and how restorative and community-based responses compare with punishment. The field's enduring question is Durkheim's: what does a society's treatment of crime reveal about its values — and what would a just response to harm look like?
