# Market Socialism: Public Ownership and the Market Mechanism in the Lange Model

A current of thought within socialist political economy that sought to combine two principles that many believed to be contradictory: social or public ownership of the means of production (the core socialist demand), and the mechanism of the market and prices (the instrument considered the preserve of capitalism) for allocating resources efficiently. The current emerged directly as a response to the "socialist calculation debate", which Ludwig von Mises raised in 1920 and Friedrich Hayek developed in the 1930s and 1940s, arguing that a centrally planned socialist economy without real market prices would be structurally unable to perform the rational economic calculation needed to allocate resources.

## The Lange model: simulated prices set by the state

The Polish economist Oskar Lange offered his best-known reply in his article "On the Economic Theory of Socialism" (published in two parts, 1936 and 1937), building on earlier work by Fred M. Taylor and with parallel contributions by Abba Lerner. He proposed a model in which the major means of production are socially owned (managed by public industrial boards), but prices are determined through a "simulation" mechanism resembling a market in form: a central planning board announces prices for goods and capital, and managers of public enterprises are instructed to follow rules (minimise average cost and set output where price equals marginal cost). Consumers and workers choose freely in markets for consumer goods and labour. Where shortages or surpluses appear, the board adjusts prices by trial and error, in the manner of a Walrasian auctioneer, until supply and demand balance. The result is meant to reproduce what a competitive market would produce without private ownership of capital; the return on public capital accrues to society as a social dividend rather than to private individuals. Lange argued that the model could be superior to actual capitalism because it can correct market failures (such as monopoly and external effects) and avoid unemployment cycles while retaining the allocative role of prices.

## The critique: the problem of incentives and dispersed knowledge

Hayek, in "Socialist Calculation: The Competitive 'Solution'" (*Economica*, 1940) and "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), replied that the difficulty is deeper than mathematical calculation: the problem is not only computing equilibrium prices in theory (which is feasible in a mathematical model), but the impossibility of gathering the dispersed and instantaneously changing knowledge held by millions of individual economic actors (their changing preferences, local circumstances, and tacit knowledge that cannot be centrally recorded) within any central planning body, however computationally capable, and that the free market is precisely the mechanism for discovering this dispersed knowledge rather than merely a calculating tool. Other critics added the problem of incentives: the managers of public factories in the Lange model lack the direct personal profit incentive that drives an individual capitalist to monitor precise efficiency, which may produce administrative sluggishness despite the theoretical soundness of the calculated prices.

## Historical applications and influence

Yugoslavia's experience after the break with Moscow in 1948 (its system of "workers' self-management", introduced from 1950) is often regarded as the closest historical analogue, though it differed in essential respects from Lange's design: enterprises were autonomous, worker-managed and exposed to real markets, whereas Lange's managers followed rules set by a planning board. Hungary's New Economic Mechanism of 1968 likewise introduced market elements into a planned economy without adopting the Lange model. The Lange model remains essentially theoretical, and the gap between model and practice should not be blurred. The current remained a living theoretical reference in debates on economic reform within the former socialist bloc (including the Hungarian reforms after 1968), and in contemporary proposals for market socialism such as John Roemer's *A Future for Socialism* (1994) and the computerization of economic planning with modern methods.

## A renewed impact on debates over computational economics

The socialist calculation debate and the Lange model regained theoretical importance in recent decades, as enormous computing capacities developed that were not available at the time of the original debate. Some contemporary researchers argue that Hayek's objections regarding dispersed knowledge, though historically valid against manual planning tools of the twentieth century, may require partial revision in light of the data-processing capacities of big data and contemporary artificial intelligence. Others reply that Hayek's core problem is not purely computational but structural and epistemic (tacit knowledge that cannot be recorded at all, however computing develops). This debate has not been settled by clear academic consensus to this day.
