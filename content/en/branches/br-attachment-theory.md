# Attachment Theory

A theory in developmental and clinical psychology that describes how children form emotional bonds with caregivers and how these bonds continue to shape relationships across the lifespan. It was founded by John Bowlby (1958) and developed by Mary Ainsworth, Mary Main and Peter Fonagy.

## Basic Principles

1. **Attachment is a biological instinct**: it is not conditioned learning but an evolutionary behavioral system for survival.
2. **Physical proximity is safe**: the goal of attachment is to stay close to the caregiver, especially in danger.
3. **The secure base**: the caregiver from whom the child sets out to explore and to whom the child returns in danger.
4. **The internal working model**: a mental representation of relationships that forms in the first years and persists into adulthood.
5. **Individual variation**: attachment patterns differ among children according to the quality of care.

## Major Contributions

### Ainsworth (1969–1978): The Strange Situation
Mary Ainsworth developed (culminating with colleagues in *Patterns of Attachment*, 1978) a standardized laboratory procedure for observing infant attachment patterns (secure, avoidant, anxious/resistant), based on an infant's response to brief separations from and reunions with the primary caregiver.

### Main and Solomon (1986): The Disorganized Pattern (D)
Mary Main and Judith Solomon described a fourth pattern (disorganized/disoriented), which is over-represented in maltreated children; Main and Hesse (1990) linked it to parental behavior that is frightening or frightened, creating a biologically insoluble dilemma where the caregiver is simultaneously the source of fear and the haven of safety.

### George, Kaplan and Main (1985): The Adult Attachment Interview (AAI)
The AAI, developed by Carol George, Nancy Kaplan and Mary Main, assesses adults' state of mind regarding attachment through the linguistic coherence and narrative collaboration of their discourse about childhood experiences, rather than through direct behavioral observation. Research consistently demonstrates that parental AAI classifications strongly predict their infants' Strange Situation classifications (intergenerational transmission of attachment).

### Peter Fonagy and Anthony Bateman: Mentalization
Building on attachment research, Fonagy and colleagues elaborated the concept of mentalization (the capacity to understand oneself and others in terms of intentional mental states such as beliefs, desires, and feelings). Anthony Bateman and Peter Fonagy developed mentalization-based treatment (MBT) for borderline personality disorder (2004), premised on the finding that early attachment trauma impairs mentalizing capacity under interpersonal stress.

## Clinical Applications

- **Borderline personality disorder (BPD)**: early disorganized attachment and childhood relational trauma are established developmental risk factors addressed through mentalization-focused interventions.
- **Anxiety and depressive disorders**: insecure attachment patterns (both anxious-preoccupied and dismissive-avoidant) are associated with vulnerabilities in emotion regulation.
- **Addiction and substance use**: insecure internal working models are frequently correlated with chemical dependence, functioning as maladaptive attempts at affect regulation.
- **Institutional neglect**: infants raised in institutional deprivation display elevated rates of disorganized and atypical attachment, with early placement in nurturing foster care facilitating developmental recovery.

### Adult Attachment and Couples Therapy
Although the Adult Attachment Interview (AAI) originated as an empirical research tool, its conceptual architecture has been widely translated into clinical couples therapy, notably within Emotionally Focused Therapy (EFT) developed by Susan Johnson and attachment-based systemic models. The AAI framework demonstrates how adults organize affect and defensiveness into autonomous, dismissing, preoccupied, or unresolved states of mind. In couples therapy, clinicians utilize these constructs to decode negative interactional cycles: partners with preoccupied tendencies typically deploy hyperactivating strategies (anxious pursuit, intense emotional demands), while dismissing partners utilize deactivating strategies (emotional withdrawal, suppression, detachment). Recognizing these patterns allows therapists to reframe interpersonal hostility as unexpressed vulnerability and attachment distress, guiding couples toward mutual emotional accessibility and the establishment of a secure dyadic base.

## Sub-Currents

- **Attachment in adulthood**: Hazan & Shaver (1987) on romantic attachment styles; Bartholomew and Horowitz (1991) on four-category adult patterns.
- **Attachment in psychotherapy**: Wallin (2007), Slade (2008).
- **Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT)**: Bateman & Fonagy.
- **Parental state of mind and infant attachment**: Main and colleagues' AAI research.
