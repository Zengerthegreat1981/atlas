# Attachment Theory

A theory in developmental and clinical psychology that describes how children form emotional bonds with caregivers and how these bonds continue to shape relationships across the lifespan. It was founded by John Bowlby (1958) and developed by Mary Ainsworth, Mary Main and Peter Fonagy.

## Basic Principles

1. **Attachment is a biological instinct**: it is not conditioned learning but an evolutionary behavioral system for survival.
2. **Physical proximity is safe**: the goal of attachment is to stay close to the caregiver, especially in danger.
3. **The secure base**: the caregiver from whom the child sets out to explore and to whom the child returns in danger.
4. **The internal working model**: a mental representation of relationships that forms in the first years and persists into adulthood.
5. **Individual variation**: attachment patterns differ among children according to the quality of care.

## Major Contributions

### Ainsworth (1969-78): The Strange Situation
She developed (with colleagues, culminating in *Patterns of Attachment*, 1978) a laboratory procedure for observing infant attachment patterns (secure, avoidant, anxious/resistant), based on the infant's response to brief separations from and reunions with the caregiver. See con-attachment-styles.

### Main and Solomon (1986): The Disorganized Pattern (D)
Mary Main and Judith Solomon described a fourth pattern (disorganized/disoriented), which is over-represented in maltreated children; Main and Hesse (1990) linked it to parental behavior that is frightening or frightened.

### George, Kaplan and Main (1985): The Adult Attachment Interview (AAI)
The AAI, developed by Carol George, Nancy Kaplan and Mary Main, assesses adults' state of mind regarding attachment from the coherence of their narration about childhood rather than from direct behavior. Studies have found that parents' AAI classifications are associated with their infants' Strange Situation classifications (intergenerational transmission).

### Peter Fonagy and Anthony Bateman: Mentalization
Building on attachment research, Fonagy and colleagues elaborated the concept of mentalization (the capacity to understand oneself and others in terms of mental states), and Bateman and Fonagy developed mentalization-based treatment (MBT) for borderline personality disorder (2004), on the premise that difficulties in mentalizing, especially in attachment contexts, contribute to the disorder.

## Clinical Applications

- **Borderline personality (BPD)**: early disorganized attachment and childhood maltreatment are among the risk factors examined in the research.
- **Anxiety and depressive disorders**: insecure attachment patterns are associated with increased risk.
- **Addiction**: insecure attachment is associated with substance use, which some accounts interpret as a way of regulating emotion.
- **Couples therapy**: partners' attachment patterns affect relationship dynamics.
- **Institutional neglect**: children raised in institutions show elevated rates of disorganized and atypical attachment, and earlier placement in family care is associated with better outcomes.

## Sub-Currents

- **Attachment in adulthood**: Hazan & Shaver (1987) on romantic attachment; Bartholomew (1990) on four adult patterns.
- **Attachment in therapy**: Wallin 2007, Slade 2008.
- **Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT)**: Bateman & Fonagy.
- **Parental state of mind and infant attachment**: Main and colleagues' AAI research.
