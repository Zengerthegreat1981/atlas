# Sraffian Neo-Ricardian Economics: Commodities Produced by Means of Commodities

A theoretical current opened by the Italian economist Piero Sraffa with his short book *Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities: Prelude to a Critique of Economic Theory* (1960). Sraffa restated the classical theory of value and distribution, in the line of Ricardo, in a system of production equations, without using marginal utility or subjective scarcity as the basis of price. The book supplied much of the formal apparatus later used against aggregate neoclassical capital theory.

## Sraffa's Model: Prices from Production, Not from Preferences

Sraffa built a relatively simple mathematical model describing an economy in which each commodity is produced using other commodities (including as intermediate production inputs) in fixed technical proportions. He showed that the relative prices of commodities and the uniform rate of profit across the whole economy can be determined from the actual technical methods of production (the quantities of labour and intermediate commodities needed per unit of output) together with a single external distributive datum (either the real wage or the rate of profit). There is no need at all to invoke the consumers' subjective "marginal utility" or "relative scarcity" as a basis for explaining prices. This is the opposite of the neoclassical method, which builds prices from consumer preferences and the balance of supply and demand.

## The Cambridge Capital Controversy

Sraffa's book became a technical reference in the Cambridge capital controversy. Joan Robinson had already attacked the aggregate production function in "The Production Function and the Theory of Capital" (1953–54). In the 1960s economists at Cambridge, England, including Robinson, Nicholas Kaldor, Luigi Pasinetti and Pierangelo Garegnani, argued the point against Paul Samuelson, Robert Solow and others at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in Cambridge, Massachusetts. Using reswitching and capital reversing, the English side argued that there is a serious technical problem in the standard aggregate neoclassical capital model. Measuring the total "quantity of capital" in a single uniform monetary unit (a requirement for building a standard neoclassical aggregate production function) requires knowing the rate of profit in advance in order to value heterogeneous capital assets (different machines valued by discounting their future returns at that same rate of profit). Yet the rate of profit itself is supposed, in that model, to be determined by this quantity of capital. Critics treated the dependence as a circularity that leaves the aggregate parables inconsistent outside special cases. Paul Samuelson acknowledged in 1966 ("A Summing Up") that reswitching is possible and that the simple parables of aggregate capital do not hold in general, although the debate over its practical importance for applied macroeconomics remained a matter of continuing disagreement among economists.

## Subsequent Influence

Sraffa (1898–1983), who had also edited the collected works of Ricardo, influenced the development of an alternative economic current (sometimes known as "Neo-Ricardian" economics) that uses Sraffa's technical tools to build an analysis of distribution and growth as an alternative both to neoclassical economics and to orthodox Marxism. It intersects with post-Keynesian economics in questioning neoclassical concepts of aggregate capital, and in emphasising that distribution (not technical efficiency alone) is fundamentally a socio-political struggle that is not determined by a purely neutral market mechanism.

## Sraffa's Standard Commodity

Sraffa offered another influential technical instrument, known as the "standard commodity": a constructed composite commodity whose means of production and net product are in the same proportions in every industry, so that the ratio of its net product to its means of production does not change with prices. Measured in this standard, the relation between the wage share and the rate of profit becomes linear (r = R(1 − w), where R is the maximum rate of profit). It thus provides an invariable measure of value when distribution changes. The instrument was later used in several analyses of the relationship between wages and profits in the aggregate economy without relying on the concept of subjective marginal utility, which established Sraffa's standing as a technical bridge between classical political economy and modern forms of non-neoclassical mathematical analysis.
