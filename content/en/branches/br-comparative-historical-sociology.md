# Comparative Historical Sociology

Comparative historical sociology explains large-scale social transformations — revolutions, state formation, democratisation, the rise of capitalism — by comparing how they unfolded across societies and over long periods of time. It treats history not as a backdrop to sociological theory but as its laboratory: causal claims are tested by setting cases side by side and asking why similar conditions produced different outcomes, or why different conditions produced the same one. Where historical anthropology studies the past through culture, meaning, and lived experience, this branch works with macro-sociological comparison, seeking the structural causes of the great divergences and convergences of modern history.

## Scope and Core Concepts

The branch's scope is the macro-historical: the formation of states, the making of classes, the timing of revolutions, and the paths to democracy and dictatorship. Core concepts include the state as an autonomous organisation claiming control over territory and violence; revolution as the rapid, basic transformation of a society's state and class structures; and path dependence, the idea that early choices constrain later possibilities. Critical junctures are moments when institutional arrangements are open to change, after which they lock in. Agrarian class relations describe how landlords, peasants, and commercial classes shape political outcomes. The branch studies state capacity, the ability of states to tax, administer, and coerce; the fiscal-military state; and the relations between war, taxation, and representation. Its central methodological ideas are most-similar and most-different case designs, and the search for causal mechanisms that operate across cases without reducing history to universal laws.

## Historical Development

The branch descends from Max Weber, whose *Economy and Society* (1922) combined comparative history with sociological theory, and from Marx's historical materialism. Its modern form was established by Barrington Moore's *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy* (1966), which compared England, France, the United States, Germany, Russia, China, Japan, and India to explain why some societies became democracies and others fascist or communist dictatorships. Theda Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) reframed the study of revolution, arguing that revolutions result from state breakdown under international pressure combined with peasant revolt, and that outcomes depend on state structures rather than revolutionary intentions. The 1980s brought the state back into sociology: the collection *Bringing the State Back In* (1985), edited by Peter Evans, Dietrich Rueschemeyer, and Theda Skocpol, made state autonomy a central theme. Michael Mann's *The Sources of Social Power* (1986) offered a rival account organised around ideological, economic, military, and political power, and Charles Tilly's *Coercion, Capital, and European States* (1990) explained European state formation through war-making and capital accumulation. James Mahoney and Dietrich Rueschemeyer's *Comparative Historical Analysis in the Social Sciences* (2003) later codified the field's methods and ambitions.

## Key Figures

Barrington Moore Jr. (1913–2005) is the branch's founding figure, whose comparative study of agrarian class relations set its agenda for a generation. Theda Skocpol is its most influential contemporary practitioner, whose structural theory of revolution and later work on American civic life reshaped political sociology. Charles Tilly (1929–2008) supplied the field's most durable account of state formation and its method of large-scale comparison. Michael Mann developed the most ambitious alternative framework, a multi-volume history of power organised around four sources. Immanuel Wallerstein, from world-systems analysis, provided the global context within which national histories unfold. Dietrich Rueschemeyer advanced the comparative study of capitalist development and democratisation, while James Mahoney systematised the field's methods of causal inference, including the analysis of critical junctures and path dependence. Perry Anderson, the historian, contributed the comparative analysis of absolutism and the lineages of the modern state.

## Method

Comparative historical sociology's method is the systematic comparison of a small number of cases over long periods, using historical evidence to test causal arguments. Researchers select cases to maximise leverage — comparing societies that are similar in most respects but differ in outcome, or different in most respects but share an outcome — and they use process tracing to follow causal chains within each case. The method depends on secondary historical literature, archives, and statistical series, and it demands deep knowledge of each case's context. Its strength is the ability to address questions too large for surveys or experiments: why revolutions succeed, why democracies endure, why states develop differently. Its characteristic risks are selection bias, the temptation to choose cases that confirm the theory, and the difficulty of generalising from a handful of cases. Recent work combines comparative history with formal methods, including qualitative comparative analysis and the study of critical junctures.

## Influence and Debates

The branch's influence reaches across sociology, political science, and history. It transformed the study of revolutions, democratisation, and state formation, and it supplied the historical depth that much of sociology lacked. Its insistence that states have interests and capacities of their own reshaped political sociology, and its methods influenced the wider revival of historical sociology. Debates persist. One concerns structure versus agency: Skocpol's structuralism has been criticised for leaving too little room for the intentions and ideologies of revolutionaries, while more cultural approaches stress the meanings actors attach to their actions. Another concerns the role of the international: whether revolutions and state formation are best explained domestically or by war, trade, and empire. A third concerns generalisation: whether comparative history yields causal mechanisms that travel across cases or only historically specific narratives. The field's defenders argue that its achievement is precisely to combine causal ambition with historical specificity.
