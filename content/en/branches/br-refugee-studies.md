# Refugee Studies

Refugee studies is the interdisciplinary study of forced displacement: why people flee, how they move, how they are received, and how exile reshapes identity, community, and politics. It draws on sociology, anthropology, political science, law, and history, but its sociological core concerns the social worlds that displacement creates — refugee camps, diaspora networks, asylum systems, and the everyday negotiations of people living between states. The field studies not only refugees in the narrow legal sense but also asylum seekers, internally displaced persons, stateless people, and those displaced by development projects, environmental change, and war.

## Scope

The field covers the causes of displacement, the journeys people undertake, and the institutions that govern them. It examines the legal regime built around the 1951 Refugee Convention and the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), founded in 1950, alongside the everyday practices of border control, detention, and resettlement. It studies camps as social spaces, with their own economies, politics, and forms of authority. It follows refugees into cities and diasporas, where questions of integration, belonging, work, and transnational ties dominate. And it studies the politics of humanitarianism: who provides aid, on what terms, and with what effects on the people aided. Refugee studies is therefore concerned with states and international organisations, but equally with the agency of displaced people themselves.

## History

Refugee studies emerged as a distinct field in the 1980s, though its roots reach back to the mass displacements of the Second World War and the creation of the international refugee regime. Early work was dominated by legal scholars and by practitioners in humanitarian agencies. A turning point came with Barbara Harrell-Bond's *Imposing Aid: Emergency Assistance to Refugees* (1986), an ethnography of refugee camps in Sudan that showed how humanitarian aid could undermine refugees' autonomy and impose outside categories on them. In the same period, Aristide Zolberg, Astri Suhrke, and Sergio Aguayo published *Escape from Violence: Conflict and the Refugee Crisis in the Developing World* (1989), which reframed refugee flows as products of state formation, civil war, and international politics rather than as natural disasters. Liisa Malkki's *Purity and Exile: Violence, Memory, and National Cosmology among Hutu Refugees in Tanzania* (1995) showed how exile reshapes national identity, contrasting camp refugees who cultivated a myth of return with town refugees who assimilated. By the 2000s the field had its own journals, research centres, and degree programmes, notably the Refugee Studies Centre at Oxford, founded in 1982.

## Key Figures and Works

Harrell-Bond (1986) produced an influential critical ethnography of humanitarian aid. Zolberg, Suhrke, and Aguayo (1989) provided the field's foundational political sociology of forced migration. Malkki (1995) demonstrated how displacement generates new forms of national consciousness. Giorgio Agamben's *Homo Sacer* (1995) supplied a widely used, if contested, theoretical vocabulary: the camp as the space where people are reduced to "bare life," outside normal legal protection. Roger Zetter's work on the labelling of refugees showed how bureaucratic categories — refugee, asylum seeker, economic migrant — shape people's life chances. Alexander Betts and Paul Collier's *Refuge: Transforming a Broken Refugee System* (2017) argued for development-based approaches that give refugees the right to work rather than warehousing them in camps. Elena Fiddian-Qasmiyeh's *The Ideal Refugees: Gender, Islam, and the Sahrawi Politics of Survival* (2014) brought gender and religion into focus, analysing how representations of Sahrawi refugee women, secularism, and democracy have been presented to Western audiences to secure support during a decades-long exile.

## Methods

Refugee studies combines ethnography, interviews, surveys, legal analysis, and historical research. Ethnography in camps and settlements captures daily life, social organisation, and the micropolitics of aid. Life-history interviews trace displacement trajectories and the remaking of identity. Quantitative work measures flows, integration outcomes, and the economic effects of hosting refugees. Legal scholarship analyses asylum decisions and the evolution of refugee law. Participatory methods, in which refugees collaborate in research design, have grown in response to ethical concerns about studying vulnerable people. The field also faces distinctive methodological problems: research in camps raises questions of consent and access; displaced populations are mobile and hard to sample; and researchers must navigate the politics of humanitarian agencies and states that control access to field sites.

## Influence and Debates

Refugee studies has influenced humanitarian policy, asylum law, and public debate about migration. Its research has documented the harms of encampment, the economic contributions refugees make to host societies, and the failures of deterrence policies. Debates in the field are sharp. One concerns the category of the refugee itself: should the field defend the legal distinction between refugees and other migrants, or treat all forced movement together? Another concerns humanitarianism: does aid empower or control its recipients? A third concerns the politics of knowledge: much research is produced in the Global North about people from the Global South, and scholars increasingly ask who speaks for refugees and whose voices count. With displacement at very high levels — driven by war in Syria, Ukraine, Sudan, and elsewhere, and increasingly by climate change — the field's central question has become more urgent: how can societies respond to forced movement with justice rather than with walls, camps, and detention?
