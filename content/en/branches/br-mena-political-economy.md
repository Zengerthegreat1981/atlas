# Political Economy of the Middle East and North Africa: The Authoritarian Social Contract and Managed Rent

The **political economy of the Middle East and North Africa** is a distinct research current within comparative political economy. It concerns itself specifically with analysing the structural relationship between patterns of rent distribution (primarily oil rent, but also external aid and strategic transfers) and the stability of authoritarian or semi-authoritarian political regimes in the region. It goes beyond purely descriptive economic history (as in the field of economic history of the Middle East) toward building explicit causal theories. These address how political authority "buys" social loyalty through the distribution of rent, and how that distribution itself becomes a constraint that regimes find hard to escape later without existential political risk.

## The Intellectual Origin: The Rentier State Literature

The concept of the "rentier state" was introduced by Hossein Mahdavi in 1970 in a study of Iran. The current's founding roots lie in the 1987 volume *The Rentier State*, edited by the Egyptian economist Hazem Beblawi and the Italian political economist Giacomo Luciani, whose contributors developed a central hypothesis. Beblawi defined a rentier state as one in which rents are external, accrue to the government and are the dominant source of its income; Luciani developed the link to representation and the "allocation state". When the state's income depends mainly on external rent (oil revenues sold abroad) rather than on taxes levied on its locally productive citizens, the structural need for representative accountability weakens. The logic is "no taxation without representation," and so, conversely, there is little need for genuine representation where there is no taxation. A political relationship arises that differs fundamentally from that of states with a tax-based economy. The state distributes rent (government jobs, subsidies, free services) in exchange for popular acceptance of the absence of effective political accountability. This is often called the "rentier social contract." Its generalisation across very different states (oil and non-oil, monarchic and republican) is itself debated, and no single pattern explains all cases equally.

## Extending the Model to Non-Oil States

Later work, including the textbook of Alan Richards and John Waterbury, extended the analysis to non-oil Arab states such as Egypt, Syria and Jordan, sometimes described as "semi-rentier" economies. Remittances from migrant workers in the oil-rich Gulf, strategic external aid (such as US aid to Egypt since the Camp David Accords of 1978-79), and Suez Canal revenues all function as alternative forms of rent. Together they allow the state to distribute social benefits, such as expanded government employment and food subsidies, without full reliance on direct local taxation. This partly explains the continuation of relatively stable authoritarian rule even in states that do not possess abundant oil of their own.

## The "Authoritarian Resilience" Argument After the 2011 Uprisings

This current renewed itself strongly after the Arab uprisings of 2011, when researchers tried to explain why some regimes, particularly the oil-rich Gulf monarchies, withstood the wave of protests more easily than non-oil republics such as Tunisia and Egypt. Gulf states introduced large additional social spending packages immediately. Saudi Arabia, for example, announced social spending packages in 2011 that were widely reported at roughly 130 billion dollars in total, made possible by its ample financial reserves. This confirmed the logic of "buying loyalty with rent." Non-oil republics, by contrast, lacked a comparable financial margin to absorb popular anger economically. Rent alone does not account for the outcomes, however: the regimes in Tunisia and Egypt fell, while Syria and Yemen slid into prolonged civil conflict.

## A Critique of Excessive Rentier Determinism

This current faces internal criticism from researchers who warn against sliding into an excessive rentier determinism that reduces all the complexity of Arab politics to a single economic variable, rent. Such criticism neglects other decisive factors, such as the structure of the military and security establishment, sectarian and ethnic identities, and international alliances that are relatively independent of the volume of rent available. Researchers also point out that some rich oil states have experienced severe political upheaval despite abundant rent (such as Iraq and Libya), while some non-oil republics have remained politically stable for long periods despite scarce rent resources. This calls for supplementary explanations that go beyond the rent variable alone.

## The Continuing Relevance of the Field

Despite these reservations, this current remains a central explanatory framework in Western and Arab academic studies of governance stability in the region. It is widely used to analyse stalled economic reform policies, such as energy subsidy reform, as a direct test of the flexibility of the existing rentier social contract and of regimes' capacity to renegotiate it without losing the popular legitimacy on which they have been built for decades in most countries of the region.

## Militarisation and Strategic Non-Oil Rent

This current broadens the concept of rent to include what some researchers call "strategic rent": massive financial and military flows that certain states receive by virtue of their sensitive geopolitical location, regardless of their own natural resources. Examples include the large US military and economic aid to Egypt since the Camp David Accords of 1979, and direct financial Gulf support to other Arab governments at critical political moments (such as Gulf support for Egypt after 2013). These strategic flows operate on a logic similar to oil rent, in that they make resources available to the state that are not directly tied to the productivity of its domestic economy or to the taxes of its citizens. This strengthens the relative autonomy of the state from local society in determining its economic priorities.

## The Army as a Direct Economic Actor

This current also concerns itself with the direct economic role, not only the political-security role, of military institutions in the region's economies. The Egyptian armed forces, for example, own extensive networks of economic companies operating in various civilian sectors, including construction, food and land, and military-linked business interests have been documented in other states of the region in different forms. This goes beyond the traditional defence function of the military toward a direct and influential role in resource allocation and in the distribution of business opportunities. It adds a further complex layer to the political economy analysis of the region, moving beyond the traditional dichotomy of "rentier state versus private sector" to a third actor of a special nature that combines security logic and commercial logic.

## Subsidy Reform as a Test of Social Contract Flexibility

Researchers in this current use the repeated attempts to reform energy and food subsidies across the region as an experimental laboratory for measuring the actual flexibility of each country's rentier social contract. When a government succeeds in passing a painful reform without protests threatening its stability (as Egypt did in stages after 2014 and 2016, under an IMF-supported programme), this suggests that alternative bases of legitimacy, such as nationalist, religious or security discourse, may have partly replaced direct rent distribution as a source of legitimacy.

## Comparison with Other Regional Experiences Outside the Region

This current draws on deliberate comparisons with other regions that have seen similar rentier dynamics, such as oil-producing Latin America (Venezuela) and Africa (Nigeria and Angola). The aim is to test whether the Arab pattern is genuinely distinctive or a special case of a wider global pattern of rentier states. These comparisons reveal that some features of rent-linked political stability in the Arab Gulf, such as the relatively small population relative to the large rent available per person, may be relatively unique. This contrasts with cases such as Nigeria, which has a higher population relative to the oil revenues available per capita. This may partly explain the variation in the degree of political stability despite the similar basic dependence on rent across these globally diverse cases.
