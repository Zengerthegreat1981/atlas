# Economic Anthropology as a Subfield

An established subfield of cultural anthropology that studies how goods and services are produced, distributed and consumed in different societies, and how the concepts of property, value, exchange and labour take shape across varied cultural frameworks that do not necessarily match the assumptions of neoclassical economics about the "rational optimising individual".

This branch is usually distinguished from the "school of Economic Anthropology" as the specific theoretical current formulated by figures such as Marcel Mauss, Karl Polanyi and Marshall Sahlins. That school forms a central, but not the whole, part of the broader branch, which also includes varied ethnographic field studies that do not all adhere to a single theoretical framework.

## The Foundational Debate: Formalists versus Substantivists

The branch arose out of a sharp methodological debate in the mid-twentieth century (most intense in the 1960s and 1970s) between two camps. The "Formalists" held that the analytical tools of neoclassical economics (scarcity, rational choice based on utility maximisation) can be applied universally to any human society regardless of its cultural context. The "Substantivists", led by Karl Polanyi, argued that in pre-capitalist societies the economy is "embedded" organically in wider social, kinship and religious relations, and cannot be isolated as an independent domain governed by the logic of the market and monetary exchange alone. This debate was never definitively settled, but it established the area of central questions on which the field still works today.

## From Gift to Debt: Extending the Questions

Many later studies in this branch drew on Marcel Mauss's famous essay "The Gift" (1925), which analysed systems of gift exchange (such as the kula exchange of the Trobriand Islands documented by Malinowski) as "total social facts" that produce mutual obligations (giving, receiving, reciprocating) going beyond purely economic exchange to establish lasting social and political bonds between groups. Later, anthropologists such as David Graeber extended this interest to the history of debt and money from a comparative ethnographic perspective, arguing that money itself often arose from relations of debt and social accounting that preceded any direct market exchange, reversing the conventional economic narrative that money solved the problem of direct "barter".

## The Informal Economy and Local Markets

The branch has also been concerned with the study of "informal economies" that operate outside the frameworks of the state and of formal companies, such as popular markets and informal credit (rotating savings associations, known in much of the Arab world as *jam'iyya*). These are treated as coherent economic systems with their own internal logic, based on social trust and kinship, rather than as mere economic "backwardness" awaiting formal modernisation. Arab researchers have also studied the dynamics of traditional markets (such as the market in the old cities of the Arab world) as complex economic-social institutions that combine commercial exchange with social hierarchy and family or sectarian belonging.

## Expansion into Late Capitalism

Since the 1990s the branch has expanded to study financial capitalism and economic globalisation itself with ethnographic tools, such as the study of stock and derivatives markets and the culture of "traders" in places such as Chicago, London and Wall Street (for example in the work of Caitlin Zaloom and Karen Ho) as cultural sites with their own practices and beliefs, just like traditional markets. This has redefined the branch from the study of "primitive or non-Western economies" to the study of the economy as a cultural practice everywhere, including the very heart of global capitalism.
