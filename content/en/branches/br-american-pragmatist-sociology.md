# American Pragmatist Sociology

American pragmatist sociology applies the philosophy of pragmatism to the study of social life, treating action, inquiry, and democracy as practical achievements rather than as applications of fixed principles. Its central claim is that meaning and truth are made in the course of doing: people understand their world by acting in it, encountering problems, and revising their habits in light of consequences. Where symbolic interactionism, its close relative, studies how selves and meanings emerge through interaction, this branch is wider: it takes pragmatism's full philosophy — its theory of action, its account of inquiry, its democratic ethics — as a framework for sociology, from the sociology of knowledge to the study of creativity, agency, and public life.

## Scope and Core Concepts

The branch's core concepts begin with habit and action: most conduct runs on habit, and action becomes reflective only when habit is interrupted by a problem. Inquiry is the process of resolving such problems through experiment, and truth is what survives the test of consequences. The self, in George Herbert Mead's account, emerges through taking the attitude of the other and of the generalised other, the organised attitudes of the community. Creativity is a central category: for pragmatists, action is not the execution of a plan but an open-ended response to a situation, and novelty is a normal feature of social life. The public, in John Dewey's sense, is the set of people affected by the indirect consequences of action, and democracy is the organised method by which a public identifies its problems and addresses them. The branch studies social problems, reform, education, and the professions as sites where inquiry and democracy meet, and it treats values as hypotheses to be tested in experience rather than as timeless foundations.

## Historical Development

Pragmatism began as a philosophy in the 1870s with Charles Sanders Peirce, but its sociological career began at the University of Chicago. Jane Addams's *Democracy and Social Ethics* (1902) brought pragmatist ethics to the settlement movement and to the study of urban life. William James's *Pragmatism* (1907) gave the philosophy its public name and its theory of truth as what works in experience. John Dewey's *Democracy and Education* (1916) applied pragmatism to schooling and to the formation of democratic citizens, and his *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938) systematised its account of how problems are resolved. George Herbert Mead's lectures, published posthumously as *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934), supplied the social psychology on which symbolic interactionism was built. Pragmatism's influence waned after the Second World War as Parsonsian structural functionalism dominated American sociology, but it revived from the 1980s onward: Hans Joas's *The Creativity of Action* (German 1992; English 1996) recovered its theory of creative action, and Mustafa Emirbayer and Ann Mische's "What Is Agency?" (1998) used pragmatist categories to rethink one of sociology's central concepts.

## Key Figures

Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914) founded pragmatism with his theory of inquiry and his maxim that the meaning of a concept lies in its practical consequences. William James (1842–1910) popularised the philosophy and developed its psychology of habit and its pluralist metaphysics. John Dewey (1859–1952) is the branch's central figure, whose philosophy of education, democracy, and public life remains its fullest statement. George Herbert Mead (1863–1931) supplied its social psychology of the self and the act. Jane Addams (1860–1935), founder of Hull-House, practised a pragmatist sociology of reform and was belatedly recognised as a sociological classic. Among contemporary figures, Hans Joas recovered pragmatism's theory of creative action for social theory, Mustafa Emirbayer developed a relational sociology drawing on Dewey and Mead, and Neil Gross, in *Richard Rorty: The Making of an American Philosopher* (2008), traced pragmatism's twentieth-century revival through its most influential public intellectual.

## Method

Pragmatist sociology's method follows from its philosophy: inquiry begins with a problem in a situation, not with a theory imposed from outside. Its researchers favour ethnography, case studies, and historical analysis, studying how actors themselves define problems and experiment with solutions. The method is fallibilist: claims are treated as hypotheses to be revised in light of consequences, and the researcher is understood as a participant in inquiry rather than a detached observer. Pragmatist sociology also has a practical, reformist orientation: research is judged in part by whether it helps a public address its problems, a standard inherited from Addams and Dewey. Its characteristic strength is its attention to process, creativity, and the open-endedness of action. Its characteristic criticism is that it lacks a theory of power: by emphasising problem-solving and deliberation, it can underplay conflict, domination, and the structural constraints that make some problems unsolvable by inquiry alone.

## Influence and Debates

The branch's influence is pervasive but often indirect. Mead's social psychology founded symbolic interactionism and shaped the sociology of the self, while Dewey's theory of the public informs the study of democracy, social movements, and the sociology of knowledge. Pragmatism supplied the philosophical background for the Chicago School's urban ethnography and for the sociology of professions, and its revival has shaped contemporary work on agency, creativity, and relational sociology. Debates continue. One concerns the relationship between pragmatism and symbolic interactionism: whether the two are one tradition or whether pragmatism's wider philosophy of action and democracy exceeds interactionism's focus on meaning. Another concerns power: critics argue that pragmatism's problem-solving model assumes a democratic public that rarely exists, while defenders reply that Dewey's account of the public was precisely a diagnosis of its eclipse. A third concerns the philosophy's standing: whether pragmatism is a distinct sociological theory or a background orientation that can be combined with other approaches.
