# Cyber Sociology

Cyber sociology is a label for the sociological study of cyberspace: online communities, virtual worlds, networked identities, and the cultures that form around computer-mediated communication. The term is associated particularly with the period when the internet was spreading into everyday life. Much of this work is now pursued under the broader heading of digital sociology, which also covers data, algorithms, platforms, and digital research methods.

## Background

The word "cyberspace" was popularised by the novelist William Gibson, notably in *Neuromancer* (1984). Donna Haraway's "A Cyborg Manifesto" (first published in 1985) used the figure of the cyborg to question boundaries between human and machine, and between nature and culture, and became an important reference for later studies of technoculture. In the 1990s researchers began to ask whether online interaction could sustain community and how it might alter the self.

## Key Works and Theorists

Howard Rheingold's *The Virtual Community* (1993) drew on participation in the WELL, an early online conferencing system, to argue that lasting social bonds can form through computer networks. Steven Jones's edited volume *CyberSociety* (1995) brought together early social research on computer-mediated communication. Sherry Turkle's *Life on the Screen* (1995) explored how people experimented with identity in online role-playing environments. Manuel Castells's *The Rise of the Network Society* (1996) placed information networks at the centre of a wider account of economic and social change.

## Empirical Examples

Daniel Miller and Don Slater's *The Internet: An Ethnographic Approach* (2000) studied internet use in Trinidad and challenged the idea of cyberspace as a realm apart: users fitted the technology into existing family relations, commerce, religion, and national identity. The collection *The Internet in Everyday Life* (2002), edited by Barry Wellman and Caroline Haythornthwaite, reported research on how online ties often supplement offline ones. Tom Boellstorff's *Coming of Age in Second Life* (2008) conducted fieldwork inside a virtual world, treating it as a social setting that can be studied in its own terms.

## Themes and Debates

Central themes include anonymity and self-presentation, norms and moderation in online groups, gaming cultures, and unequal access, often discussed as the digital divide. Early commentary tended to split between hopes that networks would level hierarchies and fears that they would isolate people. Empirical research generally complicated both views, showing that offline inequalities of gender, race, class, and nationality carry into online settings, while some groups also find new resources for connection there.

A continuing debate concerns the separation implied by the prefix "cyber." As mobile devices and social media made connection routine, many scholars argued that online and offline life are intertwined and should be analysed together. Questions raised by early cyber research, about community, identity, and control, remain active in studies of platforms, surveillance, and online harm.
