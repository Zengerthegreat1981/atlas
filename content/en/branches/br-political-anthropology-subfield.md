# Political Anthropology

Political anthropology is the study of power, authority, and the state from an ethnographic perspective. It asks how political order is made and unmade in everyday life: how chiefs, kings, bureaucrats, and revolutionaries gain and lose legitimacy, how people resist domination, and how the state appears — or fails to appear — in the places where people live. Unlike political science, which often studies institutions from above, political anthropology studies politics from below, through the lived experience of those who are governed. This entry treats the anthropological school of politics; it is distinct from entries on particular political concepts such as power or the state.

## History

Political anthropology emerged in British social anthropology in the 1940s. E. E. Evans-Pritchard and Meyer Fortes's edited volume *African Political Systems* (1940) compared African societies with centralized authority (states) and stateless societies, and argued that political order could exist without centralized government — a radical idea at the time. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) showed how a stateless society maintained order through kinship and feud. In the 1950s and 1960s, the Manchester School, led by Max Gluckman, shifted the focus from static structures to process and conflict. Gluckman's analysis of rituals of rebellion and Victor Turner's studies of social drama treated politics as something people do, not something institutions simply have. Edmund Leach's *Political Systems of Highland Burma* (1954) showed that political forms were unstable and strategically manipulated, not fixed tribal types. In the 1970s, Pierre Clastres's *Society Against the State* (1974) argued that some societies actively work to prevent the emergence of centralized power. The 1980s and 1990s brought a new focus on the state itself: James C. Scott's *Weapons of the Weak* (1985) documented the everyday resistance of Malaysian peasants, while James Ferguson's *The Anti-Politics Machine* (1990) showed how development projects in Lesotho expanded state power while claiming to be apolitical. Scott's *Seeing Like a State* (1998) analyzed how states simplify and control populations through maps, censuses, and standardized measures. In the 2000s, anthropologists studied neoliberalism, sovereignty, and the politics of security, with David Graeber's *Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology* (2004) reviving interest in stateless politics.

## Key Figures and Works

E. E. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) and the Fortes and Evans-Pritchard volume *African Political Systems* (1940) are usually treated as founding works of the field. Max Gluckman, founder of the Manchester School, shaped its focus on conflict and process; his student Victor Turner extended it toward ritual and symbolism. Edmund Leach, who trained at the LSE and worked outside the Manchester group, pursued the instability of political forms in *Political Systems of Highland Burma* (1954). Pierre Clastres's *Society Against the State* (1974) remains a touchstone for the anthropology of statelessness. James C. Scott's *Weapons of the Weak* (1985) and *Seeing Like a State* (1998) are among the most influential works in the social sciences on resistance and state power. James Ferguson's *The Anti-Politics Machine* (1990) is a classic study of development and bureaucracy. Akhil Gupta's article "Blurred Boundaries" (1995) examined the everyday state in India, showing how corruption and bureaucracy are experienced by villagers. David Graeber's *Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology* (2004) and *The Utopia of Rules* (2015) brought anarchist theory into dialogue with ethnography. John Gledhill's *Power and Its Disguises* (1994) synthesized the field's approaches to power, ideology, and resistance.

## Method

Political anthropology's method is ethnography of power: long-term fieldwork in the places where politics happens — villages, ministries, courts, markets, refugee camps, and protest movements. Its distinctive technique is the study of the state from below, observing how bureaucrats process papers, how citizens queue for services, and how policies are implemented or evaded in practice. The extended case method, developed by the Manchester School, traces a single conflict or event over time to reveal the social processes beneath it. Political anthropologists also study political rituals — elections, inaugurations, funerals of leaders — as moments when authority is displayed and contested. More recently, the field has embraced multi-sited research, following policies, commodities, and people across borders to study global governance. The method's strength is its attention to the gap between official accounts of politics and what actually happens, a gap that interviews and documents alone rarely reveal.

## Influence and Debates

Political anthropology transformed the study of politics by showing that stateless societies have politics, that resistance need not be revolutionary, and that the state is not a single thing but a set of practices and relationships. Its concepts — the social drama, everyday resistance, the anti-politics machine, seeing like a state — have entered the vocabulary of political science, history, and sociology. The field challenged the idea that the Western state is the endpoint of political evolution, documenting instead a diversity of political forms. A central debate concerns the nature of the state: is it a coherent actor or a fragmented collection of practices? Another concerns resistance: Scott's emphasis on hidden, everyday resistance has been criticized for romanticizing survival and underestimating the power of open rebellion. Feminist political anthropologists have shown how power operates through gender and the household, not only through public institutions. The field also debates its relationship to activism: many political anthropologists study movements they support, raising questions about objectivity and solidarity. As authoritarianism, populism, and state surveillance reshape politics worldwide, political anthropology's ethnographic attention to how power is actually lived remains a vital resource.

## Further comparisons

F. G. Bailey and Fredrik Barth analysed strategy, factions, and leadership. Barth's study of Swat Pathans (1959) examined how followers choose patrons. The volume *Political Anthropology* (1966), edited by Marc Swartz, Victor Turner, and Arthur Tuden, emphasised processes, goals, and public contests. Georges Balandier published an influential French synthesis in 1967.

In the United States, Morton Fried and Elman Service proposed evolutionary types such as band, tribe, chiefdom, and state. Critics questioned fixed stages and showed that mobile or decentralised peoples often took shape in relation to states. Eric Wolf's *Europe and the People without History* (1982) placed local societies inside expanding systems of trade, production, and conquest.

Drawing partly on Michel Foucault, ethnographers examined discipline, expertise, and governmentality. Research on nationalism, violence, democracy, humanitarianism, borders, and sovereignty treats the state as an uneven assemblage of people, documents, and practices. Access to powerful institutions is often limited. In situations of conflict, researchers have to weigh surveillance, partisanship, and the safety of people who speak. A very narrow definition of politics misses forms of authority carried by kinship or ritual, while a definition that includes every relation of influence loses precision.
