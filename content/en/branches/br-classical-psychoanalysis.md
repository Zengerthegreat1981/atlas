# Classical Freudian Psychoanalysis

The founding parent current of dynamic psychology, which arose in Vienna at the hands of Sigmund Freud in the 1890s, starting from the study of hysteria and the unconscious, and which formed the theoretical and clinical basis from which all later schools of psychoanalysis branched.

## The Central Thesis

Classical psychoanalysis rests on the hypothesis that human behavior, feelings, and neurotic symptoms are governed by unconscious motives and repressed instinctual conflicts that originate in early childhood (the stages of psychosexual development and the Oedipus complex). The psychic apparatus is driven by the conflict between the pleasure principle and the reality principle, and between the drives of the id and the moral prohibitions of the superego, and defense mechanisms undertake to protect the ego from the anxiety resulting from this conflict.

## Classical Clinical Practice

The classical analytic session is built on the rule of free association (Free Association), in which the client lies on the couch without direct face-to-face contact with the analyst, to facilitate the emergence of unconscious material. Treatment relies on the analysis of dreams, the client's resistances, and the interpretation of the phenomena of transference (Transference) and countertransference (Countertransference), the discharge of emotional charges, and the rebuilding of insight through the ongoing process of analytic work (Working-through).

## Its Place in the Atlas Network

The classical Freudian current represents the common trunk from which the early analytic currents (Adler, Jung, Rank) developed or split off, and whose school engaged the existential school in inquiries concerning the unconscious, anxiety, freedom, and moral responsibility.

## Limitation

The use of the strict classical framework (4 to 5 sessions a week for years) has declined in favor of contemporary and shorter-term forms, although its basic concepts still constitute the common language of psychoanalysis.

## From the Topographical to the Structural Model

Freud developed his theory in two main stages: the first topographical model (in *The Interpretation of Dreams*, 1900), which divided the psychic apparatus into consciousness, preconscious, and unconscious according to the ease of access to psychic content, and then the second structural model (in *The Ego and the Id*, 1923), which redivided it functionally into the id (the reservoir of instinctual drives), the ego (the mediator negotiating with reality), and the superego (the inner moral conscience), clarifying that defense mechanisms themselves can operate in a completely unconscious manner, which called for this deeper theoretical shift.

## Institutional Founding in Vienna

The Vienna Psychoanalytic Society was founded in 1908, growing out of informal meetings held at Freud's home every Wednesday (the Wednesday Psychological Society) since 1902, and in its early years it included figures who later split away, such as Alfred Adler (who left in 1911) and Wilhelm Stekel; Carl Gustav Jung, based in Zurich, was closely associated with the movement and was the first president of the International Psychoanalytic Association (founded 1910) before breaking with Freud. It grew into an international movement with its own congresses and journals.
