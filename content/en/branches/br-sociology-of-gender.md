# Sociology of Gender

The sociology of gender studies how societies construct, organize, and contest differences between women and men — and, increasingly, beyond the binary. It treats gender not as a natural expression of biological sex but as a social structure: a system of roles, expectations, institutions, and power relations that shapes work, family, sexuality, the body, and the self. The field asks how gender is learned, performed, and enforced in everyday interaction; how it is built into labour markets, states, and organizations; and how it intersects with class, race, and sexuality. It is distinct from feminist sociology, which is a political and theoretical tradition organized around the critique of patriarchy and the emancipation of women, and from Black feminist thought, which centres the experiences of Black women and the interlocking of race and gender. The sociology of gender draws on feminist theory but is organized as a subfield of sociology concerned with gender as a general dimension of social life, studied in women, men, and non-binary people alike.

## Scope

The field covers the gendered division of labour, including occupational segregation, the pay gap, and the unpaid domestic and care work that sustains economies. It studies families, parenting, and intimate life, and the changing norms of masculinity and femininity across generations. It examines gender in organizations, politics, and the professions, including the glass ceiling and the gendered cultures of workplaces. It studies the body, sexuality, and violence, from harassment to intimate-partner violence. It also studies gender socialization in childhood and schooling, and the production of gender in media and popular culture. Throughout, gender is analyzed as operating at three levels: the individual, the interactional, and the institutional.

## History

Gender became a sociological concept in the mid-twentieth century, as feminist scholarship challenged the assumption that sex differences explained social inequality. Simone de Beauvoir's claim that one is not born but becomes a woman supplied the founding insight that femininity is made, not given. In the 1970s, Gayle Rubin theorized the sex/gender system, showing how societies transform biological sexuality into organized inequality. The decisive conceptual shift came in 1987, when Candace West and Don Zimmerman argued that gender is something people do, not something they have: "doing gender" in interaction, accountable to the expectations of others. In the same year, R. W. Connell published *Gender and Power*, analyzing gender as a structure of power, production, and emotional relations, and later developed the concept of hegemonic masculinity. Judith Lorber's *Paradoxes of Gender* (1994) argued that gender is a social institution comparable to the economy and the family.

## Key Figures

Simone de Beauvoir founded the modern analysis of woman as socially constructed. Gayle Rubin theorized the sex/gender system and the political economy of kinship. Candace West and Don Zimmerman supplied the field's central interactionist concept, doing gender. R. W. Connell is the leading theorist of gender as a structure of power and of masculinities, including hegemonic, complicit, and marginalized forms. Judith Lorber argued for gender as a major social institution and for the degendering of social life. Barbara Risman developed the theory of gender as a social structure operating at individual, interactional, and institutional levels. Arlie Hochschild's study of the second shift showed how women's entry into paid work did not redistribute unpaid domestic labour. Michael Kimmel advanced the sociological study of masculinities, while Patricia Hill Collins and Kimberlé Crenshaw brought intersectionality into the centre of gender analysis.

## Key Works

De Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949) is the founding text of modern feminist thought on gender. Rubin's "The Traffic in Women: Notes on the 'Political Economy' of Sex" (1975) gave the field its concept of the sex/gender system. West and Zimmerman's "Doing Gender" (1987) is among the most cited articles in the sociology of gender, reframing gender as routine interactional accomplishment. Connell's *Gender and Power: Society, the Person and Sexual Politics* (1987) presented the structural theory of gender relations. Lorber's *Paradoxes of Gender* (1994) argued that gender, like class and race, is a principle of social organization. Connell's *Masculinities* (1995) established the sociology of masculinities as a field in its own right. Risman's "Gender as a Social Structure: Theory Wrestling with Activism" (2004) synthesized the field's multi-level framework and linked theory to the politics of gender change.

## Method

The sociology of gender uses the full range of sociological methods. Surveys and census data document occupational segregation, pay gaps, and the distribution of unpaid work. Ethnography and interviews reveal how gender is done in workplaces, families, and schools, and how people negotiate, resist, or reproduce gendered expectations. Conversation analysis has traced the micro-interactional production of gender. Historical and comparative research shows how gender orders vary across societies and change over time.

## Influence and Debates

The sociology of gender transformed sociology itself, making gender a core dimension of analysis alongside class and race, and it supplied the evidence base for equal-pay legislation, parental-leave policy, and anti-discrimination law. Its concept of doing gender reshaped the study of interaction, organizations, and identity. Critics debate whether the field's focus on interaction underplays the structural power of states and markets, and whether the concept of gender as a social structure can accommodate the diversity of trans and non-binary experience. Current debates concern the gendered effects of remote work and platform labour, the politics of gender identity and sex-based rights, care work and the care economy, and the global variation of gender orders under migration and religious revival.
