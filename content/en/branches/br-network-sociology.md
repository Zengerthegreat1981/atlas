# Network Sociology

Network sociology studies social life through relationships: the ties between people, organisations, and groups, and the patterns those ties form. Its premise is that what people do, know, and become depends not only on their individual attributes — age, class, education — but on their position in a network of relationships. A job found through a friend, a rumour that spreads through a school, a firm that thrives because it bridges two industries: all are network effects. Where the concept of a social network describes a single web of ties, network sociology is the subfield that theorises networks, develops methods to study them, and uses them to explain social outcomes.

## Overview

The field's basic vocabulary is simple. A network consists of nodes — people, groups, or organisations — connected by ties of kinship, friendship, work, exchange, or communication. Ties vary in strength, from close bonds to passing acquaintances, and in content, from affection to advice to money. Networks vary in density, in how clustered they are, and in whether they are centralised around a few hubs or spread evenly.

From this vocabulary the field builds powerful explanations. People in central positions hear news first and broker between groups; people on the periphery hear it late or not at all. Dense, closed networks enforce norms and sustain trust, but they can also trap members in redundant information. Networks that bridge otherwise separate groups — spanning what Ronald Burt called structural holes — give their brokers access to fresh ideas and opportunities. These structural facts help explain outcomes as different as finding a job, adopting an innovation, mobilising a protest, or spreading a disease.

## History

Network thinking has early roots: Georg Simmel analysed how individuals belong to multiple, overlapping circles, and Jacob Moreno invented sociometry in the 1930s, mapping friendships in classrooms and institutions with diagrams that are the ancestors of today's network graphs. But network sociology took its modern form in the 1970s. Mark Granovetter's "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) argued, against intuition, that acquaintances matter more than close friends for finding jobs, because weak ties reach into social circles beyond one's own. Barry Wellman's work on personal communities showed that urban life had not destroyed community but transformed it: people maintain dispersed networks of ties rather than living in a single, bounded neighbourhood.

Ronald Burt's Structural Holes (1992) showed that competitive advantage comes from brokering between disconnected groups. Stanley Wasserman and Katherine Faust's Social Network Analysis (1994) codified the methods — centrality, cohesion, equivalence, blockmodels — that made the field a rigorous discipline. The rise of the internet and of large-scale digital data then expanded the field enormously: Duncan Watts's Six Degrees (2003) brought network science to a wide audience, and studies of online networks, from friendship platforms to scientific collaboration, became central.

## Key Figures and Works

Simmel supplied the field's founding insight that social form — the pattern of ties — shapes social life independently of content. Moreno gave it its first measurement tools. Granovetter's weak-ties argument remains the field's most famous finding and a model of how a structural idea can explain an everyday outcome. Wellman redefined community for the network age, showing that ties, not places, are the substance of community.

Burt turned brokerage into a theory of advantage, showing that spanning structural holes predicts promotion, innovation, and profit. Wasserman and Faust provided the methodological canon that still anchors teaching and research. Watts connected sociology to physics and computer science, showing that small-world structures — highly clustered yet with short paths between any two people — appear across domains. Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler's Connected (2009) showed that behaviours and emotions, from obesity to happiness, spread through networks up to three degrees of separation, although later critics have questioned the strength of the causal claims.

## Methods

Network sociology's signature methods are structural: researchers collect data on who is tied to whom — through surveys, observation, records, or digital traces — and analyse the resulting structure with measures of centrality, density, clustering, and brokerage. Whole-network studies map bounded groups such as a school or a firm; ego-network studies map the personal networks of sampled individuals. Longitudinal designs track how networks form and change, and statistical models, including exponential random graph models, test which mechanisms generate observed structures. Visualisation is also central: a well-drawn network graph can reveal a pattern that tables conceal.

## Further Topics

Three extensions of the core programme deserve mention. Research on inequality and homophily shows that people tend to form ties with others who resemble them, a pattern reviewed by Miller McPherson, Lynn Smith-Lovin, and James Cook (2001); because opportunities travel through ties, homophily can reproduce existing privilege, and the same concern applies to online platforms, where recommendation systems and self-selection shape who is connected to whom. Dynamic network models treat ties as changing over time rather than as a fixed snapshot; Tom Snijders's stochastic actor-oriented models, for example, estimate how actors' choices and network structure shape one another. Finally, scholars of global governance have examined networks among states, regulators, and transnational organisations as a form of political power; Anne-Marie Slaughter's *A New World Order* (2004) argues that cooperation among national officials forms a distinctive layer of global rule, while Manuel Castells's work on the network society treats network position as a source of power.

## Influence and Debates

Network ideas have spread throughout sociology and beyond — into studies of organisations, markets, social movements, health, and the diffusion of innovations. The concept of social capital, developed by Pierre Bourdieu, James Coleman, and Robert Putnam, is in large part a network idea: the resources that flow from relationships.

Current debates concern structure and agency: does network position determine outcomes, or do people actively shape their networks? There are also debates about digital data — its scale is unprecedented, but it often records interaction without meaning — and about inequality, as network advantage compounds existing privilege. The field's central insight endures: to understand social life, look not only at individuals but at the ties between them.
