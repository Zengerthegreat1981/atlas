# Jungian Analytical Psychology

The current of analytical psychology founded by the Swiss psychiatrist Carl Gustav Jung following his theoretical break with Sigmund Freud between 1913 and 1914. It moved beyond reducing libido to the sexual drive, redefining it as general psychic energy, and formulated a symbolic and structural model that encompasses the collective unconscious and archetypes.

## Historical Context

Jung was among Freud's most prominent early collaborators, regarded by Freud as the movement's intellectual heir and elected the first president of the International Psychoanalytical Association in 1910. However, deep theoretical disagreements regarding the nature of libido (articulated in Jung's *Symbols of Transformation*, 1912, which treated libido as undifferentiated psychic energy rather than exclusively psychosexual) culminated in their personal and professional rift around 1913. Jung resigned the IPA presidency in 1914 and subsequently established his independent framework, termed "Analytical Psychology" to distinguish it from Freudian psychoanalysis. The movement was institutionalized in Zurich through the Psychological Club (1916) and later the C. G. Jung Institute (founded in 1948).

## Central Thesis

Jungian theory posits that the psyche comprises three levels: consciousness; the personal unconscious, containing repressed or forgotten individual experiences and feeling-toned complexes; and the collective unconscious, an inherited psychic substrate containing the archetypes (universal, primordial motifs and organizational potentials). Key archetypes include:
- **The Shadow**: the unacknowledged, repressed, or inferior aspects of the personality.
- **The Anima and Animus**: the contrasexual soul-images (the unconscious feminine archetype in men and the masculine archetype in women).
- **The Persona**: the adapted social facade presented to the external world.
- **The Self**: the archetype of wholeness and the unifying center of the entire psyche.

For Jung, the central goal of human psychological development is **individuation**: an ongoing lifelong process of self-realization achieved through integrating unconscious contents into conscious awareness and harmonizing psychic opposites.

## Clinical Method

Jungian analysis utilizes the method of **active imagination**, the amplification and interpretation of symbols and dreams, and the Word Association Test (developed by Jung and his colleagues at the Burghölzli psychiatric hospital in Zurich to diagnose unconscious complexes). Psychological symptoms are not viewed merely as pathologies or biological defects, but as purposive teleological communications from the unconscious directing the individual toward psychic compensation and developmental wholeness.

## Influence and Extensions

The Jungian paradigm extended into diverse domains: the interpretation of myth and fairy tales in the scholarship of Marie-Louise von Franz; the structural evolution of human consciousness in Erich Neumann; the development of "sandplay therapy" by Dora Kalff; and comparative mythology through Joseph Campbell.

## Contemporary Schools

Following Jung's death, analytical psychology diversified into distinct post-Jungian orientations (notably classified by Andrew Samuels):
- **The Classical School**: strictly adheres to Jung's original formulations, emphasizing the archetypes, the Self, and the teleological nature of individuation.
- **The Developmental School**: spearheaded by Michael Fordham in London, integrating Jungian concepts with British object relations theory and direct infant observation.
- **The Archetypal School**: led by James Hillman, shifting the focus away from ego-Self unification toward polytheistic, poetic, and imaginative deepening of psychic experience.
