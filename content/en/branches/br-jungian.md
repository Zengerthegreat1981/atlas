# Jungian Analytical Psychology

The current of analytical psychology founded by the Swiss psychiatrist and philosopher Carl Gustav Jung after his break with Sigmund Freud between 1913 and 1914. It moved beyond reducing libido to the sexual dimension toward a comprehensive psychic energy and a symbolic, mythological structure that includes the collective unconscious and the archetypes.

## Historical Context

Jung was among Freud's earliest collaborators, regarded by Freud as his heir apparent and the first president of the International Psychoanalytical Association (from 1910), but a theoretical disagreement over the nature of libido (set out in Jung's *Symbols of Transformation*, 1912, which treated libido as general psychic energy rather than purely sexual energy) led to a break between them around 1913. Jung resigned the presidency in 1914. Jung went on to develop his own approach, which he called "Analytical Psychology" to distinguish it from Freudian "psychoanalysis." It was institutionalized in Zurich through the Psychological Club (1916) and later the C. G. Jung Institute (1948).

## Central Thesis

Jungian psychology holds that the psyche consists of consciousness; a personal unconscious arising from repressed individual experiences; and a collective unconscious that carries the symbolic heritage and the primordial patterns (Archetypes) shared by humanity. Among these are the Shadow, which represents the rejected aspects of the self; the anima and animus, which represent the psychic counterpart to biological sex; the Self as the center of psychic integration; and the Persona, which represents the individual's social face. For Jung, the ultimate aim of psychological development is not mere adaptation but "Individuation": achieving the integration of the self by reconciling the opposites between consciousness and the unconscious.

## Clinical Method

Jungian analysis relies on the techniques of Active Imagination, the analysis of symbols, dreams and myths, and the Word Association Test, which Jung used and developed with colleagues at the Burghölzli clinic in Zurich to reveal psychic Complexes. The psychological symptom is viewed not as a biological defect but as a message directed from the unconscious, calling for the restoration of developmental balance.

## Influence and Extensions

The Jungian current extended into the study of myths and the interpretation of fairy tales in Marie-Louise von Franz; the study of the development of human consciousness in Erich Neumann; and Archetypal Psychology in James Hillman, who pushed Jungian ideas in a more aesthetic and poetic direction; as well as the "sandplay" therapeutic technique developed by Dora Kalff.

## Contemporary Schools

After Jung's death, Jungian analysis split into distinct tendencies, most prominently the Classical school, committed to Jung's original texts; the Developmental School led by Michael Fordham in London, which integrated ideas from British object relations theory and from the direct observation of child development; and the Archetypal school led by James Hillman, which pushed Jungian thought in an aesthetic and poetic direction closer to philosophical contemplation than to traditional clinical practice.
