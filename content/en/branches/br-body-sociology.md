# Body Sociology

Body sociology studies the human body as a social phenomenon: how societies shape bodies, how bodies carry social meanings, and how embodied experience shapes social life. For much of sociology's history the body was treated as biology, outside the discipline's concern. Body sociology insists otherwise. Bodies are classified by gender, race, age, ability, and attractiveness; they are disciplined by institutions, adorned and modified by individuals, and experienced through pain, pleasure, illness, and ageing. The body is at once a physical reality and a bearer of social meaning, and the sociology of the body studies both dimensions together.

## Scope

The field covers embodiment — how people live in and through their bodies — and the social regulation of bodies. It studies beauty norms and body image, fitness and dieting, tattooing and cosmetic surgery, disability, illness and medicine, sexuality, sport and dance, and the ageing body. It examines how institutions discipline bodies: schools that require stillness, factories and offices that organise posture and movement, prisons and hospitals that control bodily routines, and states that police dress, reproduction, and sexuality. It also studies bodily practices as forms of identity and resistance, from the deliberate cultivation of muscularity to the refusal of beauty norms. The body, in this view, is not a passive surface on which society writes; it is an active participant in social life.

## History

The field's roots lie in Marcel Mauss's essay "Techniques of the Body" (1935), which showed that even apparently natural acts — walking, swimming, sleeping — are learned socially and vary between cultures. Michel Foucault's *Discipline and Punish* (1975) and *The History of Sexuality* (1976) showed how modern power works on and through bodies, producing docile, self-monitoring subjects. Pierre Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979) analysed bodily taste — posture, accent, ways of eating — as markers of class, and his concept of habitus located social structure in the body itself. Bryan Turner established the field as a sociological specialty with *The Body and Society* (1984), arguing that the body had been sociology's "absent presence." Chris Shilling's *The Body and Social Theory* (1993) systematised the field, arguing that in late modernity the body has become a project — something individuals are expected to work on and manage. Loic Wacquant's *Body and Soul: Notebooks of an Apprentice Boxer* (2004) demonstrated the power of ethnography that takes the researcher's own body as an instrument of knowledge.

## Key Figures and Works

Mauss (1935) founded the study of bodily techniques. Foucault (1975, 1976) showed how power is exercised through the body. Bourdieu (1979) connected bodily dispositions to class. Turner (1984) gave the field its institutional identity and its historical account of why sociology had ignored the body. Mike Featherstone, Mike Hepworth, and Bryan Turner edited *The Body: Social Process and Cultural Theory* (1991), a landmark collection. Shilling (1993) provided the field's most influential synthesis, arguing that the body has become central to identity in consumer society. Wacquant (2004) turned boxing into a study of embodied craft, race, and masculinity in the Chicago ghetto. Feminist scholars have been central throughout: the study of beauty norms, eating disorders, reproductive politics, and the sexualised body has been driven largely by feminist sociology.

## Methods

Body sociology uses ethnography, interviews, visual methods, and historical analysis. Ethnography is especially important, because bodily knowledge — how to move, fight, dance, or endure pain — is often tacit and cannot be captured by questionnaires alone. Researchers have studied gyms, dance studios, boxing clubs, hospitals, and beauty salons through sustained participation. Interviews explore how people experience illness, disability, ageing, and bodily change. Visual methods, including photography and video, document bodily practices. Autoethnography, in which researchers analyse their own bodily experience, has become a recognised approach. The field also draws on medical sociology, the sociology of sport, and disability studies, and it increasingly uses digital methods to study how bodies are represented and judged on social media.

## Influence and Debates

Body sociology has reshaped how sociologists think about gender, race, class, and disability, showing that inequality is literally embodied — in health, in appearance, in the capacity to move through the world. It has influenced public health, disability policy, and debates about beauty standards and eating disorders. Its debates include the tension between constructionism and materiality: if the body is socially constructed, how do we account for its stubborn physicality — pain, illness, death? Another debate concerns agency: are people who diet, train, or undergo surgery exercising choice, or submitting to disciplinary norms? A third concerns the body in digital life: as interaction moves online, do bodies matter less, or do they matter differently, through images, avatars, and surveillance? The field's enduring insight is that the body is not a natural given that sociology can ignore, but a central site where social order is made, experienced, and contested.
