# Sociology of Family

The sociology of family studies the family as a social institution: how kinship is organised, how households are formed and dissolved, how intimacy, care, and authority are distributed within them, and how families connect to the wider economy, the state, and the life course. It treats the family not as a natural or timeless unit but as a historically variable arrangement, shaped by law, religion, labour markets, and gender norms. Its central question is how something so intimate is also so thoroughly social — and so consequential for who gets what in life.

## History

The sociology of family took shape in the mid-twentieth century, when functionalist theory gave the family a central place in the social order. Talcott Parsons and Robert Bales argued in *Family, Socialization and Interaction Process* (1955) that the modern family had become a specialised institution, stripped of production and education but essential for the socialisation of children and the stabilisation of adult personalities, with a sharp division between the husband's instrumental role and the wife's expressive role. This model was challenged from the 1960s onward. William Goode's *World Revolution and Family Patterns* (1963) compared family change across the world, documenting great variation in starting points and paths while arguing that industrialisation was pushing many societies toward conjugal-family patterns. Feminist scholars in the 1970s exposed the unpaid domestic labour on which the male-breadwinner family rested, and showed that the family could be a site of exploitation as well as affection.

## Key Figures

Talcott Parsons provided the classic functionalist account of the nuclear family, and remains the figure every later theorist argues with. William Goode was the great comparativist, who traced how family systems in the West, Africa, the Arab world, India, China, and Japan changed under industrialisation, each along its own historical path. Jessie Bernard, in *The Future of Marriage* (1972), argued that husbands and wives experience two different marriages, his and hers, because marriage benefits men more than women. Ann Oakley demonstrated in *The Sociology of Housework* (1974) that housework is real, skilled, and alienating labour, not a natural expression of femininity. Arlie Russell Hochschild showed how families manage feeling as well as money: *The Second Shift* (1989) documented the double burden of employed mothers, and her earlier work on emotional labour revealed the work of managing feeling that family life demands. Anthony Giddens theorised the transformation of intimacy, arguing that modern relationships are increasingly based on mutual disclosure and negotiated commitment rather than duty.

## Key Works

Parsons and Bales's *Family, Socialization and Interaction Process* (1955) is the foundational functionalist text. Goode's *World Revolution and Family Patterns* (1963) remains the classic comparative study. Bernard's *The Future of Marriage* (1972) reframed marriage as a gendered institution with unequal costs and benefits. Oakley's *The Sociology of Housework* (1974) made domestic labour visible as work. Hochschild's *The Second Shift* (1989) gave a name to the double day of paid employment and unpaid housework that employed mothers carry. Giddens's *The Transformation of Intimacy* (1992) argued that modern love has become a project of the self, based on the "pure relationship" that lasts only while it satisfies both partners. Cherlin's *Public and Private Families* (1996) is the standard synthesis of research on American family change, and Stacey's *In the Name of the Family* (1996) challenged the idea of a single model of "family values", drawing on her Silicon Valley fieldwork on improvised kinship in the earlier *Brave New Families* (1990). Together these works trace a field that moved from a single model of the family to a recognition of family diversity.

## Method

The sociology of family combines methods from across the discipline. Large-scale surveys and census data track marriage, divorce, fertility, household composition, and the division of domestic labour, revealing long-term trends and differences by class, race, and education. Longitudinal studies follow families over decades, showing how events such as divorce or the birth of a child reshape lives over time. Qualitative interviews and ethnography capture what surveys miss: how couples negotiate housework, how stepfamilies build relationships, how single parents manage time and money, and how children experience family change. Historical research shows that the family forms we take for granted — the nuclear household, romantic marriage, the long childhood — are recent inventions. The field also uses time-use diaries, which ask people to record everything they do in a day, to measure unpaid domestic work with a precision that ordinary surveys cannot achieve. A persistent methodological challenge is that families are hard to observe directly: much of what matters happens behind closed doors, and researchers must rely on what people say about intimate life, with all its silences and self-presentation.

## Influence and Debates

The sociology of family has shaped public debate more directly than most branches of the discipline, because everyone has a stake in how families are understood. Its research underpins debates about childcare policy, parental leave, divorce law, and the recognition of same-sex marriage. It has also been a field of fierce argument. Conservatives have read family change as decline, while sociologists have generally argued that diversity is not decay: most families, however constituted, do the work of care, socialisation, and support. Feminist scholars showed that the "traditional family" idealised in such debates rested on women's unpaid labour and limited choices. A second debate concerns individualisation: Giddens and others argued that modern intimacy has become freer and more negotiated, while critics replied that this freedom is unequally distributed, and that economic insecurity constrains the choices of poor families far more than those of the affluent. The field continues to expand, now engaging with assisted reproduction, transnational families separated by migration, and the care of ageing parents in ageing societies.
