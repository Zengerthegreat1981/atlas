# Consumption Sociology

The sociology of consumption studies how people acquire, use, display, and dispose of goods and services, and what these practices reveal about social order. It covers shopping, taste, fashion, brands, food, domestic material culture, and the markets and institutions that organise demand. It treats consumption as more than the satisfaction of individual wants: goods communicate membership and distinction, sustain relationships, and are embedded in routines.

## Foundations

Thorstein Veblen's *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899) analysed "conspicuous consumption," spending that displays wealth and status. Georg Simmel's essay on fashion described a dynamic of imitation and differentiation, in which fashions lose their value as they spread. The Frankfurt School later criticised mass-produced culture as standardising desire.

Consumption became a prominent research area from the 1970s onward. Jean Baudrillard's *The Consumer Society* (1970) argued that people consume signs and differences as much as useful objects. Mary Douglas and Baron Isherwood's *The World of Goods* (1979) treated goods as a means of making cultural categories visible and maintaining social ties. Pierre Bourdieu's *Distinction* (1979) showed, with survey evidence from France, how tastes in food, art, and leisure correspond to class position and help reproduce it.

## Further Theorists

Colin Campbell's *The Romantic Ethic and the Spirit of Modern Consumerism* (1987) proposed that modern consumerism draws on an imaginative, pleasure-seeking disposition with roots in Romanticism. George Ritzer's *The McDonaldization of Society* (1993) extended Weber's account of rationalisation to fast food and other services, stressing efficiency, calculability, predictability, and control. Juliet Schor's *The Overspent American* (1998) linked rising spending to comparison with increasingly affluent reference groups. Alan Warde has argued for practice theory, in which consumption occurs within activities such as eating or driving and follows their conventions.

## Empirical Examples

Daniel Miller's *A Theory of Shopping* (1998), based on ethnography in North London, interpreted routine provisioning as an expression of care and obligation toward others. Bourdieu's research related cultural preferences to education and social origin. Richard Peterson and Roger Kern (1996) found that high-status Americans had become more "omnivorous," enjoying a wider range of musical genres, which prompted a large debate on whether distinction had weakened or changed form.

## Debates

One debate asks whether consumers are creative agents who rework the meaning of goods or are steered by marketing, credit, and corporate power. Another concerns the relative importance of display and ordinary, habitual use. Environmental questions are increasingly central: researchers study why resource-intensive routines persist and what would make consumption sustainable. Digital platforms add further issues, including data-driven advertising, online reviews, and the blurring of consumption with unpaid production.
