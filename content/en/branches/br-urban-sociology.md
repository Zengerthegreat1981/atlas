# Urban Sociology

Urban sociology studies social life in towns and cities: how urban space is produced and divided, how people live among strangers, how communities form and dissolve, and how inequalities of class, race, ethnicity, and gender take spatial form.

## Scope

The field covers urbanisation and migration, neighbourhoods and community, residential segregation, housing, poverty, public space, urban politics and planning, social movements, gentrification, and the place of cities in the global economy.

## Classical Foundations

Friedrich Engels's *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) described the squalor and class separation of industrial Manchester and other towns. Ferdinand Tönnies contrasted community (Gemeinschaft) with society (Gesellschaft) in 1887. Georg Simmel's essay "The Metropolis and Mental Life" (1903) argued that the intensity of city stimulation and the money economy foster reserve, calculation, and a blasé attitude, together with new individual freedom. Max Weber's study of the city compared the political and economic institutions of urban communities in different civilisations. In the United States, W. E. B. Du Bois's *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) combined surveys, mapping, and history to study a Black urban population.

## The Chicago School and After

At the University of Chicago, Robert Park, Ernest Burgess, and their colleagues treated the city as a research laboratory. *The City* (1925) set out a human ecology of competition, invasion, and succession, and included Burgess's concentric-zone model of urban growth. Louis Wirth's "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938) linked the size, density, and heterogeneity of cities to impersonal social relations. Later community studies, such as Herbert Gans's *The Urban Villagers* (1962), showed that dense ethnic and working-class neighbourhoods could sustain strong ties. Jane Jacobs's *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961) criticised urban renewal and defended mixed-use streets.

## Critical and Global Perspectives

From the late 1960s, a new urban sociology influenced by Marxism challenged ecological explanations. Henri Lefebvre argued for a right to the city and analysed the social production of space. Manuel Castells's *The Urban Question* (1972) focused on collective consumption and urban conflict, and David Harvey's *Social Justice and the City* (1973) tied urban form to capital accumulation. John Logan and Harvey Molotch's *Urban Fortunes* (1987) described coalitions that pursue growth to raise the exchange value of land. William Julius Wilson's *The Truly Disadvantaged* (1987) linked concentrated poverty to job loss and social isolation, while Douglas Massey and Nancy Denton's *American Apartheid* (1993) emphasised racial segregation. Saskia Sassen's *The Global City* (1991) examined New York, London, and Tokyo as command centres of the world economy.

## Methods and Debates

Urban sociologists use censuses and surveys, mapping and spatial analysis, ethnography, interviews, and historical and comparative research. Current debates concern the existence and size of neighbourhood effects, the causes and consequences of gentrification and displacement, and whether theories built on North American and European cities fit the rapidly growing cities of Africa, Asia, Latin America, and the Arab world.
