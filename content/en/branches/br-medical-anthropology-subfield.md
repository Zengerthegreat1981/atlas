# Medical Anthropology as a Subfield

A broad subfield that studies how health, illness, and healing are understood within different cultural frameworks, and how the experience of illness is itself shaped socially, not only biologically. The subfield distinguishes between two central terms that have become its basic tools. "Disease" is the objective biological dysfunction as described by modern biomedicine. "Illness" is the subjective and social experience of suffering as lived by the patient and those around them within a particular cultural context. The two can diverge radically: a person may feel "ill" without carrying a measurable biological dysfunction, or the reverse may be true.

## Medical Pluralism and Therapeutic Plurality

The subfield developed the concept of "medical pluralism" to describe the widespread reality in which modern biomedicine coexists with other healing systems, such as folk medicine, herbal medicine, and religious healing. Patients in practice combine these resources strategically, depending on the perceived nature of the illness and the effectiveness of each system in their case. This does not necessarily follow a strict hierarchy that places biomedicine at the top. In the Arab context, this pluralism is evident in the coexistence of official medical treatment with practices such as lawful spiritual recitation (*ruqya*), visits to shrines, and traditional folk medicine. Illness is sometimes explained within spiritual frameworks (such as the evil eye or spirit possession) that stand alongside the biomedical diagnosis without necessarily replacing it.

## Arthur Kleinman and "Explanatory Models"

The physician and anthropologist Arthur Kleinman offered an influential framework of "explanatory models of illness." He argued that each party in the therapeutic encounter, physician and patient alike, carries its own explanatory model of the cause of illness, its expected course, and the appropriate treatment. He further argued that much of the failure in medical communication, and the resulting non-adherence to treatment, arises from a silent clash between the biomedical model held by the clinically trained physician and the cultural model held by the patient, without either party realizing that the clash exists.

## Structural Violence and Global Health

Researchers, including Paul Farmer, broadened the subfield's concern from narrow local cultural analysis to the link between patterns of illness and death and global poverty and structural inequality. Using the concept of "structural violence," they described how systems of poverty and social discrimination systematically produce unequal rates of illness and death between the poor and the wealthy, even when the same effective treatments are theoretically available to all. This transformed the field from the study of "cultural beliefs about illness" into a direct political critique of the global distribution of health resources.

## Modern Epidemics and the Field's Tests

The subfield gained renewed practical importance during recent global health crises, such as the Ebola outbreak and the COVID-19 pandemic. Medical anthropologists helped explain some communities' resistance to public health protocols, such as rejecting safe burial or doubting vaccines. They interpreted this resistance not as "ignorance" requiring top-down educational correction, but as a logical response within local systems of trust and meaning, which sometimes clash with protocols designed without sufficient regard for the local cultural context.
