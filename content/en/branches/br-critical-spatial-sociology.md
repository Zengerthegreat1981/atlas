# Critical Spatial Sociology

Critical spatial sociology studies space and place as socially produced and politically contested, asking how power is inscribed in territory, built environments, and everyday geographies. Its founding premise, drawn from Henri Lefebvre, is that space is not a neutral container in which social life happens but a product of social relations — and a means through which those relations are reproduced and challenged. Where urban sociology studies the city as a social form, this branch is wider: it takes space itself as its object, from the body and the home to the neighbourhood, the border, and the planet, and it treats spatial inequality as a central dimension of social inequality alongside class, race, and gender.

## Scope and Core Concepts

The branch's core concepts begin with the production of space: the claim that space is made through social practice, representations, and lived experience, in Lefebvre's threefold scheme of spatial practice, representations of space, and representational spaces. Uneven development names capitalism's tendency to produce geographical difference — booming regions alongside abandoned ones — as a condition of its own functioning. The right to the city is the demand that urban inhabitants collectively shape the spaces they inhabit, rather than merely consume them. The spatial fix describes how capital temporarily resolves its crises by moving geographically or rebuilding places. Scale refers to the levels at which social processes are organised, from the local to the global, and the branch studies how scale itself is produced politically. Place is distinguished from abstract space: a location invested with meaning, memory, and identity. The branch studies gentrification, segregation, borders, displacement, and the spatial politics of race, gender, and disability.

## Historical Development

The field emerged from the urban crisis of the late 1960s and early 1970s. Manuel Castells's *The Urban Question* (1972) argued that urban problems were expressions of capitalist contradictions rather than technical failures, and David Harvey's *Social Justice and the City* (1973) recast urban geography as a critique of how capital produces spatial inequality. Henri Lefebvre's *The Production of Space* (1974) supplied the field's philosophical foundation, arguing that every society produces its own space. Neil Smith's *Uneven Development* (1984) developed the theory of how capital differentiates space, and Edward Soja's *Postmodern Geographies* (1989) proclaimed a spatial turn across the social sciences, insisting that space had been neglected in favour of time and history. Doreen Massey's *Space, Place and Gender* (1994) brought feminist analysis to the field, showing how space and gender are mutually constituted, and Derek Gregory's *Geographical Imaginations* (1994) examined how geographical knowledge itself is entangled with power. Since then the branch has expanded into the study of globalisation, borders, digital space, and climate, while the right-to-the-city movement has carried its ideas into urban politics worldwide.

## Key Figures

Henri Lefebvre (1901–1991), the French Marxist philosopher, is the branch's founding theorist; his triad of spatial practice, representations of space, and lived space remains its central framework. David Harvey is its most influential Anglophone exponent, linking urbanisation, capital accumulation, and spatial justice across five decades of work. Manuel Castells analysed the city as a site of collective consumption and later of network society. Neil Smith theorised uneven development and gentrification, coining the rent gap as the mechanism driving neighbourhood change. Edward Soja championed the spatial turn and developed the concept of thirdspace, the lived space beyond the binaries of material and imagined geography. Doreen Massey rethought place as open and relational rather than bounded and authentic, and insisted on a global sense of place. Derek Gregory linked geography to postcolonial critique, and more recently scholars such as Mustafa Dikeç and Don Mitchell have extended the field into the politics of public space, policing, and the right to the city.

## Method

Critical spatial sociology uses mixed methods organised around place. Ethnography and participant observation capture how people inhabit, navigate, and contest spaces in everyday life. Historical analysis traces how places were produced — through planning, speculation, dispossession, and resistance — while mapping and spatial analysis visualise patterns of segregation, investment, and displacement. Interviews and oral histories recover the meanings residents attach to neighbourhoods threatened by redevelopment. The branch also studies policy documents, planning archives, and property markets to show how spatial decisions are made and who benefits. A distinctive methodological commitment is the refusal to treat space as background: the researcher must show how spatial arrangements actively shape the social processes under study, whether a housing estate's design, a border's enforcement, or a shopping mall's policing of loitering.

## Influence and Debates

The branch's influence extends across sociology, geography, urban studies, and planning. Its concepts — the production of space, the right to the city, uneven development — have shaped research on gentrification, segregation, migration, and globalisation, and have entered the vocabulary of housing movements and municipal politics. It supplied the spatial vocabulary for the study of global cities, for the sociology of borders, and for analyses of how pandemics and climate change are experienced unevenly across places. Debates persist. One concerns the balance between space and time: critics of the spatial turn warn against neglecting history, while its defenders argue that spatial analysis was never meant to replace historical analysis. Another concerns the concept of place: whether Massey's open, relational sense of place can accommodate the attachments and exclusions that make places matter politically. A third concerns scale: whether global processes can be studied without losing sight of the neighbourhoods and bodies where they are lived.
