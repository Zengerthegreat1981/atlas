# Urban Anthropology as a Subfield

A subfield that arose from the need to extend the classical anthropological method, which was based on the long-term field residence in relatively small and cohesive communities, to include large modern cities. Cities were at first seen as an environment so complex and fluid in its relations that they were thought unsuitable for the traditional method of "participant observation", which depends on comprehensive knowledge of a small, limited community.

## The Methodological Solution: Scaling Down the Field Within the City

Anthropologists arrived at a practical methodological solution. Instead of trying to study "the city" as a vast whole, the researcher chooses a particular neighbourhood or network of relations within the city (such as a working-class quarter, an immigrant community or a particular popular market) and applies to it the same intensive participant observation traditionally used in villages. The assumption is that this miniaturised urban "unit" can reveal, through its fine details, broader social dynamics concerning the city, modernity and urbanisation in general, without the need to study the entire city at once.

## Oscar Lewis and the "Culture of Poverty"

Oscar Lewis introduced an influential and controversial concept, the "Culture of Poverty", based on intensive ethnographic studies of poor families in Mexico and Puerto Rico. He argued that chronic poverty across successive generations produces a set of values and behaviours adapted to it (such as short-term planning and weak trust in official institutions), which are transmitted culturally from one generation to the next regardless of later changes in the actual economic conditions. The concept was later subjected to sharp criticism for risking an implicit blame on the poor themselves for the persistence of their poverty, ignoring the structural factors (such as institutional discrimination and housing policies) that actually reproduce poverty regardless of any presumed internal "culture" among the poor.

## Rural-Urban Migration and Social Networks

The subfield has studied how migrants from the countryside to the city maintain strong networks of kinship and village ties within their new urban environment, through mechanisms such as village associations in the city and mutual financial and employment assistance among migrants from the same village. This refutes a classical and widespread assumption in early urban sociology, which held that urbanisation necessarily dissolves traditional ties and replaces them with superficial and temporary individual relations. This dynamic is well documented in the large Arab cities that experienced intensive rural-urban migration since the mid-twentieth century.

## The Global City and Spatial Segregation

The subfield has recently expanded to include the study of "global cities" such as New York and London as central nodes in a connected world economy, where layers of low-wage migrant workers coexist with transnational financial elites within the same urban geography, yet in almost entirely separate social worlds. This makes the study of spatial segregation and class inequality within a single city a tool for understanding the structure of economic globalisation itself at a smaller and more tangible scale than macro-economic abstraction.
