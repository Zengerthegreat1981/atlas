# Sociology of Science and Knowledge

The sociology of science and knowledge studies scientific knowledge as a social product, asking how what counts as true is made, stabilised, and contested in particular times and places. It treats science not as a mirror of nature standing outside society but as a human activity carried out in laboratories, journals, funding bodies, and controversies, shaped by interests, instruments, and institutions. Where the concept of science names the enterprise itself, this branch names the sociological field that investigates it: from the classical sociology of knowledge, which asked how social position shapes thought, to the sociology of scientific knowledge, which asks how the very content of scientific claims is socially constructed, and on to contemporary studies of expertise, technology, and the public understanding of science.

## Scope and Core Concepts

The branch's scope covers the production, circulation, and authority of knowledge. Core concepts include the sociology of knowledge, Karl Mannheim's programme for relating styles of thought to social location; the ethos of science, Robert Merton's norms of universalism, communalism, disinterestedness, and organised scepticism; and the thought collective, Ludwik Fleck's term for the community whose shared practices make a fact possible. The strong programme, developed at Edinburgh, insists that true and false beliefs alike require sociological explanation, and that explanation should be symmetrical, causal, and reflexive. The laboratory study treats the lab as a site where facts are manufactured through instruments, inscriptions, and negotiations. Boundary work describes how scientists draw and police the line between science and non-science, while expertise names the socially recognised capacity to make authoritative claims. The branch also studies scientific controversies, replication, peer review, funding, and the relations between science, the state, and the public.

## Historical Development

The field has two founding moments. Karl Mannheim's *Ideology and Utopia* (1936, English edition) founded the sociology of knowledge, arguing that thought is rooted in social existence while reserving a special role for free-floating intellectuals. Robert Merton's *Science, Technology and Society in Seventeenth-Century England* (1938) founded the sociology of science, linking Puritan values to the rise of experimental science, and his later work codified the norms of the scientific community. Ludwik Fleck's *Genesis and Development of a Scientific Fact* (1935), long neglected, anticipated the field's constructivist turn by showing how facts emerge from the practices of thought collectives. Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) transformed the field by arguing that science advances through paradigms and revolutions rather than steady accumulation. David Bloor's *Knowledge and Social Imagery* (1976) launched the strong programme, Bruno Latour and Steve Woolgar's *Laboratory Life* (1979) opened the laboratory to ethnographic study, and Steven Shapin and Simon Schaffer's *Leviathan and the Air-Pump* (1985) showed how seventeenth-century disputes about the air-pump were simultaneously disputes about knowledge and political order.

## Key Figures

Karl Mannheim (1893–1947) is the founding figure of the sociology of knowledge, and Robert Merton (1910–2003) of the sociology of science. Thomas Kuhn (1922–1996), though a historian and philosopher, supplied the field's central vocabulary of paradigms, normal science, and revolutions. David Bloor and Barry Barnes developed the strong programme at Edinburgh, insisting that sociological explanation apply to scientific truth itself. Bruno Latour became the field's most influential and controversial figure, developing actor-network theory and the ethnography of laboratories. Steven Shapin, the historian of science, showed how trust, credibility, and the figure of the gentleman underpinned early modern science. Harry Collins studied the tacit knowledge and experimental practice behind scientific controversies, while Karin Knorr Cetina opened the laboratory to ethnography alongside Latour and Woolgar. Sheila Jasanoff later extended the field into science and technology studies' engagement with law, policy, and democracy, developing the idiom of co-production.

## Method

The branch's methods are as distinctive as its questions. Laboratory ethnography observes scientists at work, following instruments, samples, and inscriptions to see how facts are assembled. Discourse analysis examines how scientific texts, debates, and controversies deploy rhetoric to establish credibility. Historical case studies reconstruct past controversies — the air-pump, the discovery of the neutrino, the cold fusion affair — to show how closure was achieved. Interviews and surveys map the norms, networks, and careers of scientists, while quantitative studies track citations, collaboration, and funding. A central methodological commitment is symmetry: the analyst should explain the success of a claim and its failure with the same kinds of causes, rather than explaining success by nature and failure by society. Critics charge that symmetry slides into relativism; defenders reply that explaining how a belief came to be accepted is not the same as denying that it is true.

## Influence and Debates

The branch's influence reaches across the social sciences and into science policy. It reshaped the philosophy of science, informed the field of science and technology studies, and changed how universities teach the nature of science. Its studies of expertise inform debates about climate change, vaccines, and artificial intelligence, where the authority of science is publicly contested. Its concepts — paradigm, boundary work, co-production, the social construction of facts — have entered the vocabulary of historians, policymakers, and journalists. Debates continue. The science wars of the 1990s saw natural scientists accuse sociologists of relativism, while sociologists accused their critics of misunderstanding the argument: to show that knowledge is socially made is not to show that it is false. A second debate concerns the politics of the field: whether the study of science should serve the democratisation of expertise or remain a detached analysis. A third concerns the field's expansion into technology, medicine, and the environment, and whether the sociology of knowledge can still hold together as one field.
