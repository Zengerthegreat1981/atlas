# Sociology of Religion

The sociology of religion studies religious belief, practice, and organisation as social phenomena. It asks how religions emerge and change, how they bind communities together and divide them, how they legitimate power and inspire resistance, and how religious life is reshaped by modernity, migration, and globalisation. It does not ask whether religious claims are true; it asks what religion does in society. It overlaps with the anthropology of religion, but with a different accent: where religious anthropology typically studies belief and ritual through close ethnography of particular communities, the sociology of religion has been defined by large-scale questions about secularisation, the relationship between religion and capitalism, religious organisations and markets, and the place of religion in modern public life.

## History

The sociology of religion begins with the classical theorists. Émile Durkheim's *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) argued that religion is society worshipping itself: rituals generate collective effervescence, a shared emotional energy that renews social bonds and makes the sacred real. Max Weber, in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905), traced an elective affinity between Calvinist belief and the rational, disciplined conduct that made modern capitalism possible, and in his comparative studies of world religions he showed how different religious ethics produced different paths to modernity. In the mid-twentieth century, the dominant question became secularisation: would modernisation make religion disappear? Peter Berger's *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) gave the classic account of secularisation as the erosion of a shared religious worldview, though Berger later revised his view. From the 1980s onward, the field was transformed by the evident persistence and revival of religion — in American evangelicalism, in political Islam, in Pentecostalism across the Global South — and by new theories of religious markets and religious economies.

## Key Figures

Émile Durkheim laid foundations for the sociological study of religion with his theory of the sacred and of ritual as the source of social solidarity. Max Weber is the great comparative theorist, linking religious ethics to economic and political development. Peter Berger defined the post-war debate on secularisation and pluralism, arguing that modernity fragments religious certainty and forces believers to choose. Rodney Stark, with William Sims Bainbridge, developed the religious economies approach, treating religions as firms competing in a market for adherents and arguing that religious monopoly and state regulation, rather than modernity as such, depress participation, while competition sustains it. José Casanova challenged the secularisation thesis with his account of the deprivatisation of religion, showing how religions re-entered public life in Spain, Poland, Brazil, and the United States. Grace Davie coined the phrase "believing without belonging" to describe European religion, where belief persists even as church attendance falls.

## Key Works

Durkheim's *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) remains the field's founding text. Weber's *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905) is the classic study of religion and economic life. Berger's *The Sacred Canopy: Elements of a Sociological Theory of Religion* (1967) is the key post-war statement on secularisation and plausibility structures. Stark and Bainbridge's *A Theory of Religion* (1987) is the landmark of the religious economies approach, modelling religion as exchange with supernatural beings. Casanova's *Public Religions in the Modern World* (1994) redefined the debate by showing that religions can be modern and public without being fundamentalist. Davie's *Religion in Britain since 1945: Believing without Belonging* (1994) gave the field its most influential description of European religious change. Bruce's *God Is Dead: Secularization in the West* (2002) is the strongest recent defence of the secularisation thesis. Together these works show a field that has moved from grand theories of religion's origins to detailed accounts of its persistence and transformation.

## Method

The sociology of religion uses the full range of sociological methods. Large-scale surveys — such as the World Values Survey, the European Values Study, and national census data — track religious affiliation, belief, and practice across countries and generations, and are the main evidence in debates about secularisation. Ethnography of congregations, mosques, temples, and new religious movements reveals how religious life is actually lived: how people pray, how communities organise, and how belief is negotiated in everyday life. Historical and comparative research traces the long-run relationship between religion, state formation, and economic change. Interviews explore conversion, doubt, and the experience of the sacred. A persistent methodological challenge is measurement: affiliation, belief, and practice do not move together, and surveys that count church membership may miss the diffuse spirituality that Davie and others describe. The field therefore combines quantitative and qualitative approaches, using each to correct the other.

## Influence and Debates

The sociology of religion has shaped public understanding of some of the most contested issues of our time: the rise of religious fundamentalism, the politics of Islam, the culture wars in the United States, and the place of religion in secular states. Its central debate remains secularisation. The classic thesis — that modernisation brings religious decline — has been challenged by the vitality of religion in the United States, Latin America, Africa, and Asia, and by the growth of Pentecostalism and political Islam. Defenders reply that secularisation was always a claim about Western Europe, and that even there it describes the decline of institutional religion rather than the disappearance of belief. A second debate concerns the religious economies model: critics argue that treating religion as a market reduces faith to consumer choice and ignores the power of states, cultures, and traditions. A third concerns the boundaries of the field: as spirituality moves outside institutions — in yoga, mindfulness, and personalised belief — sociologists debate whether religion is declining or merely changing form. A field whose classics include Durkheim's study of Aboriginal Australian religion now confronts a world in which religion is simultaneously declining, reviving, and reinventing itself.
