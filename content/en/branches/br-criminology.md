# Criminology

Criminology is the systematic study of crime: why people break the law, how societies define and respond to criminal behaviour, and what happens to offenders, victims, and communities in the process. It is an interdisciplinary field, drawing on sociology, psychology, law, economics, and political science, but its sociological core asks a distinct question: how do social structures, institutions, and meanings produce crime and shape the institutions that punish it? Unlike criminal sociology, which studies crime strictly as a sociological phenomenon within the discipline of sociology, criminology is a broader field in its own right, encompassing the study of criminal law, policing, courts, prisons, and victimisation alongside sociological theory.

## Scope

Criminology covers four overlapping domains. First, the aetiology of crime: the social, economic, psychological, and biological conditions associated with offending. Second, the social construction of crime: how laws are made, which acts are criminalised, and whose behaviour is policed. Third, the criminal justice system: policing, prosecution, sentencing, imprisonment, and rehabilitation. Fourth, victims and harm: who suffers from crime, how victimisation is distributed, and how societies repair harm. Contemporary criminology also studies crimes that older traditions neglected: white-collar and corporate crime, state crime, environmental crime, cybercrime, and gendered violence. The field is therefore both empirical, measuring patterns of offending and victimisation, and critical, questioning the categories of crime and justice themselves.

## History

The classical school of the eighteenth century treated the offender as a rational actor. Cesare Beccaria, in *On Crimes and Punishments* (1764), argued that punishment should be certain, swift, and proportionate, and that its purpose was deterrence rather than revenge. The nineteenth century brought positivist criminology: Cesare Lombroso claimed in *L'uomo delinquente* (1876) that criminals could be identified by physical traits, a claim later discredited but influential in shifting attention to the offender's characteristics. The Chicago School in the early twentieth century turned criminology toward sociology. Robert Park and Ernest Burgess's studies of the city's growth and zones of settlement supplied the ecological framework, and Clifford Shaw and Henry McKay showed in *Juvenile Delinquency and Urban Areas* (1942) that high delinquency rates persisted in the same districts even as their populations changed, pointing to social disorganisation rather than individual pathology.

In the mid-twentieth century, criminology diversified. Robert Merton's strain theory (1938) linked crime to the gap between culturally approved goals and the legitimate means available to reach them. Edwin Sutherland's differential association theory (1939) argued that criminal behaviour is learned through interaction. In the 1960s, labelling theory, associated with Howard Becker's *Outsiders* (1963), argued that deviance is created by the social reaction to an act rather than by the act itself. From the 1970s onward, critical and feminist criminology challenged the field's focus on street crime, pointing to crimes of the powerful, and radical criminology linked crime to inequality and the interests of the state.

## Key Figures and Works

Beccaria (1764) remains the foundation of deterrence theory and penal reform. Lombroso (1876) founded positivist criminology, even though his biological determinism was abandoned. Emile Durkheim, in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), argued that crime is normal in every society and that punishment reaffirms collective values. Sutherland's *Principles of Criminology* (1939) systematised the field, and Sutherland's work on white-collar crime (introduced in his 1939 presidential address to the American Sociological Society and developed in *White Collar Crime*, 1949) insisted that respectable businessmen could be criminals. Merton's essay on social structure and anomie (1938) became one of the most cited theories in the field. Becker's *Outsiders* (1963) made labelling theory central to the sociology of deviance. Michel Foucault's *Discipline and Punish* (1975) reinterpreted the prison as a technique of power that produces disciplined subjects. David Garland's *The Culture of Control* (2001) analysed the late twentieth-century shift toward punitive crime control in Britain and the United States.

## Methods

Criminologists use official statistics, victimisation surveys, self-report studies, ethnography, interviews, and experiments. Official crime statistics reveal patterns but are shaped by reporting and recording practices, so victim surveys such as the British Crime Survey (begun 1982) and the American National Crime Victimization Survey were developed to measure the "dark figure" of unreported crime. Ethnographic studies of gangs, drug markets, and prisons capture the meanings offenders give to their actions. Longitudinal studies follow cohorts over decades to identify risk factors and turning points. Experimental and quasi-experimental designs evaluate interventions such as hot-spot policing or restorative justice. Increasingly, criminologists also analyse large administrative datasets and digital traces, while critical criminologists use historical and documentary methods to study state and corporate crime.

## Influence and Debates

Criminology has shaped penal policy more directly than most sociological fields. Deterrence theory underpins sentencing policy; rehabilitation models shaped probation and prison programmes; situational crime prevention, developed by Ronald Clarke, reshaped policing and urban design. The field also feeds public debate about punishment: the rise of mass incarceration in the United States, the politics of "law and order," and the racial and class inequalities of criminal justice are all central criminological concerns. Contemporary debates include whether crime is falling or merely changing form, how to respond to cybercrime and environmental harm, whether restorative justice can replace punishment, and how feminist criminology's attention to gendered violence should reshape the field. Criminology's enduring tension is between explaining crime as a problem to be controlled and understanding it as a mirror of the society that defines it.
