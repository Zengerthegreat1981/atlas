# Psycholinguistics: Mental Processing of Language

A branch lying at the intersection of linguistics and cognitive psychology. It studies the actual mental operations that humans use when they produce speech, understand it and acquire it, rather than merely describing the structure of language as an abstract system, as traditional structural linguistics does.

## Origins: The Convergence of Linguistics and Cognitive Psychology

The term began to take shape in the 1950s, with the 1953 summer seminar at Indiana University (whose proceedings appeared in 1954), which brought together linguists and psychologists to formulate a shared research basis. The field was then strongly reinforced by the "cognitive revolution" in the late 1950s and early 1960s, when Noam Chomsky presented his critique of linguistic behaviorism and put forward the idea that language is a complex cognitive system that deserves experimental psychological study of the mechanisms of its actual processing, not merely structural description.

## Core Topics

The branch is organized around several main areas: **speech production** (how sentences are planned and pronounced, and what slips of the tongue reveal about the advance mental planning of speech); **speech comprehension and reading** (how sentences are processed in real time, word by word, and how grammatical ambiguity is resolved instantly); **language acquisition in children** (one of the most fertile areas of experimental psycholinguistics, from first words to complex structures); and **lexical access** (how the brain retrieves the correct word from a huge lexical store within fractions of a second). The phenomenon of "overregularization" in the conjugation of irregular verbs (such as a child producing *goed* instead of *went* after having previously mastered the correct form) has also served as a central experimental test case for the debate between innateness and usage-based learning.

## Experimental Method

The branch relies methodologically on precise experimental designs that measure reaction time (how long a speaker takes to distinguish a real word from an invented one), track eye movements during reading (to see where the eye stops and for how long), and use functional brain imaging techniques to record neural activity during real-time language processing. This contrasts with the structural descriptive approach, which relies on analyzing written or spoken texts in isolation from the actual mechanism of their mental processing.

## Relation to Neighboring Fields

Psycholinguistics overlaps with neurolinguistics (which links these processes to specific brain structures through the study of brain damage), with computational linguistics (which sometimes attempts to model these processes mathematically), and with cognitive developmental psychology in studying how linguistic abilities gradually develop in infants.

## Measuring Real-Time Processing

Much of the experimentation in this branch relies on measuring the precise time (in milliseconds) that the brain takes to process a particular linguistic stimulus. This is based on a basic methodological assumption: very slight differences in reaction time (faster or slower by fractions of a second) reflect real differences in the complexity of the ongoing mental processing. For this purpose, "lexical decision" experiments are used (a participant is shown a string of letters and asked to press a button as quickly as possible to indicate whether it is a real word or not), as well as "semantic priming" experiments, which reveal how the presentation of a semantically related word (such as "doctor") facilitates the rapid recognition of a subsequent related word (such as "nurse") compared with an unrelated word. This shows the structure of the mental lexical network and how it is internally organized.

## First Language Acquisition: Documented Developmental Paths

Tracing the stages of first language acquisition in children is one of the most fertile experimental fields in this branch. It begins with very early phonetic discrimination in infants (the ability to distinguish sounds of languages they have not yet encountered, an ability that gradually declines after the first months as it becomes specialized only for the sounds of the surrounding language). It then passes through the babbling stage and the one-word stage, and reaches the rapid vocabulary explosion around age two and multi-word combinations. These stages are documented with high precision across many languages, although the exact timing varies slightly depending on the complexity of each language's morphological and phonological system.

## The Branch's Relation to General Cognitive Psychology

Psycholinguistics borrows much from general cognitive psychology models (such as working memory models and selective attention models), applying them specifically to language processing. At the same time, it provides important evidence to general cognitive psychology about the nature of human mental processing in general. One key question is whether language processing is a "special module," cognitively separate from the other mental abilities, or whether it uses general shared cognitive resources that are also used for non-linguistic tasks such as memory and attention. This question intersects directly with the broader debate on linguistic innateness.

## Speech Errors as a Window on Mental Planning

Speech errors (such as swapping the syllables of two words by mistake, or replacing a word with another of similar sound or meaning) are used as an important indirect piece of evidence about how the brain plans speech before actually pronouncing it. The occurrence of a regular pattern of error (not entirely random) reveals that the brain plans several parts of the sentence in advance, so that these advance planning elements may mistakenly interfere with one another.
