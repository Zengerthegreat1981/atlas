# Endogenous Growth Theory: Knowledge and Innovation as Self-Sustaining Engines of Growth

A current within neoclassical economics that emerged in the mid-1980s to address a fundamental gap in the traditional neoclassical growth model (the Solow–Swan model of 1956). That model treated technological progress as an *exogenous* driver of long-run growth, meaning a variable assumed to occur from outside the model with no internal economic explanation. This left unexplained what Solow's own growth-accounting work (1957) identified as the most important source of long-run growth. That work found that the residual not explained by measured capital and labour, which he interpreted as technical change, accounted for the larger part of the growth of output per worker in the United States over the period he studied.

## Paul Romer: knowledge as a good with increasing returns

The economist Paul Romer offered a prominent answer in his doctoral dissertation (University of Chicago, 1983), in his 1986 article "Increasing Returns and Long-Run Growth", and then in "Endogenous Technological Change" (1990). Knowledge and technical innovation, he argued, are not an arbitrary external given. They are the product of conscious, rational investment decisions (research and development, education, accumulation of human capital) that respond to economic incentives like any other investment, and can therefore be explained "internally" within the economic model itself. More importantly, knowledge is a good with unique economic properties: it is non-rival (using an idea does not prevent others from using it at the same time, unlike ordinary material goods). In Romer's 1990 model, ideas are non-rival but partially excludable (through patents and monopoly rents), which is what rewards research. Investment in knowledge can therefore yield increasing returns at the aggregate economic level, in contrast to the assumption of diminishing returns to physical capital in the Solow model. In the Solow model, once technical progress is left out, growth of output per worker falls toward zero as capital runs into diminishing returns. With technical progress put back in as an outside datum, output per worker grows at that given rate, but the model does not explain the rate. Romer's claim is that investment in ideas can sustain long-run growth from inside the model.

## Robert Lucas and human capital

Robert Lucas (known also for the "Lucas critique" of macroeconometric policy evaluation) added a parallel dimension by focusing on human capital (education and accumulated skills) as another source of internal increasing returns. He proposed that the accumulation of individual and collective knowledge, including external "knowledge spillover" effects among neighbouring workers and firms, explains a large part of the differences in growth between countries that physical capital accumulation alone does not explain.

## Related strands

Other models in the same family include the "AK" model (Sergio Rebelo, 1991), in which broadly defined capital does not face diminishing returns, and the Schumpeterian growth model of Philippe Aghion and Peter Howitt (1992), in which growth comes from quality-improving innovations that destroy the rents of earlier ones. Paul Romer shared the 2018 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel with William Nordhaus. Romer's share recognised the integration of technological innovation into long-run macroeconomic analysis; Nordhaus's share recognised the integration of climate change into that analysis.

## Policy impact

This current provided the theoretical basis for policies supporting research and development and education as tools for stimulating long-term growth, rather than as social consumption. It influenced development policy debates on the importance of the "technology gap" and of building local innovation capacity in developing countries, rather than relying only on the accumulation of traditional physical capital. The theory has faced empirical criticism over the difficulty of measuring "knowledge" and "knowledge spillovers" precisely in quantitative terms, and over the occasional overextension of abstract mathematical models into direct policy recommendations without sufficient empirical evidence.

## Application to the development gap and the Arab states

The theory influenced economic development debates about why the gap between advanced and developing economies persists despite decades of aid programmes and physical investment. If knowledge and local innovation (rather than physical capital accumulation alone) are the main drivers of sustained growth, then weak investment in quality education, research and local development in many developing Arab economies, despite sometimes large investments in physical infrastructure or in schooling poorly matched to the labour market, is one possible partial explanation for the slow transformation of economic growth into self-sustaining technological development in the region. This link remains a general analysis that needs country-by-country elaboration.
