# Aging and Gerontology

The sociology of ageing studies how societies define old age, how people move through the life course, and how age shapes resources, roles, and relations between generations. It forms part of the wider interdisciplinary field of gerontology, alongside biology, psychology, and medicine. Its sociological contribution is to show that ageing is more than a bodily process: retirement rules, pension systems, family arrangements, and cultural images help determine what it means to be old.

## Key Theories and Theorists

Early debate turned on how older people adjust to later life. Elaine Cumming and William Henry's *Growing Old: The Process of Disengagement* (1961) argued that a gradual mutual withdrawal between ageing individuals and society is normal and functional. Activity theory, associated with Robert Havighurst, held instead that well-being depends on remaining engaged. Disengagement theory was widely criticised for treating withdrawal as universal and natural.

Attention then shifted to social structure. Matilda White Riley and her colleagues developed age stratification theory, which treats age, like class, as a basis for allocating roles and rewards, and distinguishes the effects of ageing from differences between birth cohorts. The concern with cohorts echoes Karl Mannheim's essay "The Problem of Generations" (1928). Glen Elder's *Children of the Great Depression* (1974) became a key work of life course research, showing how historical events affect people differently according to the age at which they meet them.

From the late 1970s the political economy of ageing argued that dependency in old age is socially produced. Carroll Estes's *The Aging Enterprise* (1979) examined how policies and service industries define the needs of older people, and Peter Townsend described the "structured dependency" created by compulsory retirement, low pensions, and institutional care (1981). Robert Butler introduced the term "ageism" in 1969 to name prejudice based on age. Peter Laslett's *A Fresh Map of Life* (1989) described a "third age" of active, healthy years after retirement.

## Empirical Examples

Peter Townsend's *The Family Life of Old People* (1957), based on research in Bethnal Green in East London, documented the ties of support between older people and their relatives, especially between mothers and daughters. Elder's study followed people who grew up in California during the Depression. Current research examines care work and who performs it, health inequalities accumulated over a lifetime, pension reform, loneliness, migration and ageing, and the use of digital technology in later life.

## Debates

A central debate concerns how to describe successful ageing: models that stress health and activity can encourage positive images but may blame those who are ill or poor. Another concerns generational fairness, as ageing populations prompt disputes over the distribution of public spending. Researchers also ask how gender, class, and ethnicity produce very different experiences of old age within the same society.
