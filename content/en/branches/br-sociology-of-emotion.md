# Sociology of Emotion

The sociology of emotion studies feelings as social phenomena. Emotions are often treated as private, biological, and universal — something that happens inside individuals. Sociologists of emotion argue instead that what people feel, how they express it, and what their feelings mean are shaped by culture, social structure, and interaction. Grief, anger, shame, pride, love, and joy are not simply natural reactions; they are learned, managed, displayed, and judged according to social rules. The sociology of emotion asks where these expectations come from, who enforces them, and what happens when people violate them.

The field connects the most intimate aspects of experience to the largest structures of society. Emotional life is patterned by class, gender, race, and occupation: some people are expected to be warm and caring, others to be tough and unflappable; some emotions are rewarded in the workplace while others are punished. Studying emotion therefore reveals how power works at the level of feeling itself.

## Scope

The field covers several core topics. One is emotional labour: the management of feeling as part of paid work, above all in service occupations where workers must produce smiles, patience, and care on demand. Another is feeling rules: the shared norms that define which emotions are appropriate in a given situation and how they should be expressed. A third is emotional socialisation: how children learn to name, value, and control their feelings in families, schools, and peer groups. A fourth is the sociology of specific emotions — shame, guilt, envy, embarrassment, love, grief — and how their meanings and occasions vary across cultures and historical periods. A fifth is collective emotion: the shared feelings generated in rituals, crowds, social movements, and national ceremonies, and their role in producing solidarity.


## History

Classical sociology touched on emotion without making it a distinct field. Emile Durkheim analysed collective effervescence — the heightened emotion generated in religious rituals — as the source of social solidarity. Max Weber studied the rationalisation of emotional life and the tension between charismatic feeling and bureaucratic routine. Georg Simmel analysed the emotional consequences of metropolitan life and the blasé attitude it produces.

The sociology of emotion emerged as a recognised specialism in the late 1970s and 1980s. Theodore Kemper proposed a structural theory linking emotions to relations of power and status: people feel pride or satisfaction when they receive status, anger when status they expect is withheld or taken away, and fear when others' power over them seems threatening. Arlie Russell Hochschild's *The Managed Heart* transformed the field, introducing the concepts of emotional labour, feeling rules, and emotion work, and showing how the commercialisation of feeling affects workers' sense of self. Steven Gordon mapped the field's early development and distinguished the sociological study of emotion from psychological approaches. The field has since expanded into the study of love and intimacy, the emotions of migration and displacement, digital emotional life, and the affective politics of populism and protest.

## Key Figures and Ideas

Arlie Russell Hochschild is the field's central figure. Her study of flight attendants showed that service workers perform emotional labour: they manage their own feelings to produce a desired emotional state in customers, following feeling rules set by their employers. She distinguished surface acting — displaying an emotion one does not feel — from deep acting — working on oneself to actually feel it — and argued that sustained emotional labour can alienate workers from their own feelings. Her later work on the "commercialisation of intimate life" extended this analysis to families, care work, and the market for emotional services.

Theodore Kemper argued that emotions have a social-structural basis: power and status relations produce predictable emotional outcomes, and emotions in turn motivate social action. Randall Collins placed emotion at the centre of social theory: interaction rituals succeed when participants share a mood and focus, generating emotional energy that individuals carry into subsequent encounters. Thomas Scheff analysed shame and pride as the master emotions of social bonds, arguing that unacknowledged shame underlies much conflict and violence.

## Method

The sociology of emotion relies heavily on qualitative methods: in-depth interviews in which people narrate their emotional lives, ethnographies of workplaces, families, and rituals, and the analysis of diaries, letters, and online expression. Surveys measure emotional experience across populations and test theories about the distribution of feeling. Historical research traces how emotional norms and vocabularies change over time, while experimental and physiological studies are sometimes combined with sociological interpretation. The field's methodological challenge is to study something intimate and often fleeting without reducing it either to biology or to discourse alone.

## Influence and Debates

The sociology of emotion has reshaped the study of work, gender, and the family. It showed that service work is emotional work, that gender inequality is partly an inequality of feeling rules, and that intimacy is increasingly organised through markets. Its concepts — emotional labour, feeling rules, emotion work — have entered everyday language and influenced management studies, nursing, psychology, and cultural studies.

Debates continue over the balance between the biological and the social in emotion, over whether emotional labour is inherently alienating or can be a source of skill and satisfaction, and over how far feeling rules are imposed from above versus negotiated in interaction. The rise of social media, affective computing, and the politics of resentment has given these questions new urgency: emotional life is now visibly shaped by platforms, algorithms, and political movements in ways the field's founders could only anticipate.
