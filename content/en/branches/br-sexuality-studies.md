# Sexuality Studies

Sexuality studies is the interdisciplinary field, grounded in sociology, that treats sexuality as social: as a set of identities, practices, meanings, and institutions that are made and remade in history rather than simply given by biology. It asks how societies define normal and deviant sex, how sexual identities such as heterosexual, homosexual, and bisexual emerged, how desire is organised by gender, class, race, and age, and how law, medicine, religion, and the market regulate intimate life. Its central claim is that sexuality is not a private, natural drive that society merely represses or permits, but a social field in which power operates and identities are formed.

## Overview

The field is organised by a tension between essentialist and constructionist accounts. Essentialism treats sexual orientation and desire as fixed, innate properties of individuals. Constructionism argues that the categories themselves — the very idea of a "homosexual person" as a type of human being, for example — have histories, and that what counts as a sexual act, identity, or problem varies across cultures and periods. Most sociological work in the field is constructionist in orientation, while acknowledging that bodies, pleasures, and desires are real and consequential.

Sexuality studies ranges across several topics: the social organisation of desire and practice; the formation and politics of sexual identities and communities; the regulation of sexuality by states, churches, medicine, and markets; and the intersection of sexuality with gender, race, class, disability, and age. It studies marriage and family, sex work, pornography, sexual violence, reproductive politics, HIV and AIDS, and the commercialisation of intimacy. Its questions are at once intimate and structural: how does a society's sexual order sustain its wider inequalities, and how do people resist, rework, or escape that order?

## History

The field's empirical foundation was laid by Alfred Kinsey's surveys of American sexual behaviour, Sexual Behavior in the Human Male (1948) and Sexual Behavior in the Human Female (1953), which showed that same-sex experience, masturbation, and premarital sex were far more common than public morality admitted. Kinsey's scale of sexual orientation, from exclusively heterosexual to exclusively homosexual, challenged binary categories. John Gagnon and William Simon's Sexual Conduct (1973) then supplied the field's key sociological idea: sexual scripts. People learn how to be sexual — what to want, how to act, what it means — from cultural scripts, interpersonal negotiation, and inner fantasy.

The decisive theoretical break came with Michel Foucault's The History of Sexuality, Volume 1 (1976), which argued that modern societies do not repress sex so much as produce endless discourse about it, through medicine, psychiatry, law, and confession. Sexuality, for Foucault, is a historical apparatus of power. Gayle Rubin's "Thinking Sex" (1984) extended this analysis, mapping a hierarchy of sexual value in which some practices are charmed and others stigmatised, and insisting that sexuality deserves analysis in its own right rather than as a footnote to gender. The AIDS crisis of the 1980s and 1990s politicised the field, as activism and scholarship documented stigma, state neglect, and community care. Queer theory in the 1990s, above all Judith Butler's Gender Trouble (1990), challenged stable identity categories themselves, treating gender and sexuality as performed rather than possessed.

## Key Figures and Works

Kinsey established the empirical study of sexual behaviour and destabilised moral certainties about what people actually do. Gagnon and Simon made sexuality sociological by showing that desire is learned and scripted. Foucault provided the field's most influential theory of sexuality and power. Rubin mapped the politics of sexual value and argued for the autonomy of sexuality as an object of study.

Jeffrey Weeks, in Coming Out (1977), wrote an early and influential historical sociology of homosexual politics in Britain, showing how identities and movements were made in specific political conditions. Butler's theory of performativity argued that gender is not an inner essence expressed in behaviour but an effect of repeated acts — a claim with far-reaching implications for how sexuality is understood. Steven Seidman's The Social Construction of Sexuality (2003) synthesised the constructionist tradition for students, arguing that sexuality is patterned by social institutions and that sexual hierarchies are also hierarchies of citizenship and respect.

## Methods

Sexuality studies uses surveys to map behaviour and attitudes, though researchers are acutely aware that stigma shapes what people report. Life-history interviews and ethnography capture how people narrate desire, negotiate relationships, and build communities. Historical research traces how categories and regulations emerged. Discourse analysis examines how medicine, law, and media produce sexual knowledge. Increasingly, researchers study digital sexuality: dating apps, online pornography, and networked intimacy.

## Influence and Debates

The field has transformed public understanding of sexuality, informing debates on same-sex marriage, sex education, sexual violence, and reproductive rights. Its constructionist insight — that sexual categories are made, not found — underpins contemporary discussions of identity and recognition.

Current debates include the relationship between sexuality studies and queer theory, with some scholars arguing that queer theory's suspicion of identity categories weakens the politics of recognition; the globalisation of sexual identities, as Western categories travel and are reworked elsewhere; the commercialisation of intimacy through dating platforms and the sex industry; and the politics of consent, pleasure, and safety in the aftermath of the #MeToo movement. The field continues to ask its founding question: how is sexuality organised, by whom, and to whose benefit?

## Further Frontiers

Research on sexuality across the life course has shown that sexual activity and interest often continue into later life: a national US study of adults aged 57 to 85 by Stacy Lindau and colleagues (2007) found that sexual activity declined with age and poor health but remained common among those with partners. Work on digital intimacy examines how dating platforms reshape partner search; Michael Rosenfeld and Reuben Thomas (2012) documented the rise of the internet as a social intermediary in meeting partners in the United States. Scholarship on the Global South stresses that Western categories do not map neatly onto other settings, as in Serena Nanda's study of the hijra of India (1990), and that colonial-era laws, such as Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code, shaped later sexual regulation.
