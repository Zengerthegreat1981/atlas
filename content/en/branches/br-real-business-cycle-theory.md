# Real Business Cycle Theory: Technology Shocks as the Source of Fluctuation

Real business cycle theory is a current within the New Classical School. Its best-known statement is **Finn Kydland** and **Edward Prescott**, "Time to Build and Aggregate Fluctuations" (1982). It advanced a radical hypothesis contrary to all Keynesian traditions: recessions and recoveries are not deviations from an ideal equilibrium caused by market failure or sticky prices, as Keynesian theories of all kinds assume. They are instead a rational and fully efficient response by rational economic agents to real external shocks, the most important of which are **technology shocks** that affect the production function itself.

## The mechanism of the technology shock

According to this model, when a positive technological breakthrough occurs (an invention that raises the productivity of labour and capital), rational individuals respond by increasing work and investment now, to take maximum advantage of the temporarily higher productivity. They substitute work across time, working more now when work is more profitable and resting more later, and a real economic recovery results. When the positive shock recedes, or a negative shock occurs (a temporary fall in productivity, a rise in energy prices), individuals rationally withdraw temporarily from the labour market and from investment, waiting for better conditions. In aggregate measurements this appears as a "recession", although it is a rational and efficient response rather than a market failure that calls for corrective government intervention.

## Rejecting the need for stabilization policy

The most important policy conclusion of this current is that expansionary monetary and fiscal policy (as Keynesianism advocates) is useless and even harmful. If fluctuations are efficient rational responses to real shocks on the supply side, any government intervention to stimulate aggregate demand will do nothing except distort individuals' rational decisions or move nominal prices without any lasting real effect, and may even make volatility worse. The current adopted a sophisticated quantitative method, "calibrated simulation" models instead of conventional statistical estimation. This method later influenced the design of DSGE models, including among New Keynesian economists who rejected its policy conclusions but adopted its mathematical tools.

## The central critique

This theory faced broad criticism from several directions. Keynesian economists (New Keynesian and Post-Keynesian alike) doubted that a deep recession such as the Great Depression or the crisis of 2008 could be explained merely by repeated negative technological shocks. Critics asked whether productivity fell by such a magnitude in 1929 or 2008, and regarded the direct empirical evidence for this as weak. Critics also observed that high unemployment during crises appears involuntary, in the sense that workers search for jobs and do not find them, rather than a voluntary withdrawal from the labour market as the model assumes. A further objection is that the labour-supply elasticities needed to match employment fluctuations are larger than those estimated in most microeconomic studies of household labour supply. Despite this debate, Kydland and Prescott received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2004 in recognition of the impact of their methodology on quantitative macroeconomics, a recognition that extended to their wider work on time consistency of policy (Kydland and Prescott, 1977) and was accepted even by colleagues who disagreed with their policy conclusions.

## Calibration and quantitative methodology

Kydland and Prescott introduced a new quantitative method known as **calibration**. Instead of estimating the model's parameters statistically from historical data, as in conventional econometrics, the parameters (such as the elasticity of substitution between work and leisure) are chosen from independent micro-economic studies or from observed ratios in long-run data. The model's aggregate results are then simulated mathematically and their performance is compared with actual data, without a strict formal statistical test of the hypothesis of fit. This methodology itself generated wide methodological debate about how far its results can be empirically refuted compared with conventional standard approaches, since a "calibrated" model can appear consistent with the data without having been subjected to a strict statistical test capable of rejecting it.
