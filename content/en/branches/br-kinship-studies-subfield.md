# Kinship Studies as a Subfield

One of the oldest and most methodologically rigorous subfields of social anthropology, which studies how human groups organize relations of descent, marriage and family belonging through specific terminological systems and behavioral rules. These vary considerably across cultures, although all rest on the same shared biological fact of reproduction and birth.

## Lewis Henry Morgan and Classificatory Terms

The early American anthropologist Lewis Henry Morgan is widely regarded as a founder of this subfield. In his book *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity* (1871), he presented the first broad comparative survey of kinship terminology across many cultures. He distinguished between "descriptive" systems, which give each degree of kinship its own precise name (such as the distinction between "brother" and "cousin" in English), and "classificatory" systems, which group several different biological degrees of kinship under a single term (such as calling a cousin "brother," as in many systems). This opened the way to studying kinship as a logical system in its own right, rather than as a literal reflection of biological ties.

## Descent Theory versus Alliance Theory

The field later divided into two main schools. "Descent Theory," the British school, focused especially through Meyer Fortes on how kin groups are organized through lines of descent (patrilineal or matrilineal) that determine membership, inheritance and collective loyalty. "Alliance Theory," the French school developed by Claude Lévi-Strauss, focused instead on marriage itself as the central mechanism for building alliances between kin groups, through rules of "exchange of women" between groups (such as the cross-cousin marriage preferences analysed in *The Elementary Structures of Kinship*). For Lévi-Strauss, organized exogamous marriage was a form of great social "gift" that binds the whole of society into a network of permanent reciprocal exchange.

## David Schneider's Critique: Is "Kinship" a Western Concept?

David Schneider launched a radical critique of the foundations of the entire field in *A Critique of the Study of Kinship* (1984). He argued that the concept of "kinship" itself, as an independent analytical category based essentially on a presumed "biological" or "blood" tie, is a specifically modern Western cultural notion, not an objective universal category that can be applied neutrally to all cultures. Many societies do not in fact distinguish between "biological kinship" and "social kinship" (such as adoption or fosterage) in the rigid way the Western analytical framework assumes. This critique caused a deep methodological crisis in the field and pushed it toward studying kinship as culturally "imagined," rather than assuming a predetermined biological essence on which the analysis is built.

## New Kinship: Reproductive Technologies and Gay Families

The field has recently expanded, through researchers such as Marilyn Strathern, Janet Carsten and Kath Weston, to study how assisted reproductive technologies (such as artificial insemination and surrogacy) and same-sex families are reshaping the meaning of "fatherhood" and "motherhood" themselves beyond the traditional direct biological tie. This raises entirely new questions about the boundaries of kinship in an era in which the technical separation between biological pregnancy and social parenthood, legally and culturally recognized, is increasingly possible.
