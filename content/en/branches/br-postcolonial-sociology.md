# Postcolonial Sociology

Postcolonial sociology studies how colonialism and its legacies shape modern societies, sociological knowledge, and global power. It argues that empire was not a marginal episode in the history of modernity but one of its constitutive conditions: the wealth, institutions, and ideas of the modern world were made through conquest, slavery, and colonial rule, and their effects persist in contemporary inequalities of race, nation, and global standing. Where postcolonial anthropology examines colonial encounters through ethnography and cultural difference, this branch works within sociology, asking how colonialism structured the discipline's core categories — modernity, capitalism, the state, civil society — and how sociology itself must change once empire is placed at the centre of its story.

## Scope and Core Concepts

The branch's core concepts begin with coloniality, the persistence of colonial relations of power after formal decolonisation, and Eurocentrism, the assumption that European experience is the universal template of modernity. Orientalism names the system of knowledge through which the West constructed the East as its inferior other. The subaltern designates populations excluded from elite representation and from the historical record, while hybridity and the third space describe the mixed, negotiated identities produced in colonial contact. Epistemic injustice, a term coined by Miranda Fricker (2007), is widely extended in this literature to the systematic devaluing of colonised peoples' knowledge. The branch studies colonialism's institutional legacies — racial classification, land regimes, labour systems, and borders — alongside its cultural legacies in education, religion, and identity, and it examines contemporary phenomena such as migration, development, humanitarianism, and global governance as continuations and transformations of colonial relations.

## Historical Development

The branch's intellectual origins lie in anti-colonial thought. Aimé Césaire's *Discourse on Colonialism* (1950) indicted European humanism for its complicity with empire, and Frantz Fanon's *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) theorised decolonisation as a violent remaking of the colonial world and of the colonised self. Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978) transformed the field by showing how scholarship, literature, and administration together produced the Orient as an object of Western power. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) pressed the question of who can represent the most marginalised, and Homi Bhabha's *The Location of Culture* (1994) theorised hybridity and mimicry as forms of colonial ambivalence. Within sociology, the postcolonial turn arrived later: Raewyn Connell's *Southern Theory* (2007) and Gurminder Bhambra's *Rethinking Modernity* (2007) argued that sociology's classical canon had been built by ignoring empire, and Julian Go's *Postcolonial Thought and Social Theory* (2016) gave the field its first systematic statement, distinguishing postcolonial sociology from postcolonial studies in the humanities.

## Key Figures

Frantz Fanon (1925–1961), psychiatrist and revolutionary, remains the branch's moral and theoretical touchstone, linking colonial violence to the psychology of the colonised. Edward Said (1935–2003) supplied its central analysis of knowledge and power. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak brought feminist and deconstructive questions to the problem of representation, while Homi Bhabha theorised the ambivalence and creativity of colonial encounters. Among sociologists, Raewyn Connell exposed the Northern bias of sociological theory and recovered Southern intellectual traditions, and Gurminder Bhambra reconstructed modernity as a connected history made through empire and slavery. Julian Go has systematised postcolonial sociology as a distinct field, and Aníbal Quijano, the Peruvian sociologist, developed the concept of the coloniality of power, linking race, labour, and knowledge in the Americas. Together these figures span the Caribbean, South Asia, Latin America, and the settler societies, giving the branch a genuinely global range.

## Method

Postcolonial sociology's method is primarily historical and comparative, but it is distinguished by its attention to the politics of knowledge. Its researchers practise connected histories, tracing how developments in metropole and colony made each other, rather than treating colonial societies as separate cases. They read archives against the grain, recovering subaltern voices from documents written by colonial administrators, and they practise epistemic reflexivity, asking whose categories organise the research itself. Comparative work contrasts colonial formations across empires and regions, while contemporary research uses interviews and ethnography to study migration, racialisation, and diaspora. The method's central demand is that sociology decentre its own standpoint: the discipline's concepts must be tested against experiences from the global South rather than assumed to travel unchanged from the North.

## Influence and Debates

The branch has reshaped sociology's self-understanding. Its critique of the classical canon forced a reckoning with how Marx, Weber, and Durkheim treated empire, and it opened space for Southern theory, decolonial sociology, and the study of global inequalities. It influenced research on race and ethnicity, migration, development, and international relations, and it supplied the intellectual resources for movements to decolonise university curricula. Debates continue. One concerns the relationship between postcolonial and decolonial approaches: Latin American decolonial thinkers emphasise the coloniality of power and a break with Western epistemology, while postcolonial sociologists often work within and against the discipline's traditions. Another concerns the risk of reducing everything to colonialism, flattening the differences between colonial and postcolonial periods and between distinct colonial formations. A third concerns practice: whether decolonising sociology means adding Southern voices to the existing canon or rebuilding the discipline's foundations altogether.
