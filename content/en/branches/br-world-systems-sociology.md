# World-Systems Sociology

World-systems sociology studies the global capitalist system as a single historical unit, analysing how wealth, power, and inequality are produced by the division of labour between core, semi-peripheral, and peripheral zones. Where the concept of the world-system names Immanuel Wallerstein's theory itself, this branch names the sociological research programme that developed around it: a field that treats national societies not as self-contained units but as positions within one expanding world economy, and that explains development, underdevelopment, revolution, and state formation by reference to that system's history. Its central claim is that the modern world has been a single capitalist world-economy since the long sixteenth century, and that its inequalities are not accidents or stages on a common path but structural features of the system.

## Scope and Core Concepts

The branch's core concepts begin with the world-system itself: a historically bounded social system with a single division of labour, multiple political units, and multiple cultures. Within the modern capitalist world-economy, the core comprises wealthy, technologically advanced states that dominate high-profit production; the periphery supplies raw materials and cheap labour; and the semi-periphery occupies an intermediate position, exploiting the periphery while being exploited by the core. Hegemony describes the periodic dominance of a single core power — the Dutch in the seventeenth century, the British in the nineteenth, the United States in the twentieth — whose leadership rests on productive, commercial, and financial superiority. Kondratieff waves are the long cycles of expansion and contraction that drive the system's rhythm, while incorporation describes how new regions are drawn into the world-economy. The branch also studies antisystemic movements — labour, national liberation, feminist, and ecological movements — that challenge the system's logic, and the possibility of systemic crisis and transformation.

## Historical Development

The programme's roots lie in dependency theory. André Gunder Frank's *Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America* (1967) argued that underdevelopment was not a pre-capitalist residue but a product of incorporation into world capitalism, and Samir Amin's *Accumulation on a World Scale* (French 1970; English 1974) theorised unequal exchange between centre and periphery. Immanuel Wallerstein systematised these insights in *The Modern World-System I* (1974), which traced the emergence of the European world-economy in the long sixteenth century, followed by volume two in 1980, volume three in 1989, and volume four in 2011, carrying the story into the long nineteenth century. Fernand Braudel's *Civilization and Capitalism* (1979) supplied the long-duration historical depth, while Christopher Chase-Dunn's *Global Formation* (1989) formalised the comparative study of world-systems across history. Giovanni Arrighi's *The Long Twentieth Century* (1994) extended the analysis through successive cycles of hegemony, and the collapse of the Soviet Union and the rise of China have since fuelled debates about whether the system is entering a terminal crisis or a new hegemonic transition.

## Key Figures

Immanuel Wallerstein (1930–2019) is the founding figure, whose multi-volume history and editorship of the journal *Review* made world-systems analysis a recognised field. André Gunder Frank supplied the dependency tradition's sharpest formulations and later extended the argument to a five-thousand-year world system. Samir Amin, the Egyptian economist, theorised unequal exchange and delinking as a strategy for peripheral development. Fernand Braudel, the historian of the Annales school, provided the method of the longue durée that underpins the programme's historical sensibility. Giovanni Arrighi developed the theory of systemic cycles of accumulation and hegemony, while Christopher Chase-Dunn advanced comparative world-systems research, including the study of pre-capitalist systems. Janet Abu-Lughod's *Before European Hegemony* (1989) challenged Eurocentric chronologies by documenting a thirteenth-century world system centred on the Middle East and Asia, and women scholars such as Joan Smith brought gender and households into the analysis of the world-economy.

## Method

World-systems sociology is methodologically distinctive for its unit of analysis: the system, not the society. Its primary method is comparative historical analysis, tracing long-run processes — trade flows, commodity chains, state formation, class struggle — across centuries and continents. Researchers use quantitative data on trade, investment, and inequality alongside archival and historical sources, and they study commodity chains to show how value is produced and appropriated across zones. The method rejects methodological nationalism, the assumption that the nation-state is the natural container of social life, and instead follows connections across borders. Critics note the difficulty of testing such large-scale claims and the risk of functionalist reasoning, in which everything in the system is explained by its contribution to the system. Defenders reply that only a systemic, long-run perspective can explain why development in one region has so often depended on underdevelopment elsewhere.

## Influence and Debates

The branch's influence reaches across sociology, history, geography, and international political economy. It reshaped development studies by displacing modernisation theory's claim that all societies follow the same path, and it supplied the framework for globalisation studies, global commodity chain research, and the sociology of global inequality. Its concept of the semi-periphery helped explain the rise of newly industrialising countries, and its analysis of hegemony informs debates about American decline and Chinese ascent. Debates persist. One concerns the system's origins: Wallerstein dates the capitalist world-economy to the sixteenth century, while Frank and others argue for a much longer world system. Another concerns the role of culture and the state: critics charge the programme with economic determinism, and cultural and political sociologists have pressed it to take ideology, religion, and state autonomy more seriously. A third concerns the future: whether the present conjuncture is a routine hegemonic transition or, as Wallerstein argued in his later work, a structural crisis from which the system may not recover.
