# Migration and Diaspora

Migration and diaspora is the sociological study of human movement across borders and of the communities that movement creates. It asks why people move, how they settle, how they maintain ties to the places they left, and how migration transforms the societies that send and receive them. The field distinguishes migration — the movement itself, whether for work, refuge, family, or study — from diaspora, the dispersed communities that sustain a shared identity, memory, and connection across borders over generations. Where the concept of diaspora, treated elsewhere in the Atlas, names a form of dispersed belonging and identity, this branch studies migration as a social process: the networks, institutions, and inequalities that organize movement, settlement, and transnational life. Its central insight is that migration is not an exception to social order but one of its basic organizing forces.

## Scope

The field covers the causes of migration: labour demand, wage differences, war, persecution, environmental change, and family reunification. It studies the journey itself — smuggling, borders, detention, and the politics of asylum — and the settlement process: housing, work, language, schooling, and the formation of ethnic communities. It examines incorporation and its alternatives, from assimilation to segmented assimilation, in which the children of immigrants follow divergent paths into different segments of the host society. It studies transnationalism: the remittances, visits, phone calls, and political engagements that keep migrants connected to home. It covers diaspora politics, return migration, and the second generation.

## History

The sociology of migration began with the Chicago School's studies of immigration to American cities in the early twentieth century, and with the assimilation theories of Robert Park. After the Second World War, migration research expanded as guest-worker programmes brought labour migrants to Europe and as decolonization produced new flows. The 1965 reform of United States immigration law, which ended national-origin quotas, transformed the composition of migration and stimulated a new wave of research. In the 1980s and 1990s, the field was reshaped by network theory and by the new economics of migration, which treated movement as a household strategy rather than an individual calculation. Alejandro Portes and Rubén Rumbaut's *Immigrant America* (1990) synthesized the field's knowledge of settlement and adaptation. Douglas Massey and colleagues' *Worlds in Motion* (1998) compared the major theories of international migration and argued for a synthetic account. William Safran's 1991 essay on diasporas in modern societies and Robin Cohen's *Global Diasporas* (1997) established diaspora as a central analytic concept, while the transnational turn of the 1990s — led by scholars such as Nina Glick Schiller — insisted that migrants live their lives across borders rather than in a single society.

## Key Figures

Alejandro Portes is the field's leading theorist of immigrant incorporation, ethnic enclaves, and segmented assimilation. Rubén Rumbaut, his collaborator, pioneered the study of the second generation and of refugee adaptation. Douglas Massey developed the theory of cumulative causation and the comparative study of migration systems, and documented the effects of United States border policy. William Safran defined the modern concept of diaspora and its criteria. Robin Cohen systematized the study of global diasporas and their types. Saskia Sassen linked migration to globalization and the global city, showing how global economic restructuring produces new migration flows. Nina Glick Schiller founded the transnational perspective, challenging methodological nationalism — the assumption that societies and nation-states coincide. Peggy Levitt studied transnational religion and the social remittances — ideas, practices, and identities — that migrants carry home.

## Key Works

Portes and Rumbaut's *Immigrant America: A Portrait* (1990) is the field's standard synthesis of immigration to the United States. Safran's "Diasporas in Modern Societies: Myths of Homeland and Return" (1991) set out the criteria that define a diaspora. Portes and Zhou's "The New Second Generation: Segmented Assimilation and Its Variants" (1993) introduced the theory of segmented assimilation. Sassen's *The Global City: New York, London, Tokyo* (1991) linked migration to the globalization of economic command. Cohen's *Global Diasporas: An Introduction* (1997) offered an influential typology of diasporas. Massey, Arango, Hugo, Kouaouci, Pellegrino, and Taylor's *Worlds in Motion: Understanding International Migration at the End of the Millennium* (1998) compared and synthesized the major theories of migration. James Clifford's "Diasporas" (1994) argued that diaspora should be understood as a stance and a practice rather than a fixed identity, opening the concept to new uses.

## Method

Migration sociology combines quantitative and qualitative methods. Large surveys and census data track flows, settlement patterns, and the socioeconomic mobility of immigrants and their children. Ethnography follows migrants through border crossings, workplaces, and neighbourhoods, and across borders into transnational lives. Network analysis maps the social ties that channel migration to particular destinations. Comparative research contrasts migration regimes — the different ways states govern entry, residence, and citizenship — across countries. Life-history interviews recover the meanings of migration for those who move.

## Influence and Debates

The sociology of migration reshaped the study of cities, labour markets, and the welfare state, and it supplied the evidence base for debates about immigration policy, integration, and citizenship. Its theory of segmented assimilation challenged the older assumption that incorporation proceeds in a single, upward direction. Critics also question the field's focus on the United States and Europe at the expense of South–South migration, which accounts for a large share of world movement. Current debates concern climate migration and the legal status of environmental displacement, the politics of borders and detention, the integration of refugees, the role of digital platforms in migration, and the rise of nativist politics in receiving societies.
