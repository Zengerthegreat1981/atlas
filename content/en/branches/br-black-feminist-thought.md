# Black Feminist Thought

Black feminist thought is a body of theory and activism, developed chiefly by Black women, that analyses how racism, sexism, class exploitation, and heterosexism operate together. It argues that accounts which treat race and gender separately tend to centre Black men in discussions of racism and white women in discussions of sexism, leaving Black women's experience obscured. It also treats that experience as a source of knowledge about the social order as a whole.

## Roots

The tradition draws on nineteenth-century writers and activists, among them Sojourner Truth and Anna Julia Cooper, whose *A Voice from the South* (1892) addressed the position of Black women in American life. In the movements of the late 1960s and 1970s, Frances Beal described the "double jeopardy" of being Black and female, and the Combahee River Collective, a group of Black lesbian feminists in Boston, issued a statement in 1977 arguing that the major systems of oppression are interlocking.

## Key Theorists and Texts

Angela Davis's *Women, Race and Class* (1981) traced the tensions between the women's suffrage and reproductive rights movements and struggles against slavery and racism. bell hooks's *Ain't I a Woman* (1981) examined the combined effects of sexism and racism on Black women. Audre Lorde's *Sister Outsider* (1984) argued that differences among women must be acknowledged and used rather than denied. Deborah King's article "Multiple Jeopardy, Multiple Consciousness" (1988) rejected additive models in which oppressions are simply summed.

Kimberlé Crenshaw introduced the term "intersectionality" in 1989 and developed it in "Mapping the Margins" (1991). Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* (1990) gave the tradition an influential sociological statement. Collins described a "matrix of domination" organising intersecting oppressions, analysed the "controlling images" used to justify the subordination of Black women, and argued for an epistemology that values lived experience, dialogue, and personal accountability. An earlier essay (1986) described the Black woman scholar as an "outsider within," whose marginal position can reveal what insiders take for granted.

## Empirical Examples

Crenshaw's discussion of *DeGraffenreid v. General Motors* showed how a court rejected Black women's claim of combined discrimination, treating race and sex as separate categories that could not be joined. Crenshaw's later work on violence against women of colour examined how shelters, policing, and political campaigns could overlook needs shaped by race, gender, class, and immigration status together. Researchers have since applied similar analysis to employment, health, housing, schooling, and criminal justice.

## Debates

Debates concern whether Black women's standpoint is a distinct basis of knowledge and who may contribute to it; whether intersectionality loses its critical edge when detached from the political struggles that produced it; and how the framework travels beyond the United States, where Black British, African, Caribbean, and Latin American feminists have developed related but distinct arguments.
