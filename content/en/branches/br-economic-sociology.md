# Economic Sociology

Economic sociology studies production, exchange, consumption, work, and finance as social activities. It asks how markets, firms, money, and economic behaviour depend on relationships, institutions, power, and cultural meanings. Where standard economic models often begin from individuals making choices under constraints, economic sociologists examine how preferences, trust, rules, and market arrangements are themselves formed.

## Classical Foundations

Emile Durkheim's *The Division of Labor in Society* (1893) argued that contracts rest on non-contractual foundations. Max Weber analysed economic action, markets, and the rise of modern capitalism, notably in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* and *Economy and Society*. Karl Marx's analysis of capital and class and Georg Simmel's *The Philosophy of Money* (1900) are further reference points. Karl Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* (1944) argued that a self-regulating market treats land, labour, and money as commodities and provokes movements to protect society. Talcott Parsons and Neil Smelser's *Economy and Society* (1956) treated the economy as a subsystem of the social system.

## The New Economic Sociology

A revival began in the 1980s. Harrison White's "Where Do Markets Come From?" (1981) described markets as structures in which producers watch one another. Mark Granovetter's 1985 article on embeddedness argued that economic action is shaped by ongoing networks of relations, criticising both under-socialised and over-socialised accounts of behaviour. Neil Fligstein's *The Architecture of Markets* (2001) analysed markets as fields in which firms seek stability and depend on states. Viviana Zelizer showed how moral and cultural meanings shape life insurance and money. Michel Callon and Donald MacKenzie examined how economic theories and calculative devices help construct the markets they describe.

## Empirical Examples

Granovetter's *Getting a Job* (1974) showed the importance of personal contacts in finding professional, technical, and managerial work. Zelizer's *Morals and Markets* (1979) traced how Americans came to accept putting a monetary value on life through insurance. Wayne Baker (1984) found that the network structure of a securities options market affected price volatility. Brian Uzzi's research on New York apparel firms (1997) showed that close ties can support trust and problem solving, but that too much embeddedness can also limit firms. MacKenzie's *An Engine, Not a Camera* (2006) studied how financial models affected derivatives markets.

## Debates

Debates concern the meaning of embeddedness, the relative weight of networks, institutions, culture, and power, and the field's relation to economics and political economy. Current research addresses financialisation, platform work, global supply chains, valuation, inequality, and the moral limits of markets.
