# The Monopoly Capital School: Economic Surplus in the Age of Monopoly

An American Marxist current founded by Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy with their joint book *Monopoly Capital* (1966; Baran had died in 1964 and the book was completed by Sweezy), institutionally associated with the journal *Monthly Review*, which Sweezy and Leo Huberman founded in 1949. This current reformulated classical Marxist analysis of capitalism in response to a structural shift that had become evident by the mid-twentieth century: American capitalism was no longer the competitive industry of Marx's nineteenth-century account, in which many firms were presumed to take prices. It had come to be dominated by a small number of giant corporations with monopoly or oligopoly power in major industrial sectors.

## From surplus value to economic surplus

Baran and Sweezy gave central place to a broader category they called **economic surplus**: the difference between what a society produces and the cost of producing it. They treated this as the relevant aggregate for monopoly capitalism, wider than surplus value understood as the gap between the value a worker produces and the wage. Their analysis drew on earlier work on monopoly and stagnation by Michał Kalecki and Josef Steindl. This includes not only the conventional capitalist profit but also the vast "unproductive" expenditure in which large monopolistic corporations specifically expand because of their market power (intensive advertising and marketing, formal product differentiation without substantive difference, and massive government military spending). These are mechanisms for absorbing a growing surplus that normal private consumption and investment cannot fully absorb under a monopolistic structure that limits the usual price competition.

## A tendency toward chronic stagnation

Baran and Sweezy concluded that the structural tendency of monopoly capitalism is toward **chronic stagnation** rather than the sharp cyclical crisis of classical Marxist analysis. This is because the large monopolistic corporations prefer to restrict output in order to maintain high prices and profits rather than expand competitively. A vast economic surplus therefore accumulates that ordinary consumption cannot absorb, and it has no sufficient "productive" outlet apart from the unproductive channels mentioned above. Military spending in particular they regarded as a vital structural prop for American capitalism during the Cold War, not merely an incidental political decision.

## Harry Braverman: labour and monopoly capital

Harry Braverman developed an influential extension of this analysis in his book *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974), moving from the macro-level analysis of economic surplus to an analysis of how the labour process itself was transformed inside the factory and office under monopoly capitalism. He focused on the systematic **deskilling** of workers through the fragmentation of the production process and the separation of planning from execution (the scientific management associated with Frederick W. Taylor). This gives capitalist management control over the labour process and turns the relatively autonomous craft worker into an executor of fragmented, standardized operations controlled exclusively by management.

## Criticism and later development

The current faced criticism from other Marxists, who argued that the analysis of Baran and Sweezy neglected the fundamental contradictions in the production process itself (the direct extraction of surplus value from labour) in favour of an excessive focus on the problem of "absorbing" the surplus at the stage of circulation and consumption. Empirical economists questioned the accuracy of the claim that monopoly inevitably leads to chronic stagnation rather than sharp cyclical crises, especially in light of the rapid technological dynamism that some major monopolistic sectors demonstrated later (such as the digital technology industry). Nevertheless, the current has remained an influential reference for analysing contemporary capitalism based on a small number of giant corporations, and the journal *Monthly Review* has sustained this analytical line to the present through later analyses of what has been called "monopoly-finance capital."
