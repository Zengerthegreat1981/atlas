# The Agrarian Question in Political Economy: The Fate of the Peasantry under Advancing Capitalism

A current within Marxist political economy that started from a question pressing on the Socialist International at the end of the nineteenth century (the Second International): does agriculture follow the same laws of capitalist concentration and centralisation that Marx described for industry (the decline of small producers and their absorption into a wage-labouring class working for large capitalist farms), or does agriculture have a distinct economic logic that allows small peasant farming to survive and persist even within an advanced capitalist economy? The answer had decisive political consequences. If small peasants were doomed to inevitable disappearance, their alliance with the socialist labour movement was a temporary and secondary matter. If they were structurally able to survive, winning their lasting political support became a central strategic necessity for socialist parties.

## Kautsky: "The Agrarian Question" and the Thesis of Delayed Concentration

Karl Kautsky, in his landmark book *The Agrarian Question* (*Die Agrarfrage*, 1899), offered the most methodical analysis of this question. He concluded that agriculture is in fact subject to the same pressures of capitalist concentration in the general direction (the superiority of large farms in mechanisation and productivity), but at a much slower pace than industry and for reasons structural to agriculture itself. These include the biological and seasonal character of agricultural production, which resists the continuous, standardised organisation of the factory, and the willingness of the small peasant family to work longer and consume less than a wage labourer would accept (the self-exploitation of the household). This means that small peasants would continue for a relatively long period even amid partial agrarian concentration. The socialist movement therefore had to treat them as potential strategic allies rather than as a class that was disappearing and could be politically neglected.

## Lenin and Chayanov: Class Differentiation versus Family Continuity

Lenin, in *The Development of Capitalism in Russia* (1899), developed a parallel analysis, but one that placed more emphasis on internal class differentiation among the peasants themselves: a better-off rural stratum that employs wage labour, a middle peasantry, and poor peasants who sell their labour power. Later Russian debates often called the better-off stratum "kulaks". He regarded this internal class differentiation, rather than the complete disappearance of peasant farming, as the actual dynamic of capitalist agriculture in Russia. The Russian agrarian economist Alexander Chayanov (1888-1937) developed an alternative theory of the family peasant economy, which stood apart from both propositions. He argued that the peasant household makes production decisions by a logic fundamentally different from the logic of capitalist profit maximisation: a balance between the exhausting effort of family labour and the needs of satisfying the household, rather than a purely marginal profit calculation. This allows family farming to persist structurally even without an inevitable capitalist concentration.

## The Impact on Development and Agrarian Reform Debates

This current influenced agrarian reform debates in the twentieth century, including the Arab agrarian reform experiences (in Egypt, Syria and Iraq), which faced the same practical question: should agricultural development be built on co-operatives and large state farms, or on distributing land to independent small peasants? The current remains a reference in the contemporary literature of rural sociology and agricultural development studies in the economies of the Global South.

## The Question's Persistence in Contemporary Development Economics

The core of the "agrarian question" remains alive in contemporary development economics under different names. Do developing countries need large-scale agricultural industrialisation (highly productive large commercial farms) at the expense of small family farming, or can small farming, supported by co-operatives and micro-finance, achieve sufficient productivity while preserving the rural social fabric? This debate continues in the evaluation of agricultural micro-finance programmes and policies supporting smallholder farmers in various parts of the developing world, including the rural areas of Egypt, Syria and Morocco, where a large part of the rural population still depends on small family farming despite decades of capitalist concentration pressures and agricultural modernisation.
