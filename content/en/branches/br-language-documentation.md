# Language Documentation: Recording the Endangered Linguistic Heritage

A relatively recent branch of linguistics concerned with recording and archiving endangered languages in a comprehensive, systematic way (audio and video recordings, written texts, and grammatical and lexical analysis), before the world loses its last native speakers.

## The alarm: Michael Krauss's assessment

The article "The World's Languages in Crisis" (1992) by the linguist Michael Krauss is the turning point that launched this branch as an urgent research priority. He estimated that a vast proportion of the world's languages (as many as 90% in his most pessimistic estimate) could disappear or become moribund by the end of the 21st century, as the transmission of most languages to the next generation of children stops in many communities around the world. The estimate was widely discussed and helped make endangerment an urgent research priority for many linguists and for the Linguistic Society of America, which published the symposium containing Krauss's article.

## Documentation versus description

The linguist Nikolaus Himmelmann distinguished between "documentation" (the collection of a diverse and representative sample of actual recorded language use, such as natural conversations, narratives and oral rituals, archived in a way that allows varied future analyses) and the traditional "description," which produces a grammar, a dictionary and analyzed texts as one final analysis. This distinction stresses the importance of preserving the primary raw material itself, not only the analytical conclusions drawn from it, since it may be reread later with different theoretical tools.

## From documentation to revival

A practical debate arises within this field about priorities when resources are scarce. Should the effort go first toward documenting as many endangered languages as possible before it is too late (even if they remain only archival records with no living speakers later), or toward "revival," restoring the actual transmission of the languages between generations within their communities? One example is the "master-apprentice" method, developed by Leanne Hinton to connect the last elderly speakers directly with young learners. The revival experiences of Hebrew and Hawaiian are cited as rare examples of successful revival, showing that full revival is practically possible, though exceptional.

## Tools and ethical standards

The branch has developed strict ethical standards for fieldwork with endangered-language communities. Among them is the requirement that the community itself participate in defining the goals of documentation and in owning the material, so that the benefit is not limited to the external academic researcher alone. Alongside this, it has developed unified digital archiving standards (such as long-term file formats) that keep recorded material technically usable for decades.

## Elements of good documentation

Comprehensive contemporary language documentation requires several integrated elements: high-quality audio and video recordings of diverse patterns of actual language use (spontaneous conversation, storytelling, traditional oral rituals, and specialized language such as local traditional agricultural or medical vocabulary); written texts accompanied by precise word-by-word linguistic analysis (glossing), which clarifies the internal morphological structure of each word; a dictionary recording the vocabulary, its meanings and its contextual uses; a comprehensive descriptive grammar of the language's structure; and social and cultural contextual information about the speakers themselves and the conditions of everyday language use.

## Two successful revival cases in detail

The revival of Hebrew is widely cited as an unusually far-reaching case. A language long maintained mainly in writing and liturgy, rather than as a community mother tongue, became a language of everyday use through organized efforts from the late nineteenth century, associated in particular with Eliezer Ben-Yehuda and later with the institutions of the Yishuv and the State of Israel. The number of speakers grew over several generations, not in a single one. The revival of Hawaiian followed a different, smaller-scale model. Pūnana Leo ("language nest") preschools, begun in the 1980s, immerse young children in Hawaiian. The programs have produced new speakers, starting from a point at which fluent native speakers were mostly elderly and numbered in the low thousands, with very few children acquiring the language at home.

## Long-term digital archiving standards

The branch works on precise technical standards to ensure that documented materials remain technically usable for decades. These include using open-source file formats that are not tied to commercial software that may disappear in the future, keeping multiple backups in different geographic locations, and documenting metadata precisely (who the speaker is, when, where, and in what context), so that the content remains understandable and scientifically usable even decades after its original collection. This is a practical technical issue of great importance, no less important than the quality of the linguistic analysis itself.

## Training speakers themselves as documenters

A recent trend seeks to train members of the speaking community themselves in basic language documentation skills (simple audio recording and a simplified written transcript), rather than relying exclusively on an external visiting researcher. This ensures that documentation can continue locally even after any temporary external academic project has ended.
