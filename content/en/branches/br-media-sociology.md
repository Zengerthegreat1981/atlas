# Media Sociology

Media sociology studies the mass media as social institutions: who owns them, who works in them, what they produce, who uses them, and with what consequences for public life. It treats newspapers, broadcasting, film, and digital platforms not as neutral channels that carry messages but as organised systems of power — systems that decide which events become news, whose voices are heard, and which versions of reality circulate. Where media studies often focuses on texts and audiences, media sociology keeps the institutional question central: how are media organised, and whom does that organisation serve?

## Overview

The field is organised around three linked questions. First, production: how are media organisations structured, how do journalists and producers work, and how do ownership, advertising, and political pressure shape what gets made? Second, content: what patterns appear in news and entertainment — whose stories are told, which groups are stereotyped or ignored, and how do frames and narratives define problems? Third, reception and effects: how do audiences interpret media, and how does media exposure relate to attitudes, behaviour, and political participation?

Running through all three is the question of power. Media concentrate symbolic power — the power to name, frame, and make visible — and that power is unequally distributed. A small number of corporations own much of the world's media; news routines favour official sources and dramatic events; and audiences' capacity to interpret critically varies with education and resources. Media sociology documents these patterns and asks whether media sustain democracy or undermine it.

## History

The field grew out of early twentieth-century American sociology. Paul Lazarsfeld's studies of radio audiences and voting in the 1940s showed that media effects were limited and mediated by personal influence, overturning simple models of mass persuasion. With Robert Merton, Lazarsfeld argued in 1948 that mass communication could narcotise audiences — flooding citizens with information while discouraging action — and could confer status simply by covering a person or issue.

The 1970s transformed the field. Newsroom ethnographies by Gaye Tuchman and others showed that news is not a mirror of events but a constructed product: deadlines, beats, and professional norms determine what counts as news. Todd Gitlin's study of the American New Left showed how media coverage first ignored, then trivialised, then polarised a social movement. In Britain, Stuart Hall's encoding and decoding model insisted that audiences actively interpret media texts, sometimes against the grain of their intended meaning. The political economy tradition, represented by Edward Herman and Noam Chomsky's Manufacturing Consent (1988), argued that ownership, advertising, sourcing, and ideology systematically filter the news. John Thompson's The Media and Modernity (1995) synthesised these traditions, analysing how mediated interaction reshaped social life.

## Key Figures and Works

Lazarsfeld founded the empirical study of media effects and the two-step flow of communication. Tuchman's Making News (1978) remains the classic ethnography of news production, showing how the routines of journalism construct the reality they claim to report. Gitlin's The Whole World Is Watching (1980) demonstrated that media framing can make or break a movement, and that movements adapt their tactics to the demands of coverage.

Herman and Chomsky's propaganda model offered the most systematic account of structural bias in news, identifying filters that keep coverage within acceptable bounds. Thompson broadened the field's scope, arguing that media create new forms of interaction — mediated quasi-interaction — that stretch social relations across time and space. Robert McChesney's Rich Media, Poor Democracy (1999) carried the political economy argument into the era of deregulation and conglomeration, warning that concentrated commercial media were eroding the public sphere. Nick Couldry's later work on voice and media power asks whose accounts of the world are recognised as legitimate.

## Methods

Media sociology combines newsroom and studio ethnography, interviews with media workers, content analysis of large samples of output, and surveys and focus groups with audiences. Political economy relies on analysis of ownership structures, advertising markets, and regulation. Historical research traces how media systems developed in different countries, while comparative work contrasts public service and commercial models. Digital media have added new methods: analysis of platform data, study of algorithms and recommendation systems, and ethnography of online communities and creators.

## Influence and Debates

Media sociology has shaped public debate about media ownership, press freedom, and regulation, and its concepts — framing, agenda-setting, the propaganda model, encoding and decoding — are now standard across the social sciences. Its findings inform journalism training and media policy.

The field's central debates have been recast by digital platforms. Ownership is now concentrated in a handful of technology companies whose algorithms curate what billions see. Audiences have become producers, yet platform labour is precarious and unpaid content creation is the norm. Misinformation, harassment, and polarisation raise new questions about responsibility and regulation, while the decline of local news has hollowed out democratic infrastructure in many places. Media sociology's institutional lens — asking who owns the system, who works in it, and who benefits — is arguably more relevant now than when the field began.
