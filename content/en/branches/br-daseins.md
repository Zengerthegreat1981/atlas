# Daseinsanalysis

Daseinsanalysis is the oldest of the four currents of existential psychotherapy, and the one closest to philosophy and furthest from technique. Anyone who enters existential thought through this door finds something quite different from what a reader of Yalom knows: not a set of interventions for anxiety about death, but a way of understanding a human life as a whole way of being in the world. Its name comes from Heidegger's *Dasein*, being-there, and its founders were Swiss psychiatrists who tried to rebuild psychiatry on Heidegger's ontology rather than on Freud's theory of drives.

## The Central Question

The question is not "What is wrong with this person?" but "How does this person's world open up to them?" Illness here is not a fault in a mechanism but a narrowing of the ways in which someone can be in the world. Depression, for example, is not described by a list of symptoms but as a condition in which time has stopped, the future has closed, the body has grown heavy, and the distance from other people has widened. Every person has a characteristic way in which their world takes shape as a whole — in time, in space, in the body, and in relation to others — and the therapist's task is to describe that shape faithfully before doing anything else.

## Two Founders, Two Versions

Ludwig Binswanger (1881–1966), medical director of the Bellevue sanatorium at Kreuzlingen from 1911 to 1956, was the first physician to combine psychotherapy systematically with existential and phenomenological ideas. Reading Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927) gave him a vocabulary for what he had already felt clinically: that a patient must be understood through their *Weltentwurf*, their world-design, the basic pattern by which they project a world around themselves. He distinguished three dimensions of existence lived simultaneously — *Umwelt*, the surrounding physical and biological world; *Mitwelt*, the shared social world; and *Eigenwelt*, the world of one's relation to oneself. His systematic statement, *Grundformen und Erkenntnis menschlichen Daseins* (1942), argued against Heidegger himself that love, not only care (*Sorge*), is a fundamental structure of existence. His most famous case, that of Ellen West, published in 1944 and 1945, read a young woman's eating and despair as a constriction of her world-design rather than as a drive conflict.

Medard Boss (1903–1990) of Zurich produced the version that worked most directly with Heidegger himself. Boss invited Heidegger to give seminars to his students and colleagues at Zollikon, near Zurich, between 1959 and 1969; Boss recorded the exchanges and edited them for publication as the *Zollikon Seminars* (German 1987; English 2001). Where Binswanger built a theory of world-designs, Boss emphasised *Gelichtetheit*, openness: health is the capacity to let what meets us show itself, and illness is a closing-down of that openness. His *Psychoanalysis and Daseinsanalysis* (1957; English 1963) reinterpreted dreaming without Freud's censor and wish, and his *Grundriss der Medizin und der Psychologie* (1971; second edition 1975; English as *Existential Foundations of Medicine and Psychology*, 1979) extended the approach from psychiatry to medicine as a whole.

## The Wider Circle

Binswanger and Boss were not alone. Eugène Minkowski, working in France, described lived time in *Le Temps vécu* (1933) and showed how schizophrenia could be understood as a disturbance of the temporal flow of experience. Erwin Straus explored the senses and the lived body in *Vom Sinn der Sinne* (1935), and Victor von Gebsattel developed a medical anthropology in the same spirit. In the contemporary generation, Alice Holzhey-Kunz reformulated the tradition in *Leiden am Dasein* (1994), arguing that psychological suffering is suffering from one's own being — from the condition of being a Dasein at all — and that this "pre-ontological inclusion" of existence is what makes suffering intelligible.

## Method in Practice

Daseinsanalysis refuses technique on principle. There are no measured goals, no exercises, no treatment manual. The method is phenomenological description and patience: the therapist brackets theories and diagnostic labels, describes what shows itself in the patient's experience, and waits for the patient's world to open up to both of them together. It also rejects the Freudian unconscious as a theoretical construction that is never given in experience. What is unseen is not hidden in a cellar, in this view; it is simply not yet disclosed. Diagnosis is used only when administratively necessary and never taken as the frame of the work. The therapeutic relationship is not a tool applied to a disorder but a shared world in which a constricted way of being can loosen.

## Influence and Limits

The current's strongest achievement is the most precise description of pathological experience from the inside that any psychological tradition has produced, and it lives on today in contemporary phenomenological psychopathology, notably in the work of Giovanni Stanghellini, whose *Disembodied Spirits and Deanimated Bodies* (2004) carries the tradition into current debates about schizophrenia and embodiment. Much of what the American current says about "the client's world" was taken from here through intermediaries, usually without acknowledgement. Its weaknesses are equally clear: it was almost absent from English-language literature for decades, it has virtually no experimental evidence, its language is hard for a trainee to enter, and it is today the smallest of the four currents in reach. Yet for anyone who wants to know what existential psychotherapy meant before it became teachable, this is the source.
