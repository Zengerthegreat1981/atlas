# Corpus Linguistics: Language from Actual Use

A methodological branch that studies language by analyzing large collections of real texts in use (corpora), instead of relying on a native speaker's intuition (introspection) about whether an invented or artificial sentence is correct or incorrect.

## From structuralism to computing

The roots of this branch go back to the American structuralist tradition in the mid-20th century, when Zellig Harris proposed formal "discovery procedures" for extracting grammatical structure directly from a limited set of actually recorded texts, without recourse to a prior intuitive judgment. The field took a more explicitly corpus-based form with large collections of running text. Randolph Quirk's Survey of English Usage began in 1959 as a structured collection of British English. The Brown Corpus, compiled in 1961 by W. Nelson Francis and Henry Kučera and made available in machine-readable form in 1964, contains about one million words of written American English and supported automatic counts of usage on a scale that hand analysis could not match.

## Method: frequency and statistical significance

This branch rests on a central methodological idea: the meaning and use of a word cannot be understood apart from the contexts in which it actually recurs. J. R. Firth expressed this in his famous dictum "you shall know a word by the company it keeps." Studies in this branch rely on statistical measures such as collocation (the tendency of certain words to appear together more than chance would predict) and the relative frequency of particular grammatical patterns across different text types (casual conversation, newspaper article, academic text), revealing fine-grained patterns of use that are hard for individual intuition to notice.

## Expansion and diversity of corpora

Corpora have grown from relatively small collections to gigantic ones containing billions of words drawn from the internet (such as the large web-crawled corpora now available for English and other languages). They have expanded from monolingual to parallel bilingual corpora (texts translated side by side), used mainly to train machine translation systems, and from general corpora to specialized ones (legal, medical, historical) serving specific research purposes, including historical corpora of Classical Arabic across its different eras, which allow the tracing of linguistic change over time.

## Relation to other methodologies

This branch represents a historical methodological tension with generative linguistics, which traditionally relied on the native speaker's intuitive judgment about invented sentences rather than on data from actual use. The argument is that no corpus, however large, can contain every grammatically possible sentence of a language. Supporters of the corpus approach reply that exclusive reliance on intuition is subject to the researcher's biases and does not reflect the actual distribution of patterns in living use. This disagreement over the nature of acceptable linguistic evidence is still ongoing.

## Types of corpora and their classification

Corpora are classified today according to several criteria. "Synchronic" corpora capture a single moment in time in the language, as opposed to "diachronic" corpora that span successive eras to trace linguistic change over time. "General" corpora represent the diversity of language use across many domains in statistically balanced proportions (for example, a specified share of journalistic, literary and spoken texts), as opposed to "specialized" corpora restricted to a single text type (such as a legal or medical corpus) for specific research purposes. "Parallel" corpora contain the same text translated into several languages side by side, used mainly in translation studies and cross-linguistic comparison.

## Automatic tagging

Most modern corpora undergo a preliminary automatic processing called "tagging," in which each word in the corpus is assigned an automatic morphological and grammatical label (noun, verb, adjective, verb tense, noun number) by specialized software. This allows the researcher to search for precise grammatical patterns (for example, all verbs followed by two objects) without reading every text manually to find that pattern. This is a point where corpus linguistics and computational linguistics meet.

## Applied uses for lexicography and dictionaries

Corpus linguistics has changed modern dictionary-making. Instead of the traditional lexicographer relying on personal intuition and limited reading to determine a word's meanings and common uses, it is now possible to extract every actual context in which a given word occurs across millions of sentences, and to arrange them statistically by the frequency of each meaning and usage. The result is a more precise and realistic dictionary reflecting the actual contemporary use of the language, not just the individual impression of the lexicographer, however experienced.

## Replicability and independent verification

A methodological feature of this branch is that many of its results can be replicated and independently verified by other researchers. Any second researcher can access the same corpus and rerun the same statistical analysis to check the validity of the announced result, unlike an individual intuitive judgment, which is harder to verify independently with the same quantitative precision.
