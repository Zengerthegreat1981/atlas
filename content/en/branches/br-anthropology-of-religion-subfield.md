# Anthropology of Religion as a Subfield

A subfield that studies religious beliefs, rituals and institutions as observable social phenomena open to field observation and comparative analysis. It treats them neither as a matter of truth or falsehood belonging to theology alone, nor as mere functional "illusions" awaiting a reductive scientific explanation that dissolves them entirely into social or psychological causes without respect for their internal coherence as a system of meaning for believers themselves.

## Émile Durkheim and the Collective Sacred

The roots of this subfield go back to Émile Durkheim, who argued in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) that the essence of religion is not belief in supernatural beings as such, but the fundamental social distinction between the "sacred" and the "profane." Collective religious rituals, such as those of the Indigenous Australian tribes that Durkheim studied, create a state of "collective effervescence" that renews the cohesion of the social group and makes its members feel a force greater than themselves as individuals. It is this same force that they later project onto sacred symbols (such as the totem). In the Durkheimian analysis, "God" thus becomes a symbolic expression of society itself, worshipping its own collective self without direct awareness of doing so.

## Clifford Geertz: Religion as a Symbolic System

Clifford Geertz offered an influential definition of religion as a system of symbols that establishes strong, pervasive and long-lasting moods and motivations by formulating conceptions of a general order of existence and clothing them with an aura of factuality that makes them seem uniquely realistic to the believer. He called for the study of religion through "thick description" of specific rituals and practices (such as his well-known study of the cockfight in Bali), in order to grasp the deep local cultural meaning that the religious act carries for its participants, rather than reducing it to an abstract social function devoid of any particular content of its own.

## Liminal Rites and Threshold States

Victor Turner developed the concept of "liminality" (the threshold state), drawing on Arnold van Gennep's concept of "rites of passage," to describe the ambiguous intermediate phase an individual passes through during a major social transition rite (such as initiation rites). During this phase the person is temporarily freed from their ordinary social structure and enters a state of "communitas," a direct and non-hierarchical human solidarity with fellow participants in the same passage, before being reintegrated into a new social structure with their changed status.

## Religion in the Contemporary Arab Context

Researchers such as Saba Mahmood have studied how piety and Islamic religiosity are practiced as an active ethical project that the believer builds (women especially, in her study of the women's mosque movement in Cairo, published as *Politics of Piety*, 2005), through regular bodily and ritual discipline (such as regular prayer and the veil), rather than as an external constraint imposed on individuals whose "true" liberation from it is awaited. This posed a direct theoretical challenge to Western liberal assumptions about "agency," understood as necessarily resistance to the prevailing authority, rather than voluntary engagement in an ethical project that religion itself defines.
