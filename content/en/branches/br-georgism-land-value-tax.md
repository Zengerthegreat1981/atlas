# Georgism and the Land Value Tax: The Single Tax on Rent

**Georgism** is a political-economic current founded by the American economist **Henry George** in his influential book *Progress and Poverty* (1879). The book posed a question that puzzled his age: why does poverty persist and worsen despite the enormous technological progress and economic growth of the late nineteenth century? George answered that the root cause is **land rent**. As society advances and grows in population and economy, the value of land (especially urban land) rises without its owners making any productive effort. Landowners thus capture an increasing share of the fruits of collective progress through mere ownership, not production.

## The Ricardian Origin of the Rent Theory

George based himself directly on David Ricardo's theory of differential rent. In Ricardo's account, more fertile or better-located land yields a rent equal to its advantage over the poorest land in use, and this rent rises as growing demand forces poorer land into cultivation. But George extended this principle to urban and locational rent: the value of land in the centre of a crowded city rises because of population density and the public infrastructure surrounding it, not because of any particular improvement made by its owner. He concluded that rent in all its forms is a collective social product rather than an individual one, and therefore should return to society rather than be privately appropriated.

## The Solution: The Land Value Tax

George proposed a radical and simple solution, known as the "single tax": a tax that would absorb almost the entire economic rent of land value, meaning the value of the bare land itself rather than the value of buildings and improvements the owner has constructed. At the same time, all other taxes on labour, capital, trade and production would be abolished. George argued that this tax, unlike income or consumption taxes, does not distort productive incentives, because the supply of land is physically fixed. A landowner cannot "escape" or reduce production in response to the tax as a worker or investor can. This argument was later acknowledged even by neoclassical economists who do not share George's political premises.

## Impact and Diffusion

George's book sparked a broad popular political movement in the United States, Britain and Australia. It influenced the origins of the game "Monopoly," whose precursor, *The Landlord's Game*, Elizabeth Magie patented in 1904 as an educational tool to illustrate the evils of land monopoly, before it was later altered to glorify monopoly itself. The book also shaped limited practical policies that persist today, such as state and local land taxes in Australia and split-rate property taxes that tax land more heavily than buildings in some Pennsylvania cities (Pittsburgh used such a system for much of the twentieth century before abandoning it in 2001). Its practical influence on contemporary policy is marginal compared with its historical intellectual impact. Georgist thought has also remained a reference for some contemporary economists interested in urban rent and housing crises, although the full application of the "single tax" proposal has not been realised in any large economy.

## Georgism in an Arab and Rentier Context

Although Georgism arose in a purely Western industrial context, the core of its argument finds a distant analytical echo in political-economy debates about oil rent in Arab oil-exporting states. That argument holds that rent arising from the scarcity of location or natural resources is collective in origin rather than individual. The mechanism differs fundamentally, however: oil rent comes from a subsoil resource that is usually owned by the state rather than by individuals, not from urban land rent resulting from population concentration. This similarity in general principle (unearned rent not gained through individual effort should be managed collectively) does not mean identity in policy or mechanism. Any direct transfer of the Georgist model to Arab oil rentier economies requires great methodological caution, to avoid confusing two fundamentally different types of economic rent.
