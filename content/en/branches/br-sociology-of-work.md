# Sociology of Work

The sociology of work studies work as a social institution: how jobs are organised, how occupations and professions are structured, how workplaces generate cooperation and conflict, and how work shapes identity, inequality, and everyday life. It treats work not merely as an economic transaction — labour sold for a wage — but as a central site where people spend most of their waking lives, where status is assigned, skills are learned, solidarities are built, and power is exercised. Its questions range from the factory floor to the office, the hospital ward, the home, and the platform app.

## Overview

The field begins from the division of labour. Émile Durkheim argued that the specialisation of tasks holds modern societies together, creating interdependence between people who do different work. But specialisation also creates hierarchy: some occupations command high pay, autonomy, and prestige, while others are insecure, poorly paid, and stigmatised. The sociology of work asks how that hierarchy is produced and justified — through credentials, professional closure, gendered and racialised assumptions about whose work counts, and the bargaining power of different groups.

Work is also where management and workers meet. Employers seek effort, predictability, and control; workers seek decent pay, dignity, security, and meaning. The resulting tension is managed through supervision, technology, incentives, teamwork, and culture — and sometimes breaks into open conflict. The field studies all of these: the labour process, workplace culture, resistance, and the experience of work itself, from pride and craft to boredom, exhaustion, and alienation.

## History

The classical sociologists made work foundational. Durkheim's The Division of Labor in Society (1893) tied occupational specialisation to social solidarity and to anomie when regulation failed. Max Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905) linked religious ideas to the disciplined work ethic of early capitalism, and his studies of bureaucracy mapped the rationalised organisation of modern work. Karl Marx's analysis of alienation and exploitation framed work as the site where value is created and appropriated.

The modern field took shape in the mid-twentieth century with industrial sociology — studies of factories, bureaucracies, and occupations — and with Everett Hughes's Chicago school research on occupations and professional socialisation. Harry Braverman's Labor and Monopoly Capital (1974) revived labour process theory, arguing that scientific management deskilled craft workers and transferred knowledge from workers to managers. Michael Burawoy answered in Manufacturing Consent (1979), showing from his own factory ethnography that workers were not simply coerced but actively participated in shop-floor games that secured their consent to the labour process. Arlie Hochschild's The Managed Heart (1983) opened a new front, showing that service work requires emotional labour: the commercial management of feeling, most visibly in jobs such as flight attendant or nurse.

## Key Figures and Works

Durkheim established the link between the division of labour, solidarity, and anomie. Weber showed how bureaucracy and rationalisation organise modern work. Braverman made deskilling and managerial control the field's central debate, and Burawoy complicated that account by showing how consent is manufactured on the shop floor. Hochschild's concept of emotional labour has travelled far beyond sociology, into studies of care work, service work, and the gendered demands of contemporary jobs.

Richard Sennett's The Corrosion of Character (1998) captured the experience of flexible capitalism, arguing that short-term, project-based work undermines the long-term narratives through which people build character and commitment. Arne Kalleberg's Good Jobs, Bad Jobs (2011) documented the polarisation of American employment into secure, well-paid jobs and precarious, low-paid ones, showing how the quality of jobs — not just their number — has become the central problem. Eliot Freidson's studies of the professions showed how occupations secure monopoly over knowledge and markets, a process of closure that explains much of the inequality between occupations.

## Methods

Workplace ethnography is the field's signature method: researchers take jobs or observe them at close quarters to understand how work is actually done, as Burawoy did in a Chicago machine shop. Surveys and administrative data track employment, wages, hours, and job quality across populations and over time. Interviews and life histories capture how people experience careers, unemployment, and retirement. Comparative and historical research contrasts national models of work organisation and regulation. Recent work adds digital methods: studying algorithmic management, platform ratings, and the data trails of gig work.

## Influence and Debates

The sociology of work has shaped debates on industrial relations, occupational health, gender equality, and the future of jobs. Its concepts — deskilling, emotional labour, precarity, the labour process — are now part of public discussion.

Current debates centre on the platform economy, where algorithmic management replaces human supervision and workers are classified as independent contractors; on remote and hybrid work, which blurs the boundary between home and workplace; on automation and artificial intelligence, which threaten some occupations while creating new forms of control; and on care work, whose low pay and high demands expose how societies value different kinds of labour. The field's enduring question is whether work can be organised to provide security, dignity, and meaning — or whether its current organisation systematically denies those things to most workers.

## Further Themes

Three themes now cut across the field. Algorithmic management, in which software assigns tasks, monitors performance, and rates workers, has been reviewed by Katherine Kellogg, Melissa Valentine, and Angele Christin (2020), who describe how it extends managerial control while also generating new forms of worker resistance, notably in platform work. Remote work revives older questions about the boundary between home and workplace: Christena Nippert-Eng's *Home and Work* (1996) showed that people actively manage that boundary, some segmenting and others integrating the two spheres, and Hochschild's *The Time Bind* (1997) examined how work can come to dominate family time. Work and climate change raise the question of a just transition: the International Labour Organization's guidelines (2015) call for managing the shift to environmentally sustainable economies in ways that protect workers' jobs, incomes, and rights, though how far this can be achieved remains contested.
