# Feminist Sociology

Feminist sociology studies gender as a principle of social organisation and examines how inequalities between women and men, and among differently situated women and men, are produced and challenged. It also criticises the discipline itself for having often treated men's experience as the general case and for neglecting housework, care, sexuality, and violence.

## Foundations

Earlier writers including Harriet Martineau, Charlotte Perkins Gilman, whose *Women and Economics* appeared in 1898, and Jane Addams analysed women's position and social reform, although their work was long marginal to accounts of sociological theory. A distinct feminist sociology developed with the women's movement of the late 1960s and 1970s. Ann Oakley's *Sex, Gender and Society* (1972) distinguished biological sex from socially constructed gender, a distinction that organised much research and was later itself questioned. Judith Stacey and Barrie Thorne's "The Missing Feminist Revolution in Sociology" (1985) argued that gender research had grown without transforming the discipline's core frameworks.

## Key Theorists

Dorothy Smith developed a sociology beginning from women's standpoint and everyday experience, tracing how local activity is organised by wider "relations of ruling." Candace West and Don Zimmerman's "Doing Gender" (1987) described gender as an accomplishment produced and held accountable in interaction. R. W. Connell's *Gender and Power* (1987) analysed gender relations through structures including labour, power, and emotional attachment. Joan Acker argued in 1990 that organisations are gendered, including in their supposedly neutral idea of the worker. Sylvia Walby's *Theorizing Patriarchy* (1990) identified several interconnected structures of patriarchy, among them household production, paid work, the state, violence, sexuality, and culture. Patricia Hill Collins and others showed that gender must be analysed together with race, class, and sexuality.

## Empirical Examples

Oakley's *The Sociology of Housework* (1974) treated housework as work, using interviews with London housewives. Rosabeth Moss Kanter's *Men and Women of the Corporation* (1977) showed how opportunity, power, and numerical minority status shape behaviour at work. Arlie Russell Hochschild's *The Managed Heart* (1983), which studied flight attendants among others, analysed emotional labour. *The Second Shift* (1989), written with Anne Machung, examined how employed couples divided domestic work and how many women carried an additional load.

## Methods and Debates

Feminist sociologists use the full range of methods, often with explicit attention to reflexivity, relations between researcher and participant, and the uses of knowledge. Debates concern whether there is a distinct feminist method; how to speak of "women" without erasing differences; the relationship between material inequality and discourse; and the implications of transgender studies and global feminisms for theories of gender.
