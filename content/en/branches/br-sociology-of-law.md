# Sociology of Law

The sociology of law studies law as a social institution: how legal rules are made, how they operate in practice, whom they serve, and how they change. It treats law not as a self-contained system of rules but as a human product, shaped by power, culture, and history, and shaping them in turn. Where legal scholarship asks what the law says and how it should be interpreted, the sociology of law asks what law does in society — how disputes are actually handled, who can use the courts, how legal professionals work, and how ordinary people understand and experience legality in everyday life.

## Overview

The field's central insight is the gap between law in books and law in action. Statutes and judgments promise equality before the law, yet outcomes vary systematically by class, race, gender, and resources. Sociologists of law investigate that gap: the discretion of police officers and prosecutors, the bargaining that resolves most criminal cases before trial, the costs and delays that deter civil claims, and the informal norms that govern workplaces, families, and neighbourhoods alongside, or instead of, formal law.

The field also studies legal pluralism — the coexistence of multiple legal orders within one society. State law is only one source of binding norms; religious law, customary law, corporate codes, and community practices also regulate conduct. In colonial and postcolonial societies, and in transnational settings such as trade and migration, these overlapping orders are the norm rather than the exception. Finally, the sociology of law studies law's role in social change: law can entrench inequality, but it has also been a tool of emancipation, from civil rights litigation to constitutional challenges to discriminatory statutes.

## History

The field's classical foundations lie in the work of Émile Durkheim, Max Weber, and Eugen Ehrlich. Durkheim treated law as the visible symbol of social solidarity: repressive law, which punishes offences against the collective conscience, dominates in societies held together by likeness, while restitutive law, which restores relationships, grows with the division of labour. Weber analysed law's rationalisation, tracing the rise of formally rational legal systems and the bureaucratic, professional administration of justice in modern states. Ehrlich, working in the multi-ethnic borderlands of the Habsburg Empire, argued that the "living law" of everyday social associations mattered more than state codes, becoming an important precursor of the study of legal pluralism.

The field took institutional shape in the twentieth century, particularly in the United States, where the law and society movement of the 1960s and 1970s built research centres, journals, and the Law and Society Association. Lawrence Friedman's Law and Society (1977) offered a synthesis for the movement, connecting legal institutions to social structure and culture. From the 1980s onwards, the field absorbed critical legal studies, feminist scholarship, and Pierre Bourdieu's analysis of the juridical field, broadening its questions from the operation of courts to the cultural power of legal categories.

## Key Figures and Works

Durkheim remains the field's touchstone for the claim that law expresses and sustains social solidarity. Weber's account of rational-legal authority and the professionalisation of law underpins studies of legal bureaucracy and the legal profession. Ehrlich's Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law (1913) is still cited for its insistence that law lives in social practice, not only in statute books.

Friedman's synthesis showed how legal systems respond to social change and how access to law is stratified. Bourdieu's essay "The Force of Law" (1987) analysed the juridical field as a site of struggle over the power to name what is lawful, with legal professionals monopolising a specialised language that distances law from ordinary people. Patricia Ewick and Susan Silbey's The Common Place of Law (1998), based on interviews with ordinary Americans, showed that legal consciousness is not a single attitude but a shifting repertoire: people treat law as distant and majestic, as a game to be played strategically, and as an arena of resistance, sometimes within a single conversation. Mathieu Deflem's Sociology of Law (2008) synthesised the classical tradition for a new generation.

## Methods

The sociology of law is methodologically plural. Courtroom and law-office ethnography observes how legal work is actually done. Interviews and surveys capture legal consciousness — what people know about law, when they turn to it, and when they avoid it. Quantitative studies analyse sentencing patterns, litigation rates, and the social distribution of legal outcomes. Historical and comparative research traces how legal institutions developed differently across societies, while socio-legal studies of the Global South examine customary law, informal justice, and the legacies of colonial legal systems. Increasingly, researchers study online dispute resolution, algorithmic decision-making, and the platform governance that now regulates speech and commerce.

## Influence and Debates

The sociology of law has reshaped how lawyers, policymakers, and social scientists think about legal reform. Its findings on discretion and inequality inform debates about sentencing, policing, and access to justice; its work on legal consciousness shapes policies on legal aid and public legal education.

Live debates include whether law can drive social change or mainly ratifies changes achieved elsewhere; how globalisation and transnational regulation are transforming state-centred legal orders; and how to study law in digital settings where platforms, not courts, set the rules. Feminist and critical race scholars continue to press the field on whose experiences count as legally relevant. The field's enduring question is the one Ehrlich posed: if law lives in society rather than in codes, how should we recognise it, study it, and hold it to account?

## Further Themes

Three themes extend the field's classic concerns. On transnational law and global legal pluralism, Sally Engle Merry's review "Legal Pluralism" (1988) and Boaventura de Sousa Santos's notion of interlegality describe how state, customary, and international norms overlap, and Gunther Teubner has argued that global private regimes, such as those of trade and sport, generate law largely outside the state. On access to justice, the Florence Access-to-Justice Project led by Mauro Cappelletti and Bryant Garth (1978) identified barriers of cost, information, and organisation and described successive reform efforts, and its comparative framework has been widely applied outside Europe and North America, including in postcolonial settings where plural legal systems coexist. On digital settings, Lawrence Lessig (1999) argued that software architecture regulates behaviour much as law does, and research on how people experience platform rules, moderation, and online dispute resolution as law-like authority is still emerging.
