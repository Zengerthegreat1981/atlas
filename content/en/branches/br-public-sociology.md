# Public Sociology

Public sociology is sociology that engages audiences beyond the university in dialogue about matters of common concern. It includes writing for general readers, contributing to public debate, and collaborating with communities, unions, associations, and social movements. The label gained wide currency through Michael Burawoy in the early 2000s, although the practices it names are much older.

## Precursors

W. E. B. Du Bois combined empirical research, as in *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899), with public argument about racial inequality. Jane Addams and colleagues at Hull-House linked social investigation to reform. C. Wright Mills's *The Sociological Imagination* (1959) urged sociologists to connect personal troubles with public issues of social structure. Books such as David Riesman and colleagues' *The Lonely Crowd* (1950) and *Habits of the Heart* (1985), by Robert Bellah and colleagues, reached broad readerships. Herbert Gans used the term "public sociology" in a 1988 presidential address to the American Sociological Association.

## Burawoy's Programme

In a 2004 presidential address to the same association, published as "For Public Sociology" (2005), Burawoy distinguished four kinds of sociology. Professional sociology develops theories and methods for an academic audience. Policy sociology addresses problems defined by a client. Public sociology opens a conversation with publics about goals and values. Critical sociology examines the foundations of professional research programmes. The scheme separates instrumental from reflexive knowledge, and academic from extra-academic audiences. Burawoy argued that the four depend on one another.

Burawoy also distinguished traditional public sociology, which reaches broad and dispersed audiences through books, journalism, and commentary, from organic public sociology, in which researchers work closely and reciprocally with a particular public.

## Empirical Examples

Examples include community-based and participatory research on housing, health, or environmental hazards; expert contributions to court cases and official inquiries; teaching, which Burawoy treated as engagement with a first public, students; and accessible ethnographies or analyses that inform debate about poverty, work, or discrimination. Contemporary sociologists also use blogs, podcasts, and social media.

## Debates

The programme prompted extensive discussion. Some critics argued that it risks tying the discipline to particular political positions and weakening its claim to rigorous knowledge. Others held that the four-part scheme separates activities that are intertwined, or that it undervalues critical and activist traditions. Scholars writing from the Global South noted that in many settings public engagement has long been a normal part of sociological work, and that the framing reflects the organisation of the discipline in the United States. Further questions concern academic incentives, which often give limited credit for public work, the risks faced by researchers under political pressure, and how publics can shape research questions as well as receive findings.
