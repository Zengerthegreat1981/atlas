# Youth Sociology

Youth sociology studies young people as a social category: how societies define youth, how young people navigate the passage to adulthood, and how they create cultures, identities, and politics of their own. Youth is not simply a biological stage between childhood and adulthood; it is socially constructed, and its length, meaning, and boundaries vary across history and culture. In many contemporary societies the transition to adulthood has become longer and less predictable, as education extends, stable employment arrives later, and marriage and parenthood are postponed. Youth sociology asks how young people experience these changes and how they respond to them.

## Scope

The field covers youth cultures and subcultures, education and the transition from school to work, family and peer relationships, leisure and consumption, digital life, political participation, and youth deviance. It studies how class, gender, ethnicity, and place shape young people's opportunities and identities. It examines institutions that govern youth — schools, youth justice systems, welfare services — and the ways young people resist, evade, or remake them. Youth sociology also studies youth as a political force, from student movements to climate activism, and as a market category, targeted by advertising, fashion, and entertainment industries. Its central tension is between seeing young people as adults in the making, defined by what they are becoming, and seeing them as social actors in their own right, with cultures and concerns worth studying on their own terms.

## History

The modern idea of adolescence was formulated by the psychologist G. Stanley Hall in *Adolescence* (1904), which portrayed the teenage years as a universal period of storm and stress. Sociologists soon challenged this biological account. Karl Mannheim's essay "The Problem of Generations" (1928) argued that people who share formative historical experiences develop a distinctive generational consciousness. In the post-war period, James Coleman studied American high schools in *The Adolescent Society* (1961), showing that teenagers formed a social world with its own status system, centred on athletics and popularity rather than academic achievement. British cultural studies transformed the field in the 1970s: Paul Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977) showed how working-class boys' resistance to school prepared them, ironically, for working-class jobs, while Dick Hebdige's *Subculture: The Meaning of Style* (1979) read punk and other youth styles as symbolic resistance. From the 1990s onward, the field shifted toward the study of transitions: Andy Furlong and Fred Cartmel's *Young People and Social Change* (1997) argued that individualisation had made youth transitions more risky and less predictable, while Johanna Wyn and Rob White's *Rethinking Youth* (1997) urged sociologists to treat young people as agents rather than as problems.

## Key Figures and Works

Hall (1904) invented adolescence as a scientific object, even as later sociologists rejected his biological determinism. Mannheim (1928) gave the field its theory of generations. Coleman (1961) mapped the social world of the high school. Willis (1977) showed how class is reproduced through young people's own cultural practices. Hebdige (1979) established the study of subcultural style. Furlong and Cartmel (1997) analysed the lengthening, individualised transitions of late modernity. Wyn and White (1997) reframed youth as a relational and negotiated status. More recently, scholars such as Dan Woodman have studied how young people navigate precarious work and delayed adulthood, and research on digital youth cultures has examined how social media reshapes friendship, identity, and political engagement.

## Methods

Youth sociology uses surveys, interviews, ethnography, and increasingly digital methods. Large longitudinal surveys track transitions from school to work across cohorts. Ethnography in schools, neighbourhoods, and online spaces captures the meanings young people give to their lives. Life-history interviews reveal how individuals narrate their own transitions. Participatory methods, in which young people act as co-researchers, have become important as the field has moved toward recognising youth agency. Research ethics are especially sensitive here: studying minors requires careful attention to consent, confidentiality, and protection, and researchers must avoid reproducing the adult gaze that treats young people as problems to be solved.

## Influence and Debates

Youth sociology has shaped education policy, youth justice, and employment programmes, and it informs public debate about issues from youth unemployment to knife crime to screen time. Its debates remain lively. One concerns structure and agency: how much do young people's choices matter against the constraints of class, labour markets, and housing? Another concerns the category of youth itself: as transitions stretch into the thirties, does "youth" still describe a meaningful stage, or has it become a prolonged condition? A third concerns subcultures: in an age of digital platforms and globalised consumption, do distinctive youth subcultures still exist, or has style become a matter of individual curation? A fourth concerns generational politics: the language of generations — baby boomers, millennials, Generation Z — is widely used in public debate, but sociologists disagree about how much explanatory power generational categories really carry. The field's enduring contribution is to insist that young people are not merely future adults but makers of social life in the present.
