# Evolutionary Sociology

Evolutionary sociology applies the logic of evolution — variation, selection, and inheritance — to the study of social life. It asks how human nature, shaped by millions of years of primate and hominin evolution, constrains and enables social organisation, and how societies themselves evolve over time. Where most sociology treats human beings as blank slates written on by culture, evolutionary sociology insists that the slate is already marked: evolved dispositions toward kinship, status, reciprocity, and group belonging shape what kinds of societies are possible, and why some institutions recur across cultures while others fail.

The field has two related branches. One studies the evolution of societies: the long-term development of social complexity, from small bands to chiefdoms, states, and empires. The other studies the evolutionary foundations of behaviour: how natural and sexual selection shaped human psychology, emotions, and social capacities, and how those capacities play out in modern institutions. Both branches share a conviction that sociology cannot ignore biology, and that Darwinian thinking can illuminate social phenomena from the family to the state.

## Scope

Evolutionary sociology covers the emergence and development of institutions: kinship, marriage, religion, law, and the state, each examined as a product of selective pressures operating over long time scales. It examines cooperation and altruism: how humans, unlike most species, sustain cooperation among non-kin in large groups, and what mechanisms — reciprocity, reputation, punishment, shared norms — make this possible. It studies emotions as evolved adaptations, with particular attention to the emotions that bind groups together. It also studies the demographic and ecological dimensions of societal evolution: how population, resources, and technology interact to produce growth, collapse, and transformation.

The field is explicitly interdisciplinary, drawing on primatology, evolutionary psychology, anthropology, genetics, and neuroscience, as well as on the sociological tradition itself.

## History

Evolutionary thinking was present at the birth of sociology. Herbert Spencer applied evolutionary ideas to society, and Emile Durkheim, Max Weber, and Karl Marx all theorised long-term social development, though none used Darwinian mechanisms in the modern sense. In the early twentieth century, evolutionary approaches fell into disrepute, associated with social Darwinism, eugenics, and the justification of inequality. Sociology turned decisively toward culture and away from biology.

The revival began in the 1970s. Edward O. Wilson's *Sociobiology: The New Synthesis* argued that social behaviour in all species, including humans, has a biological basis shaped by natural selection, provoking fierce controversy. Sociologists who took the challenge seriously sought to build an evolutionary sociology that avoided the determinism of sociobiology. Gerhard Lenski's work on the evolution of human societies, and his typology of hunting and gathering, horticultural, agrarian, and industrial societies, provided a sociological framework for long-term development. Joseph Lopreato developed a sociobiological sociology of human behaviour. Alexandra Maryanski and Jonathan Turner, in *The Social Cage*, argued that human nature was shaped by millions of years of life in small primate groups, and that modern societies, with their large scale and weak ties, chafe against that inherited nature — producing alienation, anomie, and the search for community.

## Key Figures and Ideas

Edward O. Wilson supplied the provocation: his synthesis of population biology and social behaviour forced sociology to confront the biological foundations of social life, even for those who rejected his conclusions. Gerhard Lenski provided the field's macro-sociological framework, showing how technology and subsistence shape the scale and complexity of societies, and how inequality grows with surplus. Jonathan Turner has been the field's most systematic theorist, developing an evolutionary account of the emergence of institutions, the role of emotions in bonding, and the selection pressures that shaped human sociality.

Alexandra Maryanski's contribution is the primate legacy thesis: humans carry the social psychology of apes — individualistic, mobile, weakly bonded — into a world of large-scale societies that demand the opposite, and much of social life is an attempt to manage that mismatch. Richard Machalek has worked on the evolution of macrosociety and the comparison of human and non-human social organisation.

## Method

Evolutionary sociology uses comparative and historical methods: comparing societies across time and across species, tracing the emergence of institutions, and testing hypotheses against the ethnographic and archaeological records. It uses primatological evidence about ape social behaviour, psychological experiments on evolved dispositions, and formal modelling of selection processes. It also draws on the standard sociological methods of survey and ethnographic research, applying them to questions about kinship, status, and cooperation. The field's methodological signature is its long time horizon and its comparative scope: it seeks patterns that hold across societies and species, not only within a single historical moment.

## Influence and Debates

Evolutionary sociology has influenced the study of institutions, emotions, and inequality, and it has forced a productive debate about the relationship between biology and sociology. Its defenders argue that ignoring evolution leaves sociology unable to explain why certain social patterns recur across cultures, why institutions so often fail, and why human beings are the way they are.

The field remains controversial. Critics warn against biological reductionism — explaining complex social phenomena by invoking evolved dispositions — and against the misuse of evolutionary arguments to naturalise inequality, gender roles, and aggression. Defenders reply that evolutionary sociology is not determinism: evolved dispositions are tendencies, not destinies, and they operate through culture and institutions. The debate reflects a deeper tension in sociology between the search for universal explanations and the insistence on historical specificity — a tension that evolutionary sociology, at its best, makes productive rather than merely polarising.
