# Environmental Sociology

Environmental sociology studies the relationship between human societies and the natural environment. It asks how social structures, economies, cultures, and politics shape the use of nature — and how environmental change, in turn, shapes societies. Pollution, deforestation, climate change, and resource depletion are not merely technical or natural problems; they are social problems, produced by particular ways of organising production and consumption, and distributed unequally across classes, races, and nations. Environmental sociology insists that nature is not an external backdrop to social life but a participant in it: societies depend on ecosystems, transform them, and are transformed by their degradation.

The field emerged from a critique of sociology itself. Classical sociology treated the environment as a given, focusing on how societies were organised internally while taking nature for granted. Environmental sociologists argue that this "human exemptionalism" — the assumption that human societies are exempt from ecological constraints — must be abandoned.

## Scope

The field covers the social causes of environmental problems: how industrial production, consumer culture, urbanisation, and global trade generate pollution and resource use. It studies environmental inequality: how poor communities and communities of colour bear disproportionate exposure to hazards, from toxic waste sites to air pollution — the subject of environmental justice research. It examines environmental attitudes, values, and movements: who worries about the environment, who mobilises to protect it, and how environmentalism varies across class and culture. It studies the politics of the environment: how states regulate pollution, how corporations resist or embrace green policies, and how international institutions govern shared resources.


## History

Environmental sociology crystallised in the late 1970s, in the wake of the modern environmental movement. William Catton and Riley Dunlap's 1978 article proposed a "new ecological paradigm" to replace the dominant paradigm that treated human societies as exempt from nature. Their 1979 review in the *Annual Review of Sociology* defined the field and set its research agenda. Allan Schnaiberg's *The Environment: From Surplus to Scarcity* developed the treadmill of production theory, arguing that economic growth and environmental degradation are linked by the structure of capitalist production.

In the 1980s and 1990s, the field expanded rapidly. Ulrich Beck's *Risk Society* argued that modern societies are increasingly organised around the production and management of risks — nuclear accidents, chemical pollution, climate change — that are global, invisible, and unequally distributed. John Bellamy Foster revived Marx's concept of the metabolic rift, arguing that capitalism disrupts the nutrient cycles between town and country and between humanity and nature. Research on environmental justice, pioneered by Robert Bullard, documented the racial and class patterning of hazard exposure in the United States. In 2000, Paul Crutzen and Eugene Stoermer proposed the term Anthropocene for a new geological epoch defined by human impact on the planet, giving the field a powerful framing for its claim that society and nature can no longer be studied apart. The field has since grown to cover climate change, energy transitions, food systems, and the sociology of disasters, with its own journals, sections, and international networks.

## Key Figures and Ideas

William Catton and Riley Dunlap founded the field by challenging sociology's exemptionalist assumptions and insisting that the environment is a core sociological variable. Their new ecological paradigm remains the field's orienting framework. Allan Schnaiberg supplied its political economy: the treadmill of production explains why growth-oriented economies systematically degrade their environments, and why reforms that do not address the treadmill tend to fail.

Ulrich Beck shifted attention from pollution as a local nuisance to risk as a defining feature of late modernity, arguing that environmental risks undermine the institutions — science, the state, insurance — that are supposed to manage them. John Bellamy Foster connected environmental sociology to Marxist theory through the metabolic rift, showing how capitalist agriculture and urbanisation break the cycles through which societies return nutrients to the soil. Robert Bullard established environmental justice as a research programme and a movement, documenting that hazardous facilities are disproportionately sited in Black and poor communities. Paul Crutzen's Anthropocene concept, though geological in origin, has become a central reference point for sociologists arguing that human societies are now a planetary force.

## Method

Environmental sociology uses surveys of environmental attitudes, ethnographies of affected communities and of environmental movements, historical analysis of resource use and regulation, and comparative studies of national environmental policies. It draws on environmental science data — on emissions, land use, and health — and combines it with sociological analysis of the institutions that produce those patterns. Case studies of disasters, controversies, and conflicts are a staple method, as are studies of organisations, from corporations to regulatory agencies to activist networks.

## Influence and Debates

Environmental sociology has influenced policy and public debate on climate change, pollution, and environmental justice, and it has pushed sociology as a whole to take the material world seriously. Its concepts — the treadmill of production, the metabolic rift, the risk society, environmental justice — are widely used beyond the field.

Debates continue over the causes of environmental degradation: is capitalism the root cause, or do population, technology, and affluence matter as much? Over the prospects for reform: can green technology and ecological modernisation reconcile growth with sustainability, or is a more fundamental transformation required? And over the field's relationship to the natural sciences: how should sociologists use ecological knowledge without reducing social explanation to biology? As climate change accelerates, these debates have moved from the margins of sociology to its centre.
