# Person-Centered Therapy (Rogers)

The most important founding current of the Third Force (humanistic psychology), developed by the American psychologist Carl Rogers from the early 1940s in revolt against the authoritarianism of the psychoanalyst and the expertise of the medical diagnostician, placing its trust in the client's innate "actualizing tendency."

## Historical Context

Rogers first presented his ideas systematically in a lecture at the University of Minnesota in 1940 entitled "Newer Concepts in Psychotherapy," and then elaborated them in his book *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951). Over the decades the current developed from "Nondirective Therapy" to "Client-Centered Therapy" and finally to the "Person-Centered Approach," widening its scope to include education, management, and conflict resolution, not only psychotherapy.

## The Central Thesis

Rogers assumes that every human being has an innate tendency toward growth, maturity, and the realization of latent potential, which he called the "Actualizing Tendency." Psychological disturbance arises when the child is exposed to "Conditions of Worth" from the surroundings, that is, when the child is made to feel that the love and approval of the parents depend on particular behaviors, which leads to an inner split between the real self and the socially imposed ideal self.

## The Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

In his 1957 paper "The Necessary and Sufficient Conditions of Therapeutic Personality Change," Rogers proposed six conditions for therapeutic change. Of these, three are conditions the therapist provides: congruence and authenticity (Congruence), meaning that the therapist is sincerely present without an artificial professional mask; unconditional positive regard (Unconditional Positive Regard), meaning respect for and acceptance of the client as a person of intrinsic worth regardless of behavior; and accurate empathic understanding (Accurate Empathic Understanding), meaning sensing the client's inner world as the client sees it, not as the therapist interprets it from outside. The remaining conditions are that the two people are in psychological contact, that the client is in a state of incongruence (vulnerable or anxious), and that the client at least minimally perceives the therapist's empathy and regard.

## Influence and Extension

Rogers's ideas influenced the development of the Focusing approach by his student Eugene Gendlin, Abraham Maslow and the wider humanistic psychology movement, and the development of Motivational Interviewing techniques used today in addiction treatment.

## Terminological and Institutional Shift

The change of name over the decades reflected a real development in Rogers's ambition. Having begun with the term "Nondirective Therapy" in the 1940s to stress the rejection of therapist direction of session content, he moved to "Client-Centered Therapy" in the 1950s as the core therapist conditions took shape, and then to the "Person-Centered Approach" in the 1970s (the term is prominent in his *A Way of Being*, 1980), when he extended the application of his principles beyond the clinic to education, management, and the facilitation of large groups.
