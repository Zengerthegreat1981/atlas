# Sustainability Sociology

Sustainability sociology studies how societies can meet human needs without undermining the ecological and social conditions on which future generations depend. It examines sustainable development as a social project — its policies, institutions, movements, and contradictions — rather than as a technical problem of resource management. The field asks who defines sustainability, who bears the costs of transition, and whether green growth, degrowth, or ecological modernization can reconcile economic expansion with planetary limits. It is distinct from environmental sociology, its close neighbour: where environmental sociology studies the full range of human–environment relations, including pollution, risk, and the social construction of nature, sustainability sociology focuses specifically on the normative project of sustainable development, on governance for long-term futures, and on the justice of transitions. Sustainability is treated here as a contested political idea, not a settled destination.

## Scope

The field covers the institutions of sustainability: international agreements, national climate policy, corporate sustainability reporting, certification schemes, and city-level transition plans. It studies sustainable consumption — how households, lifestyles, and everyday practices are enrolled in green transitions — and the inequalities of those transitions, since the poor often pay the highest price for environmental policies while enjoying the fewest benefits. It examines environmental justice, asking which communities host waste, bear flood risk, or lose land to conservation. It studies social movements for climate justice, food sovereignty, and degrowth, and the new forms of work and livelihood that green transitions create and destroy.

## History

Sustainability entered sociology through environmental sociology and through development studies. Catton and Dunlap's 1978 call for a new ecological paradigm challenged sociology's assumption that human societies are exempt from ecological constraints, while Allan Schnaiberg's work on the treadmill of production showed how capitalist growth systematically generates environmental harm. The turning point was the Brundtland Commission's *Our Common Future* (1987), which defined sustainable development as development that meets present needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs, and which made sustainability a global policy language. The 1992 Rio Earth Summit institutionalized that language in international agreements. Ulrich Beck's theory of the risk society, published in German in 1986, gave sociologists a framework for understanding how modernization produces hazards — climate change, nuclear risk, chemical pollution — that escape national control. In the 1990s and 2000s, ecological modernization theory argued that capitalism could reform itself through green technology and market instruments, while critics, including John Bellamy Foster's theory of the metabolic rift, argued that such reforms could not resolve the contradiction between accumulation and ecological limits.

## Key Figures

The World Commission on Environment and Development, chaired by Gro Harlem Brundtland, authored the definition of sustainable development that still frames the field. Ulrich Beck theorized the risk society and the cosmopolitan politics of climate change. Riley Dunlap and William Catton founded environmental sociology's ecological paradigm, on which sustainability sociology builds. Allan Schnaiberg's treadmill of production remains the field's central account of why growth degrades environments. John Bellamy Foster revived Marx's concept of the metabolic rift to explain the rupture between society and nature under capitalism. Arthur Mol, working with Gert Spaargaren and others, developed ecological modernization theory, arguing that environmental reform can be built into markets, states, and technology. Anthony Giddens brought climate change into mainstream social theory, arguing that it requires a new politics of the long term. Vandana Shiva and other voices from the Global South insist that sustainability must include food sovereignty, indigenous knowledge, and the rights of communities displaced by development itself.

## Key Works

Catton and Dunlap's "Environmental Sociology: A New Paradigm" (1978) supplied the ecological premise on which sustainability sociology rests. Schnaiberg's *The Environment: From Surplus to Scarcity* (1980) set out the treadmill of production. Beck's *Risk Society: Towards a New Modernity* (1986) theorized manufactured risk as the defining condition of late modernity. The Brundtland Commission's *Our Common Future* (1987) defined sustainable development and launched it as a global project. Foster's "Marx's Theory of Metabolic Rift: Classical Foundations for Environmental Sociology" (1999) reconnected sustainability debates to Marx's analysis of capitalism and nature. Mol's *Globalization and Environmental Reform: The Ecological Modernization of the Global Economy* (2001) presented the strongest case for reformist green modernization. Giddens's *The Politics of Climate Change* (2009) argued that climate policy requires new institutions capable of acting on long-term risks that voters and markets discount.

## Method

Sustainability sociology combines several methods. Policy analysis traces how sustainability goals are translated into indicators, targets, and regulations, and where they are diluted. Comparative case studies contrast national and urban transition strategies, from renewable energy in Denmark to community forestry in Nepal. Survey and interview research examines sustainable consumption, public attitudes to climate policy, and the lived experience of energy transitions. Participatory and action research works with communities designing their own sustainable futures.

## Influence and Debates

Sustainability sociology shaped climate policy, the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals, and the study of just transitions, and it gave social science a language for linking ecological limits to inequality. Its central debate pits ecological modernization against more radical positions: can green technology and market reform deliver sustainability, or does sustainability require degrowth, redistribution, and a break with capitalist accumulation? A third concerns the role of the state versus markets and communities in governing transitions. Current work examines the politics of net zero, the social consequences of renewable energy expansion, the contest over degrowth, and whether the Sustainable Development Goals, adopted in 2015, have changed development practice or merely relabelled it.
