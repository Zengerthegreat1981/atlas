# Ethnomethodological Microsociology

Ethnomethodological microsociology studies how ordinary people produce and recognise social order in everyday life, treating order not as something imposed from above but as an ongoing practical achievement. Its name, coined by Harold Garfinkel, combines ethno, the methods of a people, with methodology: the study of the methods members use to make their actions intelligible, accountable, and orderly. Where conversation analysis, its best-known offshoot, concentrates on the organisation of talk-in-interaction, ethnomethodology is the broader programme: it studies how people do everything from queuing and diagnosing illness to conducting science and following rules, and how the sense of a shared, objective world is assembled moment by moment out of ordinary practical reasoning.

## Scope and Core Concepts

The branch's core concepts begin with the member: not a sociological category but any competent participant in a social setting, whose practical knowledge is the analyst's topic. Indexicality names the dependence of meaning on context — the same words mean different things in different situations, and members work to resolve that ambiguity. Reflexivity describes how accounts of a situation are part of the situation they describe: saying "this is a meeting" helps make it a meeting. Accountability means that actions are produced to be observable and reportable as what they are — a greeting, a complaint, a diagnosis. The documentary method is the way members treat particular instances as documents of an underlying pattern, and the pattern as the sense of the instances. Breaching experiments, in which Garfinkel's students deliberately violated taken-for-granted expectations, revealed the normally invisible work of maintaining order. The branch also studies practical reasoning, the tacit methods by which members decide what to do next, and the phenomenon of the taken-for-granted world itself.

## Historical Development

Ethnomethodology emerged from Garfinkel's dissatisfaction with Talcott Parsons's theory of action, under whom he studied at Harvard. Garfinkel argued that Parsons's actors were cultural dopes, judged by norms they never actually used, and that sociology should study how actors themselves produce order. His programme was announced in *Studies in Ethnomethodology* (1967), a collection of essays including the famous studies of Agnes, the intersex person who passed as a woman, and of jurors deliberating. Harvey Sacks, Garfinkel's student, developed conversation analysis from the same roots, and his *Lectures on Conversation* (1992, published posthumously) became a foundational text. John Heritage's *Garfinkel and Ethnomethodology* (1984) gave the movement its first systematic exposition, and the collection *Structures of Social Action* (1984), edited by J. Maxwell Atkinson and John Heritage, consolidated conversation analysis as a field. Garfinkel's later work, gathered in *Ethnomethodology's Program* (2002), restated the programme's ambitions, while Alain Coulon's *Ethnomethodology* (1995) introduced it to a wider European audience. The programme continues in studies of work, science, medicine, and law.

## Key Figures

Harold Garfinkel (1917–2011) is the founding figure, and his work remains the branch's touchstone. Harvey Sacks (1935–1975) turned ethnomethodology's attention to the fine organisation of conversation, founding conversation analysis. Emanuel Schegloff, Sacks's collaborator, developed the analysis of turn-taking, repair, and sequence organisation. John Heritage is the leading interpreter of Garfinkel and a major conversation analyst in his own right. Aaron Cicourel applied ethnomethodological insights to measurement, juvenile justice, and medical reasoning, showing how official statistics are produced through practical decisions. Don Zimmerman and Melvin Pollner extended the programme into the study of work and organisations, while Anne Warfield Rawls, Garfinkel's editor and interpreter, has argued for his relevance to sociological theory as a whole. In Britain, Wes Sharrock and Rod Watson developed ethnomethodological studies of work and of the philosophy of the social sciences.

## Method

Ethnomethodology's method is radically empirical and resolutely anti-theoretical in the conventional sense: it refuses to explain members' actions by invoking structures, norms, or interests that members themselves do not display. Its primary data are recordings and transcripts of naturally occurring interaction, field notes from ethnographic observation, and documents produced in the course of work. Analysis proceeds by detailed description: showing, turn by turn or step by step, how participants display their understanding of what is happening and how that understanding is ratified or repaired. Breaching experiments, though rarely used today, remain the programme's most famous device for making the invisible visible. The method's strength is its fidelity to the phenomenon: it shows order being made rather than assuming it. Its characteristic criticism is that it brackets out power, history, and inequality, describing the machinery of interaction while saying little about who benefits from it.

## Influence and Debates

The branch's influence is wide but often unacknowledged. Conversation analysis, its most successful offshoot, transformed the study of talk, medicine, law, and media. Ethnomethodological studies of work shaped the design of technology and the study of organisations, and its critique of measurement influenced the sociology of statistics. Its insistence that order is an achievement rather than a given challenged functionalist and structuralist sociology, and its attention to practical reasoning influenced philosophy, linguistics, and human–computer interaction. Debates continue. One concerns the scope of the programme: whether ethnomethodology should remain a descriptive science of members' methods or engage with the critical questions of power and inequality. Another concerns its relationship to conversation analysis: some treat CA as the empirical core of ethnomethodology, others as a separate field that has drifted from Garfinkel's concerns. A third concerns the theory's radicalism: whether the claim that social facts are members' accomplishments leaves any room for sociology to explain what members cannot see.
