# Sociology of Education

The sociology of education studies schools, universities, and other settings of learning as social institutions. It asks how education transmits knowledge and values, how it selects and certifies people for unequal positions, and why educational success remains linked to family background, class, race, and gender even where access has widened.

## Scope

The field covers access and attainment, curricula and the definition of valid knowledge, classroom interaction, teachers' work, school organisation, higher education, and the relation between credentials and labour markets. It also examines state policy, private and religious schooling, school choice, and the global expansion of mass education.

## History

Emile Durkheim, who taught pedagogy alongside sociology, treated education as the methodical socialisation of the young and as a means of sustaining moral solidarity; his lectures appeared posthumously in works such as *Education and Sociology* (1922) and *Moral Education* (1925). Max Weber linked examinations and diplomas to the rise of bureaucracy and to struggles between status groups. After 1945, functionalist writers such as Talcott Parsons analysed the school class as an agency of socialisation and selection. Large surveys then challenged expectations that schooling by itself would equalise life chances: the Coleman Report, *Equality of Educational Opportunity* (1966), found that family background and the social composition of schools were strongly associated with achievement.

## Key Theorists and Works

Pierre Bourdieu and Jean-Claude Passeron, in *The Inheritors* (1964) and *Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture* (1970), argued that schools reward the cultural capital of privileged families while presenting the results as individual merit. Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis, in *Schooling in Capitalist America* (1976), proposed a correspondence between the social relations of schooling and those of the workplace. Basil Bernstein studied how language codes and the organisation of curriculum and pedagogy are related to class. Michael F. D. Young's edited volume *Knowledge and Control* (1971) made the curriculum itself an object of sociological inquiry. Raymond Boudon explained inequality through the choices families make at successive points in schooling. Randall Collins, in *The Credential Society* (1979), argued that rising qualification requirements reflect competition between status groups more than the technical demands of jobs.

## Empirical Examples

Paul Willis's *Learning to Labour* (1977), an ethnography of working-class boys in an English town, showed how their rejection of school culture helped lead them into manual work. James Rosenbaum and others studied tracking and its effects on later opportunities. Annette Lareau's *Unequal Childhoods* (2003) described how middle-class and working-class or poor families follow different child-rearing styles that fit unevenly with school expectations. Comparative research on social mobility, including Shavit and Blossfeld's *Persistent Inequality* (1993), found that in most of the countries studied class differences in educational transitions changed little despite expansion.

## Methods

Researchers use large-scale and longitudinal surveys, statistical models of attainment and mobility, international assessments, classroom ethnography, interviews, and historical analysis of curricula and school systems.

## Debates

Debates concern how far schools reproduce inequality and how far they reduce it, the relative weight of family, school, and neighbourhood, the effects of testing, tracking, and market-style reforms, and the value of expanding higher education when credentials lose scarcity. Later work asks how class combines with race, gender, and migration, and how people resist or reinterpret what schools demand of them.
