# Risk Sociology

Risk sociology studies how societies perceive, produce, distribute, and govern danger. Modern life is organised around risks that earlier societies never faced: nuclear accidents, climate change, financial crises, pandemics, and the side effects of new technologies. The sociology of risk asks who defines a danger as a risk, who bears its costs, and how institutions try to manage what cannot be fully controlled. Its central insight is that risk is not only a technical problem of probability but a social and political one: the same hazard is experienced differently by different groups, and disputes about risk are disputes about power, knowledge, and trust.

## Scope

The field covers the perception of risk, the social distribution of hazards, the institutions of risk management, and the politics of precaution. It studies environmental risks such as pollution and climate change; technological risks such as nuclear power and artificial intelligence; health risks such as pandemics and food safety; and financial and social risks such as unemployment, debt, and the individualisation of welfare. It examines how experts, governments, corporations, and media communicate risk, and how publics respond — with trust, scepticism, or panic. It also studies how the language of risk has spread into everyday life: individuals are now expected to manage their own health, finances, and employability as personal risks. Risk sociology thus connects the study of catastrophic events with the study of ordinary life under conditions of uncertainty.

## History

The field was transformed by Ulrich Beck's *Risk Society: Towards a New Modernity* (1986), which argued that advanced industrial societies had entered a new phase in which the dominant problems were no longer the distribution of wealth but the distribution of manufactured risks. Unlike the hazards of earlier eras, these risks were global, invisible, and incalculable — radiation, chemical pollution, genetic modification — and they affected rich and poor alike, though not equally. Anthony Giddens developed a parallel account in *The Consequences of Modernity* (1990), analysing trust, expertise, and the disembedding of social relations. Mary Douglas and Aaron Wildavsky's *Risk and Culture* (1982) had earlier shown that the selection of risks is cultural: groups attend to the dangers that threaten their way of life and ignore others. Charles Perrow's *Normal Accidents* (1984) argued that in tightly coupled, complex technological systems, accidents are inevitable rather than exceptional. Niklas Luhmann's *Risk: A Sociological Theory* (German 1991; English 1993) distinguished risk, as a consequence of decisions, from danger, as something imposed from outside.

## Key Figures and Works

Beck (1986) gave the field one of its defining theses, and later developed the idea of a "world risk society" in which global risks demand cosmopolitan political responses. Giddens (1990) connected risk to reflexivity and the restructuring of trust in expert systems. Douglas and Wildavsky (1982) established the cultural theory of risk perception, showing how social organisation shapes what counts as dangerous. Perrow (1984) provided the classic analysis of technological accidents. Luhmann (1993) offered a systems-theoretical account of risk and decision. Paul Slovic's *The Perception of Risk* (2000) collected decades of psychological research showing that lay perceptions of risk are shaped by dread, familiarity, and control rather than by statistics alone. More recent work has applied risk sociology to climate change, pandemics, financialisation, and the governance of artificial intelligence.

## Methods

Risk sociology uses surveys of risk perception, interviews, ethnography, discourse analysis, and historical case studies of disasters. Survey research measures how different groups perceive hazards and whom they trust. Ethnography in communities living near hazardous facilities, or in organisations managing risk, reveals how risk is experienced and negotiated in practice. Discourse analysis examines how media and official reports frame dangers and assign blame. Historical case studies — of Chernobyl, the 2008 financial crisis, or the COVID-19 pandemic — trace how risks unfold and how institutions respond. The field also draws on science and technology studies to examine how expert knowledge about risk is produced and contested.

## Influence and Debates

Risk sociology has influenced environmental policy, disaster management, public health, and the regulation of new technologies. Its concepts — the risk society, manufactured risk, the precautionary principle — have entered public debate. Debates in the field are substantial. One concerns Beck's thesis: critics argue that risk has always been central to modernity, that Beck exaggerates the novelty of manufactured risks, and that his account understates how unequally risks are distributed by class and geography. Another concerns expertise: when experts disagree, as they often do about climate change or new technologies, how should societies decide? A third concerns the individualisation of risk: the shift from collective welfare to personal responsibility for health, employment, and security is, critics argue, a political choice presented as a natural fact. The field's enduring contribution is to show that living with risk is not a technical challenge to be solved by better calculation, but a political condition to be governed democratically.
