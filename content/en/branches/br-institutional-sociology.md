# Institutional Sociology

Institutional sociology studies the established rules, norms, and shared understandings that structure social life, and how organisations depend on them. Its central claim is that behaviour and organisational form cannot be explained by technical efficiency or individual interest alone: actors also seek legitimacy and rely on taken-for-granted models of what is appropriate.

## Foundations

Institutions were central to classical sociology, especially in Emile Durkheim's account of social facts and Max Weber's analysis of authority and bureaucracy. Philip Selznick's *TVA and the Grass Roots* (1949) showed how the Tennessee Valley Authority's commitments to local interests, through a process of co-optation, reshaped its goals. This "old" institutionalism emphasised values, informal relations, and the ways organisations become ends in themselves. Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann's *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966) offered a general account of how habitual actions become institutionalised.

## The New Institutionalism

John Meyer and Brian Rowan's 1977 article argued that organisations adopt formal structures that function as myths and ceremonies, signalling conformity to wider expectations, while often decoupling those structures from everyday work. Paul DiMaggio and Walter Powell's "The Iron Cage Revisited" (1983) asked why organisations in a field come to resemble one another. They identified coercive pressures, from law and resource dependence; mimetic pressures, imitation under uncertainty; and normative pressures, linked to professions. Their edited volume of 1991 consolidated the approach, and W. Richard Scott's *Institutions and Organizations* (1995) distinguished regulative, normative, and cultural-cognitive elements of institutions.

## Empirical Examples

Pamela Tolbert and Lynne Zucker's study of civil service reform in American cities (1983) found that local characteristics predicted early adoption better than later adoption, which they related to the growing legitimacy of the reform. Lauren Edelman (1992) examined how organisations responded to ambiguous civil rights law by creating visible structures that demonstrated compliance. Research in schools, hospitals, universities, and corporations has similarly traced the spread of managerial practices.

## Later Developments and Debates

Early new institutional work was criticised for stressing stability and conformity while saying little about interest, power, and change. Responses include research on institutional entrepreneurs, social movements, and institutional work. Roger Friedland and Robert Alford (1991) argued that societies contain several institutional logics, such as those of market, state, family, and religion, which may conflict; Patricia Thornton, William Ocasio, and Michael Lounsbury later developed this perspective. Debates continue over how to measure legitimacy, how actors can change institutions that shape their own understanding, and how sociological institutionalism relates to rational choice and historical institutionalism in economics and political science.
