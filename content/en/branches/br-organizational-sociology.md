# Organizational Sociology

Organizational sociology studies formal organisations, including bureaucracies, corporations, public agencies, parties, and voluntary associations. It examines how they are structured, how people within them actually behave, how they relate to their environments, and what power they exercise over society.

## Classical Foundations

Max Weber described bureaucracy as a form of administration based on written rules, hierarchy, specialised offices, trained officials, and impersonal conduct, and linked it to legal-rational authority. Weber saw it as technically effective but also as a source of constraint. Robert Michels's *Political Parties* (1911) argued that even democratic organisations tend toward oligarchy. Studies at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works, reported by Fritz Roethlisberger and William Dickson in *Management and the Worker* (1939), drew attention to informal groups, although interpretations of the findings have been disputed.

## Bureaucracy in Practice

Mid-century research showed that organisations do not simply follow their charts. Robert Merton's "Bureaucratic Structure and Personality" (1940) described how devotion to rules can displace the goals they serve. Alvin Gouldner's *Patterns of Industrial Bureaucracy* (1954), based on a gypsum mine and plant, distinguished different ways in which rules are imposed, accepted, or ignored. Peter Blau's *The Dynamics of Bureaucracy* (1955) observed informal consultation among officials. Michel Crozier's *The Bureaucratic Phenomenon* analysed French organisations in which control over uncertainty became a source of power.

## Decisions and Environments

James March and Herbert Simon's *Organizations* (1958) emphasised bounded rationality and routines. Tom Burns and G. M. Stalker's *The Management of Innovation* (1961) contrasted "mechanistic" and "organic" systems suited to different conditions. Michael Hannan and John Freeman's population ecology (1977) explained organisational diversity through founding, failure, and environmental selection. Jeffrey Pfeffer and Gerald Salancik's *The External Control of Organizations* (1978) analysed dependence on external resources. New institutional theory, developed by John Meyer, Brian Rowan, Paul DiMaggio, and Walter Powell, stressed legitimacy and imitation.

## Empirical Examples

Rosabeth Moss Kanter's *Men and Women of the Corporation* (1977) showed how positions and numbers shape careers and gender inequality. Charles Perrow's *Normal Accidents* (1984) examined failures in complex, tightly coupled systems. Diane Vaughan's *The Challenger Launch Decision* (1996) explained the 1986 space shuttle disaster through the "normalization of deviance," a gradual acceptance of warning signs as expected and tolerable risk within NASA and its contractors.

## Debates

Debates concern rationality and its limits, the balance between adaptation and environmental selection, and the place of power, class, gender, and race in theories of organisation. Current research examines networks, platforms, algorithmic management, and the changing boundaries of the firm.
