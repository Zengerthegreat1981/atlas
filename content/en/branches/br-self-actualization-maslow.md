# Self-Actualization and Human Motivation (Maslow)

The comprehensive humanistic theoretical framework formulated by Abraham Maslow, founder of the "third force" in psychology, moving beyond the study of neurosis and of animals toward the study of higher human potentials and positive motivation.

## The Central Thesis

Maslow holds that human motives are organized in a hierarchy that runs from basic deficiency needs (D-Needs) to growth and being needs (B-Needs: truth, beauty, justice and unity). The self-actualized person is distinguished by an accurate perception of reality, spontaneity, a focus on external problems rather than on the self, autonomy, and the undergoing of meaning-laden "peak experiences".

## The Hierarchy of Needs

Maslow first presented his famous hierarchy in his landmark article "A Theory of Human Motivation" in 1943, ordering needs from the base to the top: physiological needs (food, water, sleep), then safety needs, then belonging and love needs, then esteem needs (self-respect and the esteem of others), and finally self-actualization at the top. In his later writings Maslow added the level of "Self-Transcendence" above self-actualization, stressing that the highest forms of human growth go beyond concern with the self toward serving an end or value higher than oneself.

## Peak Experiences and Being-Values

Maslow described "Peak Experiences" as fleeting moments of intense awareness of unity and harmony with the cosmos, lived more by self-actualizing people than by others, and usually linked to the perception of what he called "Being-Values" (B-Values) such as truth, beauty, justice, simplicity and playfulness, which Maslow regarded as genuine psychological needs and not merely optional aesthetic values.

## Influence and Critique

Maslow's hierarchy deeply influenced management, education and positive psychology, but it has been subject to methodological criticism for the difficulty of empirically testing its strict hierarchical sequence, since people are observed sacrificing basic needs (safety, food) for higher values (freedom, belief), which sits uneasily with a strict hierarchical order; Maslow himself described the ordering as not rigid.

## Relation to Later Currents

Maslow's model directly paved the way for the founding of humanistic psychology as the "third force" alongside psychoanalysis and behaviorism, and then for the current of Transpersonal Psychology, which Maslow helped launch in his last years as a "fourth force" concerned with spiritual experiences and expanded consciousness, going beyond individual self-actualization toward broader collective and cosmic dimensions.
