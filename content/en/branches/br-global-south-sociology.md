# Global South Sociology

Global South sociology is the study of social life from the standpoint of the Global South — the countries of Latin America, Africa, Asia, and Oceania that were shaped by colonialism and that remain disadvantaged in the global order — and a critique of a sociology built largely from Northern experience. It argues that mainstream sociology has universalized the history of Europe and North America, treating their path to modernity as the model for all societies and their concepts as neutral tools. Global South sociology asks what sociology looks like when it starts from the South: from colonialism, dependency, and resistance rather than from industrialization and secularization as the North experienced them. It is distinct from the concept of the Global South itself, which names a geopolitical position and a set of societies: this branch is the sociological tradition that theorizes from that position, developing Southern epistemologies — ways of knowing rooted in Southern experience — and challenging the division of intellectual labour in which the North theorizes and the South supplies data.

## Scope

The field covers the sociology of development and underdevelopment, dependency, and unequal exchange, asking why global capitalism produces wealth in some places and poverty in others. It studies colonialism and its afterlives: the coloniality of power, knowledge, and being, and the persistence of racial and epistemic hierarchies after formal independence. It examines Southern social movements — landless workers, indigenous movements, feminist and environmental movements — and the forms of knowledge they produce. It covers the sociology of knowledge itself: academic dependency, the dominance of Northern journals and theories, and the marginalization of Southern scholars.

## History

Global South sociology has roots in the anti-colonial thought of the mid-twentieth century and in dependency theory, developed in Latin America in the 1960s and 1970s. Samir Amin's *Unequal Development* (1976) argued that the world economy systematically transfers value from the periphery to the centre. The world-systems analysis of Immanuel Wallerstein, published in 1974, gave that argument a historical framework. In the 1980s and 1990s, Arturo Escobar's critique of development showed how the very idea of development constructed the Third World as a problem to be solved by Northern expertise. Aníbal Quijano's theory of the coloniality of power, formulated in 2000, argued that colonial domination outlived colonialism in the form of racial classification and Eurocentric knowledge. Walter Mignolo developed the idea of decolonial thought and the geopolitics of knowledge. Raewyn Connell's *Southern Theory* (2007) brought the critique into the heart of sociology, showing how the discipline's founding theorists ignored the colonial world and how thinkers from places such as India, Iran, Africa, and Latin America offer alternative foundations. Boaventura de Sousa Santos's *Epistemologies of the South* (2014) argued for an ecology of knowledges in which scientific knowledge coexists with indigenous, popular, and feminist knowledges.

## Key Figures

Samir Amin theorized unequal development and the structural transfer of value from periphery to centre. Aníbal Quijano formulated the coloniality of power, the field's central concept. Walter Mignolo developed decolonial theory and the critique of Eurocentric knowledge. Arturo Escobar, an anthropologist and critic of development, showed how development discourse produces the realities it claims to describe. Raewyn Connell is the field's leading sociological voice, arguing for a sociology that takes Southern theory seriously. Boaventura de Sousa Santos developed the epistemologies of the South and the idea of an ecology of knowledges. Syed Farid Alatas analyzed academic dependency and the captive mind in Southern social science.

## Key Works

Wallerstein's *The Modern World-System: Capitalist Agriculture and the Origins of the European World-Economy in the Sixteenth Century* (1974) supplied the historical framework for understanding global inequality. Amin's *Unequal Development: An Essay on the Social Formations of Peripheral Capitalism* (1976) theorized the mechanisms of unequal exchange. Escobar's *Encountering Development: The Making and Unmaking of the Third World* (1995) is the classic critique of development as discourse and power. Quijano's "Coloniality of Power, Eurocentrism, and Latin America" (2000) formulated the theory of coloniality. Mignolo's *Local Histories/Global Designs: Coloniality, Subaltern Knowledges, and Border Thinking* (2000) developed the decolonial critique of knowledge. Alatas's "Academic Dependency and the Global Division of Labour in the Social Sciences" (2003) analyzed the structure of intellectual inequality. Connell's *Southern Theory: The Global Dynamics of Knowledge in Social Science* (2007) made the case for a sociology grounded in Southern experience, and Santos's *Epistemologies of the South: Justice against Epistemicide* (2014) argued for recognizing and protecting the knowledges of the oppressed.

## Method

Global South sociology is methodologically distinctive. It combines historical and comparative analysis of colonialism, dependency, and global inequality with ethnography of Southern communities and movements. It practices what Santos calls an ecology of knowledges: research that takes indigenous, popular, and activist knowledge seriously as knowledge, not merely as data. It also practices a sociology of sociology — citation analysis, studies of publishing, and institutional analysis — to document how academic dependency works.

## Influence and Debates

Global South sociology has reshaped debates about development, globalization, and the decolonization of the university, and its concepts — coloniality, epistemicide, academic dependency, Southern theory — are now used across the social sciences. It influenced curriculum reform, the decolonize-the-university movement, and the study of global inequality. Critics argue that the field's critique of Northern sociology has outpaced its construction of alternatives, and that Southern sociology must also address the inequalities within Southern societies — of class, gender, caste, and ethnicity — rather than treating the South as a unified victim.
