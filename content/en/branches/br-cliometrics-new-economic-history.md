# Cliometrics and the New Economic History: Measuring the Past with Econometric Tools

**Cliometrics** (from Clio, the muse of history in Greek mythology, and *metrics*, measurement) is a methodological current that has changed the writing of economic history since the late 1950s. It applies formal econometric tools (statistical regression and quantitative modelling) and explicit economic theory to primary historical sources, alongside the qualitative narrative that had dominated economic history.

## The Founding Moment: Williamstown, Purdue and the Railroad Study

Many practitioners date the current to the joint sessions of the Economic History Association and the NBER Conference on Research in Income and Wealth at Williamstown, Massachusetts, in 1957. The label "cliometrics" was coined around 1960 by the mathematical economist Stanley Reiter. The first conference devoted to the new methods met at Purdue University in December 1960, organised by Lance Davis and Jonathan Hughes, and further meetings continued there through the 1960s. The study that did most to establish the field internationally was Robert Fogel's *Railroads and American Economic Growth* (1964). Fogel estimated the "social saving" of railroads: the extra cost of moving freight by canals and wagons had the railroads not been available. For 1890 he put that saving at only a few per cent of national product, much smaller than many narrative histories had assumed, and argued that railroads were not indispensable to American growth. The result opened a long debate about the uses and limits of counterfactual measurement.

## The American Slavery Study: A Sensitive and Controversial Example

Robert Fogel, together with Stanley Engerman, produced a quantitative study of the economics of slavery in the American South in *Time on the Cross* (1974), which proved intensely controversial. Using quantitative data from plantation records, they sought to refute a common historical narrative that described slavery as an economically inefficient system that would have collapsed on its own without the Civil War. The study provoked intense ethical and methodological controversy. Critics charged that it appeared to "evaluate the efficiency" of a criminal system in isolation from its human brutality. The debate illustrates the limits of the supposed ethical neutrality of purely quantitative analysis when applied to subjects that are deeply morally charged.

## Counterfactual Methodology and Opportunity-Cost Accounting

The core of the cliometric method is building explicitly and quantitatively defined "counterfactual" scenarios: what would have happened economically had a particular historical event not occurred? This distinguishes it from traditional descriptive history, which usually limits itself to describing what actually happened without a precise quantitative estimate of what could have happened instead. The approach relies on explicit economic theoretical assumptions (such as partial-equilibrium cost comparisons) to estimate these alternative scenarios. This makes it a powerful tool for testing causal historical hypotheses more precisely, but it remains open to criticism about how realistic the theoretical assumptions used to construct the hypothetical alternative world are.

## Applications to Ottoman and Arab Economic History

Cliometric tools were later applied, though more limitedly because of the scarcity of reliable quantitative data, to aspects of Ottoman and Arab economic history. These include estimating the volume of Ottoman trade from historical customs records, analysing quantitatively the effect of the opening of the Suez Canal (1869) on patterns of international and regional trade, and attempts to estimate historical gross domestic product for Ottoman regions from partial available tax data. This sub-field remains less data-mature than its European or American counterpart, because of large gaps in the systematic documentation of historical quantitative records in many Arab and Ottoman archives.

## Criticism of the Limits of the Quantitative Approach

This current faces continuing criticism from traditional economic historians. They warn that quantitative reduction may strip historical analysis of rich institutional and cultural context that is difficult to translate into numbers (such as social relations, values and informal political power). They also argue that the quality of any cliometric result depends entirely on the quality of the underlying primary historical data, which remains incomplete or biased in many non-Western contexts in particular. This calls for additional methodological caution when generalising quantitative results drawn from data-rich contexts (such as North America) to contexts that are less quantitatively documented, such as the Arab region.

## The Nobel Prize of 1993 and the Formal Recognition of the Field

Recognition by the economics profession was marked in 1993, when the Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences in Memory of Alfred Nobel was shared by Robert Fogel and Douglass North "for having renewed research in economic history by applying economic theory and quantitative methods in order to explain economic and institutional change". The award joined two strands: Fogel's quantitative counterfactuals, and North's account of institutions, including property rights and legal rules, in long-run growth. That institutional strand also made North a principal figure in the New Institutional Economics.

## The Debate over "Why Did China Not Industrialise First?"

Cliometric tools have been used to address one of the greatest puzzles in world economic history: why Western Europe experienced the Industrial Revolution first, even though China was technologically and economically at a comparable or more advanced level until the eighteenth century. In *The Great Divergence* (2000), the economic historian Kenneth Pomeranz offered a comparative historical analysis, drawing on quantitative estimates, that attributes the difference partly to Europe's access to abundant coal resources and American colonies, which allowed it to overcome the constraints of traditional agricultural land. This cross-civilisational comparison inspires researchers in Arab and Islamic economic history to attempt similar explanations of the developmental gap between the Arab region and Western Europe, after centuries of relative early Arab-Islamic advancement.

## The Limits of Data in the Arab and Ottoman Archive

Applying cliometrics to Arab economic history faces specific data challenges that go beyond those usual in any quantitative historical research. While European archives hold systematic and temporally continuous tax and trade records spanning several centuries, the Ottoman and Arab records available in digital form and in a state suitable for systematic quantitative analysis are more scattered and less complete. Yet there are genuinely rich archives (such as the Ottoman Sharia court records) that have not yet been fully exploited in large-scale systematic cliometric projects. This represents a promising future research opportunity, not yet exploited sufficiently to understand Arab economic history with rigorous quantitative tools comparable to those applied in other contexts.

## Its Continuing Methodological Importance for Contemporary Political Economy

This current remains an important methodological reference for any contemporary attempt to understand the deep historical roots of developmental inequality between countries and regions. It provides tools for distinguishing genuine causal explanations from accidental historical coincidences, by building quantitatively rigorous counterfactual scenarios instead of relying on a persuasive narrative that has not been statistically tested with sufficient precision. The method continues to develop as broader digital historical data and more powerful computing tools become available, allowing the analysis of very large historical data sets that were not accessible to the founding generation of this field in the 1950s and 1960s.
