# New Economic Geography: Industrial Clustering and Increasing Returns in Space

A current within neoclassical economics, launched by Paul Krugman with his influential 1991 article "Increasing Returns and Economic Geography." In it, Krugman reformulated an old question raised by Alfred Marshall in the nineteenth century about "industrial districts" (why particular industries cluster geographically in specific regions rather than spreading evenly across space), using rigorous modern mathematical tools amenable to quantitative modelling. For decades this question had belonged to descriptive, non-mathematical economic geography, separated from the prevailing neoclassical theories of trade and growth.

## The clustering model: increasing returns and transport costs

Krugman built a mathematical model combining three elements: increasing returns at the level of the individual plant (unit cost falls as the volume of production rises, which pushes firms to concentrate in a few locations rather than spread out); transport costs for goods and inputs between locations (which push firms to locate near large markets and suppliers to reduce these costs); and the mobility of manufacturing workers between regions in response to real wages. The interaction of these three forces produces a dynamic of **circular causation**: the clustering of industries in one region attracts additional workers and suppliers, which lowers costs further and attracts more industries, so that a dense industrial "core" forms against less industrialized "peripheries." This outcome can emerge from an abstract mathematical model without assuming any initial natural geographic advantage of the winning region. The initial advantage may be purely historical and accidental, and then be reinforced by itself.

## The core–periphery pattern and regional development

This framework offered a coherent mathematical explanation of core–periphery development patterns that resemble, in their apparent description (though not necessarily in their causal explanation), patterns discussed by the dependency and world-system literatures from a radically different angle (political-historical rather than mathematical-abstract). It has influenced debates on regional development policy about when government intervention to break a historical geographic "lock-in" is economically justified (if one region's advantage is accidentally historical rather than the product of a real lasting advantage) and when it is an ineffective intervention running against natural market forces.

## Academic recognition and criticism

Krugman was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2008, partly in recognition of this work (the prize citation referred to his analysis of trade patterns and the location of economic activity). The current, however, faced criticism from traditional economic geographers, who regarded its models as overly simplified and abstract compared with the institutional, historical and political complexity that actually governs the real geographic distribution of economic activity (such as local institutions, the historical policies of the state, and relations of power). They argued that reducing economic geography to mathematical equations about transport costs and increasing returns alone neglects essential factors that cannot be modelled directly in mathematics.

## An application to rapid Arab urbanization

The tools of the new economic geography offer a possible analytical lens for understanding the excessive urban concentration in some Arab capitals (Greater Cairo, Beirut, Amman), which attract a very high share of national economic and population activity compared with secondary cities. This concentration can be partly explained by the very dynamics of increasing returns and circular causation (the concentration of job opportunities attracts migrants, which expands the local market and attracts more investment). In the Arab case, however, additional political and historical factors intertwine (the administrative centralization of the state, and the legacy of colonial-era planning) that the abstract mathematical model alone does not capture.

## Balanced regional development policies

The conclusions of the new economic geography have also been used to evaluate "regional rebalancing" policies that some governments attempt to adopt (such as building new cities or industrial zones outside the capital to relieve congestion). The models suggest that agglomeration forces are cumulative and can lock in an existing core, so that a new centre may need to reach a sufficient scale (adequate infrastructure, an initial local market) before it can compete with it. This partly explains why many government "new city" projects have struggled to achieve real economic independence from the existing major urban centres, despite sometimes massive government investment in them.
