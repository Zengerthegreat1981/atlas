# Political Sociology

Political sociology studies the relations between society and politics: how power is distributed and exercised, how states form and act, and how classes, organisations, movements, and identities shape political life. Its scope extends beyond elections and government to the social conditions that determine which interests are organised and which issues become political.

## Classical Foundations

Karl Marx linked state power to class relations. Max Weber, in "Politics as a Vocation" (1919), defined the state by its successful claim to a monopoly of legitimate physical force within a territory, and distinguished traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational authority. Robert Michels's *Political Parties* (1911) argued for an "iron law of oligarchy" in organisations. Antonio Gramsci's prison writings used the concept of hegemony to analyse rule through consent as well as coercion.

## Debates on Power

In the United States, C. Wright Mills's *The Power Elite* (1956) argued that leaders of corporate, military, and political institutions formed an interconnected elite. Robert Dahl's *Who Governs?* (1961), a study of New Haven, found that influence was spread among different groups across issue areas, supporting a pluralist account. Peter Bachrach and Morton Baratz (1962) replied that power also operates by keeping issues off the agenda. Steven Lukes's *Power: A Radical View* (1974) added a third dimension: the shaping of wants and perceptions.

## Parties, States, and Movements

Seymour Martin Lipset's *Political Man* (1960) examined the social bases of democracy and voting. Lipset and Stein Rokkan (1967) traced European party systems to historical cleavages such as centre against periphery, church against state, and owners against workers. Theda Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) and Charles Tilly's work on state formation treated states as organisations shaped by war, finance, and class conflict. Michael Mann distinguished ideological, economic, military, and political sources of power.

## Empirical Examples

Doug McAdam's *Political Process and the Development of Black Insurgency* (1982) explained the American civil rights movement through changing political opportunities, organisational strength, and shared perceptions that change was possible. Gosta Esping-Andersen's *The Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism* (1990) compared liberal, conservative, and social democratic welfare regimes and related them to class coalitions. Research on voting examines how class, religion, education, gender, and region affect political alignment.

## Current Debates

Current questions include the decline or transformation of class voting, the rise of populist and nationalist movements, democratic erosion, the effects of digital media on mobilisation, and the power of corporations and transnational institutions. Political sociologists also debate how far states act autonomously and how culture and identity should be related to material interests.
