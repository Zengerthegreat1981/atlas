# Frankl's First-Person Account of Meaning in the Camp

Viktor Frankl's account of his years in the Nazi concentration camps is not a history of those camps. It is a description of what it was like, from the inside, to go on finding meaning when nearly everything that makes a life liveable had been taken away. First published in German in 1946 and in English in 1959 (as *From Death-Camp to Existentialism*, later *Man's Search for Meaning*), the book is the raw material on which Frankl built logotherapy — and a clear practical example of the phenomenological method: describe experience as it is lived, before theorising about it.

## The Difference from the Historical Event

The companion entry on the camps themselves (evt-frankl-camps) records the facts: when Frankl was deported, which camps he was sent to, what happened there. This entry records something else — the texture of the experience as Frankl himself described it. The distinction matters because the two kinds of knowledge answer different questions. History tells us what was done to the prisoners; Frankl's first-person account tells us what a prisoner could still do within himself while it was being done. That inner record is what makes this entry the foundation of an entire therapeutic school rather than a memoir.

Frankl was careful about the status of his account: it was a witness's report, not a scientific study, and he warned against generalising from his own survival. What he offered was what he noticed, in himself and in his fellow prisoners, about how human beings endure — and sometimes fail to endure — extreme suffering.

## Remembering His Wife

The most famous passage in the book describes a forced march at dawn, in bitter cold, when Frankl's thoughts turned to his wife, Tilly. He did not know whether she was alive — she was not; she died in Bergen-Belsen — but in that moment, he writes, the image of her face filled his mind so completely that the cold, the hunger, and the guards fell away. "Love," he concluded, "is the only way to grasp another human being in the innermost core of his personality" (*Man's Search for Meaning*, Part One).

The passage is often quoted sentimentally, but its philosophical weight is precise: love was not a consolation prize for a man who had lost everything else; it was a way of knowing. In remembering his wife, he was not escaping reality but reaching the deepest reality available to him: the person he loved remained present to him as a meaning, regardless of whether she was physically alive.

## The Last Freedom

From such observations Frankl drew the principle that became the centre of logotherapy: "Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one's own way" (*Man's Search for Meaning*, Part One). The guards controlled his body, his movements, his food, and his sleep. They could not control the stance he took towards what was happening to him. Frankl noticed that prisoners who kept some task before them — a manuscript to finish, a child waiting, a loved face held in memory — were better able to get up each morning than those who had lost every attachment to the future.

He summed up the principle with a line from Nietzsche: "He who has a why to live can bear almost any how" (Friedrich Nietzsche, *Twilight of the Idols*, "Maxims and Arrows", §12; quoted by Frankl in *Man's Search for Meaning*). Frankl treated the line as a daily test: could he find a "why" that would carry him through this particular day?

## The Three Avenues of Meaning — Tested, Not Invented

Frankl held that meaning can be found in three ways: through creative work or a deed, through experiencing something or encountering someone in love, and through the attitude we take towards unavoidable suffering. A common misunderstanding is that he invented this scheme in the camps. He did not. He had begun formulating it in the late 1920s, and he carried a complete manuscript of his first book on the subject, *Ärztliche Seelsorge*, sewn into the lining of his coat when he was deported in 1942. The manuscript was confiscated and destroyed in the camp.

What the camp did was test the scheme under the harshest possible conditions. With no work available and no loved ones beside him, only the third avenue remained: the attitude towards suffering itself. The classification thus changed, in Frankl's own account, from a desk hypothesis into lived certainty. After his liberation in 1945, he rewrote the confiscated manuscript and published it in German in 1946, and later in English as *The Doctor and the Soul* (1955), founding logotherapy on the claim that the "will to meaning" is the primary human drive — not Freud's will to pleasure, and not Adler's will to power.

## Why Phenomenology Needs This Account

This entry is the Atlas's working example of the phenomenological tradition associated with Husserl (ctx-phenomenology-husserl): description of experience as lived, before any theorising about it. Frankl did not arrive at his theory of meaning by deduction and then illustrate it with camp stories. He described what he lived, noticed what the descriptions had in common, and only then named the pattern. The method is the message: if you want to know what meaning is, you must begin with someone's actual experience of finding it, or losing it.

That is also why the account has therapeutic force. A therapist working existentially does not hand a client Frankl's conclusions as doctrines. The conclusions matter because they were earned inside an experience the reader can recognise — the discovery that even when nothing can be changed about a situation, something can still be chosen about one's stance towards it.
