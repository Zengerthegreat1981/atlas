# Bourdieu's Habitus: Between Philosophy and Empirical Sociology

The concept of **habitus** in Pierre Bourdieu is a model case of a concept born as a solution to an abstract philosophical dilemma, the duality of structure and agency, which then became a densely used tool of empirical sociological analysis. The word is Latin (*habitus*), a translation of Aristotle's term *hexis*, meaning a stable acquired state. It passed through Thomas Aquinas, through Marcel Mauss, who spoke of "techniques of the body" (lecture of 1934, published 1935), and through Erwin Panofsky, whom Bourdieu translated into French in 1967, writing a postface for the translation.

## The Origin in the Islamic Heritage: Malaka and Custom

In Arabic, Ibn Khaldun anticipated a very close idea. In the *Muqaddimah* (1377) he used the concept of *malaka* ("faculty" or "acquired disposition"), a firmly established quality of the soul that forms through repetition of an act until it becomes like a second nature, and on this basis he spoke of the faculty of language, the faculty of craft, and the faculty of knowledge. A line often quoted from the *Muqaddimah* expresses a close idea: that the human being is the child of his customs and habits more than of his nature and temperament. In addition, Bourdieu conducted his earliest studies in Algeria, including studies of Kabyle Amazigh society and of workers, and there he formulated his ideas about deeply rooted dispositions confronting the market economy. There is no published evidence that Bourdieu was influenced by Ibn Khaldun. The kinship between the two concepts is one of structure, not of historical lineage.

## The Two Angles

The philosophical angle presents habitus as a theoretical solution to a prior duality: French structuralism (Lévi-Strauss), which reduces individual action to an objective, determining structure, against existential phenomenology (Sartre), which over-extends the freedom of the conscious agent. In *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (French 1972, English 1977), and then in *The Logic of Practice* (1980), Bourdieu set out habitus as a "structured structure predisposed to function as a structuring structure." It is the product of a social history inscribed in the body, and it produces new practices. The sociological angle applies the concept to studying how aesthetic taste and cultural consumption are shaped by class in *Distinction* (1979), and how the educational institution reproduces class inequality across generations without direct material coercion.

## The Actual Relation

The empirical use cannot be separated from its theoretical roots. The systematic aim of going beyond the duality of structure and agency is what justifies analysing the choice of music, furniture, and clothing as an unconscious expression of an embodied social history, rather than as random individual preferences or as the product of a mechanical class structure. In *An Invitation to Reflexive Sociology* (1992), Bourdieu and Loïc Wacquant offer a later methodological formulation that links habitus to the field and to capital.

## Criticisms

Critics, among them Richard Jenkins in *Pierre Bourdieu* (1992), argue that the concept tends toward determinism, since it struggles to explain individual change and surprise. Bernard Lahire, in *The Plural Man* (1998), offered an empirical critique: individuals carry multiple and sometimes contradictory dispositions, produced by socialisation in different contexts, so that a single homogeneous habitus is not sufficient. The difficulty of measurement also remains: habitus is inferred from practices and is not observed directly, so the inference runs in an explanatory circle.
