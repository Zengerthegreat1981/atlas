# Family Systems Therapy and Behaviorism: A Quiet Integration

The two traditions are theoretically distant: behavioral approaches explain conduct by learning history and contingencies of reinforcement, while family systems approaches explain symptoms as part of patterns of interaction within a relational whole. In clinical practice, however, they have been combined since the 1970s in a number of evidence-based models for families. Many of these hybrids do not describe themselves as a union of the two schools.

## Starting Point: Different Questions (1950s to 1970s)

### Behavior therapy and the individual
- Wolpe's *Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition* (1958) and Eysenck's edited volume *Behaviour Therapy and the Neuroses* (1960) established behavior therapy as an approach to the individual's anxiety and related problems.
- Early behavior therapy treated the family largely as part of the environment of the person being treated, in the sense of a source of stimuli and reinforcement.

### Family systems theory
- **Bateson and colleagues (1956)** proposed the double-bind hypothesis in "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia," treating symptoms as communication within families.
- **Minuchin** developed structural family therapy (*Families of the Slums*, 1967; *Families and Family Therapy*, 1974), with boundaries, subsystems and hierarchy as central concepts.
- **Bowen** developed family systems theory with differentiation of self at its core (*Family Therapy in Clinical Practice*, 1978).

The questions differ: behavior therapy asks which behavior is to change and what maintains it; systemic therapy asks which pattern of interaction maintains the problem and how the family structure might change.

## The Intersection: Behavioral and Systemic Family Models (1970s Onward)

### Behavioral family and couple therapy
- **Robert Liberman** published an early behavioral approach to family and couple therapy (1970) and later worked on family and skills-training interventions for schizophrenia.
- **Richard Stuart (1969)** and **Neil Jacobson and Gayla Margolin (1979)** developed behavioral marital therapy using social learning and behavior-exchange principles; the couple is analyzed as a system of mutual reinforcement.

### Functional Family Therapy (FFT)
- **James Alexander and Bruce Parsons (1973)** reported a short-term behavioral family intervention with delinquent families, which developed into functional family therapy.
- FFT combines a systemic view of the function that problem behavior serves for family members with behavioral techniques and attention to attributions. It is classed as an evidence-based program for adolescent offending in several reviews.

### Parent Management Training
- **Gerald Patterson** (1926-2016) and colleagues at the Oregon Social Learning Center developed parent training from social learning and operant principles (*Families*, 1971; *Coercive Family Process*, 1982).
- Patterson's analysis of coercive cycles between parent and child describes a repeating interaction pattern, which is the most direct example of a behavioral account that is also systemic in form.
- Parent management training is one of the best-supported treatments for child conduct problems.

### Multisystemic Therapy (MST)
- **Scott Henggeler** and colleagues developed MST from the late 1970s and 1980s, drawing on family systems theory and Bronfenbrenner's social ecology, with behavioral and cognitive-behavioral techniques.
- It targets serious antisocial behavior in adolescents by working with family, school, peers and neighborhood.

### Multidimensional Family Therapy (MDFT)
- **Howard Liddle** developed MDFT, which integrates family systems ideas with developmental and cognitive-behavioral elements, particularly for adolescent substance use.

### Brief Strategic Family Therapy (BSFT)
- **José Szapocznik** and colleagues developed BSFT, which combines structural and strategic family therapy. It is mainly systemic rather than behavioral.

### Family adaptations of other approaches
- Dialectical behavior therapy for adolescents incorporates multifamily skills groups (Miller, Rathus and Linehan, 2007).

## Core Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Behaviorism | Family systems therapy |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | The individual's behavior and its contingencies | The family as a relational system |
| Theory of change | Reinforcement, extinction, shaping, skills training | Restructuring, altering interaction patterns, reframing |
| Language | Antecedents, behavior, consequences | Boundaries, triangles, alliances, circularity |
| Goal | Modify target behaviors | Change the pattern that maintains the problem |
| View of causation | Often linear (contingency leads to behavior) | Circular (mutual influence) |
| Research tradition | Experimental and outcome research | Mostly clinical, with later outcome research |

## Points of Convergence

1. **Present focus:** both attend more to current interaction than to early history.
2. **Practical and goal-directed:** both usually set explicit goals.
3. **Skill teaching:** parent and couple training teaches specific interaction skills.
4. **Less emphasis on unconscious interpretation** than psychoanalytic work.
5. **Outcome research:** several hybrid models (FFT, MST, MDFT, parent training) have been tested in randomized trials.

## Chains of Influence

- **Operant and social learning theory** (Skinner, Bandura) provide the learning concepts behind parent training and behavioral couple therapy.
- **Bronfenbrenner's ecological theory** and family systems thinking inform MST.
- **Structural family therapy** influences several models that address family organization (for example MDFT and BSFT); direct borrowing from Minuchin by FFT should not be assumed without a citation.

## Philosophical Debate

- **Is "structure" an independent reality or a stable behavioral pattern?** For Minuchin, structure is a real organizing feature of the family. A behavioral analyst would describe it as regularities in interaction.
- **Must meaning be understood for change to occur?** Systemic therapists generally emphasize the function and meaning of symptoms for the family; behavioral therapists emphasize observable contingencies. Hybrid models often do both.

## The Contemporary Situation

- **Practice:** much family therapy today is eclectic, using systemic formulation alongside behavioral and cognitive techniques.
- **Evidence:** among the best-supported family-based treatments are parent management training, functional family therapy, multisystemic therapy and multidimensional family therapy. These are hybrid or behavioral in character. Evidence for some classical structural and Bowen-style therapies is more limited, and this reflects the amount of research done as well as effectiveness.
- **Family psychoeducation for schizophrenia** (for example McFarlane et al., 1995, on multiple-family groups) is associated with lower relapse rates alongside medication. It is not a purely behavioral intervention.

## Clinical Differences

- **Purely systemic orientations** (structural, Bowenian, strategic): often used for complex family conflict and relational problems.
- **Behavioral family approaches** (parent training, behavioral couple therapy): used for specific behaviors in children and adolescents.
- **Integrative models** (FFT, MST, MDFT): used for adolescent conduct and substance problems.
These are descriptive categories and not treatment recommendations.

## Summary

Contemporary family therapy draws structural and pattern ideas from the systems tradition and reinforcement, skills training and observational learning from the behavioral tradition. The integration has been gradual and pragmatic. It reflects the view that a family's difficulties involve behavior, thought and relationship together, so that neither tradition alone is sufficient.
