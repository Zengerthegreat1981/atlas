# Integrative Therapy (Common Factors) and CBT: A Challenge to the Specificity Claim

The common factors perspective, developed by researchers such as Jerome Frank, Michael Lambert, John Norcross, and Bruce Wampold, challenges the view that the superiority of a particular therapy such as CBT lies in its specific techniques. It holds that psychotherapies are broadly effective, with small differences between bona fide treatments, and that shared elements (the relationship, expectations, a credible rationale) carry much of the benefit. CBT proponents respond that specific techniques matter for particular disorders and that CBT has the strongest evidence base for many conditions. This entry compares the two positions; no direct exchange between Aaron Beck and the common factors theorists is used here.

## Starting Point: The "Dodo Bird Verdict"

- **Saul Rosenzweig** (1907-2004), in "Some Implicit Common Factors in Diverse Methods of Psychotherapy" (*American Journal of Orthopsychiatry*, 1936), argued that different therapies work through shared factors and quoted the Dodo of *Alice in Wonderland*: "Everybody has won and all must have prizes."
- **Luborsky, Singer, and Luborsky (1975)** later named the equivalence finding the "Dodo bird verdict" on the basis of a comparative review.
- Subsequent meta-analyses have produced both findings of near-equivalence and findings of modest differences.

## Lambert (1992): Proportions of Change

Michael Lambert (1992), reviewing outcome research, proposed an approximate allocation of improvement in psychotherapy:

- **Extratherapeutic factors** (client characteristics, life events, social support): about 40%
- **Common factors** (including the therapeutic relationship): about 30%
- **Expectancy and placebo effects:** about 15%
- **Specific techniques:** about 15%

These percentages are a heuristic synthesis, not precise estimates, and have been criticized and qualified by later researchers. They should not be read as a decomposition of a single study.

## Wampold and the Contextual Model

Bruce Wampold, in *The Great Psychotherapy Debate* (2001; 2nd ed. with Zac Imel, 2015), contrasts:

1. The **medical model**: specific ingredients of a treatment, tied to a diagnosis, account for its benefits.
2. The **contextual model**: benefits arise through the relationship, expectations created by a credible explanation and treatment, and the client's engagement in health-promoting actions; specific ingredients matter mainly as part of a coherent, believable treatment.

Wampold reads the evidence as showing that bona fide therapies produce similar outcomes, that therapist effects and the alliance explain more outcome variance than differences between treatments, and that evidence for specific ingredients is limited. Therapies as a group show large effects relative to no treatment (typically around 0.8 standardized units). Meta-analytic work on the alliance finds a modest but robust association with outcome (a correlation of roughly .28; Flückiger and colleagues, 2018).

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | CBT | Common factors / integrative |
|---|---|---|
| Basic assumption | Specific techniques are key mechanisms | Shared factors account for much of the effect |
| Treatment role | Protocol-driven, disorder-specific | Relationship and context are central, technique supportive |
| Research emphasis | Randomized trials of specific protocols | Process-outcome, therapist effects, and alliance research |
| Therapist | Skilled in delivering a protocol | Skilled in building the relationship and credible rationale |

## Points of Convergence

1. Neither denies that CBT is effective.
2. Both are empirical and use outcome research.
3. CBT texts emphasize the therapeutic relationship (Beck's "collaborative empiricism"), and common factors theorists accept that techniques are part of a credible treatment.
4. Most clinicians integrate elements of both.

## The Response from CBT Researchers

- Meta-analysts such as Tolin (2010) found CBT modestly superior to other therapies in some comparisons, though Cuijpers and others have found differences between bona fide psychotherapies for depression to be small.
- Researchers such as Steven Hollon emphasize that specific treatments have demonstrated efficacy for particular disorders and that the relationship matters while technique adds to it.
- Norcross and colleagues (2011; Norcross and Lambert, 2019) reviewed evidence on therapy relationship elements, including alliance, empathy, and goal consensus, as effective contributors to outcome through task-force reports of APA Division 29.

## The Four Routes to Integration

Norcross and Goldfried's *Handbook of Psychotherapy Integration* describes:

- **Common factors**: identifying shared change principles.
- **Technical eclecticism**: choosing techniques from different schools by evidence and fit.
- **Theoretical integration**: combining theories into a new framework.
- **Assimilative integration**: incorporating techniques into a primary theoretical home.

## Limits

Evidence on relative efficacy differs by disorder (for example, strong support for exposure-based CBT in anxiety disorders and OCD), so general claims of equivalence should be made with care. The common factors argument does not show that techniques are irrelevant, only that their specific contribution is hard to separate from context.
