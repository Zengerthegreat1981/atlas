# Family Systems Therapy and Psychoanalysis: Early Rejection and Later Bridges

Family systems therapy arose in the 1950s partly in reaction to psychoanalysis. Its central unit is the family or relational system, whereas classical psychoanalysis worked with the individual patient and his or her inner world. Yet several founders of family therapy were psychoanalytically trained, and from the late 1950s onward analytically oriented family and couple therapies developed, so the relationship is one of early rivalry followed by partial integration.

## Starting Point: Distance from Psychoanalysis (1950s to 1970s)

### Bateson and the Palo Alto Group
- **Gregory Bateson** (1904-1980) was an anthropologist and cyberneticist. His work with Margaret Mead (*Balinese Character*, 1942) was informed by psychoanalytic ideas, but his later communication research took a cybernetic and systemic direction.
- The Bateson project in Palo Alto (funded from 1952) studied communication in schizophrenia, with Jay Haley, John Weakland and William Fry; Don Jackson joined as a consultant. The Mental Research Institute (MRI) was founded in 1958 by Don Jackson; Haley, Weakland, Virginia Satir and, later, Paul Watzlawick are associated with it.
- In 1956 the group proposed the **double-bind** hypothesis ("Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia"), describing contradictory communication within relationships as a context for schizophrenic communication. It was an influential hypothesis, but it has not been supported as a cause of schizophrenia.

### Minuchin
- **Salvador Minuchin** (1921-2017), born in Argentina, developed structural family therapy at the Wiltwyck School and then the Philadelphia Child Guidance Clinic (*Families of the Slums*, 1967; *Families and Family Therapy*, 1974).
- His central concepts are structure, boundaries, subsystems and hierarchy. His method emphasizes active intervention in family interaction rather than interpretation of individual history.

### Bowen
- **Murray Bowen** (1913-1990), an American psychiatrist trained in psychoanalytic psychiatry at the Menninger Clinic, began with research on mother-patient pairs in schizophrenia and developed family systems theory, with differentiation of self as its core concept (*The Use of Family Theory in Clinical Practice*, 1966; *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice*, 1978).
- Bowen's concepts include the family projection process and multigenerational transmission. He moved away from psychoanalytic practice, but his theory preserves ideas of emotional development and anxiety.

## Core Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Psychoanalysis | Family systems therapy |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | The individual | The family or relational system |
| Account of suffering | Unconscious conflict, early relationships | Interaction patterns, boundaries, triangles, multigenerational processes |
| Therapist's position | Analyst interpreting transference | Active participant in the family system |
| Confidentiality | Individual | Shaped by the family format (secrets are handled differently) |
| Technique | Free association, interpretation | Restructuring, enactment, mapping, reframing |
| Metaphor | Intrapsychic depth | Systems and cybernetics |
| Duration | Often long-term | Frequently shorter; varies by model |

## Points of Convergence

1. **Context:** psychoanalysis itself treats the individual as shaped by early family relationships. Freud's treatment of "Little Hans" (1909) took place mainly through the child's father, which some historians regard as a precursor of family work.
2. **Recurring patterns:** psychoanalysis speaks of repetition and defensive patterns, systems theory of interaction patterns. The terms differ and the theories are not identical, but they share an interest in recurrence.
3. **Non-genetic understanding of distress:** both view psychological suffering as arising partly from relationships.
4. **Attention to the past:** psychoanalysis focuses on childhood; Bowen and others on family history across generations.
5. **Unspoken communication:** psychoanalysis attends to the unconscious; systemic therapy to metacommunication and unspoken rules.

## Analytic and Dynamic Bridges (1950s Onward)

1. **Nathan Ackerman** (1908-1971), a child psychiatrist and psychoanalyst, is a founding figure of family therapy in the United States. His *The Psychodynamics of Family Life* (1958) combined psychoanalytic understanding with family interviewing. His Family Institute in New York (founded 1960) was renamed the Ackerman Institute in 1971.
2. **Lidz, Wynne and others** studied family interaction in schizophrenia in the 1950s (Theodore Lidz's concepts of marital schism and skew; Lyman Wynne's pseudomutuality), often from a psychodynamic base.
3. **Henry Dicks** at the Tavistock Clinic (*Marital Tensions*, 1967) developed an object-relations view of marital interaction.
4. **Ivan Boszormenyi-Nagy** (1920-2007) developed contextual therapy (*Invisible Loyalties*, with Geraldine Spark, 1973; *Foundations of Contextual Therapy*, 1987), in which unconscious loyalties across generations shape family life.
5. **David and Jill Scharff** developed object relations family therapy (1987) and object relations couple therapy (1991), in which family and partners are understood through projective identification and shared unconscious fantasy.
6. **August Aichhorn** (1878-1949), in *Wayward Youth* (1925), applied psychoanalytic ideas to delinquent youth in institutional care. This was not family therapy, but it is an early example of psychoanalysis attending to the social environment.

## Chains of Influence

- **Psychoanalysis to Bowen and Ackerman:** direct, since both were psychoanalytically trained.
- **Cybernetics and communication theory to Bateson and the Palo Alto group:** the group's key framework, not psychoanalysis.
- **Object relations theory to Dicks and the Scharffs:** direct.
- **Minuchin:** his method is largely independent of psychoanalytic technique; he developed structural ideas from clinical work with disadvantaged families.

## Philosophical Debate

- **Is the family a real system or a psychic construction?** Systems theory treats family patterns as observable regularities; psychoanalysis attends also to psychic reality, the internal representations that individuals carry. The two accounts address different levels and are not identical.
- **How are family secrets handled?** Systemic therapists address secrets in the open structure of communication; analytic therapists attend to what is unconscious or defended. Pincus and Dare's *Secrets in the Family* (1978) is an analytic treatment.
- **Can an individual change without the family?** Systems approaches emphasize the family context; psychoanalytic work with individuals often assumes that internal change alters relationships.

## The Contemporary Situation

- **Practice:** many family and couple therapists combine ideas from several traditions, including psychodynamic concepts of transference and defense.
- **Research:** behavioral and systemic family therapies have a larger body of randomized-trial evidence than analytic family therapies, although psychodynamic couple and family approaches are also studied.
- **Eating disorders:** family-based treatment of adolescent anorexia nervosa (the Maudsley approach, Lock and Le Grange) is a structured, behaviorally oriented model derived from the Maudsley work, and is not a psychoanalytic treatment.

## The Clinical Difference Today

- **Psychoanalysis and psychodynamic therapy:** usually individual, directed at personal history and internal patterns.
- **Systemic therapy:** directed at the family or couple as the unit of treatment.
- **Analytic family and couple therapy:** attempts to integrate both views.
These are descriptive categories rather than recommendations.

## Why the Relationship Still Matters

- Many practitioners draw on both families of ideas, using systemic formulation of current interaction alongside psychodynamic understanding of internal patterns.
- Complex family situations, including histories of trauma, often call for attention to both the individual and the relational context.
