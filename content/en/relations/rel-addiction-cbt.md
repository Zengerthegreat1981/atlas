# Addiction, Behaviour Change and CBT: Motivational Interviewing and Relapse Prevention

Cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) has not been replaced in the treatment of substance use problems, but it has been supplemented by methods that arose within the addiction field itself, chiefly **motivational interviewing (MI)** and **relapse prevention (RP)**. Contemporary practice often combines these with CBT skills training, medication, and, for many people, mutual-help groups. This entry is descriptive and is not clinical guidance.

## Three Ways of Thinking about Addiction

- **Moral model.** Heavy drinking and drug use are treated as failures of will or character, as in much of the nineteenth- and early twentieth-century temperance movement. The remedy is willpower, moral reform, or religion.
- **Disease model.** Alcoholics Anonymous (AA, founded 1935) and Narcotics Anonymous (NA, 1953) rest on the idea that addiction is an illness of the person and that abstinence is the goal. E. M. Jellinek's *The Disease Concept of Alcoholism* (1960) gave the model an influential scientific formulation, describing varieties and phases of alcoholism.
- **Learning model.** Behavioural and social-learning approaches treat substance use as learned behaviour shaped by reinforcement, expectations, and context. Albert Bandura's work on self-efficacy (1977) was influential here.

The historical debate between the disease and learning models was never settled in a single stroke. Neuroscientific accounts, such as the "brain disease" model described by Volkow, Koob, and McLellan (2016), emphasise changes in neural circuits, while learning-based accounts emphasise skill, expectation, and context. Many clinicians now treat them as complementary levels of description.

## Motivational Interviewing

William R. Miller (University of New Mexico) described the approach in a 1983 article, "Motivational Interviewing with Problem Drinkers" (*Behavioural Psychotherapy*), and, with Stephen Rollnick (Cardiff), developed it in the book *Motivational Interviewing: Preparing People to Change Addictive Behavior* (1991), later revised as *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change*. Its central idea is that many people with substance problems are ambivalent about change, and that a collaborative, non-confrontational conversation can strengthen their own motivation.

Miller has acknowledged the influence of Carl Rogers's client-centred approach, especially accurate empathy and respect for the person's autonomy, while MI adds a directional element: the practitioner selectively draws out and reinforces the person's own reasons for change. MI is also commonly discussed alongside the Transtheoretical Model of change (Prochaska and DiClemente, 1983), though the two were developed separately.

Standard descriptions refer to the core skills summarised as **OARS** (open questions, affirmations, reflective listening, summaries) and to guiding principles: express empathy, develop discrepancy between present behaviour and values, roll with resistance, and support self-efficacy.

## Relapse Prevention

G. Alan Marlatt (1941-2011) and Judith Gordon published *Relapse Prevention* (1985). Their cognitive-behavioural model treats relapse as a process that can be understood and anticipated rather than as a moral failure. Key elements include identifying high-risk situations (such as negative emotion or social pressure), building coping skills and self-efficacy, and addressing the **abstinence violation effect**, in which a person who has a lapse interprets it as total failure and proceeds to full relapse. Techniques include reframing a lapse as a learning opportunity and planning responses in advance.

## Other Developments

- **Behavioural and family approaches.** The Community Reinforcement Approach (Hunt and Azrin, 1973) arranged a person's environment so that sober living was rewarded; the later family-oriented variant CRAFT trains relatives in supportive strategies.
- **Matrix Model.** A structured outpatient programme developed in Los Angeles in the 1980s, mainly for stimulant users, combining CBT elements, relapse prevention, family education, and support-group participation over about sixteen weeks.
- **Mutual-help alternatives.** Secular Organizations for Sobriety (founded 1985 by James Christopher), Moderation Management (1994), and SMART Recovery (1994) offer alternatives to the AA model, with SMART drawing on CBT and motivational principles.
- **Harm reduction.** Policies and practices that aim to reduce the harms of drug use without requiring abstinence, such as needle exchange (an early scheme operated in Amsterdam in 1984), methadone maintenance (developed in the 1960s by Vincent Dole and Marie Nyswander), naloxone for opioid overdose (approved in the United States in 1971), and supervised heroin-assisted treatment (introduced in Switzerland in the 1990s).
- **Medications.** Methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone for opioid use disorder, and several medications for alcohol use disorder, have strong evidence in guidelines and are commonly combined with psychosocial treatment. AA's literature does not oppose prescribed medication, though some members and groups have historically been sceptical of it.

## Project MATCH

Project MATCH (1997) was a large United States multi-site trial of treatments for alcohol use disorder, enrolling about 1,700 people. It compared Twelve-Step Facilitation, cognitive-behavioural coping skills therapy, and a brief motivational enhancement therapy. Outcomes were broadly similar across the three, and most of the hypotheses that particular patients would do better in particular treatments were not confirmed. The trial concerned alcohol, not other drugs, and its results should not be generalised beyond that.

## Points of Divergence and Convergence

| Dimension | Disease model (AA) | CBT / MI / RP | Harm reduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concept of addiction | Chronic illness of the person | Learned, modifiable behaviour and beliefs | Pattern of use whose harms can be reduced |
| Aim | Abstinence | Abstinence or reduced use | Reduced harm |
| Lapse | Danger sign | Event to analyse and learn from | Expected possibility |
| Evidence base | Observational, plus trials such as Project MATCH | Randomised trials | Randomised and observational studies |

The approaches converge in treating addiction as a real condition and not a mere choice, in valuing change, and in using groups or social support. They differ over whether a "higher power" is needed, whether abstinence is the only goal, and how far the person is seen as able to choose and change.

## Limits

Outcomes vary widely with the substance, severity, setting, and the person's circumstances. Evidence for MI and relapse prevention is generally supportive but effect sizes are modest and vary between trials. Psychedelic-assisted therapy for substance use is under investigation and is not an established treatment. Comparisons of AA with professional treatments are complicated because AA is a voluntary peer fellowship rather than a clinical intervention.
