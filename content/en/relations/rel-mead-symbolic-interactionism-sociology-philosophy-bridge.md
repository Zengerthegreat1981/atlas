# George Herbert Mead Between Pragmatist Philosophy and Sociological Symbolic Interactionism

George Herbert Mead (1863–1931) published no book during his lifetime and did not classify himself as a sociologist. He was a professor of philosophy at the University of Chicago from 1894, a friend of John Dewey, and influenced by William James. Yet his lectures in social psychology, which his students compiled after his death into *Mind, Self, and Society* (1934), became the direct theoretical foundation of an entire sociological current.

## Philosophical Roots

Mead belongs to classical American pragmatism, which holds that thought is a tool for solving problems of action rather than a mirror of reality. He criticized the dualism between mind and body, and between the individual and society, making mind a social process that arises from the exchange of gestures. In Islamic philosophy there is a distant connection, sometimes mentioned, between the idea of the emergence of the self within civilization (*al-'umrān*) in Ibn Khaldun and Mead's idea. However, there is no evidence of influence, nor of Mead's knowledge of this, so the connection remains a comparison of interpretive character and nothing more.

## Mead's Central Concepts

Mead divides the self into the "I," the active and spontaneous agent, and the "Me," which represents the internalized attitudes of others. The self arises through stages: imitation of gestures; then play, in which the child takes on the role of one person; then the organized game, in which the child learns to anticipate the roles of everyone and arrives at the "generalized other," which represents the voice of the community. This structure explains how the individual becomes social from within.

## Blumer and the Sociological Turn

Mead did not use the term "symbolic interactionism." It was coined by Herbert Blumer in 1937, in a chapter in *Man and Society*, edited by Emerson P. Schmidt. Blumer took Mead's lectures in Chicago and became a professor of sociology there and later at Berkeley. In *Symbolic Interactionism: Perspective and Method* (1969), Blumer consolidated his perspective into three premises: people act toward things on the basis of the meanings those things have for them; meanings arise from social interaction; and meanings are modified through an interpretive process. Blumer also argued methodologically that research should be "exploratory" and grounded in the empirical world, rather than conducted through statistical variables.

## The Question of Interpretive Fidelity

The relationship between Mead and Blumer is not a simple application. Hans Joas and others have shown that Blumer neglected the structural and evolutionary dimension of Mead's thought (the role of institutions, and the physical-social analysis of the gesture) and focused on the interpreting individual. The Iowa School (Manford Kuhn) later attempted to recover the more structural aspect. Sheldon Stryker distinguished structural roles in *Symbolic Interactionism: A Social Structural Version* (1980), and Erving Goffman developed a dramaturgical analysis in *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959) that in some respects goes beyond Mead. Symbolic interactionism is therefore not purely Meadian but a selective reading of Mead's legacy.

## Limits and Criticism

The sociological perspective derived from Mead is criticized for ignoring power and class structure (a Marxist critique), for relying on a small number of field observations (a positivist critique), and for the theoretical vagueness of the concept of the active "I." From the philosophical side, Mead remains a source for research into recognition and mutual understanding, and Habermas drew on him in *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981) in establishing the concept of intersubjectivity.
