# British Empiricism and Behaviorism: From Locke to Watson

Behaviorism shares important assumptions with **British empiricism** (Locke, Berkeley, Hume): knowledge and character are shaped by experience, and complex mental life is built from simpler elements by association. This entry treats the link as a **philosophical affinity and an indirect intellectual background**. It does not claim that Watson or Skinner were directly shaped by reading Locke or Hume; the documented proximate sources of behaviorism lie elsewhere, in animal psychology, evolutionary thought, and the physiology of conditioned reflexes.

## The Philosophical Background: Locke and Hume

John Locke (1632-1704), in *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689, dated 1690), rejected innate ideas. In Book II he likened the mind at the outset to "white paper, void of all characters," and answered the question of how it comes to be furnished with the single word "experience." The notion is often summarised by the Latin phrase *tabula rasa*, which is not Locke's own wording. Locke also gave an account of how simple ideas combine into complex ones and of the role of association (the chapter "Of the Association of Ideas" was added in the fourth edition, 1700).

David Hume (*A Treatise of Human Nature*, 1739-40) analysed the mind into impressions and ideas and proposed resemblance, contiguity in time or place, and cause and effect as the principles of association. These associationist principles were later taken up in nineteenth-century psychology (for example by James Mill, J. S. Mill, and Alexander Bain), which is the more direct route by which the empiricist tradition reached experimental psychology.

## The Proximate Sources of Behaviorism

- **Evolutionary and comparative psychology**: Charles Darwin's *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872), George Romanes's *Animal Intelligence* (1882), and C. Lloyd Morgan's *An Introduction to Comparative Psychology* (1894), which gave Morgan's Canon: do not interpret an action as the outcome of a higher psychical faculty if it can be explained by one lower in the psychological scale.
- **Animal learning research**: Edward Thorndike's *Animal Intelligence* (1898), including what he later formulated as the law of effect.
- **Physiology of conditioned reflexes**: Ivan Pavlov (1849-1936), whose work on digestion in dogs led to the study of conditional reflexes (English edition of *Conditioned Reflexes*, 1927), and Vladimir Bekhterev's reflexology.
- **Watson's programme**: John B. Watson's "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" (*Psychological Review*, 1913) argued that psychology should be a purely objective experimental branch of natural science. Watson and Rosalie Rayner's "Little Albert" study (1920) reported conditioning of an emotional response in an infant; the study is now regarded as methodologically weak and ethically objectionable.
- **Later systems**: Clark Hull's neobehaviorist system (1943) and B. F. Skinner's operant analysis (*The Behavior of Organisms*, 1938; *Science and Human Behavior*, 1953).

Historians such as Edwin Boring and Kerry Buckley (*Mechanical Man*, 1989) place Watson in this context of functionalism, animal psychology, and objectivist physiology. A direct, documented citation of Hume or Locke by Watson is not established.

## What Was Preserved

1. **Environmentalism**: the emphasis on experience as the source of what the organism comes to do or know.
2. **Associationism**: the idea that complex behaviour is built from elementary links, resemblance and contiguity in empiricism, stimulus-response or response-consequence relations in behaviorism.
3. **Naturalistic method**: explanation without appeal to innate rational principles.

## What Was Changed

| Dimension | Locke / Hume | Watson | Skinner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | Ideas (impressions) | Stimulus-response units | Operant response and its consequences |
| Consciousness | Central subject matter | Excluded from scientific psychology | Private events treated as behaviour, not as causes |
| Method | Reflection and argument | Laboratory observation | Experimental analysis of behaviour |
| Aim | Theory of knowledge | Prediction and control of behaviour | Prediction and control through environmental contingencies |

Locke and Hume were philosophers of knowledge, not psychologists in the modern sense; their introspective analysis is not continuous with behaviorism's rejection of introspection.

## Limits

The comparison is conceptual. Behaviorism diverged from empiricism in discarding the mind's ideas as a topic, and later cognitive psychology reintroduced internal representations. The affinity should not be read as proof that behaviorism "derives" from Locke.
