# Psychoanalysis and Humanistic Psychology: From Schism to Motivational Convergence

The relation differs from the analysis-behaviorism rivalry. Humanistic psychology, which took shape in the United States in the 1940s to 1960s as a "third force," defined itself partly against both psychoanalysis and behaviorism, yet several of its figures drew on psychoanalytic sources (Carl Rogers was influenced by Otto Rank's followers, and Abraham Maslow admired Freud's clinical insight while rejecting what he saw as a pathology-centered view). Later research on motivation and on experiential psychotherapy has brought the traditions closer again.

## The Point of Divergence: Rogers and Maslow Against a Pathology-Centered Model

- **Carl Rogers** (1902-1987): worked in the 1930s with children at the Rochester Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children, where he encountered the relationship-centered therapy of Otto Rank's followers in social work (he heard Rank speak in Rochester in 1936). He published *Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1942) and *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951), in which the therapist is not an interpreting expert and the client is credited with a capacity for self-direction.
- **Abraham Maslow** (1908-1970): trained as an experimental psychologist; he proposed a hierarchy of needs in "A Theory of Human Motivation" (1943) and developed it in *Motivation and Personality* (1954). He turned attention from what disables a person to what supports growth, and in 1954 called for a "positive psychology."

Both argued that psychoanalysis, in their reading, overweighted pathology and drive conflict. Summarized in simplified form:
- Freud: mental life is structured by conflict among drives, conscience and reality (id, ego, superego).
- Rogers: people have a formative tendency toward growth (the actualizing tendency); distress arises when conditions of worth distort the self-concept.
- Maslow: needs are organized in levels, with self-actualization at the top, and frustration of lower needs impedes growth.

## Points of Divergence (Level One: Theoretical)

| Dimension | Psychoanalysis | Humanistic (Rogers/Maslow) |
|---|---|---|
| Human nature | Conflictual | Oriented toward growth |
| Source of suffering | Unconscious conflict, early experience | Conditions of worth, frustrated needs |
| Model of health | Structural balance, capacity to love and work | Congruence, self-actualization |
| Technique | Free association, interpretation | Empathic listening, non-directiveness |
| Role of therapist | Analyst interpreting transference | Facilitator offering empathy, congruence and unconditional positive regard |
| Goal | Insight and change in personality structure | Congruence and growth |

## Points of Divergence (Level Two: Philosophical)

- **Knowledge:** Freud gives priority to unconscious determinants; Rogers gives priority to the client's subjective experience (the phenomenal field).
- **The self:** Freud's person is internally divided; Rogers speaks of a self-concept that may be congruent or incongruent with organismic experience.
- **Society:** in *Civilization and Its Discontents* (1930), Freud sees culture as requiring instinctual renunciation; Maslow was more optimistic that a supportive environment can foster growth (peak experiences, "B-values").
- **Empirical testing:** Rogers and colleagues pioneered the recording and research of therapy sessions and the use of self-report measures, notably in the 1950s. Freud relied on clinical case material. Maslow's evidence for self-actualization (small, selected biographical samples) has been criticized as methodologically weak.

## Points of Convergence

- **Both treat the therapeutic relationship as central.** Freud's analysis of transference and Rogers's core conditions both place the relationship at the heart of the work.
- **Both worked at some distance from the medical model.** Rogers explicitly criticized diagnostic labeling in therapy. Freud defended lay analysis and was not confined to psychiatric categories, though he did not reject diagnosis.
- **Both influenced experiential approaches.** Gendlin's focusing (*Focusing*, 1978), developed from collaboration with Rogers, and Greenberg's emotion-focused therapy offer points of contact with analytic concern for implicit meaning.
- **Ego psychology.** Hartmann's work on adaptation (1939) and Erikson's psychosocial stages (1950) stressed healthy development within psychoanalysis; they are often compared with humanistic interest in growth, although these were internal developments of psychoanalysis.
- **Conditions of worth and the superego.** Rogers's account of internalized conditions of worth can be compared, as an interpretive analogy, with Freud's superego as an internalized parental standard. Rogers did not adopt Freud's structural model.

## Neo-Freudianism as a Bridge

The label "Neo-Freudian" is applied to analysts who emphasized social and cultural factors:
- **Karen Horney** (1885-1952): described "basic anxiety" as a child's feeling of being isolated and helpless in a potentially hostile world.
- **Erich Fromm** (1900-1980): combined psychoanalysis with social theory (*Escape from Freedom*, 1941) and later humanistic ethics.
- **Harry Stack Sullivan** (1892-1949): developed an interpersonal theory of psychiatry that emphasized relationships.

Their work is often described as a bridge, but direct influence on Rogers and Maslow is better documented for Rank (on Rogers) and for Fromm and Horney (on Maslow, who knew several of the European emigres) than as a general school-to-school descent.

## Chains of Influence

- **Freud to Rank to Rogers:** documented in part. Rank broke with Freud in the 1920s and emphasized will and the here-and-now relationship; Rogers took up these themes mainly through Rank-influenced social workers.
- **Maslow and positive psychology:** Maslow used the term "positive psychology" in 1954. Martin Seligman, as American Psychological Association president in 1998, relaunched the term, and he cites humanistic predecessors while distinguishing his empirical orientation.
- **Rogers and contemporary practice:** person-centered principles (empathy, acceptance, congruence) have been absorbed into most psychotherapies as part of the common relational ground.

## Unresolved Debates

- **Is human nature fundamentally good or conflicted?** The positions of Rogers and Freud are as much philosophical as empirical. Evidence from developmental and evolutionary psychology supports neither in pure form.
- **Are the unconscious and the actualizing self opposed?** Gendlin's concept of the "felt sense" has been read by some as a bridge between implicit knowing and immediate experience. This remains an interpretation rather than an empirical finding.
- **Is insight necessary for change?** Rogers emphasized the relationship rather than interpretation. Meta-analyses (for example Horvath and Symonds, 1991; Flückiger and colleagues, 2018) show a reliable association between therapeutic alliance and outcome across approaches, which is consistent with Rogers's emphasis but does not show that technique is unimportant.

## Contemporary Integration

- **Motivational interviewing** (Miller and Rollnick, 1991): grounded in a Rogerian, empathic style, and combined with directive strategies to elicit change talk.
- **Emotion-focused therapy** (Greenberg, Rice and Elliott, 1993): draws on person-centered and Gestalt traditions together with emotion research.
- **Focusing-oriented therapy** (Gendlin): builds on Rogers's work on experiencing.
- **Self-determination theory** (Deci and Ryan, 1985): a research program that identifies autonomy, competence and relatedness as basic psychological needs. It is sometimes seen as an empirical successor to humanistic motivation theory, but it is not a simple update of Maslow's hierarchy.

## Clinical Differences Today

- **Psychoanalytic and psychodynamic therapy:** often used where patients seek deeper understanding of recurring patterns.
- **Person-centered therapy:** commonly emphasizes the relational conditions for growth and is used in counseling and in training generally.
- **Integrative practice:** many clinicians draw on both traditions, and meaning-centered approaches such as Frankl's logotherapy are sometimes linked with humanistic-existential thought, although Frankl's own lineage is separate.
