# Marxian Alienation: From Philosophical Concept to Sociological Class-Conflict Theory

Karl Marx wrote the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* in Paris in 1844, and they were not published in full until 1932, about half a century after his death. Since then, a single text has served as a reference for two fields that differ in method and in question: a philosophy of humanity asking what a person loses when their labor is reduced to a commodity, and a sociology asking how classes are organized and how historical conflict moves. This text examines the actual bridge between the two fields and identifies where they connect and where they diverge.

## Hegelian and Feuerbachian Roots of the Concept

Marx did not invent the word *Entfremdung*. Hegel used the terms *Entfremdung* and *Entäusserung* in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) to describe the spirit's departure from itself and its return to itself. Ludwig Feuerbach, in *The Essence of Christianity* (1841), reversed the picture: it is human beings who project their perfect attributes onto a God outside themselves, and thereby become impoverished. Marx took this inversion and moved it from religion to economics: what the worker projects outside himself is not a divine image but the product of his own labor.

In Arabic literature, the term has been translated variously as *al-ightirāb* (alienation), *al-istilāb* (dispossession), and *al-taghrīb* (estrangement). It is usually noted that the first term carries mystical and literary connotations from older usage (*al-ghurba* in Arabic poetry and among the Sufis) that do not exist in the German original. As for Ibn Khaldun in the *Muqaddimah*, he links civilization (*al-'umrān*) to labor and earning, and he is sometimes compared with Marx on the economic foundations of society. However, Ibn Khaldun has no equivalent concept of alienation, and any kinship between them is interpretive rather than historical influence.

## The Philosophical Angle: Four Dimensions of Alienation

In the manuscript "Estranged Labour," Marx identifies four aspects: the worker's alienation from the product of his labor, which belongs to another; from the act of production itself, which becomes compulsion rather than expression; from "species-being" (*Gattungswesen*), that is, the human capacity for free and conscious creation; and from other people, as competition replaces cooperation. Herbert Marcuse read the manuscripts in this direction in his well-known review of their 1932 publication; then Erich Fromm in *Marx's Concept of Man* (1961); and then Bertell Ollman in *Alienation* (1971), which offered the most precise academic account of its structure.

## The Sociological Angle: Class and Structure

In the *Communist Manifesto* (1848) and *Capital* (1867), attention shifts from the condition of the individual worker to the structure of relations of production: two classes defined by their position relative to the means of production, and a contradiction between the forces of production and the relations of production that drives history. In the first volume of *Capital*, the concept of "commodity fetishism" appears, in which relations between people look like relations between things. This is a structural formulation that goes further than the language of the humanist manuscripts. Later, György Lukács in *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) developed the concept of reification and linked it to class consciousness, making him an influential early figure in combining the two paths.

## The Actual Connection and the Dispute Over It

There are two opposing positions. Proponents of continuity (Ollman, Fromm, and Marcuse) argue that alienation is the ethical foundation that gives class conflict its meaning: the worker moves against the system only because he lives a tangible loss in his work and his humanity. Louis Althusser, in *For Marx* (1965), argues that there is an "epistemological break" around 1845, and that the language of alienation remained a Feuerbachian inheritance that the mature Marx abandoned in favor of concepts such as the mode of production and the social formation. The most widespread contemporary approach holds that the concept is present in the *Grundrisse* (1857–1858) and in *Capital* in other forms, without settling the dispute.

## The Empirical Extension in Sociology

American sociology tried to move the concept out of philosophy and into measurement. Melvin Seeman, in an article published in the *American Sociological Review* in 1959, distinguished five meanings: powerlessness, meaninglessness, normlessness, social isolation, and self-estrangement. Robert Blauner, in *Alienation and Freedom* (1964), studied workers in four industries and found that alienation intensifies on the assembly line and diminishes in continuous-process industries, where the worker has some degree of control. This finding shows that the philosophical concept can be tested, but it also empties it of its Marxist dimension, which concerns ownership of the means of production.

## The Limits of the Bridge

Philosophical alienation remains burdened by the hypothesis of a free "human essence" that is realized in non-alienated labor, a hypothesis that structuralists and positivists alike deny. Sociological class conflict remains describable without reference to any individual psychological state. The relationship between the two fields is one of mutual nourishment rather than logical derivation, and whoever reads one in isolation from the other loses half the meaning.
