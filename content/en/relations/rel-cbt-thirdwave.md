# CBT and the Third Wave: From Evolution to Quiet Schism

The "third wave" (ACT, DBT, MBCT, and related approaches) grew out of the cognitive-behavioral tradition. Its founders were trained in behavior therapy or CBT, and several began by trying to help patients for whom standard protocols seemed insufficient (for example chronically suicidal patients with borderline personality disorder, or people with recurrent depression). The resulting models differ in philosophy from Beck's cognitive therapy. Whether this is best described as an evolution of CBT or as a quiet schism is disputed, and the literature offers no conclusive judgment.

## Starting Point: Change or Accept?

- **Classical CBT (Beck, 1976)**: emotional distress is maintained by distorted or unhelpful appraisals. Examining and modifying them reduces distress.
- **ACT (Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson, 1999)**: attempts to suppress or control inner experience tend to be counterproductive. The aim is psychological flexibility: accepting private experience while acting on chosen values.
- **DBT (Linehan, 1993)**: developed for chronically suicidal people with borderline personality disorder. It balances acceptance (mindfulness, distress tolerance) with change (emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness), under a dialectical philosophy.
- **MBCT (Segal, Williams, and Teasdale, 2002)**: designed to prevent relapse in recurrent depression by combining cognitive therapy elements with mindfulness training, so that patients relate to thoughts as mental events rather than facts.

## The Central Philosophical Difference

| Dimension | Classical CBT | Third wave (ACT/DBT/MBCT) |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Reduce symptoms by modifying cognition and behavior | A valued life (ACT) / emotion regulation (DBT) / relapse prevention (MBCT) |
| Stance on thoughts | Evaluate and restructure | Observe or defuse from thoughts rather than primarily change their content |
| Stance on emotion | Change the disturbing emotion via appraisal and behavior | Accept emotion as part of experience; change what can be changed (DBT) |
| Stance on the self | Modify patterns of thinking | "Self-as-context" (ACT): the observing perspective |
| Mindfulness | Not a core component | Central, in different forms in each model |

The contrast is a matter of emphasis. Beck's model also includes distancing from thoughts and behavioral experiments, and critics such as Hofmann and Asmundson (2008) asked whether acceptance- and mindfulness-based therapies were a "new wave or old hat."

## Lineage and Development

- Ellis (*Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy*, 1962) and Beck (1976) were central figures in the cognitive tradition; Bandura's social learning theory (1977) is a parallel cognitive-behavioral source rather than a link in the same chain.
- Lazarus's multimodal therapy (BASIC-ID) was a technically eclectic behavioral development of the 1970s and 1980s.
- Mahoney and others advanced a constructivist cognitive therapy from the 1970s onward.
- Kabat-Zinn began Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center in 1979, drawing on Buddhist meditation practice in a secular medical program. MBCT adapted MBSR together with cognitive therapy components.
- Hayes developed ACT from the 1980s, alongside relational frame theory (RFT), which treats language and cognition as learned relational behavior. Hayes (2004) named the "third wave" in this sense.

## Buddhist Influences

- MBSR and MBCT draw explicitly on Buddhist meditation, in secular form.
- Linehan, who trained in Zen, incorporated mindfulness and acceptance practices into DBT.
- ACT is not explicitly Buddhist, although its emphasis on acceptance has parallels.

Critics from Buddhist traditions argue that secular mindfulness loses its ethical and spiritual context, while some behaviorists worry about importing contemplative ideas. These are debates about framing, not settled findings.

## Clinical Evidence (in Outline)

- **Borderline personality disorder**: DBT has the strongest randomized-trial evidence among the third-wave approaches; other treatments, including schema-focused and mentalization-based therapies, also have support.
- **Recurrent depression**: an individual-patient-data meta-analysis (Kuyken et al., 2016) found MBCT reduced relapse risk compared with usual care, with benefit similar to maintenance antidepressant treatment. Cognitive therapy also has relapse-prevention effects.
- **Anxiety disorders**: standard CBT remains the best-established treatment; ACT has encouraging results, with a less extensive evidence base.

## Positions on "Evolution or Schism"

- Hayes presents ACT as part of the continuing development of behavioral and cognitive therapy.
- Others, including some cognitive-behavioral researchers, question whether the third wave is distinct in theory or in outcomes.
- Linehan developed DBT within the CBT framework and added substantial new elements.
- Kabat-Zinn positions MBSR as a mindfulness program, not as psychotherapy.

## Bridges Between the Traditions

- The Unified Protocol (Barlow and colleagues, 2011) is a transdiagnostic treatment combining cognitive-behavioral and acceptance-oriented emotion-regulation elements.
- Behavioral activation (for example Lejuez and colleagues, 2001) returns to the behavioral tradition, with some cognitive features.
- Process-based therapy (Hayes and Hofmann, 2018) proposes organizing interventions around change processes rather than diagnostic protocols.

## Philosophical Debate

- Is mental health the absence of symptoms, or a meaningful life with emotional flexibility that may coexist with symptoms?
- Is suffering to be reduced or accepted? Third-wave models emphasize changing one's relationship to unavoidable distress.
- Are thoughts the self? Beck's model treats thoughts as hypotheses to test; ACT and MBCT emphasize observing them as events.

In practice, many clinicians integrate elements from both traditions.
