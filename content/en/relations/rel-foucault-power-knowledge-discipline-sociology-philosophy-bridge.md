# The Shared Foucault: From Critical Philosophy of Madness and Power to the Sociology of Power/Knowledge and Discipline

Michel Foucault is a philosopher who traced his project from *History of Madness* (1961) to *History of Sexuality*, and his applications in sociology branch into two readings: the reading of power-knowledge and discourse, and the reading of disciplinary power. This connection shows how philosophy relates to its sociological applications without being identical to them.

## Three angles on a single project

The general philosophical angle follows the full trajectory: from the critique of psychiatry in *History of Madness*, to *The Birth of the Clinic* (1963), and *The Order of Things* (1966), which addressed the "episteme" that determines what can be said in a given era; then *The Archaeology of Knowledge* (1969); then a shift to genealogy in *Discipline and Punish* (1975); then to "biopower" in *The Will to Knowledge* (1976) and the lectures *Security, Territory, Population* (1977-1978); and finally to "technologies of the self" in his late period. The sociological angle of power-knowledge studies how knowledges, such as psychiatry, sexuality, and statistics, produce forms of governance that classify and monitor individuals. The angle of disciplinary power applies the analysis of *Discipline and Punish* to the prison, the school, the factory, and the barracks, and centers on the **panopticon**, the prison design devised by Jeremy Bentham in 1791, in which invisible surveillance makes the prisoner monitor himself.

## How sociology received Foucault

Foucault's imprint on sociology has spread through many studies. David Garland, in *Punishment and Modern Society* (1990), studied the transformations of punishment within a framework that was both Foucauldian and critical. Haggerty and Ericson, in "The Surveillant Assemblage" (2000), argued that contemporary surveillance does not gather in a single building but in networks of data. In an Arab context, Timothy Mitchell in *Colonising Egypt* (1988) used the concept of discipline to explain how the modern state of nineteenth-century Egypt reorganized the army, the school, the village, and the exhibition. Edward Said, in *Orientalism* (1978), built on Foucault's concept of discourse to show how European knowledge produced "the Orient." The link to Arab society rests on the studies by Mitchell and Said, not on an Arab grounding within Foucault's own tradition.

## The actual relationship

The two sociological readings are not repetitions of the philosophy. They are institutional applications of two central theses: the organic link between knowledge and power, and the claim that disciplinary power is a modern pattern distinct from the older sovereign power, which rests on the public spectacle of punishment. Neither can be understood without its philosophical background: the critique of modern Western rationality and its relationship to exclusion.

## Critiques

Nancy Fraser (1981) criticized Foucault for offering no normative basis that justifies resistance to power. Habermas, in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985), argued that his work undermines the normative foundation of his own critique. Thomas Mathiesen (1997) observed that today's society is a "viewer society" rather than a "panopticon," since many watch a few. Some historians hold that the shift from corporal punishment to discipline was not as pure as Foucault portrayed it. Nevertheless, his framework remains a productive tool of analysis.
