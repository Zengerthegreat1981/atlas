# Somatic Markers in Damasio and Spinoza's Theory of the Emotions

The relationship turns on the idea that emotions and bodily signals are not an obstacle to rational thought but part of its foundation. The neuroscientist Antonio Damasio, in a book meant to reread Spinoza in the light of neuroscience, linked his clinical observations to the seventeenth-century philosopher who rejected Descartes's dualism.

## Emotions and the Body in the Arabic Heritage

The view that emotion affects the body was not foreign to Islamic medicine and philosophy. Ibn Sina, in *al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (Book I), devoted a section to the "psychic symptoms," namely anger, joy, sorrow, and fear, and showed their effect on the temperament of the body and on the preservation of health. He also divided the movement of the spirit during emotion between expansion and contraction. Miskawayh, in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq*, presents the emotions as powers of the soul that need to be corrected by reason rather than eradicated. This does not mean identity with Spinoza or Damasio, but it shows that linking emotion to body and mind is old in Islamic thought.

## Spinoza

Baruch Spinoza, in the *Ethics* (*Ethica*, published after his death in 1677), rejected Descartes's dualism of two substances and said that mind and body are two aspects of one substance (parallelism, Book II, Proposition 7). He defined the emotions (*affectus*) as changes in the body's capacity to act, which increase or decrease it, and in the corresponding idea: joy is a transition to greater perfection, sorrow a transition to lesser perfection, and desire is the essence of the human being (Book III). The root of this is *conatus*, the striving of each thing to preserve its being (Book III, Proposition 6). Emotion is therefore a necessary expression of this striving, not an incidental disturbance.

## Damasio: The Somatic Markers

In *Descartes' Error* (1994), Damasio presented the somatic marker hypothesis: when a person faces a decision, past experiences associated with emotional bodily signals, such as changes in heart rate or muscle tension, are called up and bias the choice before conscious reasoning, narrowing the possibilities. He based this on the case of Phineas Gage (1848) and on patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, such as the patient Elliot, described by Eslinger and Damasio (1985), who retained his logical intelligence but could not make sound decisions in his life. Bechara, Damasio, Damasio, and Anderson (1994), in the journal *Cognition*, introduced the Iowa Gambling Task and showed that patients with such damage keep choosing the disadvantageous decks of cards; later work by the same group (1996, 1997) reported that they also lack anticipatory skin-conductance responses. Damasio later explicitly engaged the Spinozist framework in *Looking for Spinoza: Joy, Sorrow, and the Feeling Brain* (2003).

## Critique of the Hypothesis and Limits of the Link

The somatic marker hypothesis is disputed. Dunn, Dalgleish, and Lawrence (2006), in *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, reviewed the evidence and found it inconclusive, because the Iowa test admits other explanations. Maia and McClelland (2004), in *PNAS*, showed that participants may know more than the researchers assumed, so an unconscious signal is not necessarily required. On the philosophical side, scholars of Spinoza dispute the accuracy of Damasio's reading. Some argue that he attributes to Spinoza concepts of modern physiology, and that Spinoza's parallelism is metaphysical rather than neuroscientific. Furthermore, Spinoza holds that reason is freed from bondage to the emotions through active emotions and knowledge (Book V), which Damasio does not treat with the same depth. The conclusion is that the bridge works as a source of inspiration and interpretation more than as experimental evidence for Spinoza's philosophy.
