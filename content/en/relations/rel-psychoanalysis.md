# Psychoanalysis

Psychoanalysis is the school that many founders of existential therapy came out of — and then broke with. Nearly every early existential therapist had first been trained as an analyst, and the break was never clean: it carried much of the old house with it — the seriousness about suffering, the attention to avoidance, and the conviction that the therapeutic relationship itself is material for the work. What changed was the explanation of suffering. Existential therapy is often misread as ignoring the unconscious; it reworks the psychoanalytic inheritance rather than discarding it.

## What Was Kept

The existential founders kept more of psychoanalysis than their polemics suggest. First, they kept the idea that suffering is meaningful rather than a malfunction: a symptom is a communication, a way of living with something unbearable. Second, they kept the centrality of avoidance: Freud's defences — repression, denial, displacement — survive in existential language as the evasion of freedom, death, and responsibility. Third, they kept the therapeutic relationship as the place where the work happens, not merely its setting. Transference — the patient reliving old patterns with the therapist — is read as a live sample of how the person meets another here and now. Irvin Yalom's *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980) is the clearest example: it calls itself dynamic in structure and existential in content, its four ultimate concerns — death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness — playing the role Freud's drives once played.

## What Was Rejected

What was rejected is the explanatory machinery. First, childhood determinism: in Freud's model the present is explained by the past, above all by early childhood, and the adult is the outcome of infantile conflicts. The existential reply is that the past is a fact, not a fate — a person is not only what their history made them but what they are doing with that history now. Second, the structural unconscious was rejected, or at least radically reworked. For Freud it is a hidden region with its own laws, a storehouse of repressed drives. For Sartre, in *Being and Nothingness* (1943), this picture is itself bad faith: it splits the person into a knower and a known and lets the conscious self disown its own choices. What is not faced is not stored in a separate chamber; it is lived, avoided, and enacted in the present. Third, the analyst as expert interpreter: in the classical model the analyst decodes the patient's material from a position of knowledge, and interpretation is the curative act. Binswanger and Boss replaced the interpreting analyst with an encounter between two people, and the therapist's task became description and presence rather than decoding.

## The Practical Difference

The difference shows up in the question each therapy asks. Psychoanalysis asks, "Where did this come from?" — and follows the thread back through the patient's history until the symptom's origin is found and, in the classic hope, dissolved by insight. The existential therapist asks, "What are you doing with this now, and what are you avoiding by continuing it?" The first treats the present as an effect; the second as an activity, something the person is maintaining, often at a cost they have stopped noticing. A patient who sabotages closeness is, in the analytic telling, repeating an early wound; in the existential telling, they are also choosing the safety of isolation over the risk of being known — a choice that can be examined, owned, and changed. But they point in different directions: one toward archaeology, the other toward responsibility.

## Three Routes Out of Analysis

Binswanger, a friend and correspondent of Freud for thirty years, was the first to bring Heidegger into the clinic; his *Grundformen und Erkenntnis menschlichen Daseins* (1942) replaced Freud's drive theory with an analysis of the patient's world. Boss went further: a classically trained analyst, he studied with Heidegger directly and argued, in *Psychoanalysis and Daseinsanalysis* (1963; German original 1957), that psychoanalytic concepts could be re-grounded in phenomenology rather than metapsychology. Sartre took the most philosophical route, proposing in *Being and Nothingness* (1943) an "existential psychoanalysis" that drops the unconscious and reads a life for its fundamental project. Frankl's route was the sharpest break: formed in the Vienna circle, he parted from both Freud and Adler to build a therapy around meaning rather than drives. Yalom's was the most integrative, keeping a dynamic structure while changing its contents.

## Contemporary Syntheses

A minority of contemporary writers are actively rebuilding the bridge. The most sustained attempt is the phenomenological contextualism of Robert Stolorow and George Atwood, set out in *Contexts of Being* (1992): psychoanalysis rethought as the study of emotional experience as it takes form within relational contexts, with trauma understood not as an event stored in the mind but as severe emotional pain that found no relational home in which it could be held and integrated. These currents are a minority in both traditions — but they are active, and they keep the two schools from hardening into caricatures of one another.

## Why the Distinction Still Matters

The two schools still compete for the same territory: the serious, long-term treatment of suffering that does not reduce to symptom management. Presented as though it had nothing to do with psychoanalysis, existential therapy loses its depth — its account of avoidance, of the unconscious life of defences, and of the relationship as material. Presented as merely a variant of psychoanalysis, it loses its edge — the present as a choice, not only an effect, and the therapist as a fellow participant rather than an expert decoder. The founders lived it: a break that kept what it could not do without, and a debt neither side has fully settled.
