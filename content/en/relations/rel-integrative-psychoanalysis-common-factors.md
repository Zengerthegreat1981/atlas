# Integrative Therapy (Common Factors) and Psychoanalysis: An Old Defense

Psychoanalysis and the common factors perspective both treat the therapeutic relationship as central, but under different names and with different theories of how it works. Freud, who died in 1939, never used the term "common factors" (Rosenzweig's foundational paper appeared in 1936, and there is no evidence that Freud commented on it). The convergence on the relationship is a later conceptual comparison, not a historical borrowing. In psychoanalysis the relationship is examined through **transference** and the interpretation of it; in the common factors literature it is studied as the **therapeutic alliance** and related relationship variables.

## Freud and the Therapeutic Relationship

- Transference was noticed in clinical form in *Studies on Hysteria* (Breuer and Freud, 1895) and discussed in the "Dora" case (1905). "The Dynamics of Transference" (1912) is the principal early paper on its use in treatment.
- The patient repeats earlier relational patterns in relation to the analyst; the analysis of this repetition is a core of the work.
- Freud recommended analytic neutrality and abstinence: the analyst does not gratify the patient's wishes but analyzes them.
- In "The Dynamics of Transference," Freud distinguished the unobjectionable positive transference from transference that serves resistance. Later analysts developed the notion of an alliance, including Zetzel (1956, "therapeutic alliance") and Greenson (1965, "working alliance"). Bordin (1979) generalized the alliance across therapies as bond, goals, and tasks.

## The Relational Turn

- **Stephen Mitchell** (1946-2000), in *Relational Concepts in Psychoanalysis* (1988), argued for a relational model in which mind is formed in relationships rather than through drives alone.
- **Jay Greenberg and Stephen Mitchell**, *Object Relations in Psychoanalytic Theory* (1983), compared drive and relational models.
- **Merton Gill**, *Analysis of Transference* (1982), emphasized the here-and-now interaction between patient and analyst.
- **Michael Balint** (*The Basic Fault*, 1968) and **Heinz Kohut** (*The Analysis of the Self*, 1971) gave the relationship with the analyst new emphases (basic fault; selfobject transferences).

## Empirical Work Bridging the Two

- **Lester Luborsky** (1920-2009) developed the helping alliance concept and Supportive-Expressive psychotherapy (1984), a manualized psychodynamic therapy.
- **Blagys and Hilsenroth** (2000) reviewed process research to identify distinctive features of psychodynamic-interpersonal technique compared with other therapies.
- **Jonathan Shedler** (2010) reviewed evidence for the efficacy of psychodynamic therapy and argued that it works partly through processes that continue after therapy ends.
- Common factors researchers (Wampold; Norcross and Lambert) include psychodynamic therapies among those showing generally comparable outcomes.

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Psychoanalysis | Common factors |
|---|---|---|
| Central mechanism | Insight and working through transference | Relationship, expectancy, and other shared factors |
| Role of the relationship | Subject of interpretation | Predictor of outcome; alliance is measured |
| Technique | Interpretation, neutrality, and others | Technique matters mainly within a credible, coherent treatment |
| Evidence tradition | Case-based, with growing trial evidence | Meta-analytic and process-outcome research |

## Points of Convergence

1. Both treat the therapeutic relationship as central.
2. Both attend to the whole person rather than only symptoms.
3. Many psychodynamic therapists now draw on alliance research, and relational analysts emphasize the real relationship.

## Debate: Transference or Alliance?

- A classical view holds that transference is unconscious and a source of resistance, whereas the alliance is the conscious collaborative bond. Others argue that the alliance can also involve transference.
- Integrative perspectives often treat the "real relationship," transference, and alliance as distinct but interacting aspects.

## Limits

Equating transference with alliance blurs a real theoretical distinction. Moreover, psychoanalytic theory makes specific claims about unconscious conflict and development that common factors accounts do not address.
