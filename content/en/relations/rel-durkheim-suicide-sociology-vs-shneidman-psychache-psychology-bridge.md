# Suicide: Durkheim's Structural Sociology versus Shneidman's Individual Psychache

A classic example of how a single human phenomenon, ending one's own life, can be explained at two entirely different analytical levels: the structural-social level of Émile Durkheim, and the individual-psychological level of Edwin Shneidman. The two bodies of work arose in different times and places (France in 1897; the United States mainly from the 1980s and 1990s), and this entry is an analytical comparison, not a claim of historical influence.

## The social angle: Durkheim

In *Suicide: A Study in Sociology* (1897), Durkheim gathered statistics from across Europe to show that suicide rates are relatively stable within each society and differ between societies according to religion, marital status, and social cohesion. He distinguished four types: egoistic suicide, resulting from weak integration; altruistic suicide, resulting from excessive integration; anomic suicide, resulting from disorder in moral regulation during economic crises and social transformation; and fatalistic suicide, resulting from excessive forced regulation. His aim was to demonstrate that a "social fact" is explained by other social facts, not by individual psychological factors, which made the book one of the foundations of empirical sociology.

## The psychological angle: Shneidman

Edwin Shneidman, one of the founders of suicidology in the United States, built his theory on the idea of "psychache": an unbearable inner pain resulting from the frustration of vital psychological needs, such as belonging, achievement, and autonomy. He presented the idea in *Definition of Suicide* (1985) and in *The Suicidal Mind* (1996). For him, suicide is not an escape from life but an escape from pain. It occurs when pain reaches a level beyond what the individual can bear, and vision narrows until only one exit remains. The therapist's goal is to relieve the pain or raise the capacity to bear it.

## Two levels of explanation, not two competing positions

Each answers a different question. Durkheim asks why rates differ among groups and periods; Shneidman asks why this particular individual acts now. There are bridges between the two levels: Durkheim's anomic suicide implies that a breakdown of norms may generate the individual pain Shneidman describes. Thomas Joiner, in *Why People Die by Suicide* (Harvard University Press, 2005), proposed a theory that combines the two levels: thwarted belongingness (disconnection) and perceived burdensomeness, both of which are social phenomena lived within the psyche, together with an "acquired capability" for self-harm.

## A view from the Arab-Islamic heritage

Islam prohibits the killing of the self in categorical terms, and official statistics show lower rates in most Muslim societies. However, the accuracy of these statistics is contested, because religious and social stigma pushes cases to be hidden or recorded as accidents. Durkheim offers a social explanation for this general pattern: he found that groups with stronger religious and social cohesion protect their members, and he compared Protestants, Catholics, and Jews on this point. This converges with what modern studies of religiosity show regarding a protective effect, without implying that a religious person does not suffer the psychological pain that Shneidman described.

## Limits of each approach

Durkheim has been subject to methodological criticisms: his reliance on incomplete official statistics, and the "ecological fallacy" of explaining an individual's behavior from group rates. Shneidman has been criticized because "psychache" is a concept that is difficult to measure, and because most people who experience severe pain do not attempt suicide, which calls for understanding other factors. Each model also neglects biology: severe psychiatric disorders, impulsivity, and hereditary history. Combining the levels remains more fruitful than choosing between them.
