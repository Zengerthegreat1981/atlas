# Psychedelic-Assisted Therapy and Transpersonal Psychology: A Shared Historical Root

Psychedelic-assisted therapy (PAT) and transpersonal psychology share historical roots in mid-twentieth-century research on LSD and other psychedelics and in an interest in non-ordinary states of consciousness. The relationship is a historical and conceptual overlap, not an identity: modern clinical trials use standard psychiatric outcome measures, while transpersonal psychology is a broader movement concerned with spirituality and consciousness.

## Starting Point: LSD

- **Albert Hofmann** (1906-2008), a Swiss chemist at Sandoz in Basel, first synthesized LSD-25 in 1938 and discovered its psychoactive effects in 1943. His account is in *LSD: My Problem Child* (1979).
- In the 1950s and 1960s, LSD and related substances were studied in psychiatry, in treating alcohol dependence, in end-of-life distress, and in psychotherapy. Many of these studies were small and methodologically limited by modern standards.
- **Walter Pahnke**'s "Good Friday Experiment" (1962) examined mystical experience under psilocybin in a religious setting, and is a historical link between psychedelic research and the study of mysticism.

## Stanislav Grof

**Stanislav Grof** (born 1931, Prague) began research with LSD in Czechoslovakia in the late 1950s and moved to the United States in 1967. He wrote *Realms of the Human Unconscious* (1975) and *LSD Psychotherapy* (1980). With Abraham Maslow, Anthony Sutich, and others he was a co-founder of the transpersonal movement in the late 1960s (including the *Journal of Transpersonal Psychology*, 1969). After psychedelic research became legally restricted, he and Christina Grof developed Holotropic Breathwork (1970s) as a non-drug method for inducing non-ordinary states. Grof's account of the psyche (perinatal matrices, "inner healing intelligence") is a theoretical framework not accepted in mainstream psychiatry.

## Restriction and Revival

- Sandoz withdrew LSD from distribution in the mid-1960s, and the US Controlled Substances Act (1970) placed LSD and psilocybin in Schedule I, severely limiting research for decades.
- **MAPS** (Multidisciplinary Association for Psychedelic Studies) was founded by Rick Doblin in 1986 to support research and policy change.
- Griffiths and colleagues at Johns Hopkins published a controlled study on psilocybin and mystical-type experiences in 2006 (*Psychopharmacology*).
- Later trials include psilocybin for distress in patients with cancer (Griffiths et al., 2016; Ross et al., 2016), for major depression (Davis et al., 2021), and MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD (Mitchell et al., 2021, *Nature Medicine*, a phase 3 trial). The FDA declined to approve MDMA-assisted therapy in 2024, citing the need for further data. The evidence for psychedelic therapies is promising but still developing, and methodological problems (blinding, expectancy, therapist effects) are active debates.

## Transpersonal Frameworks in PAT

1. **Set and setting**: the role of the person's mindset and the environment, emphasized in early psychedelic research and still central in protocols (preparation, a supportive setting, and follow-up integration sessions).
2. **Mystical experience**: researchers use questionnaires such as the Mystical Experience Questionnaire; in some studies the intensity of mystical-type experience predicted outcomes. Whether this is necessary for benefit is debated.
3. **Ego dissolution**: a feature of intense psychedelic experiences, treated as therapeutic by some and as a risk by others.
4. **Integration**: meaning-making after the session.

## Ethics and Risks

- **Power and dependence**: patients are in a highly suggestible state, so therapist boundaries and consent are critical. Abuse allegations in some psychedelic communities have shown the risk.
- **Psychological risks**: challenging experiences, and exclusion of people with a history of psychosis or bipolar I disorder in most trials; medical contraindications also apply.
- **Spiritual bypassing**: John Welwood (1984) described the use of spiritual practice to avoid psychological work.
- **Therapy versus experience**: debates over how much of the effect is pharmacological and how much is expectation and context.

## Related Substances and Policy

- Ketamine is approved as an anesthetic and, as esketamine, for treatment-resistant depression; it is pharmacologically different from classic psychedelics.
- Oregon (Measure 109, 2020) and Colorado (2022) adopted state-level regulated psilocybin programs; Australia permitted authorized prescribers to use psilocybin and MDMA in specified conditions from 2023.
- Traditional plant medicines (ayahuasca, peyote) are used within Indigenous and religious contexts, with specific legal protections, such as for the Native American Church in the United States.

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | PAT (clinical research) | Transpersonal psychology |
|---|---|---|
| Aim | Treating diagnosed conditions | Study of spiritual and non-ordinary experience and growth |
| Method | Randomized trials, standard outcome measures | Phenomenological, theoretical, experiential |
| Tools | Pharmacological agents in medical settings | Meditation, breathwork, and other practices as well as psychedelics |

## Limits

The history is intertwined, but not all PAT researchers adopt transpersonal frameworks, and transpersonal psychology does not depend on psychedelics. Claims about mystical experience as a mechanism and about Grof's cartography remain contested.
