# Humanistic Psychology

Humanistic psychology is the closest and most confusing neighbour of existential therapy. The two share so much — a rejection of the medical model, the centrality of the therapeutic relationship, respect for the client's lived experience, and the same American milieu in the same decades — that they are routinely merged into a single "humanistic-existential" bloc. The merger is understandable, but it is a mistake: the differences concern human nature, limits, and what a therapist owes a suffering person. And the confusion has practical consequences: much of what circulates as existential therapy is humanistic therapy wearing existential vocabulary.

## What They Share

Both schools grew out of dissatisfaction with the two dominant psychologies of the mid-twentieth century: psychoanalysis, with its determinism and its pathologising language, and behaviourism, with its reduction of the person to stimulus and response. Both insisted that the client is a person, not a case, that the relationship is itself the healing factor rather than a vehicle for techniques, and that the client's experience should be taken on its own terms rather than translated at once into diagnostic categories. The two movements were also institutionally intertwined: the Association for Humanistic Psychology was founded in the early 1960s with Maslow, Sutich, and Bugental among its initiators, Rollo May was closely associated with the movement, and *Existence* (1958), edited by May, Ernest Angel, and Henri Ellenberger, which introduced European existential thought to American readers, was received by many as a humanistic text. Maslow's naming of humanistic psychology as the "third force" after psychoanalysis and behaviourism, in *Toward a Psychology of Being* (1962), was a banner existential writers could also march under. That shared history is why the label "existential-humanistic" came to feel natural — and why it needs examining rather than assuming.

## The First Difference: Human Nature

The first and deepest difference concerns human nature. Carl Rogers, in *On Becoming a Person* (1961), assumes an innate actualising tendency: given empathy, genuineness, and unconditional positive regard, a person naturally grows toward health, wholeness, and goodness. The humanistic picture is optimistic at bottom: the organism knows its own direction, and the therapist's task is to remove the obstacles to its unfolding. Existential therapy rejects that assumption outright. For Rollo May, a person is capable of good and of evil, and growth is not an automatic tendency but a painful choice, made again and again against resistance. May named this darker capacity the daimonic — the elemental power that can turn creative or destructive according to whether it is integrated — and pressed the point in his "Open Letter to Carl Rogers" (1982), arguing that a psychology without a serious account of evil cannot understand the people who actually come to therapy.

## The Second Difference: Limits

The second difference follows from the first. Existential therapy insists on death, finitude, guilt, and isolation as givens of existence: conditions that cannot be transcended, outgrown, or reframed away, only faced. Yalom's *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980) organises the whole of therapy around four such ultimate concerns — death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness — and treats the work as confrontation rather than relief. Humanistic psychology, by contrast, leans toward possibility and self-actualisation: its horizon is what a person might become. This is not to say humanistic therapists deny death or suffering — James Bugental's *The Search for Authenticity* (1965) is a profoundly serious book about presence in the face of pain — but the centre of gravity differs. The existential therapist begins where human possibility ends; the humanistic therapist begins where it starts. Kirk Schneider and Orah Krug's *Existential-Humanistic Therapy* (2010) is the most deliberate attempt to hold both together, and its very existence testifies to how real the tension is.

## The Third Difference: Tone

The third difference is one of tone, and it is the one clients feel first. Humanistic therapy is warm and reassuring: Rogers made unconditional positive regard a condition of growth, and the humanistic therapist's stance is one of affirmation. Existential therapy is warier. It regards premature reassurance as a betrayal of what the client actually lives — a way of comforting the client out of an encounter with the truth of their situation. When a client says they are afraid of dying, the humanistic response tends toward acceptance and support; the existential response tends toward staying with the fear, refusing to soften it, and asking what the fear is protecting and pointing to. This is not coldness: van Deurzen and Spinelli have shown existential work can be profoundly attentive and tender. But the tenderness is of a different kind — that of someone who will not look away, rather than someone who promises it will be all right.

## Why the Distinction Matters

The distinction matters for a plain reason: when the two are merged, the existential contribution is usually the part that disappears. A therapy that keeps the warmth, the relationship, and the language of authenticity, but drops the confrontation with death, guilt, and the capacity for evil, is a humanistic therapy with existential decorations — and it leaves clients unprepared for the very experiences that brought many of them to therapy. This is a recurring pattern: some writing presented as existential therapy is humanistic in substance and existential only in vocabulary, and part of the impression that the school is gentle talk without edge comes from exactly this substitution. The schools remain close neighbours, and Schneider and Krug show a genuine synthesis is possible. But a synthesis is only honest when both parties are present in it, and the existential party is the one most often quietly dropped.
