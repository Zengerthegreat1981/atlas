# Martin Buber's Philosophy of I and Thou and Client-Centered Therapy

In *I and Thou* (*Ich und Du*, 1923), Martin Buber set out a fundamental distinction between two modes of relation: "I-Thou" (*Ich-Du*), a mutual encounter in which both parties are present with their whole being and neither is reduced to an object, and "I-It" (*Ich-Es*), in which the other is treated as a thing to be used, analyzed, or classified. Ronald Gregor Smith translated it into English in 1937. Buber's roots lie in Jewish Hasidism, whose tales he had been collecting since 1906; for him, the encounter appears in everyday life and opens onto the "Eternal Thou."

## The Dialogue with Rogers

Buber met Carl Rogers, the originator of client-centered therapy, in a public dialogue at the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor in April 1957, moderated by Maurice Friedman. The text was later published within Friedman's book *The Knowledge of Man* (1965). Buber's essay "Distance and Relation," on the I-Thou relation and its bearing on psychotherapy, had appeared in the journal *Psychiatry* in 1957. This encounter is among the most famous dialogues between a philosopher and a therapist.

## Philosophical Roots and Psychological Application

In his article published in 1957 in the *Journal of Consulting Psychology*, Rogers had formulated the conditions of therapeutic change: congruence, unconditional positive regard, and empathic understanding. He saw in *I and Thou* a description of what happens when the therapist is fully present. But the dialogue revealed a subtle disagreement. Buber insisted that the therapeutic relationship is unequal by nature: the therapist bears professional responsibility, the patient came seeking help, and the therapist practices what he called "inclusion" (*Umfassung*): he lives the situation from the patient's side while retaining his own side, and the patient cannot do the same in return. Rogers, for his part, held that the relationship can approach a mutual encounter despite the professional asymmetry, and that the client at moments experiences being understood and may in turn understand the therapist.

## Influence on Contemporary Practice

This disagreement, between Buber's philosophical symmetry in an absolute sense and Rogers's approximate therapeutic symmetry, was never resolved. It has remained a reference point in humanistic and existential literature, including Maurice Friedman's work on dialogical psychotherapy (*The Healing Dialogue in Psychotherapy*, 1985). The influence of the idea of the relationship as the first factor of healing has also grown within research on the effectiveness of psychotherapy. Reviews such as Norcross's edited volume on therapeutic relationships report that the therapeutic alliance is a consistent predictor of outcome, though the size and interpretation of this association are debated.

## Limits of the Comparison

Rogers's and Buber's descriptions of the subject differ. Buber starts from an ontology of relation and holds that the human being exists only in relation, whereas Rogers relies on an innate tendency toward self-actualization. The "I-Thou" relation in Buber is not produced by technique and cannot be intended; it is a "grace" that happens, which makes turning it into a therapeutic condition a matter of dispute, because a therapist who seeks it may turn it into an "I-It." Moreover, Buber's concept has a religious horizon, connected to God as the Eternal Thou, whereas Rogers adopted a secular approach.
