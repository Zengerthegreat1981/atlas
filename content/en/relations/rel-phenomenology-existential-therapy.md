# Philosophical Phenomenology and Existential Psychotherapy: From Husserl to Binswanger and Boss

Phenomenology is one of the main philosophical sources of existential psychotherapy. The transfer was uneven: some therapists (Binswanger, Boss) drew directly on Husserl and Heidegger, while others (May, Frankl, Yalom) drew on a wider mixture of existential, religious, and psychological sources.

## The Philosophical Origin: Husserl

**Edmund Husserl** (1859-1938) founded phenomenology as a rigorous method for describing the structures of experience:

- **Epoché and reduction**: suspending assumptions about the external world in order to describe experience as it appears.
- **"To the things themselves"**: a return to description.
- **Intentionality**: consciousness is always consciousness of something.
- **Lived experience (*Erlebnis*)** and the life-world.

Husserl aimed to ground philosophy as a rigorous science, not to found a therapy.

## Heidegger

In *Sein und Zeit* (1927), Martin Heidegger (1889-1976), once Husserl's assistant, departed from Husserl's transcendental approach toward an analysis of **Dasein**, the being for whom its own being is at issue. His existential analytic treats being-in-the-world, mood and attunement (for example *Angst*), being-toward-death, authenticity, and temporality. Heidegger did not design a therapy, but his analysis became a major resource for some psychiatrists.

## Jaspers: The Philosopher-Psychiatrist

**Karl Jaspers** (1883-1969) worked in the psychiatric clinic at Heidelberg and wrote *Allgemeine Psychopathologie* (1913), which established a phenomenological method of describing patients' subjective experience (distinguishing understanding from causal explanation). He later became a philosopher (professor at Heidelberg, then Basel) and developed existential philosophy (*Philosophie*, 1932), including "limit situations" and "existential communication." He founded no therapeutic school, but influenced phenomenological psychiatry.

## Ludwig Binswanger

**Ludwig Binswanger** (1881-1966) belonged to a family of psychiatrists who ran the Bellevue Sanatorium in Kreuzlingen. He was a friend of Freud and a reader of Husserl and later Heidegger. He developed *Daseinsanalyse*, a phenomenological-anthropological psychiatry, in works such as *Grundformen und Erkenntnis menschlichen Daseins* (1942) and in case studies, including that of Ellen West (published 1944-45). Rather than explaining patients through drives alone, he described the structure of their worlds and ways of being.

## Medard Boss

**Medard Boss** (1903-1990), a Swiss psychiatrist, met Heidegger in the late 1940s and, from 1959 to 1969, hosted the **Zollikon Seminars** in which Heidegger addressed psychiatrists. Boss developed a stricter Heideggerian *Daseinsanalyse* (*Grundriss der Medizin und der Psychologie*, 1971). Boss's own account is a main source for his relationship with Heidegger, and the extent of Heidegger's endorsement of his clinical use of Heidegger's ideas should be judged from the published seminars and correspondence.

## Rollo May

**Rollo May** (1909-1994) wrote *The Meaning of Anxiety* (1950) and, with Ernest Angel and Henri Ellenberger, edited *Existence* (1958), which introduced European existential psychiatry (Binswanger and others) to American readers. *Love and Will* (1969) applied existential themes to relationship and intention. May's thinking drew on several sources, including Kierkegaard and Tillich, and was less strictly phenomenological than Binswanger's or Boss's.

## Viktor Frankl

**Viktor Frankl** (1905-1997) developed logotherapy and existential analysis, centered on the will to meaning, and described his experiences in the camps in *Ein Psychologe erlebt das Konzentrationslager* (1946; English 1959, later retitled *Man's Search for Meaning*). His intellectual sources include Scheler and the existential-phenomenological tradition, but he was not a student of Jaspers, and his school differs from Binswanger's and Boss's Daseinsanalysis.

## Irvin Yalom

**Irvin Yalom** (born 1931) systematized existential psychotherapy for a clinical audience in *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980), organizing it around death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness. His sources include Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre, and May, among others, and his work uses phenomenological description alongside interpersonal group therapy (*The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy*, 1970; *Staring at the Sun*, 2008).

## What Was Preserved from Phenomenology

1. Description before interpretation.
2. Intentionality and the primacy of lived experience.
3. A bracketing or suspension of prior assumptions (in a looser clinical sense than Husserl's epoché).
4. Anxiety as disclosing something about existence.
5. Authenticity versus absorption in "the they" (*das Man*).

## What Was Lost or Transformed

1. Husserl's technical apparatus (transcendental reduction and its terminology) was not used clinically.
2. Heidegger's project of rethinking ontology became a framework for describing patients' worlds.
3. Later existential therapies (May, Yalom) are broader and less strictly phenomenological.

## Contemporary Debate

- **Herbert Spiegelberg** (*Phenomenology in Psychology and Psychiatry*, 1972) is a historical introduction to phenomenology's use in psychology and psychiatry.
- **Havi Carel** (*Phenomenology of Illness*, 2016) applies phenomenology to illness experience.
- Phenomenological approaches have less outcome research than CBT, and are often taught as a perspective and an attitude rather than a technique.
