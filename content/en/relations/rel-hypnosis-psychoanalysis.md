# Hypnosis and Psychoanalysis: Historical Divergence

A long and complex historical relationship between hypnosis and psychoanalysis: the second was born from the womb of the first, then Freud separated from it in the 1890s, and each then followed an independent path before converging in some respects.

## The Shared Beginning

Freud studied under Charcot at the Salpêtrière in Paris between 1885 and 1886, and saw how hypnosis could produce hysterical symptoms and remove them. He translated Hippolyte Bernheim's book *Suggestion and Its Therapeutic Applications* (1886; its German translation appeared in 1888–1889), and visited Bernheim in Nancy in 1889. During this period he worked with Josef Breuer on the "cathartic method" (rooted in Breuer's treatment of Anna O. in 1880–1882, and published in *Studies on Hysteria*, 1895), in which the patient, under hypnosis, recovers memories of the trauma so that the symptom disappears. Pierre Janet, in *Psychological Automatism* (1889), introduced the concept of dissociation, which founded the understanding of hysteria and dominated the discussion of the unconscious.

## The Separation (c. 1892–1896)

Freud did not abandon hypnosis all at once. Around 1892 he tried the "pressure technique," pressing his hand on the forehead instead of hypnotising, and then formulated "free association," from which the basic rule of analysis emerged. He stated his reasons for abandoning it: that many patients could not be hypnotised deeply, that the results disappeared when the patient's attachment to the therapist ended, and that hypnosis concealed the resistance, which he saw as the key to understanding. This explains why the analysis of resistance and transference became the foundation of psychoanalysis. Yet Freud, in *Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego* (1921), returned to hypnosis to explain submission to the leader through love and identification.

## After the Separation

Some of his students tried to modify the Freudian technique in a direction close to suggestion. Sándor Ferenczi developed the "active technique," and then "mutual analysis" (his clinical diary of 1932), which is not hypnosis but an attempt to remove the distance on which analysis is founded. During the First World War, Ernst Simmel used hypnosis for war neurosis. As for Milton Erickson (not Erik Erikson, the author of the stages of psychosocial development), in the 1940s he developed "indirect hypnosis," which relies on flexible suggestion and metaphor, and conceived of the unconscious as a creative resource rather than a store of repression. Then Ernest Hilgard, in *Divided Consciousness* (1977), presented a theory of "neodissociation" that revives Janet's idea: attention is divided among streams of which the individual's awareness varies.

## Contemporary Evidence

Analytic reviews have shown that hypnosis is useful alongside cognitive-behavioural therapy: Kirsch, Montgomery and Sapirstein (1995) found that the hypnotic addition increased the effect, and Montgomery, DuHamel, and Redd (2000) found a moderate to large effect in relieving pain. Its use remains limited by fears of false memories, and historians of science have warned against attributing historical accuracy to hypnotically induced recall.

## The Value of the Separation

Defenders of the separation say that it gave analysis a rigorous method founded on speech, the relationship and interpretation. Critics say that it lost an effective tool for reaching forgotten experiences and for brief treatment. Modern therapy tends toward selective combination, with some therapists using hypnosis within a dynamic or cognitive framework. Henri Ellenberger, in *The Discovery of the Unconscious* (1970), recounted how the history of hypnosis became entangled with the history of dynamic psychiatry, and Alan Gauld wrote *A History of Hypnotism* (1992).
