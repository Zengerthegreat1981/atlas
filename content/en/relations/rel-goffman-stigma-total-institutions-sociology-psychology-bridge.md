# Goffman Between Sociological Stigma/Total-Institution Analysis and Clinical Psychology

A rare case in which a sociologist meets the field of clinical psychology without having written for it: Erving Goffman (1922–1982) studied the psychiatric hospital and stigma using an ethnographic field method, and his work became a reference in the critique of institutional confinement and in studies of self-stigma. The experience has two faces: sociological and psychological.

## The Sociological Angle

Between 1955 and 1956, Goffman carried out fieldwork at St. Elizabeths Hospital in Washington, D.C., as an assistant to the director of athletic activities. He published *Asylums: Essays on the Social Situation of Mental Patients and Other Inmates* (1961), which defined the "total institution" as a place where a number of people, cut off from the wider society, live their lives within a single framework under central administration, such as a prison, a barracks, a monastery or a mental hospital. He described the "moral career of the patient" in three stages: pre-admission, the period of residence (including the "stripping" of identity, the rituals of admission, and the economy of privileges), and discharge. In *Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity* (1963) he distinguished between the "discredited" stigma (visible) and the "discreditable" (potentially concealable), and analysed strategies of passing, covering, and control of information.

## The Impact on Psychology and Psychiatry

Goffman's thesis was that some of what is read as a symptom of illness is the effect of confinement itself. Russell Barton had preceded him with *Institutional Neurosis* (1959), and then the study by Wing and Brown, *Institutionalism and Schizophrenia* (1970), confirmed that negative symptoms are linked to the poverty of the institutional environment. After Goffman, a movement to discharge patients from the asylums spread; examples include the Kennedy Community Mental Health Act (1963) in America and the Basaglia Law (1978) in Italy. His idea of stigma became the source of later theories: Thomas Scheff's labelling theory (1966), Bruce Link and colleagues' modified labelling theory (1989), and Link and Phelan's definition of stigma (2001) as the convergence of labelling, stereotyping, separation, loss of status and discrimination within a context of power. From social stigma was born the concept of "self-stigma," associated with Corrigan.

## A Comparison with the Medieval Arab Hospital

The medieval Arab image of the hospital is different. Michael Dols (1987) showed that the *maristan* was not only a detention shelter but a treatment institution supported by endowment (*waqf*), which provided food and care and discharged patients after recovery. The Mansuri hospital in Cairo (Qalawun, 1284) included a section for the insane, who were treated with music, the bath and food. This does not cancel confinement and restraint, but it indicates that Goffman's "total institution" expresses a specific historical pattern of the large modern government asylums, not every hospital.

## The Limits of Goffman

Goffman did not write as a psychologist, and his method is descriptive, based on one year in one hospital. He was criticised for underestimating the role of the illness itself, and because the severity of his description does not reflect the improvement of asylums later. On the other hand, he showed that some "symptoms" interact with the environment. Among the limits of linking this to psychology is that discharging patients without adequate community services led to homelessness and gaps in care, which called for a critical review of the movement.
