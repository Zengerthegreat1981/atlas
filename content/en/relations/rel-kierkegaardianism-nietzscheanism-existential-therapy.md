# Early Existential Philosophy (Kierkegaard and Nietzsche) and Existential Psychotherapy: From Anxiety to Frankl and Yalom

Existential psychotherapy draws, in different ways and to different degrees, on nineteenth-century existential thought. Kierkegaard (1813-1855) and Nietzsche (1844-1900) did not write about psychotherapy, and the relationship is one of selective borrowing and reinterpretation by later authors, who also drew heavily on Husserl, Heidegger, Jaspers, Sartre, and Tillich. The strength of the lineage varies by figure and is documented below only where it is firm.

## Kierkegaard

- **Anxiety**: in *The Concept of Anxiety* (*Begrebet Angest*, 1844, published under the pseudonym Vigilius Haufniensis), Kierkegaard distinguished anxiety from fear: fear has a definite object, anxiety relates to possibility, and he called anxiety "the dizziness of freedom."
- **Despair**: *The Sickness Unto Death* (1849) analyzes despair as a failure to become a self before God.
- **Stages of existence**: the aesthetic, the ethical, and the religious (developed in *Either/Or*, 1843, and later works).
- **Subjectivity**: the emphasis on the individual's inward, committed relation to truth.

## Nietzsche

- **The death of God** (*The Gay Science*, 1882): a diagnosis of the collapse of the shared metaphysical and religious foundations of European values, not a celebration.
- **Will to power**, **self-overcoming**, and the revaluation of values (*Zarathustra*, *Beyond Good and Evil*, *On the Genealogy of Morality*).
- **Eternal recurrence**: a test of one's affirmation of life.
- **Perspectivism** and the critique of the "true world" (*Twilight of the Idols*, written 1888 and published 1889, recounts "how the true world became a fable").

## Karl Jaspers as an Intermediary

Karl Jaspers (1883-1969), who trained as a psychiatrist (*General Psychopathology*, 1913) before becoming a philosopher, wrote on both Kierkegaard and Nietzsche (including *Nietzsche*, 1936) and developed the notion of "limit situations" (death, suffering, struggle, guilt). He is a documented bridge between existential philosophy and psychiatry.

## From Philosophers to Therapists

### Alfred Adler
Adler (1870-1937) broke with Freud in 1911. Some commentators see echoes of Nietzsche's will to power in Adler's early notions of striving for power and superiority, but Adler's own debt is debated and later replaced by emphasis on social interest (*Gemeinschaftsgefühl*).

### Viktor Frankl
Frankl (1905-1997), founder of logotherapy and existential analysis, described the "will to meaning" in contrast to Freud's will to pleasure and Adler's will to power. He survived the Nazi camps and published *Ein Psychologe erlebt das Konzentrationslager* in 1946 (English: *From Death-Camp to Existentialism*, 1959; later retitled *Man's Search for Meaning*). Frankl drew on the existential-philosophical tradition (including Kierkegaard, Scheler, Heidegger, and Nietzsche, whose maxim about having a "why" to live he often quoted), but he was not a student of Jaspers. His own concept of "noogenic neurosis" arises from meaninglessness.

### Binswanger and Boss
Binswanger and Boss drew chiefly on Husserl and Heidegger (see the related entry on Daseinsanalysis).

### Rollo May
May (1909-1994) published *The Meaning of Anxiety* (1950), based on his doctoral dissertation, which surveys theories of anxiety, including Kierkegaard's, Freud's, and others'. With Angel and Ellenberger he edited *Existence* (1958), which introduced European existential psychiatry to the United States. May was also deeply influenced by Paul Tillich.

### Irvin Yalom
Yalom (born 1931) in *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980) organized the field around four "ultimate concerns": death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness. He draws on Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre, and others, and his novel *When Nietzsche Wept* (1992) is a fictional treatment of Nietzsche and Breuer.

## Concept Map

| Concept | Philosophical source | Therapeutic use |
|---|---|---|
| Anxiety as a response to freedom | Kierkegaard (via Heidegger, Tillich) | May: anxiety as potentially constructive; Yalom: death, freedom anxiety |
| Meaning and its loss | Nietzsche (death of God), Kierkegaard, Frankl's own work | Frankl: existential vacuum; Yalom: meaninglessness |
| Responsibility and authenticity | Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre | Emphasis on choice and responsibility |
| Limit situations | Jaspers | Confronting death, suffering, guilt |

## Preserved and Transformed

**Preserved:** anxiety as a human condition rather than only a symptom; attention to meaning, freedom, and finitude; philosophy as a way of reflecting on life.

**Transformed:** the theological dimension (Kierkegaard's Christianity and Nietzsche's critique of it) is largely secularized in therapy; Nietzsche's sharp critique of morality and the doctrine of eternal recurrence are rarely used clinically; Frankl's "will to meaning" is a new formulation, not Nietzsche's.

## Critique

Philosophers have noted that therapists often use these thinkers' terms in new contexts without engaging the original texts. Therapists have likewise stressed the difference between philosophical analysis and clinical help. Contemporary existential therapy is not a single tradition, and sources include van Deurzen, Spinelli, and Cooper (see Sources).
