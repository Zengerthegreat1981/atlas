# Humanistic Psychology and Positive Psychology: A Son Who Forgot His Father

Humanistic psychology (Maslow, Rogers, and others, from the 1940s and 1950s) and positive psychology (launched by Martin Seligman in his 1998 American Psychological Association presidency and in Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi, 2000) share a concern with the good life, strengths, and growth rather than only pathology. Maslow himself used the phrase "positive psychology" as the title of the final chapter of *Motivation and Personality* (1954). Positive psychology's founders acknowledged humanistic psychology as a precursor but argued that it had not built a cumulative empirical research base. Critics of positive psychology (for example Bohart and Greening, 2001) argued that it overlooked humanistic work that had already addressed its themes. The relationship is one of thematic overlap with methodological and philosophical divergence, not a documented line of direct descent.

## Starting Points

- **Maslow**: "A Theory of Human Motivation" (1943); *Motivation and Personality* (1954), including the hierarchy of needs and self-actualization; *Toward a Psychology of Being* (1962), with the contrast between deficiency motivation and growth ("being") motivation and the study of peak experiences.
- **Rogers**: *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951) and *On Becoming a Person* (1961); the actualizing tendency and the fully functioning person.
- **Seligman**: *Authentic Happiness* (2002) and *Flourish* (2011), with PERMA (positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, accomplishment); with Peterson, the VIA classification of 24 character strengths (2004).

## Points of Convergence

- Both direct attention to strengths, growth, meaning, and well-being rather than only symptom reduction.
- Maslow's peak experiences and growth motivation have thematic affinities with engagement and meaning in PERMA.
- Csikszentmihalyi's flow (1990) is related to peak experience, though it was developed as an empirical program.
- Meaning in PERMA is thematically close to Frankl's logotherapy, an existential-humanistic source.

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Humanistic psychology | Positive psychology |
|---|---|---|
| Method | Phenomenological and qualitative, with growing mixed methods | Largely quantitative measurement, experiments, and randomized trials |
| Core aim | Self-actualization, the fully functioning person | Flourishing and well-being as a researchable construct |
| Philosophical roots | Phenomenology, existentialism, organismic theory | Aristotelian eudaimonia, empirical psychology, cross-cultural virtue research |
| Measurement | Not central | Central (for example the VIA Inventory, the Flourishing Scale, PANAS) |
| Institutions | Small, associational (for example the Association for Humanistic Psychology) | Large research networks (for example the International Positive Psychology Association) |
| Suffering | A source of growth and meaning | Often framed as a condition to be treated alongside promoting well-being, with increasing attention to post-traumatic growth |

## Debates

- **Is happiness the same as self-actualization?** Maslow treated self-actualization as growth of potentials, not as happiness as such. Seligman moved from a happiness-centered theory in *Authentic Happiness* to a multidimensional theory of well-being in *Flourish*.
- **Is the study of the good life best done by measurement?** Positive psychology says yes; many humanistic psychologists counter that quantification can lose the lived meaning of experience.
- **Cultural and ethical assumptions:** humanistic critics have argued that positive psychology under-attends to context, suffering, and moral complexity.

## Contemporary Bridges

- Self-determination theory (Deci and Ryan, 1985, and later) is an empirical theory of basic psychological needs (autonomy, competence, relatedness) that shares humanistic concerns about growth and motivation, though it is not a measurement of Maslow's self-actualization.
- Positive CBT (Bannink, 2012) and positive psychotherapy (Rashid and Seligman, 2018) apply well-being interventions in clinical settings.
- Mindfulness in positive psychology has been the subject of edited volumes (for example Ivtzan and Lomas, 2016).
- Brent Robbins and others have argued for a renewed humanistic positive psychology (2008).

## Institutional Scale

Positive psychology has reached applied settings: educational programs, positive organizational scholarship (centered at the University of Michigan), and the US Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program (announced 2009), the latter criticized for evidence and ethics. National well-being policies, such as Bhutan's Gross National Happiness and the UK's What Works Wellbeing initiative, are related developments rather than outcomes of positive psychology alone. Humanistic psychology has had greater influence on counseling practice than on large-scale programs.

## Limits of the Comparison

Neither field is monolithic. Positive psychology includes qualitative and humanistic-leaning work, and humanistic psychology includes quantitative research, for example on person-centered therapy outcomes. A careful treatment compares specific constructs and methods instead of treating either field as a single position.
