# Somatic Therapy and Existentialism: Merleau-Ponty as Phenomenological Bridge

Somatic psychotherapies grew largely out of clinical practice (Reich, Lowen, Levine and others), while existential-phenomenological philosophy developed an account of the body as the subject's way of being in the world. Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908-1961) is the central philosopher of this account. The link between the two traditions is mostly conceptual and retrospective: therapists and theorists have used phenomenology to articulate what body-oriented therapy is about, but there is no documented direct line from Merleau-Ponty to Reich or to most somatic founders. This entry treats the bridge as an interpretive one.

## Somatic Therapy Without a Developed Philosophy

Wilhelm Reich's work (muscular "character armor," orgone theory) was grounded in clinical observation and in his own theory of sexual economy, not in phenomenology. The orgone concept was not scientifically supported (see the entry on psychoanalysis and somatic therapy). Later somatic approaches such as Levine's Somatic Experiencing and Ogden's sensorimotor psychotherapy dropped Reich's energy theory and drew on neurobiology, attachment research and clinical practice. They did not generally supply their own philosophical account of the body, which is one reason phenomenology has appealed to later commentators.

## Merleau-Ponty and the Lived Body

- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** (1908-1961): French philosopher; he engaged closely with Husserl's work (including unpublished manuscripts he consulted in Leuven in 1939), with Gestalt psychology and with neurology. He held chairs at Lyon, the Sorbonne (psychology and pedagogy, 1949) and the Collège de France (1952).
- **Main work:** *Phénoménologie de la perception* (1945).

### Core Ideas

- **The lived body** (*le corps propre*, the body one lives and is, as opposed to the body as an object of science): perception is not the passive reception of stimuli by a mind, but bodily engagement with the world.
- **Embodied subjectivity:** the subject is not a pure thinker housed in a body. Merleau-Ponty argues that consciousness is first a matter of what the body can do ("I can") before it is a matter of "I think."
- **Critique of two stances:** he criticized both empiricism (the person as a mechanism of stimulus and response) and intellectualism (the person as a constituting mind), seeking a third description in terms of embodied experience.
- **Phenomena of the body:** his analyses of habit, the phantom limb, body schema and motor intentionality are used by later theorists to discuss how trauma, illness or habit change the way the world appears.

Husserl's distinction between *Leib* (lived body) and *Körper* (physical body) and Sartre's account of the body in *Being and Nothingness* (1943) form the background to his work.

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Somatic therapy | Existential phenomenology |
|---|---|---|
| Origin | Clinical practice | Philosophical description |
| Aim | Change in bodily patterns, regulation, release of chronic tension | Describing and understanding embodied experience |
| Concept of the body | Tension, sensation, arousal, nervous system | The lived body, body schema, motor intentionality |
| Method | Breathing, movement, touch, tracking of sensation | Phenomenological description and reduction |
| Treatment of causation | Often explanatory (physiological or energetic) | Suspends causal explanation in favor of description |
| Evidence | Clinical and limited empirical research | Philosophical argument |

## Points of Convergence

1. **Rejection of strict mind-body dualism:** both treat mind and body as inseparable aspects of experience.
2. **Emphasis on the present:** somatic therapy works with current sensation; phenomenology describes how things currently appear.
3. **First-person orientation:** both ask how experience is lived from the inside.
4. **Process orientation:** both see experience as dynamic rather than static.

## Documented Links in Later Thought

- **Embodied cognition:** Varela, Thompson and Rosch's *The Embodied Mind* (1991) draws explicitly on Merleau-Ponty and links phenomenology with cognitive science and Buddhist meditation. (Francisco Varela, 1946-2001; Evan Thompson, born 1962; Eleanor Rosch, born 1938.) Shaun Gallagher's *How the Body Shapes the Mind* (2005) develops body schema and body image in this tradition.
- **Phenomenological psychiatry:** Thomas Fuchs and others use Merleau-Ponty's concepts to describe disturbances of embodiment in psychiatric conditions.
- **Phenomenological psychology:** the Duquesne University program (Adrian van Kaam, Amedeo Giorgi and colleagues) taught phenomenological methods in psychology from the 1950s and 1960s.
- **Focusing:** Eugene Gendlin (1926-2017), a philosopher and psychotherapist who worked with Carl Rogers, drew on phenomenology and developed the "felt sense" as bodily felt meaning (*Focusing*, 1978); his philosophical work engages phenomenological sources.
- **Daseinsanalysis:** Martin Heidegger's *Zollikon Seminars* (held 1959-1969 with the psychiatrist Medard Boss, published 1987) discuss bodily being (*Leiblichkeit*) in the clinical context, representing an existential approach that preceded most somatic therapy.
- **Dance/movement therapy:** pioneers such as Marian Chace (1896-1970) developed their methods from practice, though later authors have interpreted dance therapy through embodiment theory.

Some contemporary trauma and body-oriented authors invoke embodiment philosophy, but claims that specific methods (Somatic Experiencing, Hakomi, interpersonal neurobiology) derive from Merleau-Ponty should not be assumed without a citation by the author in question.

## Deeper Debates

- **Is the body one or two?** Merleau-Ponty keeps the distinction between lived body and objective body but treats them as moments of one embodied existence. Reich's energy theory treated the body as the seat of one continuous process of excitation.
- **Is "energy" a phenomenon or an explanation?** Phenomenology would treat talk of orgone or "energy" as an unsupported explanatory claim and prefer to describe sensations and patterns as lived. This is an interpretive point, and Reich did not engage with this critique.
- **Is trauma bodily or phenomenological?** Somatic therapists emphasize dysregulated arousal and defensive responses; phenomenologists emphasize altered ways of inhabiting the world. The two accounts can be complementary.
- **Does description guide intervention?** A frequent criticism is that phenomenology describes experience but does not itself offer methods of change, which somatic therapies provide.

## Limits of the Comparison

- The link between somatic therapy and existential phenomenology is a bridge built by later interpreters, not a documented history of influence.
- Phenomenology does not validate any particular therapeutic technique.
- Terms such as "embodied" are used loosely in contemporary psychology and should be checked against the specific author's usage.

## Clinical Relevance

- **Therapeutic listening:** attention to posture, breath and gesture is common to somatic and phenomenological approaches, and clinicians often describe it as part of attending to the person's lived experience.
- **Understanding symptoms:** disturbances such as chronic tension, panic or dissociation can be described both physiologically and as changes in how the body and world are experienced.
- This entry is descriptive and is not a guide to treatment.
