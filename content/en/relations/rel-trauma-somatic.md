# Trauma-Focused Therapy and Somatic Therapy: Somatic Experiencing as Bridge

Somatic Experiencing (SE), developed by Peter A. Levine from the 1970s, is the best-known attempt to treat trauma through bodily sensation and autonomic regulation rather than through detailed narrative exposure. It sits alongside the structured, trial-tested trauma-focused therapies (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, EMDR) and alongside other body-oriented methods. The relation is a combination of contrast in theory and partial convergence in practice, and the evidence base for the two sides differs substantially.

## Starting Point: Levine and Observations of Animal Defense

- **Peter A. Levine** (born 1942) holds doctorates in medical biophysics and in psychology. He has described his thinking as shaped by observation of how wild animals recover from threat and by work with trauma survivors, and by stress physiology.
- **The hypothesis:** in the face of overwhelming threat, humans, like other animals, mobilize defensive responses (fight, flight or freeze). If these responses are not completed, the arousal and immobility may persist and underlie symptoms. Levine's work uses the idea of discharging the "survival energy" that has been mobilized.
- Levine's best-known books are *Waking the Tiger* (1997) and *In an Unspoken Voice* (2010).
- The "energy" language is metaphorical and not measured physically. Arguments from animal behavior to human PTSD are analogies and not demonstrations.

## The Basic Hypotheses of SE

- Traumatic stress is held to involve autonomic nervous system dysregulation and incomplete defensive responses, in addition to (or more than) problems of memory and belief.
- Treatment attends to bodily sensation ("felt sense"), pendulation between activation and calm, and titration (working with small amounts of activation at a time) to avoid overwhelm.
- It does not require a detailed narrative of the traumatic event, which distinguishes it from prolonged exposure.

## Comparison with Other Trauma Therapies

| Dimension | PE (Foa) | CPT (Resick) | EMDR (Shapiro) | SE (Levine) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Main target | Fear structure and trauma memory | Beliefs about the trauma | Memory and associated beliefs and sensations | Bodily arousal and defensive responses |
| Technique | Imaginal and in vivo exposure | Cognitive restructuring, written accounts | Recall with bilateral stimulation | Sensation tracking, titration, pendulation |
| Typical length | About 8-15 sessions | About 12 sessions | Variable | Variable |
| Role of talk | Central | Central | Moderate | Secondary to bodily awareness |
| Detailed exposure | Essential | Limited | Indirect | Not required |
| Trial evidence | Extensive | Extensive | Extensive | Limited but growing |

## Points of Divergence

1. **Theory of trauma:** CBT models stress learned fear associations and appraisals; SE stresses autonomic and defensive-response physiology.
2. **Exposure:** PE, CPT (to a lesser extent) and EMDR involve engaging with the traumatic material. SE avoids full exposure and uses gradual contact with sensation.
3. **The concept of energy:** CBT has no such concept; SE's "survival energy" is not an established physical quantity.
4. **Evidence:** PE, CPT and EMDR have large randomized-trial evidence and guideline recommendations. SE has a smaller body of research: for example, a randomized trial in PTSD (Brom et al., 2017) and a trial in chronic low back pain with PTSD symptoms (Andersen et al., 2017), with systematic reviews noting that studies are few and methodologically limited.

## Points of Convergence

1. **Trauma is treated as real and as a cause of symptoms.**
2. **Graded approach:** both emphasize pacing, whether through hierarchy-based exposure or titration.
3. **Resources and safety:** stabilization and the development of coping resources precede or accompany trauma work in many models.
4. **The therapeutic relationship:** alliance in CBT, attunement in SE.
5. **Completion or processing:** CBT speaks of emotional processing, SE of completing a defensive response; both aim at the resolution of symptoms.

## Related Developments

- **Sensorimotor psychotherapy** (Pat Ogden; Ogden, Minton and Pain, 2006) combines bodily attention with psychodynamic, attachment and cognitive ideas and uses the "window of tolerance."
- **EMDR** (Francine Shapiro, 1948-2019): developed from 1987 and published in 1989. It is a structured protocol whose explanation is given in terms of an "adaptive information processing" model. The mechanisms of the eye movements are debated. EMDR is a trauma-focused treatment and is not a somatic therapy in the strict sense, though it attends to bodily sensation.
- **Window of tolerance** (Daniel Siegel, 1999): the zone of arousal in which a person can function and process experience, bounded by hyperarousal and hypoarousal. It is widely used in trauma therapies, including SE and sensorimotor approaches.
- **Polyvagal theory** (Stephen Porges, introduced 1994): proposes that autonomic states shape social engagement and defense. It is used by many somatic therapists (for example Deb Dana, *The Polyvagal Theory in Therapy*, 2018) but its physiological claims are disputed.
- **Tension and Trauma Releasing Exercises (TRE)** (David Berceli): a series of exercises intended to evoke muscular tremor; research evidence is limited.
- **Trauma-sensitive yoga** (David Emerson and colleagues, Trauma Center): a randomized trial in women with chronic treatment-resistant PTSD (van der Kolk and colleagues, 2014) reported reductions in symptoms compared with a control condition.
- **Structural integration** (Ida Rolf, 1896-1979) and other body-work traditions are historically related approaches to the body, though they are not trauma therapies and a direct line to SE should not be assumed.
- **Neuroscience of embodiment:** Antonio Damasio's *Descartes' Error* (1994) argued that bodily states are integral to emotion and reasoning, a view often cited by somatic therapists.
- **Popular synthesis:** Bessel van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014).

## Philosophical and Scientific Debates

- **Is trauma bodily or cognitive?** CBT emphasizes appraisal and memory; somatic models emphasize arousal and the body. Many contemporary clinicians treat these as complementary.
- **Is reprocessing necessary?** Trauma-focused therapies assume that processing the memory is necessary; SE proposes that regulation of the body is sufficient. Current evidence does not settle the question.
- **Is "energy" a scientific concept?** Physiological arousal can be measured (heart rate, cortisol, skin conductance), but "survival energy" as used in SE is a model-based metaphor, and critics regard it as unfalsifiable.
- **Does the body "keep the score"?** Procedural and implicit memory are well established; claims that tissues store literal traumatic memories are not.

## Contemporary Status

- **Guideline-recommended treatments:** trauma-focused CBT (including PE and CPT) and EMDR are recommended in major guidelines (for example NICE 2018, WHO 2013, APA 2017 with differences in strength).
- **Body-oriented methods:** SE, sensorimotor psychotherapy, Hakomi and others are widely practiced and taught, with less research evidence and no equivalent guideline status.
- **Training:** the training networks are separate (SE, EMDR, sensorimotor).

## Clinical Relevance

- Clinicians often combine approaches, for example stabilization with body-oriented work followed by trauma-focused processing in cases of complex or chronic trauma.
- The choice of approach depends on assessment, the person's preferences, and available evidence.
- This entry is descriptive and not a recommendation of treatment.

## Method versus Phenomenon

- CBT models are manualized and amenable to randomized trials; SE emphasizes a moment-to-moment, individualized process, which makes standardized research more difficult, though the existing trials show that it is possible.
- The practical conclusion is that the two groups differ in the strength of their evidence but are not mutually exclusive.

## Concluding Notes

- SE shifts the focus of trauma treatment from memory and belief to bodily regulation.
- EMDR and sensorimotor approaches form middle points between cognitive and bodily models.
- The polyvagal framework and the window of tolerance supply a widely used vocabulary, though not all of its claims are settled.
- The trend in practice is integration.
