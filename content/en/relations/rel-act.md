# ACT and the Third Wave

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy — ACT — is the existential school's most embarrassing neighbour. It says very similar things, it has the evidence, and it entered the health systems existential therapy never reached. Where existential therapy arrived at its recommendations through philosophy and the consulting room, ACT arrived at strikingly convergent ones through behaviour analysis, laboratory research, and randomised trials. The convergence is real, and it forces an uncomfortable question: is what ACT found simply what existential therapists were saying in another language — or something different that only looks similar?

## The Convergence

The overlap runs through the whole therapeutic stance. First, acceptance instead of fighting pain: the client is trained to make room for painful thoughts and feelings rather than struggling against them — experiential avoidance, which ACT identifies as the central driver of suffering. Second, values as a compass instead of symptom relief: the aim is a life lived in line with what the client cares about, not the elimination of symptoms. Third, self-as-context: a person is the place where thoughts and feelings occur, not their content — strikingly close to the existential description of consciousness as a horizon rather than a thing. Fourth, openness to painful experience, and fifth, the recognition that a measure of suffering belongs to human life itself. ACT's question — "what do you want your life to be about?" — is close to Frankl's about the values that give life meaning, and to the "ultimate concerns" Yalom placed at the centre of *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980). Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson set out the approach in *Acceptance and Commitment Therapy* (1999), reviewed in *Behaviour Research and Therapy* (2006); the second edition appeared in 2012.

## The Divergence

Yet the philosophical bases could hardly be further apart. ACT rests on functional contextualism, a philosophy of science developed by Hayes from the behaviour analysis of B. F. Skinner, and on Relational Frame Theory (RFT) — set out by Hayes, Dermot Barnes-Holmes, and Bryan Roche in *Relational Frame Theory* (2001) — which explains how language itself becomes a source of suffering, as thoughts are fused with and taken literally. The existential school rests on phenomenology — the description of lived experience as it presents itself, in the tradition of Heidegger and Sartre — deliberately avoiding operational definitions. The second divergence is methodological. Measurement, standardisation, and experimentation are at the core of ACT: protocols, questionnaires, and randomised trials are how the therapy knows itself and improves. In existential therapy they are marginal; the encounter and its interpretation carry the weight. A meta-analysis by A-Tjak and colleagues (2015) found ACT more effective than control conditions across a wide range of clinical problems, and broadly comparable to established treatments such as CBT — evidence of a kind and scale existential therapy has not produced. The two schools agree on much of what a therapist should do, while disagreeing on why it works, on what a human being is, and on how one knows it works.

## Why It Is Embarrassing

A school grown out of a tradition many existentialists reject — behaviourism, the psychology of rats and reinforcement schedules — reached recommendations existential therapists recognise as their own, produced wide evidence for them, and entered public health systems, university curricula, and clinical guidelines. ACT is not presented as a descendant of existential thought, and the similarity is best read as convergence in outcome rather than historical borrowing. The question now asked inside the existential field is unavoidable: if the same clinical wisdom can be reached by a method existential therapy regards as philosophically mistaken, what exactly does the existential philosophy add? One honest answer is "nothing that matters" — quietly gaining ground among practitioners who adopt ACT techniques in existential vocabulary.

## The Risk

Existential therapy could dissolve into the third wave of cognitive-behavioural therapies and survive only as its philosophical background — quotations in an ACT training manual, a decorative depth behind interventions carrying another name. The process is already visible: acceptance, values work, and mindfulness are now standard third-wave fare, and a therapist can practise them competently without ever reading a page of existential philosophy. If that is all that remains, the school has been absorbed rather than answered, and its distinctive claims — about death, about freedom as burden rather than opportunity, about the therapist as a fellow participant — will fade from the literature. The live encounter between the two traditions, the subject of a separate dialogue entry, resists this absorption — but the risk is real and already underway.

## The Opportunity

But there is also a genuine opportunity. The third wave needs what existential therapy has. ACT treats values as choices a client endorses and measures with questionnaires — but it has a thin account of what a value is, why one value should bind a person rather than another, and what meaning has to do with death and freedom. Existential thought offers depth on exactly these questions: Frankl's account of meaning as discovered rather than invented, Yalom's account of the givens that structure every choice, and the phenomenological description of living toward a value rather than merely holding one. The exchange would not be one-sided: existential therapy would gain a discipline of measurement and a habit of testing its claims, and ACT a richer account of value and meaning. Whether values in ACT are merely preferences, or demand a wider ethical horizon of the kind Frankl described, remains open — and it is where existential philosophy has the most to contribute. The neighbour is embarrassing — but embarrassment is also an invitation: to say clearly what the existential tradition knows that the third wave does not, and to learn, in return, how to show it.
