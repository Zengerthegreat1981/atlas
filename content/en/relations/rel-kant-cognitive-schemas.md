# Kantian Epistemology and Cognitive Schemas

In the *Critique of Pure Reason*, Immanuel Kant made a Copernican revolution by moving the centre of knowledge from the external object to the mental structure that organises it, one of the philosophical roots from which the theory of schemas in cognitive psychology drew. But the descent is not direct: the schema in psychology came through neural and clinical channels before it was linked to Kant.

## Perception as Inference: Ibn al-Haytham

This idea has a decisive Arab precursor. In the *Book of Optics* (*Kitab al-Manazir*, c. 1011–1021), Ibn al-Haytham showed that seeing is not accomplished by sensation alone, but is combined with discrimination and inference: a person perceives distance, size and shape by reference to prior experience and judgements formed by habit, and that is why visual error occurs. Abd al-Hamid Sabra edited this book and translated part of it (1989). Researchers have compared this with what Helmholtz called "unconscious inference," but the link between Ibn al-Haytham and Kant is not documented.

## Kant: Categories and Schematism

In the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781), Kant held that sensory intuitions are "blind" without concepts, and that the understanding organises experience through a priori categories (such as causality and substance) and the forms of intuition (time and space). Because the category is abstract and intuition is sensory, there is a mediating "transcendental schema" (the chapter on schematism) connected to time: the schema of causality is regular succession. What Kant meant by the "schema" is a rule for producing images, not a memory structure.

## From Kant to the Psychological Schema

The term entered psychology through several routes. Henry Head and Holmes (1911), in the "body schema," took it from the study of brain lesions; Frederic Bartlett, in *Remembering* (1932), used it to argue that memory is a construction organised by a prior schema rather than a copy of the original; and Jean Piaget, in *The Origins of Intelligence in Children* (1936), made it the unit of cognitive development. Then David Rumelhart (1980) formulated schema theory as "the building blocks of cognition" in cognitive psychology and artificial intelligence. It is not known that Bartlett derived the term from Kant, since he took it from Head's neurological research; Piaget, for his part, was a reader of Kant, but he formulated the concept from his observations of child development.

## The Predictive Brain

Proponents of predictive processing, such as Karl Friston (2010) and Andy Clark (2013), hold that the brain generates expectations about the world, compares them with sensory inputs and corrects errors, so that it resembles a computational model of a Kantian idea: no experience without a structure that organises it. This picture leads some to describe the brain as "Kantian." But the difference matters: Kant's structure is a priori, fixed and universal, whereas the models of the brain are acquired, revisable and vary between individuals.

## Limits

Statements that Kant is the "explicit philosophical foundation" of schema theory are an exaggeration, because Kant did not write about memory and learning, and because his schemas are not empirical. The more precise claim is that Kant formulated the idea that the mind adds its structure to experience, and that cognitive psychology reformulated it as a testable hypothesis. In clinical work, Aaron Beck introduced schemas as stable cognitive structures that shape interpretation in depression (*Depression*, 1967), and Jeffrey Young later developed schema therapy for entrenched maladaptive schemas (Young, Klosko, and Weishaar, 2003). Neither drew on Kant directly; their schemas come from the cognitive and clinical traditions described above, and any parallel with Kant is an interpretive comparison.
