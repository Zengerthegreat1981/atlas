# Buddhism and the Third Wave of Therapies: Importing Mindfulness from Vipassana to MBSR, ACT, and DBT

Mindfulness practice, which has roots in Buddhist meditation, entered clinical psychology and medicine from the late 1970s and became central to several approaches often grouped as the "third wave" of behavioural and cognitive therapies. The transfer is well documented, but how far the clinical programmes reproduce, adapt, or depart from Buddhist practice is disputed, and the programmes differ in how much they draw on Buddhism at all.

## The Buddhist Background

*Sati* (Pali; *smṛti* in Sanskrit) is a term of early Buddhism whose primary sense is recollection or keeping in mind. "Right mindfulness" (*sammā-sati*) is the seventh factor of the Noble Eightfold Path. The *Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta* (Majjhima Nikāya 10; a longer version in Dīgha Nikāya 22) describes four foundations of mindfulness: the body, feelings (*vedanā*), mind, and mental phenomena (*dhammas*). Meditation traditions such as Theravāda *vipassanā* (insight) and Zen develop attention and clear seeing, and in Buddhist teaching mindfulness works alongside ethical conduct (*sīla*) and wisdom (*paññā*), with the aim of liberation from suffering. Bhikkhu Bodhi's article "What Does Mindfulness Really Mean?" (2011) notes that the modern clinical sense of non-judgmental present-moment awareness is narrower than the traditional meaning.

## Four Transfers

**MBSR (1979).** Jon Kabat-Zinn (born 1944), who trained in molecular biology and practised Zen and insight meditation (his teachers included Philip Kapleau, Seung Sahn, and Thich Nhat Hanh, and he took part in retreats at the Insight Meditation Society), founded the Stress Reduction Clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center in 1979. Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction is an eight-week programme with weekly group sessions, an all-day retreat, and daily home practice, built around body scan, sitting meditation, and mindful movement. It deliberately uses secular language and omits Buddhist doctrinal content. Kabat-Zinn's book *Full Catastrophe Living* (1990) popularised it, and his 2003 article "Mindfulness-Based Interventions in Context" describes his rationale.

**MBCT (1990s).** Zindel Segal, Mark Williams, and John Teasdale adapted MBSR together with cognitive-therapy elements for people at risk of relapse into depression (*Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression*, 2002). In a large trial, MBCT with support to taper medication was found not inferior to maintenance antidepressant medication in preventing relapse (Kuyken et al., 2015, *The Lancet*), and an individual-patient-data meta-analysis (Kuyken et al., 2016, *JAMA Psychiatry*) found reduced relapse risk compared with usual care or non-MBCT comparators. British guidance recommends MBCT for people in remission from recurrent depression.

**ACT (1980s-1999).** Steven C. Hayes (born 1948) developed Acceptance and Commitment Therapy from contextual behavioural science and Relational Frame Theory, a behavioural account of language and cognition; the main manual, by Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson, appeared in 1999. ACT's emphasis on acceptance, defusion from thoughts, and values resembles themes in contemplative traditions, and Hayes has acknowledged such resonances, but ACT was built from behavioural theory and is not derived from Buddhist doctrine.

**DBT (1993).** Marsha Linehan (born 1943) developed Dialectical Behavior Therapy for chronic suicidality and borderline personality disorder. Its skills training covers mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. Linehan has said that her own Zen practice informed the combination of acceptance and change at the heart of DBT. DBT is among the best-evidenced treatments for this population.

## Comparison

| Dimension | Buddhist practice | MBSR / MBCT | ACT | DBT |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aim | Liberation from suffering | Stress reduction; relapse prevention in depression | Psychological flexibility and valued living | Emotion regulation and a life worth living |
| Ethical framework | Precepts (*sīla*) integral | Not taught as doctrine | Values clarification | Skills-based |
| Metaphysics (karma, rebirth) | Part of the teaching | Excluded | Excluded | Excluded |
| Format | Lifelong practice with a teacher and community | Time-limited group course | Time-limited therapy | Structured, often year-long programme |
| Setting | Monastery, centre | Clinic, hospital | Clinic | Clinic |

## What Was Kept and What Changed

Kept: the practice of observing experience with attention and without immediate reaction, breath and body-based meditation, and the stance toward thoughts and feelings as passing events. Changed or dropped: the ethical and doctrinal context, the aim of liberation, the teacher-lineage, and the monastic or lay community. Clinical programmes also add measurement, manuals, and defined outcomes.

## Critiques

- **From Buddhist scholars and practitioners.** Bhikkhu Bodhi (2011) and others argue that detaching mindfulness from ethics and wisdom changes its meaning. Ronald Purser and David Loy's essay "Beyond McMindfulness" (2013) and Purser's book *McMindfulness* (2019) criticise the commercialisation of mindfulness and its use to help people adapt to stressful workplaces. Robert Sharf's "Is Mindfulness Buddhist? (And Why It Matters)" (2015) argues that clinical mindfulness and Buddhist practice should not be equated.
- **From the research literature.** Van Dam and colleagues' "Mind the Hype" (2018) argued that claims for mindfulness outran the evidence, noting problems with definition, measurement, and study quality. Reports of adverse effects of meditation have prompted calls for careful screening and supervision, particularly for people with trauma or psychosis.
- **Defences.** Kabat-Zinn and others reply that mindfulness is a universal human capacity that the Buddhist tradition articulated rather than owns, and that secular presentation widens access.

## Limits

This entry compares clinical programmes with Buddhist sources; it does not establish that the programmes are Buddhist, nor that they are not. Clinical use of mindfulness is evidence-supported for some conditions and not others. Mindfulness-based programmes are not suitable for every person and are not a substitute for individual clinical assessment.
