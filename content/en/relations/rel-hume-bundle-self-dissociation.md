# Hume's Bundle of Perceptions and Dissociative Disorders

In *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739), David Hume argued that the "self" is not a fixed substance but a succession of perceptions and impressions, with no permanent center that unifies them. This philosophical thesis is compared conceptually with what dissociation diagnoses describe, such as dissociative identity disorder and depersonalization disorder, which involve a disturbance of the unified sense of self. The comparison is speculative: no primary text links the two.

## Hume: "I cannot find a self"

In Book I of the *Treatise* (Part 4, Section 6), Hume says that when he enters into what he calls his innermost self, he finds nothing but a particular perception: heat or cold, pain or pleasure, and never catches himself without a perception. The mind is "a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity," and the connection between them is attributed to imagination and memory. However, in the Appendix (1740) he retracted, admitting that he cannot find a solution to explain the unity of these perceptions. Derek Parfit, in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), developed this line into a theory that downplays the importance of personal identity, and Thomas Metzinger, in *Being No One* (2003), brought it back into neuroscience.

## The older answer: Ibn Sina's suspended man

Ibn Sina preemptively opposed any theory that reduces the self to successive sensory perceptions. In *al-Shifa'* (the book on the soul), he imagined a human being created all at once, suspended in the air, unable to see or touch, who nonetheless affirms his own existence: the soul knows itself without the mediation of perception. Michael Marmura studied the philosophical context of this argument (1986). The contrast between Hume and Ibn Sina is thus a contrast between an empiricism that denies an independent self and a rationalism that affirms it through direct intuition.

## Dissociation in the clinic

In DSM-5 (2013; retained in DSM-5-TR, 2022), dissociation includes dissociative identity disorder, depersonalization/derealization disorder, and dissociative amnesia. In depersonalization, the patient feels estranged from himself or observes himself from outside, while reality testing remains intact, as Mauricio Sierra describes in *Depersonalization: A New Look at a Neglected Syndrome* (2009). In dissociative identity disorder, self-states multiply with gaps in memory. Putnam described it in 1989, and Ian Hacking in *Rewriting the Soul* (1995) questioned it, analyzing the influence of culture and therapists in its making.

## Where the comparison fails

The difference is fundamental. Hume describes the experience of every human being if examined carefully, whereas dissociation is a clinical disorder. A person with depersonalization does not "discover" a Humean truth; he suffers a deficit in the "sense that the experience is mine," which Sass and Parnas (2003) describe in schizophrenia. Dan Zahavi, in *Subjectivity and Selfhood* (2005), builds on this to argue that the minimal self, the pre-reflective awareness that the experience is mine, is a pillar that is shaken in the disorder, so the bundle of perceptions is not enough to explain it. In this way clinical cases are used as an argument against Hume, not as support for him. The link between the two therefore becomes a point of philosophical discussion more than an empirical finding.
