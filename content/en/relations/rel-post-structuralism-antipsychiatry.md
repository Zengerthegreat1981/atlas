# Post-Structuralism and Anti-Psychiatry: From Foucault to Szasz

Post-structuralist thought (Foucault, Derrida, Deleuze and Guattari) and the anti-psychiatry movement (Laing, Cooper, Szasz, Basaglia) overlapped in a critique of psychiatric institutions and of psychiatry's claims to knowledge, but they were distinct currents with different methods, politics, and aims. They are not a single lineage. In particular, Szasz was a libertarian critic of coercive psychiatry who did not belong to the post-structuralist tradition, and Foucault did not identify himself as an anti-psychiatrist. This entry compares the two currents and does not assert that one derives from the other.

## Post-Structuralism

**Michel Foucault** (1926-1984), in *Folie et déraison: Histoire de la folie à l'âge classique* (1961), traced how "madness" came to be constituted as an object of medical knowledge and confinement, from the "Great Confinement" of the seventeenth century to the birth of the asylum. Later works developed related themes:

- *Naissance de la clinique* (1963) on the medical gaze.
- *Surveiller et punir* (1975) on disciplinary power.
- *La Volonté de savoir* (1976), the first volume of the *History of Sexuality*, on biopower and the production of sexual discourse.
- *Le Pouvoir psychiatrique* (lectures of 1973-74, published 2003) on psychiatric power.

Foucault's central claims concern the historical constitution of categories and the relation of power and knowledge, not a denial that distress exists.

**Jacques Derrida** (1930-2004) developed deconstruction (*De la grammatologie*, 1967; *L'écriture et la différence*, 1967), which questions binary oppositions and the stability of meaning.

**Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari**, in *L'Anti-Œdipe* (1972) and *Mille plateaux* (1980), criticized psychoanalytic Oedipal explanation and proposed "schizoanalysis" and a view of desire as productive.

## Anti-Psychiatry

- **R. D. Laing** (1927-1989): *The Divided Self* (1960) offered an existential-phenomenological account of schizophrenia and "ontological insecurity"; *The Politics of Experience* (1967) and work on families explored how distress can be understood in its interpersonal context.
- **David Cooper** (1931-1986): *Psychiatry and Anti-Psychiatry* (1967) introduced the term "anti-psychiatry."
- **Thomas Szasz** (1920-2012): *The Myth of Mental Illness* (1961) argued that "mental illness" is a metaphor and that many conditions called psychiatric are "problems in living"; *The Manufacture of Madness* (1970) compared psychiatric coercion with historical persecutions. Szasz opposed involuntary psychiatric treatment on liberal-libertarian grounds. His claims are contested, and mainstream psychiatry rejects the thesis that psychiatric disorders are not real conditions.
- **Franco Basaglia** (1924-1980): led the Italian reform movement that culminated in Law 180 (1978), which phased out the old psychiatric hospitals in favor of community services. His book *L'istituzione negata* (1968) described the Gorizia experience.

## Points of Comparison

| Post-structuralist theme | Anti-psychiatric theme | Point of contact |
|---|---|---|
| Madness as historically constituted (Foucault) | Critique of the concept of mental illness (Szasz) | Skepticism toward fixed medical essence; different arguments |
| Power/knowledge (Foucault) | Psychiatric coercion (Szasz, Basaglia) | Psychiatry as an institution of social control |
| Critique of psychoanalytic Oedipus (Deleuze and Guattari) | Laing's critique of family and diagnosis | Dissatisfaction with dominant clinical explanations |
| Discipline and the asylum (Foucault) | Institutional critique (Goffman, Basaglia) | Confinement as social practice |

## Essential Differences

| Dimension | Foucault | Szasz | Laing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Method | Historical genealogy and archaeology | Philosophical and legal-libertarian argument | Existential-phenomenological and clinical |
| Madness | Historically constituted object | A metaphor; "illness" applies only to bodily disease | A comprehensible response within relationships |
| Politics | Critique of power, no program | Libertarian | Radical left |
| Practical orientation | Genealogical critique | Abolition of coercion | Therapeutic communities (the Philadelphia Association) |

## The Derrida-Foucault Exchange

Derrida's "Cogito et histoire de la folie" (a 1963 lecture, published 1964 and reprinted in *L'écriture et la différence*, 1967) criticized Foucault's reading of Descartes's treatment of madness in the *First Meditation*, and questioned whether a history of madness could be written without remaining within the language of reason. Foucault replied in 1971 in "Mon corps, ce papier, ce feu," an appendix to later editions of *Histoire de la folie*, accusing Derrida of reducing discourse to texts. The exchange is a landmark dispute over method and not directly about psychiatry.

## Influence

- Foucault's work shaped critical psychiatry, sociology, and the history of medicine, for example Nikolas Rose's *Governing the Soul* (1990) and the critical psychiatry volumes edited by David Ingleby (1981).
- Judith Butler's *The Psychic Life of Power* (1997) combines Foucault, Freud, and Hegel.
- The Italian reform of 1978 is the best-known policy outcome of anti-psychiatric ideas.

## Limits

Both currents have been criticized for romanticizing madness, for lacking clinical alternatives, and for underestimating the burden of severe mental illness. Szasz's thesis in particular is rejected by most psychiatrists, and Foucault's historical claims have been challenged by historians.
