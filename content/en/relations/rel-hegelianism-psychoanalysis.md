# Hegelianism and Psychoanalysis

The relation between Hegelian philosophy and psychoanalysis, especially in its Lacanian and Frankfurt School extensions, is one of the deepest bridges between philosophy and psychology. The dialectic of recognition and the struggle between master and slave became a key to understanding desire and the self's division. Most importantly, this bridge was not built by Freud, but by a French reading of Hegel in the 1930s and after.

## Kojève and Hyppolite: Hegel in France

Between 1933 and 1939, Alexandre Kojève gave lectures on the "Phenomenology of Spirit" at the École Pratique des Hautes Études in Paris. Among those who attended were Lacan, Bataille, Merleau-Ponty, and Raymond Aron. The lectures were published as *Introduction to the Reading of Hegel* (1947). Kojève read Hegel anthropologically, making desire, recognition, and the struggle for status the motor of history. Jean Hyppolite offered another reading in *Genesis and Structure of Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit* (1946). In 1954 Lacan invited him to his seminar to comment on Freud's essay "Negation" (*Die Verneinung*, 1925), in which Freud used the word *Aufhebung*. This commentary was published within Lacan's *Écrits* (1966).

## Desire and Recognition in Lacan

In the essay "The Subversion of the Subject and the Dialectic of Desire" (1960, in *Écrits*), Lacan tied desire to the Other: the human being desires to be desired, desires what the Other desires, and the ego is formed only through the mirror of the Other and then through language and the symbol. This is a direct extension of Kojève's claim that human desire is "desire for desire." Yet Lacan did not take the end of the Hegelian dialectic: for him the self-division remains, and desire never ends in satisfaction, whereas phenomenology moves toward "absolute knowledge."

## Negation and Sublimation

Hegel's concept of negativity (*Negativität*) intersects with repression: both show that consciousness is formed through the negation of what it excludes. The link is drawn between Hegel's *Aufhebung*, meaning negation with preservation and elevation, and Freudian sublimation and negation. Herbert Marcuse treated Hegel in *Reason and Revolution* (1941), before writing *Eros and Civilization* (1955), and from him the thread passed into the Frankfurt School. In *The Sublime Object of Ideology* (1989) and *Less Than Nothing* (2012), Slavoj Žižek made Hegel and Lacan the philosophers of the divided subject.

## Limits of the Bridge

Freud did not read Hegel and was not influenced by him, and he claimed ignorance of philosophy. The bridge is therefore Lacanian-French, not Freudian. Kojève's own reading also calls for caution: it is a selective reading that ignores Hegel's logic, religion, and the state. In *Anti-Oedipus* (1972), Deleuze and Guattari objected to the Hegelian-Oedipal analysis that reduces desire to lack, and they formulated a productive desire. In the clinic, Hegel provides no technique; his influence lies primarily in the conception of the self and of recognition, not in procedures. The closest example is the conception of the therapeutic relationship as mutual recognition in Jessica Benjamin and Axel Honneth.
