# Daseinsanalysis and Heideggerian Phenomenology: From "Being and Time" to the Clinic

One of the best-documented transfers of philosophy into clinical psychiatry: **Daseinsanalysis** draws on Heidegger's existential phenomenology. Two figures are central: **Ludwig Binswanger** (1881-1966), who from the 1920s and 1930s developed a phenomenological-anthropological psychiatry (*Daseinsanalyse*) influenced by Husserl and, increasingly, by Heidegger's *Being and Time*; and **Medard Boss** (1903-1990), a Swiss psychiatrist who corresponded and later collaborated with Heidegger and developed a stricter Heideggerian version. Neither was a formal student of Heidegger. Boss met him in the late 1940s, and from 1959 to 1969 Heidegger gave the **Zollikon Seminars** at Boss's invitation to Swiss psychiatrists (published in German in 1987).

## The Philosophical Context: Heidegger

In *Sein und Zeit* (1927; *Being and Time*), Heidegger analyzed human existence as **Dasein**:

- **Being-in-the-world** (*In-der-Welt-sein*): no primary separation between subject and object.
- **Possibility:** Dasein is characterized by its potentials-for-being rather than a fixed essence.
- **Anxiety (*Angst*):** a mood that discloses the groundlessness of everyday existence.
- **Being-toward-death:** the recognition of finitude as constitutive of authentic existence.
- **The "they" (*das Man*):** the everyday, anonymous mode of existing.
- **Temporality:** existence as stretched between past, future, and present.
- **Care (*Sorge*)** as the fundamental structure of Dasein.

## Medard Boss

Boss, trained in medicine and psychoanalysis, sought to reconstruct psychotherapy on Heideggerian foundations. His principal works include *Sinn und Gehalt der sexuellen Perversionen* (1947), *Der Traum und seine Auslegung* (1953; English *The Analysis of Dreams*), *Psychoanalyse und Daseinsanalytik* (1957), and *Grundriss der Medizin und der Psychologie* (1971; English *Existential Foundations of Medicine and Psychology*). Boss rejected the Freudian unconscious as a hypothesized mental entity and understood symptoms and dreams as ways of existing in the world to be understood on their own terms.

## Ludwig Binswanger

Binswanger directed the Bellevue Sanatorium in Kreuzlingen and remained a lifelong friend of Freud, writing *Erinnerungen an Sigmund Freud* (1956). His works include *Traum und Existenz* (1930), *Grundformen und Erkenntnis menschlichen Daseins* (1942), and *Drei Formen missglückten Daseins* (1956), on *Verstiegenheit* (extravagance), *Verschrobenheit* (eccentricity), and *Manieriertheit* (manneredness). English selections appear in *Being-in-the-World* (1963).

Binswanger and Boss differ. Binswanger drew on Husserl and Heidegger and added dimensions such as love and "being-with" (*Miteinandersein*), for which Heidegger faulted him in the Zollikon context. Boss held closer to Heidegger's own analysis of Dasein and criticized Binswanger's departures.

## Borrowed Clinical Concepts

| Heideggerian concept | Use in Daseinsanalysis |
|---|---|
| Being-in-the-world | The patient is understood as a world-relating existence rather than a mind in a body |
| Angst | Understood as disclosing existence, not only as a symptom |
| Being-toward-death | Finitude as a dimension of meaning |
| *Das Man* | Absorption in anonymous norms as a mode of living |
| Temporality | Disturbed temporal structures in melancholia and mania (Binswanger) |
| Disclosure and truth (*alētheia*) | Therapy as helping what is concealed show itself |

## The Clinical Stance

- The therapist aims to describe and understand how the patient lives rather than to interpret hidden causes.
- Phenomena (experiences, dreams, bodily states) are taken as they show themselves.
- Binswanger analyzed existence through world-structures and spatial-temporal themes (for example *Verstiegenheit*, a "high-flown" mode of being that loses footing).

## Essential Differences

| Dimension | Heidegger | Boss | Binswanger |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aim | Fundamental ontology | Clinical application of Heidegger's analysis | Phenomenological-anthropological psychiatry |
| Freud | Marginal | Rereads and revises Freud existentially | Close friend; respectful critique |
| Unconscious | No Freudian concept | Rejects it as a hypothesized entity | Reinterprets it phenomenologically |
| Technique | None | Descriptive attention, minimal interpretation | Case-based phenomenological analysis |

## Related Figures

- **Rollo May** (1909-1994) and colleagues edited *Existence* (1958), which introduced Binswanger and European existential psychiatry to American readers; May's own thought drew on several sources, including Tillich and Kierkegaard.
- **Erwin Straus** (1891-1975) wrote *The Primary World of Senses* (English 1963) in the phenomenological tradition.
- **Alfried Längle**'s *Existenzanalyse*, which developed from Viktor Frankl's logotherapy, is a separate school and should not be conflated with Daseinsanalysis.

## Critique and Limits

- The complexity of Heidegger's language limits clinical accessibility.
- There is little empirical outcome research; the approach emphasizes understanding over measurement.
- Training in a rigorous phenomenological attitude is difficult to standardize.
- Heidegger's own political record is a separate and contested matter.
