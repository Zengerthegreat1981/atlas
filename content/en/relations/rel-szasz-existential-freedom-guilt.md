# Szasz and Existentialism: Freedom and Moral Responsibility

A critical comparison between Thomas Szasz and existentialism on freedom and moral responsibility, in the context of psychiatry.

## Capacity and responsibility in Islamic jurisprudence

The question of the relationship between madness and responsibility is old in Islamic jurisprudence. A hadith narrated by Abu Dawud and others says: "The pen is lifted from three: from the sleeper until he awakens, from the child until he reaches puberty, and from the insane person until he regains reason." Jurists built the capacity for legal obligation (*taklif*) on reason, and treated the loss of reason as an excuse that removes liability. Michael Dols studied this legal status of the madman in *Majnun: The Madman in Medieval Islamic Society* (Clarendon Press, 1992). This corresponds to Szasz's argument in reverse: jurisprudence treats the madman as not obligated, in order to protect him, whereas Szasz holds that a claim of non-responsibility due to mental illness strips a person of dignity and justifies coercion.

## Thomas Szasz's position

Thomas Szasz (1920-2012) published *The Myth of Mental Illness* (Hoeber-Harper, 1961), arguing that what is called mental illness consists of "problems in living," not illnesses in the medical sense, because illness requires a proven structural lesion, and psychiatric diagnosis is a moral and social judgment dressed in medical clothing. In *Law, Liberty, and Psychiatry* (Macmillan, 1963) and then *Psychiatric Justice* (1965), he criticized involuntary commitment and the extension of madness. He is a classical liberal, drawing on John Stuart Mill and Ludwig von Mises, and sees the individual as responsible for his actions. He repeatedly rejected being labeled an "antipsychiatrist," distinguished himself from Laing and Cooper, and argued that antipsychiatry produces a new quackery, as in his book *Antipsychiatry: Quackery Squared* (Syracuse University Press, 2009).

## Existentialism: freedom and guilt

By contrast, existentialism in Sartre holds that the human being is "condemned to be free," and that denying one's freedom is "bad faith," so responsibility is absolute. In Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927, sections 56-60), existential guilt (*Schuldig-sein*) is a structure of Dasein itself, not linked to an act that violates a rule: the human being is guilty because he is thrown into a being whose ground he does not possess, and he chooses by excluding possibilities. Laing, in *The Divided Self* (1960), draws on existentialism to understand psychosis as a meaningful response to an unbearable situation.

## Shared ground

The two sides converge in rejecting the reduction of psychological suffering to a single organic cause; in restoring the meaning a person gives to his life; in criticizing institutional psychiatry when it becomes an instrument of control; and in emphasizing choice and responsibility. Existential therapists such as Yalom, in *Existential Psychotherapy* (Basic Books, 1980), use the language of responsibility in a therapeutic form, but they do not share Szasz's rejection of psychiatry or his denial of mental illness.

## Key differences

The first difference lies in the source of commitment: Szasz builds on political liberal freedom and the individual's right to be left alone by the state, while existentialism builds on the structure of being and the individual's commitment to meaning. The second difference concerns guilt: Szasz's writing stresses personal accountability and the moral character of psychiatric categories, whereas for Heidegger guilt (*Schuldig-sein*) is a constitutive structure of existence and not a matter of blame for particular acts. The third difference concerns freedom: Szasz's freedom is freedom from external coercion, that is, from the state, whereas Sartre's freedom is an ontological claim that arises from the absence of a fixed human essence and, in his account, persists even under constraint (the prisoner remains free to attempt escape or to take an attitude toward his situation). It is not known that Szasz officially adopted any existential framework, so the bridge is a comparative measure that he himself does not attribute.

## Limits

Against Szasz's thesis stands the fact that severe disorders such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder have substantial biological contributions documented by genetic and neurobiological research, so critics argue they cannot be reduced to "problems in living." Szasz himself accepted that conditions with demonstrable brain pathology are diseases, but classed them as neurological and not as mental. Sartre's absolute individual freedom is also open to criticism, notably by Beauvoir, for neglecting the social situation. The combination of Szasz and existentialism is therefore best understood as an intersection in protest against reductionism, not as an agreement in theory.

## Documented exchanges with existential psychotherapists

The best-documented exchange is with R. D. Laing, an existential psychiatrist, rather than a private correspondence with later existential therapists such as Yalom. Francisco Balbuena Rivera reports that in the mid-1970s Szasz published a sharp critique of Laing in a series of pieces in *The New Review*. Laing chose not to answer in that forum. Leon Redler replied for the Philadelphia Association, the therapeutic community associated with Laing. Balbuena's later paper concludes that colleagues around Laing would have preferred a meeting in person, that Szasz did not see Laing when he was in London, and that Laing still did not answer the criticisms in public or in print. The dispute was about how to understand breakdown and what role professionals and institutions should have, and each side, on Balbuena's reading, misstated the other.

Laing did review three of Szasz's books together in the *New Statesman* on 20 July 1979, under the title "Round the bend." Szasz returned to Laing in a 2004 essay in *The Psychoanalytic Review*, "Knowing What Ain't So," and again in *Antipsychiatry: Quackery Squared* (2009), after Laing's death in 1989. Daniel Burston's essay "Szasz, Laing and Existential Psychotherapy" treats the pair as sharing criticisms of institutional psychiatry while differing as libertarian and existentialist. Szasz himself wrote that existential philosophers had influenced his views of psychotherapy. These are published attacks, a review, and later commentary. They are not a joint clinical programme.
