# Elyn Saks: From Lived Experience of Schizophrenia to Mental Health Law Scholarship

Elyn Saks's life story combines a lived experience of schizophrenia with a legal specialization that established itself in major American universities. This connection shows how the clinical and the legal meet around a single question: when may the state restrict a person's liberty because of a mental illness?

## From Lived Experience to Law

Elyn Saks is a professor of law, psychology, and psychiatry at the University of Southern California, and a MacArthur Fellow (2009). In her memoir *The Center Cannot Hold* (2007), she described her journey with schizophrenia, from her studies at Oxford and then Yale Law School, through her hospitalizations, physical restraint, and forced medication. She drew on this experience in her academic work, including her book *Refusing Care: Forced Treatment and the Rights of the Mentally Ill* (2002), which examined the patient's right to refuse medication. She founded the Saks Institute for Mental Health Law, Policy, and Ethics at the USC Gould School of Law.

## The Central Legal Questions

Saks's work converges on three intersections. The first is involuntary civil commitment. In *O'Connor v. Donaldson* (1975), the US Supreme Court held that the state may not confine a person with a mental illness who poses no danger and can live safely in the community. In *Addington v. Texas* (1979), the Court required "clear and convincing" evidence for commitment. The second is the right to refuse treatment, addressed in federal appeals court cases such as *Rogers v. Okin* (1st Cir. 1980) and *Rennie v. Klein* (3d Cir. 1983). The third is the insanity defense in criminal law, studied by Michael Perlin in *The Jurisprudence of the Insanity Defense* (1994). The international dimension is represented by the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006), whose Articles 12 and 14 affirm legal capacity and prohibit deprivation of liberty on the basis of disability alone, which calls national laws on involuntary commitment into question.

## A View from Islamic Jurisprudence

The Islamic tradition has an early treatment of these questions. The hadith "The pen is lifted from three: from the sleeper until he awakes, from the child until he reaches puberty, and from the insane until he regains his reason" (related by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi) established that the loss of reason lifts legal obligation, and jurists built on it the rules of legal capacity, guardianship (*hajr*), and criminal responsibility. Michael Dols, in *Majnun: The Madman in Medieval Islamic Society* (1992), studied how jurisprudence and medicine treated the insane, and how guardianship coexisted with care in the *bimaristan*. This point meets the modern insanity defense, but Islamic jurisprudence did not know the American model of civil commitment or of refusal of medication.

## Impact and Debate

Saks appeared as a rare witness combining legal competence and lived experience, and her influence has been felt in discourse on "responsibility and freedom" that rejects the automatic presumption of incapacity upon illness. Her position is nevertheless not free of disagreement. She benefited from intensive psychoanalytic treatment and from medication that enabled her professional success, which some regard as a model that cannot be generalized, while others maintain that her argument is that a patient's capacity for decision-making is measurable and varies, and that restrictions must be imposed only to the extent necessary. The tension remains between protecting the patient from danger to themselves and their freedom to err.
