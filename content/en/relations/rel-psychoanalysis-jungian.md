# Psychoanalysis and Jungian Analysis: A Founding Schism

One of the best-known breaks in the history of psychology. Carl Gustav Jung (1875-1961) was for several years Freud's chosen heir and the first president of the International Psychoanalytical Association (IPA). The personal and theoretical rupture of 1912-1914 led Jung to found what he called analytical psychology. The two traditions have since developed separately, with distinct institutions, vocabularies and clinical practices.

## Before the Schism: The Founding Friendship (1900-1911)

- **1900:** Freud publishes *The Interpretation of Dreams* (title page dated 1900). Jung, working at the Burghölzli clinic in Zurich under Eugen Bleuler, studied it and applied Freudian ideas in his own association research.
- **1906:** Freud and Jung begin to correspond after Jung sends Freud his work on word association.
- **1907:** the two meet for the first time in Vienna; accounts describe an extended conversation of about thirteen hours.
- **1909:** both travel to the United States to lecture at Clark University (Worcester, Massachusetts). Freud regarded Jung as the non-Jewish, non-Viennese successor who could carry psychoanalysis beyond its Viennese circle, and referred in letters to him as his "crown prince."
- **1910:** at the Nuremberg Congress the IPA is founded and Jung is elected its president, with Freud's strong backing.

## The Schism (1911-1914)

- **1911-1912:** Jung publishes *Wandlungen und Symbole der Libido* in two parts (English: *Psychology of the Unconscious*, 1916; later revised as *Symbols of Transformation*). The work, which treats myth and fantasy as expressions of general psychic energy and not only of infantile sexual wishes, made the theoretical disagreement visible. Freud rejected Jung's broadened concept of libido.
- **1912:** Jung's Fordham University lectures in New York set out his divergences publicly.
- **1913:** the personal correspondence ends; the Munich Congress of that year is the last at which they met. Jung resigns as editor of the *Jahrbuch*.
- **1914:** Jung resigns the presidency of the IPA (April) and leaves the association (July). In the same year Freud publishes "On the History of the Psycho-Analytic Movement," a polemical account that criticizes Jung and Adler.

The correspondence is available in *The Freud/Jung Letters*, edited by William McGuire (1974).

## Essential Theoretical Differences

| Dimension | Freud (psychoanalysis) | Jung (analytical psychology) |
|---|---|---|
| Libido | Sexual drive energy (broadened but rooted in sexuality) | General psychic energy |
| The unconscious | Personal unconscious (repressed material) | Personal unconscious plus the collective unconscious |
| Structural concepts | Id, ego, superego | Ego, persona, shadow, anima/animus, Self |
| Dreams | Disguised wish fulfillment; manifest and latent content | Compensatory and prospective functions; symbolic expression of the whole psyche |
| Sexuality | Central to development and neurosis | One important instinctual domain among others |
| Developmental aim | Resolution of conflict, capacity to love and work | Individuation: integration of conscious and unconscious |
| Myth and symbol | Expressions of disguised wishes and conflicts | Expressions of archetypal patterns |
| Technique | Free association, interpretation of transference | Amplification, active imagination, dialogue with images |

## Points of Divergence

1. **The unconscious:** both affirm it, but Jung enlarged it with the collective unconscious and archetypes (terms he developed in the late 1910s).
2. **Sexuality:** central for Freud; for Jung, libido is a general energy and sexuality is only one of its channels.
3. **Dreams:** Jung did not regard the manifest content as simply a disguise and used amplification (comparing images with mythic and cultural parallels) alongside personal association.
4. **Transference:** Freud treated it as repetition of early relationships to be analyzed. Jung's *Psychology of the Transference* (1946) interprets it through alchemical symbolism as a process involving both partners.

## Points of Convergence

1. **The unconscious as central:** both place unconscious processes at the center of explanation.
2. **Association:** Jung's own word-association method helped to lend experimental support to Freud's ideas and later shaped his concept of the complex.
3. **Dreams as primary material:** both read dreams, although in different ways.
4. **Depth orientation:** both practice long-term exploratory therapy.
5. **Early experience:** both recognize the influence of early experience, though Jung added inherited patterns.
6. **Shared critique:** both stand apart from approaches that restrict psychology to observable behavior.

## Relation to Other Psychoanalytic Departures

- **Adler** left the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society in 1911 and founded Individual Psychology; he and Jung left Freud separately and developed very different systems.
- **Rank** moved away from Freud in the 1920s after *The Trauma of Birth* (1924).
- **Lacan** presented his work as a "return to Freud" from the 1950s onward, in contrast to Jung's explicit departure.
- Jungian thought influenced later currents such as James Hillman's archetypal psychology (from the 1970s).

## Philosophical and Scientific Debates

- **Is the collective unconscious a psychic reality?** Jung held that archetypal structures are inherited predispositions that appear across cultures. Critics argue that the concept is difficult to test and that cross-cultural similarities may be explained by shared human experience and cultural transmission.
- **Are archetypes independent of individual experience?** Jung distinguished the archetype as such (a pattern) from archetypal images shaped by culture. Freudians tend to derive such images from individual and family experience.
- **Is individuation a lifelong process?** Jung described individuation as a process extending across life, particularly in its second half. Classical psychoanalysis tends to define therapeutic aims in terms of resolving conflicts rather than a developmental goal of wholeness.

## Institutional Status

- **IAAP** (International Association for Analytical Psychology), founded in 1955, is the main international body of Jungian analysts.
- **IPA** remains the international body of Freudian and post-Freudian psychoanalysis. The two organizations have separate training standards.
- Jung's *Psychological Types* (1921) was a major theoretical work published after the break; his *Red Book* (*Liber Novus*) was published only in 2009.

## Dialogue in the Clinic Today

- Some contemporary analysts, particularly in relational and intersubjective traditions, show renewed interest in dreams, symbol and the analyst's participation in the analytic field, themes that have affinities with Jungian thought.
- Jungian clinical practice uses active imagination, dream work and sandplay therapy (the latter developed by Dora Kalff on the basis of Jungian ideas).
- The choice of framework shapes formulation: a Freudian clinician will tend to look for conflict and repetition of early relationships, a Jungian clinician for symbolic and compensatory movement toward integration.
