# Humanistic-Existential and Gestalt Therapy: Shared Phenomenological Roots

Gestalt therapy and humanistic-existential psychology were not opponents. They emerged in the same mid-twentieth-century climate and share an emphasis on immediate experience, awareness, responsibility, and the rejection of purely mechanistic accounts of persons. Their relationship to phenomenology differs in directness, however, and academic Gestalt psychology (Berlin) and Perls's Gestalt therapy are distinct enterprises: Perls borrowed the vocabulary of figure and ground and some field-theoretical ideas, but the founders of academic Gestalt psychology did not regard his therapy as their own.

## Starting Points

- **Edmund Husserl** (1859-1938): founder of phenomenology; "back to the things themselves"; consciousness is always consciousness of something.
- **Max Wertheimer** (1880-1943), with Köhler and Koffka: Gestalt psychology, in which perception is organized wholes rather than sums of parts.
- **Kurt Lewin** (1890-1947): field theory; behavior as a function of the person and the environment.
- **Fritz Perls** (1893-1970): *Ego, Hunger and Aggression* (1942, South Africa; 1947, London); then, with Ralph Hefferline and Paul Goodman, *Gestalt Therapy* (1951), the founding text. Influences on Perls include Gestalt psychology, Kurt Goldstein, Wilhelm Reich, psychoanalysis, and the dialogical philosophy of Martin Buber.
- **Carl Rogers**: *Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1942) and *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951), the person-centered approach.
- **Abraham Maslow**: "A Theory of Human Motivation" (1943) and the hierarchy of needs.
- **Heidegger**, *Being and Time* (1927), and the existential-phenomenological tradition behind existential therapy.

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Humanistic-existential (Rogers, Maslow, May, Frankl) | Gestalt (Perls and colleagues) |
|---|---|---|
| Philosophical frame | Phenomenology and existentialism | Gestalt/field ideas, with phenomenological and dialogical influences |
| The self | Center of experience (self-concept, actualization) | Process-oriented: the self as an ongoing organizing function at the contact boundary |
| The past | Variously important (Rogers: conditions of worth; Frankl: the past as secured meaning) | Emphasis on the present; the past appears as "unfinished business" |
| The body | Varies; important in the existential-phenomenological tradition | Central to awareness work |
| Technique | Empathic listening and relational conditions | Experiments, enactment, "empty chair" |
| Resistance | Rogers: little emphasis; respect for the client's pace | Treated as a contact style to be explored |
| Goal | Growth, meaning, congruence | Awareness and completion of experience |

## Points of Convergence

- Both oppose reducing persons to diagnoses, though neither is simply against clinical assessment.
- Both prioritize present experience and its description over theoretical explanation.
- Both value the therapeutic relationship, authenticity, and the client's responsibility.
- Both treat the body and felt experience as sources of awareness.

## Lineage and Legacy

- Lewin influenced Gestalt therapy mainly through field theory, via Goodman's theoretical writing in the 1951 text; the link from Berlin Gestalt psychology to Perls is looser than a direct chain.
- Erving and Miriam Polster, *Gestalt Therapy Integrated* (1973), systematized the approach.
- Eugene Gendlin, a student of Rogers, developed the felt sense and Focusing (1978); a Gestalt influence on Focusing is sometimes claimed but is not established here.
- Rogers and Perls both appeared in the film *Three Approaches to Psychotherapy* (the "Gloria" films, 1965, with Albert Ellis), but no documented direct statement by either about the other is used in this entry.
- Greenberg's Emotion-Focused Therapy integrates Rogers's empathy, Gendlin's experiencing, and Gestalt techniques such as two-chair work.
- Encounter groups of the 1960s and 1970s drew on both traditions, as studied by Lieberman, Yalom, and Miles in *Encounter Groups: First Facts* (1973).

## Philosophical Debates

- **Is the Self a fixed entity?** Gestalt theory treats it as a process at the contact boundary; Rogers's self-concept is a more stable construct; Maslow's self develops through need satisfaction.
- **Past versus present:** Gestalt privileges the here-and-now; Rogers considers earlier conditions of worth; Frankl stresses the past as secured meaning.
- **Confrontation versus acceptance:** a stylistic difference between Perls's frustrating, confrontational demonstrations and Rogers's non-directive stance.

## Clinical Differences Today

- Person-centered therapy emphasizes relational conditions and empathy.
- Gestalt therapy emphasizes experiments and contact-boundary work.
- Existential therapy (for example Yalom) addresses death, freedom, meaning, and isolation as ultimate concerns.
- Contemporary integrations exist, such as EFT.
