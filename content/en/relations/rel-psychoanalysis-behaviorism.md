# Psychoanalysis and Behaviorism: The Foundational Rivalry

One of the oldest methodological rivalries in clinical psychology. It is conventionally dated from John B. Watson's 1913 manifesto "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It," which rejected introspection and the study of consciousness in favor of observable behavior. Psychoanalysis, developed by Freud from the 1890s, took the opposite route into the hidden structure of mental life. Freud did not write a sustained reply to Watson, so the rivalry is largely a construction of later commentators and of the two schools' successors. From the mid-twentieth century, and especially after the cognitive turn (Beck, 1976), each side began to borrow from the other.

## Before the Rivalry: Shared Aspirations

Both movements arose in a period in which psychology was trying to establish itself as a natural science, and both were dissatisfied with a psychology confined to the introspective description of conscious contents. Freud took the unconscious as a central subject matter; Watson took overt behavior. Freud's "Project for a Scientific Psychology" (written 1895, published posthumously) shows an early ambition to ground psychology in neurophysiology. The disagreement was therefore less about whether psychology should be scientific than about what counts as legitimate data.

## Points of Convergence

- **Both are applied.** Freud developed the "talking cure," and later behaviorists (Wolpe, Skinner's students) developed systematic behavior therapy; both claimed that theory should serve clinical change.
- **Both are skeptical of introspection as a sole source of knowledge.** Freud held that conscious self-report is incomplete because motives are unconscious; Watson rejected introspection as a scientific method.
- **Both assume that behavior has causes.** Freud's psychic determinism looks to unconscious motives and early experience; behaviorism looks to environmental contingencies and learning history.
- **Early translations.** John Dollard and Neal Miller's *Personality and Psychotherapy* (1950) restated psychoanalytic ideas such as conflict, repression and transference in learning-theory terms, showing that the rivalry was never total.

## Points of Divergence (Level One: Method)

| Dimension | Psychoanalysis | Classical behaviorism |
|---|---|---|
| Subject of study | The unconscious, drives, inner conflict | Observable behavior, motor and verbal responses |
| Data | Free association, transference, dreams, slips | Response rates, conditioning procedures, measured outcomes |
| Causality | Early experience shapes present patterns | Current stimuli and reinforcement history |
| Technique | Free association, interpretation, analysis of resistance | Extinction, counterconditioning, shaping, reinforcement |
| Criterion of success | Insight and structural change | Modification of target behavior |

## Points of Divergence (Level Two: Philosophy)

- **The self:** for Freud, the person is divided among id, ego and superego. For Skinner, an inner originating "self" is an explanatory fiction; behavior is a function of genetic and environmental history.
- **Freedom:** Freud wrote that the ego is "not master in its own house" (1917), which qualifies the claim to rational self-mastery without denying all agency. Skinner argued in *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* (1971) that the traditional notion of autonomous freedom should be abandoned.
- **Epistemic value:** for psychoanalysis, manifest behavior is a surface requiring interpretation. For Skinner, appeals to inner entities are explanatory fictions unless tied to observable operations. His "Critique of Psychoanalytic Concepts and Theories" (1954) states this objection directly.
- **Attitude toward science:** Freud regarded psychoanalysis as a natural science but relied on clinical observation rather than experiment. Watson and Skinner required experimental control and operational definition.
- **Hypothetical constructs:** MacCorquodale and Meehl (1948) distinguished "intervening variables" from "hypothetical constructs." Their distinction became a standard reference in debates about whether unobservable mental entities can be scientifically legitimate.

## The Turning Point: Selective Integration

The shift came less between Freud and Watson than between their later successors.

- **Wolpe (1958)**, in *Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition*, developed systematic desensitization from conditioning theory and criticized psychoanalytic treatment of neurosis.
- **Beck (1976)**, who had trained in psychoanalysis, reinterpreted emotional disorders in terms of testable cognitive processes.
- **Bandura (1977)**: social learning theory showed that learning occurs through observation without direct reinforcement and emphasized cognitive mediation, reopening the door to mental processes within a learning-based framework.
- **Hayes and colleagues**: relational frame theory (developed from the late 1980s, with a book-length statement in 2001) treats language and symbolic processes as behavioral subject matter, and acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) builds on it.

By the 1980s the two traditions were less often treated as opposed camps and more often as neighboring approaches within evidence-based practice, each retaining its own clinical identity.

## Historical Relations (Documented and Interpretive)

- **Freud and Watson:** behaviorism defined itself against introspective psychology; its relation to psychoanalysis was oppositional but not a direct debate.
- **Watson to Skinner:** Skinner regarded himself as continuing the behavioral program but built radical behaviorism, which, unlike Watson's methodological behaviorism, treats private events as behavior.
- **Psychoanalysis to Beck and Ellis:** both began as psychoanalytically trained clinicians before developing cognitive therapies in reaction to what they saw as the limits of analytic practice.
- **Skinner and Bandura:** Bandura's social learning theory developed in critical dialogue with operant theory, stressing observational learning and reciprocal determinism rather than purely external control.

## Contemporary Bridges

1. **CBT** (Beck, Ellis): combines behavioral techniques with cognitive formulation.
2. **DBT** (Linehan): draws on behavioral science, cognitive therapy and Zen-influenced acceptance practices.
3. **ACT** (Hayes): grounded in relational frame theory and a contextual behavioral philosophy.
4. **Applied behavior analysis (ABA):** descended from Skinner's operant work and now an independent field, notably in work with autistic children, and itself the subject of ethical debate.

## Unresolved Debates

- **Are the two forms of determinism comparable?** Psychoanalysis offers interpretive explanation of individual meaning; behaviorism offers functional analysis aimed at prediction and control. They answer different questions, and there is no decisive adjudication between them.
- **Does insight produce change?** Psychoanalytic therapists hold that insight is central. Cognitive-behavioral therapists hold that change comes through new learning, behavioral experiments and cognitive revision. Outcome research does not settle the mechanism, and evidence for the efficacy of psychodynamic therapy has also been reported.
- **Are common factors the answer?** Wampold (2001) argued that factors shared across therapies account for much of the benefit of treatment. This is a separate position from the question of how the schools differ in theory.

## Why the Rivalry Still Matters Clinically

- **Formulation:** an analytic clinician typically asks about early relationships and recurring patterns; a behavioral clinician typically asks about current antecedents, consequences and reinforcement.
- **Duration and format:** classical psychoanalysis is long-term, often meeting several times a week for years, whereas behavioral and cognitive-behavioral treatments are commonly time-limited. Format varies widely within both traditions.
- **Therapeutic stance:** analytic work privileges interpretation and the relationship; behavioral work privileges structured exercises, measurement and homework.
