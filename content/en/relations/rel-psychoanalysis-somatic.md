# Psychoanalysis and Somatic Therapy: Reich as Schism, Levine as Bridge

Wilhelm Reich (1897-1957) was a prominent and gifted member of the second generation of psychoanalysts. His emphasis on the body in character formation and on sexual economy led him out of the psychoanalytic movement in the 1930s, and his later work (orgone theory) placed him outside mainstream science. Decades later, therapists such as Peter Levine, Pat Ogden and others reintroduced the body into trauma treatment in forms that did not depend on Reich's energy theory. The relation between psychoanalysis and the somatic therapies is therefore one of schism followed by selective, partial bridges.

## Starting Point: Reich Inside Psychoanalysis (1920s to 1930)

- **Wilhelm Reich** (1897-1957): born in Galicia (then Austria-Hungary), a medical student in Vienna, admitted to the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society in 1920. He became a leading figure in analytic technique, directing the Vienna technical seminar in the 1920s.
- **Early works:** *Der triebhafte Charakter* (The Impulsive Character, 1925); *Die Funktion des Orgasmus* (The Function of the Orgasm, 1927), linking sexual satisfaction to psychological health; and *Charakteranalyse* (Character Analysis, 1933), which developed the concepts of "character armor" and its expression in muscular tension.
- **Political engagement:** Reich joined the Austrian Communist Party in 1928 and worked on sexual politics; in 1930 he moved to Berlin. His *Mass Psychology of Fascism* appeared in 1933.
- **Position within the movement:** a respected clinician and teacher, though not a member of Freud's inner circle in the way later accounts sometimes suggest.

## The Break (1933-1934)

- **The central ideas:** sexual repression and the inability to achieve orgastic satisfaction as the basis of neurosis; character as a defensive structure with bodily expression.
- **Vegetotherapy:** a technique developed in the mid-1930s in Scandinavia that combined talk with work on breathing and muscular tension and, in Reich's practice, direct physical contact.
- **Disagreement:** Freud's later writings (for example the revision of drive theory, with a death drive) moved away from Reich's emphasis on sexual economy, and Reich's political activism worried the leadership of the International Psychoanalytical Association (IPA). The Berlin society excluded him in 1933, and at the 1934 IPA congress in Lucerne he was effectively dropped from the association. Accounts differ about the roles of individuals in this decision; Myron Sharaf's biography *Fury on Earth* (1983) is a standard source.
- **Exile:** after leaving Germany in 1933 he lived in Denmark, Sweden and then Norway (from 1934), and moved to the United States in 1939.

## Reich's Later Stages (1934-1957)

- **From the late 1930s:** Reich announced the discovery of "bioelectrical" and then "orgone" energy and constructed "orgone accumulators" which he claimed could treat illness. Orgone energy has not been confirmed by independent scientific testing.
- **United States:** he worked in New York and, from the 1940s, at a research center named Orgonon in Maine.
- **Legal conflict:** in 1954 the US Food and Drug Administration obtained an injunction against interstate distribution of the accumulators. Reich was convicted of contempt of court in 1956 for violating it, and he died in federal prison in November 1957.

## Legacy of Reich's Somatic Ideas

- **Alexander Lowen** (1910-2008) and **John Pierrakos** (1921-2001): co-founded bioenergetic analysis (the Institute for Bioenergetic Analysis, 1956); Pierrakos later developed Core Energetics. Lowen trained with Reich; Pierrakos worked with Lowen and was a patient and colleague in that circle rather than a direct pupil of Reich.
- **Other extensions:** Stanley Keleman's formative psychology, David Boadella's biosynthesis, and Charles Kelley's Radix are among the lines of body-oriented psychotherapy derived from Reichian ideas.
- These schools vary widely in how far they retain Reich's energy concepts.

## Earlier Precursors Within Psychoanalysis

Reich was not the only analyst to attend to the body. Freud's early work on conversion hysteria treated bodily symptoms as expressions of unconscious conflict, and Sándor Ferenczi experimented with more active and relational techniques. These strands later became important to those who sought a rapprochement.

## The Second Turning Point: Peter Levine and Somatic Experiencing

- **Peter A. Levine** (born 1942): holds doctorates in medical biophysics and psychology and developed **Somatic Experiencing** (SE) from the 1970s. His best-known books are *Waking the Tiger* (1997) and *In an Unspoken Voice* (2010).
- **The idea:** traumatic stress involves incompletely discharged defensive responses (such as freezing). Therapy attends to bodily sensation and gradual, titrated processing, without requiring full narrative re-exposure.
- **Position:** SE does not adopt Reich's orgone theory or the analytic method of free association and interpretation. It draws on observations of animal defensive behavior and on clinical practice.
- **Evidence:** a randomized trial by Brom and colleagues (2017) found improvement in PTSD symptoms with SE compared with a waitlist; evidence overall remains limited, and larger comparative trials are lacking.

## Later Bridges

1. **Sensorimotor psychotherapy** (Pat Ogden; presented in Ogden, Minton and Pain, *Trauma and the Body*, 2006): combines attention to bodily sensation and movement with attachment-informed and psychodynamic concepts, and with ideas from neuroscience.
2. **Hakomi** (Ron Kurtz, 1934-2011): a body-centered, mindfulness-based method, developed from the late 1970s; the Hakomi Institute was founded in 1981. Kurtz drew on Gestalt, Rogerian and Eastern contemplative sources, together with systems thinking.
3. **Focusing** (Eugene Gendlin, 1926-2017): the "felt sense" as bodily, pre-verbal meaning; developed from Gendlin's work with Rogers in Chicago (*Focusing*, 1978).
4. **Polyvagal theory** (Stephen Porges, introduced 1994): proposes a role for autonomic regulation in social engagement and defense. It is widely used in trauma therapy, and its physiological claims have been criticized by other researchers.
5. **Popular synthesis:** Bessel van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014) brought the idea of embodied trauma to a wide audience.

## Philosophical Contrasts

| Dimension | Classical psychoanalysis | Reichian and somatic approaches |
|---|---|---|
| Unit of analysis | Speech, fantasy, the unconscious | The body, tension, sensation |
| Cause of suffering | Unconscious conflict, early relationships | Chronic bodily patterns, or incomplete defensive responses (Levine) |
| Speech | Central | Secondary or complementary |
| Touch and movement | Generally avoided, with strict boundaries | Used or emphasized in many approaches |
| Sexuality | Central to early theory | Central for Reich; not for most later somatic therapies |
| Scientific evidence | Mixed; mostly clinical and outcome research | Reich: not supported; Levine and others: limited but growing |

## Points of Convergence

1. **Mind and body:** psychoanalysis began with bodily symptoms (hysteria) and treats the body as a site of unconscious meaning.
2. **What is not said:** analysis attends to the unconscious; somatic therapy to what the body expresses or holds.
3. **History:** both tie current patterns to formative experience, whether in childhood relationships or in chronic bodily defense.
4. **Relationship:** both rest on deep therapeutic trust, with particular ethical sensitivity where the body is involved.

## Chain of Influence

- **Freud to Reich:** Reich was a student and colleague before breaking away.
- **Reich to Lowen and Pierrakos:** Lowen was Reich's pupil; he and Pierrakos developed bioenergetics, and Pierrakos later core energetics.
- **Reich to Levine:** not a documented descent; SE developed from other sources and distances itself from orgone theory.
- **Levine and Gendlin to Ogden and others:** body-centered methods influenced later psychodynamic and trauma therapies.

## Deeper Debates

- **Is trauma bodily or psychological?** Reich: bodily armor. Classical analysis: repressed memory and conflict. Contemporary trauma therapists: both, with emphasis on regulation of arousal.
- **Does the body remember?** Neuroscience supports procedural and implicit memory, which makes the question partly one of definition. Claims that bodily tissue stores trauma literally are not established.
- **Is orgone energy real?** Reich held that it was; there is no scientific support for it.
- **Is touch therapeutic?** Somatic schools use it in various ways; psychoanalytic ethics stress boundaries, and professional codes require informed consent.

## EMDR: A Related but Distinct Development

Francine Shapiro (1948-2019) developed eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR) from 1987 and published it in 1989. It is a structured trauma therapy involving bilateral stimulation, recall of traumatic material and cognitive reprocessing. It is not derived from Reich or from psychoanalysis. Guidelines such as the World Health Organization's 2013 recommendations include EMDR among trauma-focused treatments, alongside trauma-focused CBT. It is mentioned here because it is often grouped with body-oriented trauma therapies in practice.

## Why the Relationship Matters Clinically

- **Trauma therapy:** body-oriented methods are often used alongside trauma-focused psychological treatments, although the evidence for combined models varies.
- **Personality disorders and attachment trauma:** attention to bodily states is used to support regulation before and alongside verbal exploration.
- **Understanding suffering:** the idea that body and mind are not separate is supported by affective neuroscience and psychosomatic research.
- All of these approaches are descriptive here and do not substitute for clinical assessment.
