# Behaviorism and Cognitive Psychology: The Cognitive Revolution Within Behaviorism

Cognitive psychology arose partly against behaviorism and partly out of it. Some of its precursors, such as Edward Tolman, were themselves behaviorists who introduced unobserved mediating constructs, and Albert Bandura's social-cognitive theory grew out of the learning-theory tradition while criticising radical behaviorism. The relation is therefore one of both rupture and continuity, and historians do not agree on a single date or event that marks the change.

## Behaviorist Positions Before the Revolution

- **Methodological behaviorism**: John B. Watson's manifesto "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" (1913) proposed that psychology should study observable behaviour rather than introspected consciousness.
- **Neobehaviorism**: Clark Hull's *Principles of Behavior* (1943) and related work introduced hypothetical intervening constructs (such as drive and habit strength) defined by their relation to observable stimuli and responses.
- **Radical behaviorism**: B. F. Skinner (*The Behavior of Organisms*, 1938) explained behaviour through its reinforcement history. Radical behaviorism does not deny private events such as thoughts and feelings; it treats them as behaviour to be explained, not as causes of behaviour.

## Tolman and the Cognitive Map

Edward C. Tolman (1886-1959) called his view purposive behaviorism (*Purposive Behavior in Animals and Men*, 1932). In "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men" (1948), he argued that rats in mazes learn a spatial representation of the environment rather than merely a chain of responses, and he defended intervening variables as legitimate scientific constructs. His work is often treated as an early bridge between behaviorist methods and later cognitive theory.

## The Cognitive Revolution (1950s-1960s)

Several developments from the mid-1950s are usually cited together:

- **Information theory and computing models**: the 1956 Symposium on Information Theory at MIT, and George Miller's paper on the limits of short-term memory capacity (1956).
- **Donald Broadbent**, *Perception and Communication* (1958), a filter model of attention.
- **Noam Chomsky's** 1959 review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* (1957), which argued that language could not be explained by reinforcement alone and which was widely influential, though Skinner's defenders contend it misread his position.
- **Miller, Galanter, and Pribram**, *Plans and the Structure of Behavior* (1960), which proposed the TOTE (Test-Operate-Test-Exit) unit as an alternative to the simple stimulus-response unit.
- **Ulric Neisser**, *Cognitive Psychology* (1967), which gave the field its name and a textbook synthesis.

Behaviorism did not disappear. Behaviour analysis, a descendant of Skinner's work, continues as a research and applied field (for example applied behavior analysis).

## Bandura and Social-Cognitive Theory

Albert Bandura (1925-2021) was not a student of Skinner. His work developed from the social-learning tradition of Neal Miller and John Dollard (1941) and was explicitly critical of the view that learning requires direct reinforcement of the learner. His Bobo doll studies (1961) showed learning by observing a model, *Social Learning Theory* (1977) set out the framework, and the 1977 *Psychological Review* paper "Self-Efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change" introduced self-efficacy. Bandura emphasised that people form expectations and self-regulate, so his approach retained empirical methods while reintroducing cognitive processes, and it was later renamed social-cognitive theory.

## Cognitive Therapies

Aaron T. Beck (*Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders*, 1976) and Albert Ellis (*Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy*, 1962; rational-emotive therapy from the mid-1950s) both developed therapies focused on beliefs and appraisals. Both men had been trained in psychoanalysis and came to treat it as insufficiently testable, so the cognitive therapies descend from several sources, not only from behaviorism. Donald Meichenbaum's *Cognitive-Behavior Modification* (1977) explicitly combined behavioural technique with attention to self-talk. These approaches converged into cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT).

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Classical and radical behaviorism | Cognitive psychology |
|---|---|---|
| Object of study | Observable behaviour (including private events as behaviour, in Skinner's view) | Internal representations and information processing |
| Explanation | Environment-behaviour relations, reinforcement history | Mental processes mediating stimulus and response |
| Role of thoughts | Behaviour to be explained, not an independent cause | Causal, measurable internal events |
| Language | Verbal behaviour shaped by contingencies | Rule-governed, generative competence (Chomsky) |

## Points of Convergence

Both traditions value empirical methods and experimental control, and both support clinical applications. Contemporary CBT combines behavioural techniques (exposure, activation, reinforcement) with cognitive ones (examining and testing beliefs). Newer "third-wave" therapies (ACT, DBT, mindfulness-based approaches) and Relational Frame Theory (Hayes, Barnes-Holmes, and Roche, 2001), an account of language and cognition developed from behavioural principles, show the continuing exchange between the traditions.

## Unresolved Debates

- Whether thoughts are causes of behaviour or behaviour to be explained.
- Whether cognitive therapy works by changing the content of beliefs or through behavioural and other mechanisms, a question on which evidence is mixed.
- How far observational learning requires reinforcement.

## Limits

The label "revolution" itself is contested by historians, some of whom see a more gradual change driven by new technologies, wartime human-factors research, and linguistics. This comparison documents conceptual and historical links, not a single causal chain.
