# American Pragmatism and Humanistic Psychology: From William James to Rogers

American pragmatism and humanistic psychology share an emphasis on experience, practical consequences, democratic values, and the person as an active agent. The connections are partly documented and partly thematic. For Dewey's influence on Carl Rogers there is biographical evidence. For William James's influence on Rogers there is no comparable documentation; the link is a comparison made by later commentators. Maslow's debt to James is more often noted in connection with peak and mystical experience. The relationship is therefore a combination of documented influences and parallels, not a single lineage.

## The Philosophical Context: Pragmatism

**Charles Sanders Peirce** (1839-1914), in "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), formulated the pragmatic maxim: the meaning of a concept lies in its conceivable practical effects.

**William James** (1842-1910), in *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), and *Pragmatism* (1907), developed:

- **Pragmatic truth**: ideas are tested by their practical consequences and fit with experience.
- **Pluralism**: a world with many perspectives and no single absolute standpoint.
- **Radical empiricism**: experience, including relations, is the basic material of philosophy.
- **The stream of consciousness**: consciousness as a continuous, selective process rather than a collection of atoms.
- **A study of religious experience** that took mystical and conversion experiences seriously on their own terms.

**John Dewey** (1859-1952), in *Democracy and Education* (1916), *Experience and Nature* (1925), and *Art as Experience* (1934), developed an experiential and experimental philosophy of inquiry and learning and a democratic educational ideal.

## Functional Psychology

At the University of Chicago, Dewey and **James Rowland Angell** (1869-1949) developed a functionalist psychology focused on how mental activity helps organisms adapt. Angell's "The Province of Functional Psychology" (*Psychological Review*, 1907) is the standard statement. This tradition is part of the background of American psychology, including the empirical and applied orientation of humanistic psychology, but a direct line from it to Rogers or Maslow is not established.

## Rogers and Dewey

**Carl Rogers** (1902-1987) studied at Union Theological Seminary and Teachers College, Columbia University, where he encountered Dewey's ideas through teachers such as William Heard Kilpatrick, a leading follower of Dewey (see Kirschenbaum's biography). Dewey's emphasis on experience, learning by doing, and democratic relationships is reflected in Rogers's client-centered approach and in his later work on student-centered learning (*Freedom to Learn*, 1969). James is a more distant presence; he is not documented as a direct source for Rogers.

## Maslow and James

**Abraham Maslow** (1908-1970) wrote about peak experiences and self-actualization (*Motivation and Personality*, 1954; *Toward a Psychology of Being*, 1962; *Religions, Values, and Peak-Experiences*, 1964). Commentators compare his treatment of peak experiences with James's study of mystical and religious experience, and Eugene Taylor (*William James on Consciousness Beyond the Margin*, 1996) has argued for James's place in the lineage of humanistic and transpersonal psychology. These are comparisons and arguments about lineage, not statements by Maslow that can be cited here.

## Concept Map

| Pragmatist concept | Humanistic concept | Point of contact |
|---|---|---|
| Pragmatic evaluation of ideas by consequences | Evaluating therapy by its effects and client experience | Focus on practice and results |
| Pluralism | Respect for individual differences in experience | Rejection of a single standard |
| Stream of consciousness | Present-centered, experiential awareness | Experience as process |
| Radical empiricism | Phenomenological attention to experience | Primacy of experience |
| Dewey's educational democracy | Student-centered learning, client-centered therapy | Participation and respect for the learner or client |
| Inquiry | Qualitative and experiential research | Problem-oriented exploration |

## Essential Differences

| Dimension | Pragmatism | Humanistic psychology |
|---|---|---|
| Discipline | Philosophy | Psychology and psychotherapy |
| Aim | Theory of meaning, truth, and inquiry | Personal growth and therapeutic practice |
| The self | Social and processual (James, Mead, Dewey) | Often a core self with an actualizing tendency |
| View of human nature | Plural and situational | Tends to emphasize a basic tendency toward growth (Rogers, Maslow) |

Rogers's and Maslow's optimism about a growth tendency is not part of classical pragmatism, which emphasized fallibilism and inquiry.

## Mutual Contributions

- **Pragmatism contributed** respect for experience, a focus on function and consequences, an experimental attitude, and a democratic orientation.
- **Humanistic psychology contributed** clinical applications, attention to subjective meaning, and a language of growth.

## Contemporary Bridges

- Contemporary pragmatist philosophers (for example Rorty, *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*, 1979) influenced debates on knowledge and language; their connection to humanistic psychology is indirect.
- Pragmatist ideas are also visible in research traditions that evaluate approaches by their usefulness (for example pluralistic and qualitative methods).

## Limits

The comparison risks reading later humanistic ideas into James and Dewey. Pragmatism is not a psychology of growth, and humanistic psychology is not an application of pragmatism.
