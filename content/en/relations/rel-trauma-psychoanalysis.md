# Trauma-Focused Therapy and Psychoanalysis: From Neglect to Recognition

Psychological trauma stands at the origin of psychoanalysis. In the 1880s and 1890s Janet, Breuer and Freud traced hysterical symptoms to unassimilated experiences. Freud's later shift of emphasis toward fantasy and drive made the real event less prominent in classical theory, although many psychoanalysts continued to work on trauma, especially in relation to war. The formal recognition of post-traumatic stress disorder in 1980 and the development of structured trauma-focused treatments from the 1980s reopened the field. Today psychoanalytic and trauma-focused approaches agree that trauma is central, but they differ in how it is understood and treated.

## Starting Point: Janet, Breuer, Freud (1880s-1890s)

- **Pierre Janet** (1859-1947), in *L'automatisme psychologique* (1889), proposed that overwhelming experiences produce dissociation of memories from conscious awareness.
- **Josef Breuer** (1842-1925), a Viennese physician, treated "Anna O." (Bertha Pappenheim) in 1880-1882. She called the method the "talking cure" or "chimney sweeping"; symptoms eased as she recounted their origins.
- **Studies on Hysteria** (Breuer and Freud, 1895, preceded by their "Preliminary Communication" of 1893) argued that hysterical symptoms are tied to memories of traumatic events that were not discharged ("abreaction").

## Freud's Shift (1896-1905)

- In 1896 Freud presented the "seduction theory," attributing hysteria to childhood sexual abuse.
- In a letter to Wilhelm Fliess of 21 September 1897 he wrote that he no longer believed in his "neurotica," citing clinical doubts about the frequency of such events and the difficulty of distinguishing memory from fantasy.
- Subsequent theory (including the *Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality*, 1905) gave increasing weight to infantile sexuality and fantasy.
- Freud did not abandon trauma altogether. His later work, notably *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920), dealt with repetition in war neuroses and traumatic dreams.
- The shift has been judged very differently. Jeffrey Masson's *The Assault on Truth* (1984) argued that Freud suppressed the reality of abuse; many historians regard this as an oversimplification and stress Freud's own clinical reasons for changing his view. Feminist and trauma scholars have continued to debate the consequences.

## Wars and Psychoanalysis (1914-1950)

- **World War I:** "shell shock" (a term associated with Charles Myers, 1915) was first thought to be physical and then understood as psychological. At the 1918 Budapest congress, Freud's circle (Ferenczi, Abraham, Simmel, Jones) discussed war neuroses, and Freud wrote on the subject in 1919-1920.
- **Interwar and Ferenczi:** Sándor Ferenczi's late paper "Confusion of Tongues Between Adults and the Child" (1932/1933) emphasized the reality of child abuse and was received with hostility in the psychoanalytic community.
- **World War II:** Abram Kardiner's *The Traumatic Neuroses of War* (1941) and Grinker and Spiegel's *Men Under Stress* (1945) drew on psychoanalytic thinking to describe combat stress. Treatment used brief, sometimes pharmacologically assisted abreaction.
- Psychoanalysis therefore contributed to the understanding of trauma throughout the century, though classical theory emphasized internal conflict and the mainstream of psychiatry later underweighted trauma until the 1970s.

## Formal Recognition (1970s-1980)

- Work with Vietnam veterans (Robert Lifton, Chaim Shatan) and with survivors of rape and abuse (Burgess and Holmstrom, "rape trauma syndrome," 1974) built the clinical and political case.
- **1980:** the DSM-III included post-traumatic stress disorder. A defining feature was an external precipitating event as part of the diagnosis.
- **Judith Herman**'s *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) integrated work on war, domestic violence and abuse and proposed a "complex" post-traumatic syndrome.

## Development of Trauma-Focused Treatments (1980s-2000s)

- **Prolonged Exposure (PE):** developed by Edna Foa and colleagues from the 1980s (building on Foa and Kozak's 1986 emotional processing theory); typically about 8-15 sessions.
- **Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT):** Resick and Schnicke (1992), a cognitive treatment targeting beliefs about the trauma.
- **EMDR:** Francine Shapiro (1948-2019) developed eye movement desensitization and reprocessing from 1987 and published in 1989.
- **Guidelines:** the UK's NICE guidelines (2005, updated 2018) recommend trauma-focused cognitive-behavioral therapy and EMDR. The WHO (2013) recommends trauma-focused CBT and EMDR. The American Psychological Association's 2017 guideline strongly recommends CBT, CPT, cognitive therapy and prolonged exposure, and conditionally recommends EMDR and several other treatments.

## Main Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Psychoanalytic approaches | Trauma-focused therapies (PE, CPT, EMDR) |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Meaning, conflict, relational patterns | Traumatic memory and its appraisals |
| Duration | Usually long-term | Usually time-limited (roughly 8-20 sessions) |
| Technique | Free association, interpretation, transference | Imaginal and in vivo exposure, cognitive restructuring, bilateral stimulation (EMDR) |
| Stance on memory | Memory is shaped by later meaning and fantasy | Trauma memory is fragmented and needs processing |
| Focus on childhood | Central | Relevant when the trauma is in childhood |
| Evidence | Fewer trials for PTSD | Extensive randomized trial evidence |

## Points of Convergence

1. **What is not said:** the unconscious in analysis, avoided or dissociated memory in trauma therapy.
2. **Trauma as foundational:** both accept that early or severe experiences shape psychological structure and symptoms.
3. **The relationship:** analysis treats transference as material; trauma treatments emphasize safety and trust.
4. **Repetition:** the repetition compulsion in psychoanalysis and the re-experiencing symptoms (flashbacks, nightmares) in PTSD.
5. **Meaning:** analysis asks what the trauma means to the person; trauma-focused therapy asks how to process the memory, though CPT also addresses meaning.

## Bridges

- **Dissociation and structural theory:** Onno van der Hart (born 1941), Ellert Nijenhuis and Kathy Steele (*The Haunted Self*, 2006) developed a theory of structural dissociation that builds on Janet's work and on Herman's phase-oriented model (safety and stabilization, processing of traumatic memories, reintegration).
- **Relational psychoanalysis:** Philip Bromberg (*Standing in the Spaces*, 1998) and others emphasized dissociation and multiple self-states, bringing trauma theory into psychoanalytic work.
- **Sensorimotor psychotherapy and Somatic Experiencing:** body-oriented approaches (see the entries on trauma and somatic therapy).
- **Internal Family Systems** (Richard Schwartz, 1990s): a model of "parts" developed within family therapy and widely used with trauma survivors; it can be compared with psychoanalytic ideas of self-states, though it is a distinct approach with limited controlled evidence.
- **Mentalization-based and other psychodynamic treatments** are used with people whose trauma involves attachment disruption.

## Deeper Debates

- **Is trauma a sufficient cause?** Trauma-focused models emphasize the external event. Psychoanalytic models emphasize how the event interacts with prior vulnerability and meaning. In practice both factors are usually considered.
- **How reliable are traumatic memories?** Memory research (Elizabeth Loftus and colleagues, for example Loftus and Pickrell, 1995) shows that memory is reconstructive and open to suggestion. Traumatic memories may also be vivid and intrusive. Neither the claim that memory is simply accurate nor the claim that it is simply malleable captures the evidence.
- **Recovered memories:** the 1980s and 1990s "memory wars" involved cases of memories reported to be recovered in therapy and, in some, wrongful accusations (for example the McMartin preschool case). Professional consensus holds that the accuracy of recovered memories cannot be assumed and that therapists should avoid suggestive methods.
- **Personal or political?** Herman and others stress the social and political context of trauma, including abuse of power. Psychoanalysis tends to emphasize the individual's inner world, although relational and social-trauma analysts address collective trauma.

## Complex PTSD

- ICD-11 includes complex PTSD (CPTSD) as a separate diagnosis. It requires the core PTSD symptoms plus persistent "disturbances in self-organization": affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, and difficulties in relationships. It is associated with prolonged or repeated trauma, often interpersonal.
- DSM-5-TR does not list CPTSD as a separate diagnosis (it includes a dissociative subtype of PTSD).
- Treatment is typically phase-oriented or modified trauma-focused therapy. Evidence is still developing.

## Intergenerational Trauma

- Psychoanalysts have described transmission of trauma across generations through relational means (Bergmann and Jucovy, *Generations of the Holocaust*, 1982; Abraham and Torok's concept of the "phantom").
- Biological research by Rachel Yehuda and colleagues has reported altered cortisol regulation and epigenetic differences in some offspring of Holocaust survivors. Samples are small, and the interpretation is debated.

## Clinical Differences Today

- Trauma-focused CBT, PE, CPT and EMDR are first-line options in several guidelines for PTSD.
- Psychodynamic and body-oriented approaches are used as alternatives or complements, especially where trauma is chronic, relational or accompanied by dissociation, with a lower level of trial evidence.
- This summary is descriptive and does not replace clinical assessment.

## Summary

- Trauma was at the origin of psychoanalysis, was partly marginalized in classical theory, and was repeatedly rediscovered through war, feminism and clinical research.
- Trauma-focused therapies provided structured, testable treatments, while psychoanalysis maintained attention to meaning, relationship and dissociation.
- Current practice and research often combine the two perspectives.
