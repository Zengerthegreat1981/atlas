# Advaita Vedānta and Transpersonal Psychology: From Shankara to Wilber

Transpersonal psychology, which took shape in the late 1960s, has drawn openly on Asian contemplative traditions, and Advaita Vedānta (non-dualism) is among the most frequently cited. The most explicit use of Advaita comes from **Ken Wilber** (born 1949), who built a theoretical synthesis of psychology and spiritual traditions. **Stanislav Grof** and **Abraham Maslow** also draw parallels. This entry sets out the connections and, equally, where they stop: the relation is one of selective borrowing and comparison, not a faithful transmission of the Vedāntic system into psychology.

## Advaita Vedānta

Śaṅkara, traditionally dated 788-820 CE (many modern scholars place him in the eighth century), wrote commentaries on the *Brahmasūtra*, the principal Upaniṣads, and the *Bhagavad Gītā*, and is credited with the independent work *Upadeśasāhasrī*. The central doctrines are:

- **Brahman**: the one ultimate reality.
- **Ātman**: the self, taught to be identical with Brahman (*tat tvam asi*, "you are that").
- **Māyā** (or ignorance, *avidyā*): the superimposition by which the one reality appears as a plurality of separate things and selves.
- **Mokṣa**: liberation through knowledge (*jñāna*) of one's non-difference from Brahman. Traditional preparation includes discrimination, dispassion, the "sixfold discipline," and longing for liberation.

The *Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad* (with Gauḍapāda's commentary) describes waking, dreaming, and deep sleep, and a "fourth" (*turīya*), which Advaita interprets as the underlying witness consciousness. Ramana Maharshi (1879-1950) popularised a practice of self-inquiry ("Who am I?", *ātma-vicāra*) in the twentieth century, emphasising direct experience over textual study.

## Ken Wilber

Wilber's *The Spectrum of Consciousness* (1977) proposed that human identity can be understood along a spectrum of levels, from a narrow sense of self (persona, ego, and body-mind levels) to the transpersonal level of undivided "Mind" that he explicitly equates with Brahman-Ātman in the Vedāntic and related Mahāyāna traditions. Later works, including *Up from Eden* (1981) and *Sex, Ecology, Spirituality* (1995), extended this into a developmental and evolutionary scheme; the integral framework with "quadrants" and "levels" (often abbreviated AQAL) belongs to his later work and is distinct from the 1977 spectrum. Among Wilber's best-known ideas is the **pre/trans fallacy**: the risk of confusing pre-rational (childlike) states with trans-rational (contemplative) ones.

Wilber draws on many sources besides Advaita, including Mahāyāna Buddhism (Nāgārjuna, Yogācāra), Plotinus, Sri Aurobindo, Jean Gebser, James Mark Baldwin, Jean Piaget, and Jürgen Habermas. He adopts Vedāntic concepts but integrates them into a broader developmental model that Śaṅkara would not recognise, so the relation is syncretic rather than orthodox.

## Stanislav Grof and Abraham Maslow

Grof (born 1931) and Christina Grof developed **Holotropic Breathwork** in the 1970s. Grof's books, such as *Realms of the Human Unconscious* (1975) and *The Adventure of Self-Discovery* (1988), describe "transpersonal" experiences of unity and compare them with Eastern traditions, including Vedānta, while treating them as parallels rather than identities.

Maslow (1908-1970) studied **peak experiences** in *Religions, Values, and Peak-Experiences* (1964) and contributed, with Anthony Sutich, to launching the *Journal of Transpersonal Psychology* in 1969. Maslow's framework is humanistic and psychological; it is not a cosmological doctrine like Advaita.

## Conceptual Comparison

| Advaita concept | Transpersonal parallel (as used by Wilber and others) | Reservation |
|---|---|---|
| Brahman | Ultimate or absolute consciousness ("Mind" in Wilber) | Brahman is a metaphysical claim, not a psychological state |
| Ātman | Essential or witness self | Advaita denies a separate individual self; psychology treats selves as persons |
| Māyā / avidyā | Illusion of separateness; ego identification | Different explanatory roles |
| Mokṣa | Awakening, self-realisation | In Advaita, liberation is knowledge of what is already so |
| *Turīya* | Witnessing or non-dual awareness | Wilber's use is interpretive |

## Differences

- **Method and authority**: Advaita traditionally depends on a teacher-student lineage, scriptural study, and ethical preparation; transpersonal psychology uses empirical and clinical methods, workshops, and in some contexts induced states.
- **Ontology**: Advaita holds that plurality is ultimately unreal; transpersonal psychology, and especially Wilber's stage models, treat development as real and progressive.
- **Social context**: Wilber's model includes social and cultural dimensions that classical Advaita does not address.
- **Contested scholarship**: Some Indologists and psychologists criticise transpersonal readings for flattening differences among traditions (the "perennialist" assumption that all mysticism describes the same experience), and the question of whether mystical experience is cross-culturally uniform remains disputed. Robert Forman's edited volume *The Problem of Pure Consciousness* (1990) argues for a cross-cultural "pure consciousness event," while constructivist critics such as Steven Katz argue that mystical experience is shaped by the tradition in which it occurs.

## Limits

This is a comparison of concepts, not a claim that Śaṅkara's thought is the origin of transpersonal psychology. Evidence of direct borrowing is strongest for Wilber, who names Vedānta in his own writing, and weaker for the rest. Transpersonal psychology remains a minority field, and its claims are contested within academic psychology.
