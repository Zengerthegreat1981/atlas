# From Weberian Bureaucratic Rationalisation to Milgram's Psychological Agentic State

Max Weber analysed bureaucracy as a rational social structure in which the official is subject to hierarchy and rule; half a century later, Stanley Milgram analysed what happens psychologically within the individual when they work inside a hierarchy of this kind.

## Obedience in the Arab-Islamic Tradition

The Arab-Islamic heritage organised the question of obedience within the state from an early period, and set an ethical limit to it: "there is no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator," and in the sound hadith "obedience is only in what is good" (*innama al-ta'a fi al-ma'ruf*). This limit bears on Milgram's question, since it holds that a command from an authority does not relieve the person of responsibility for the act. (The wording "no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator" is a widely cited prophetic saying whose chains of transmission are graded differently by hadith scholars, whereas "obedience is only in what is good" is found in the collections of al-Bukhari and Muslim.) Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah* (1377), described the organs of the state (the vizierate, the chancery, the diwan, and tax collection), and linked the emergence of the administrative apparatus to the maturity of civilisation and its transition to luxury and weakness (in Franz Rosenthal's English translation, Pantheon Books, 1958). However, Ibn Khaldun describes a personal sultanic organisation based on *asabiyyah* (group solidarity), not a legal rational bureaucracy in the Weberian sense, so the comparison is descriptive.

## Weber: The Rational Structure

Weber described bureaucracy in *Economy and Society* (1922) as the purest type of rational-legal domination: functions specified by rules, a hierarchy, written documents, and trained officials appointed on the basis of competence and paid a salary, who perform their work "without anger and without bias" (*sine ira et studio*). In *Politics as a Vocation* (1919) he wrote that the official's honour lies in carrying out their superiors' orders conscientiously, as if they were consistent with their own conviction, even if they contradict their opinion, while the politician, by contrast, must bear personal responsibility for the act. The phrase "iron cage" comes from the conclusion of *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905), not from the chapter on bureaucracy; its common translation (*stahlhartes Gehäuse*, a casing as hard as steel) is a matter of debate among translators. Attributing it to bureaucracy is therefore a later reading, not a Weberian formulation.

## Milgram: The Agentic State

In 1963 Milgram published "Behavioral Study of Obedience" in the *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*: participants at Yale University were asked to administer increasing electric shocks to a person playing the learner, and 65% of them continued in the basic experiment to the highest level (450 volts). In *Obedience to Authority* (1974) he explained this result by a shift from the autonomous state to the agentic state, in which the individual sees themselves as an instrument for carrying out the purposes of a legitimate authority; their sense of responsibility is transferred to the authority, and they experience a moral tension which they resolve through justification. He linked this to the context of the trial of Eichmann in Jerusalem and to Hannah Arendt's book *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (Viking, 1963).

## The Relationship Between the Two Analyses

There is no documented evidence that Milgram drew on Weber in his 1974 book; the connection is analytical and interpretive (both concern how an individual's moral judgment recedes within a formal hierarchy), and Milgram's explicit reference point is the Eichmann trial (1961) and Arendt's account of it. Nevertheless it is fruitful: Weber shows how structure makes the moral act recede (the division of labour separates the source of the command from its executor and its outcome), and Milgram measures the mechanism of psychological response under these conditions. The most lucid link between the bureaucratic structure and genocide is made by Zygmunt Bauman in *Modernity and the Holocaust* (Polity, 1989): he held that administrative rationality removes the agent from the victim and converts the goal into a technical task, and in support of this he discussed Milgram's experiments, reading their results in light of the social and technical distance between the commander and the victim.

## What Resists the Reading

Subsequent data open a gap between the two concepts. Gina Perry, in *Behind the Shock Machine* (New Press, 2013), based on Milgram's archive, reported that a substantial number of participants doubted that the shocks were real. Alexander Haslam and Stephen Reicher proposed the model of "engaged followership," which holds that people obey when they identify with the scientific goal, not when they surrender their will. Jerry Burger, in *American Psychologist* in 2009, replicated a milder procedure that stops at 150 volts and found a rate of obedience close to Milgram's results. This means that the bridge between Weber and Milgram, if accepted, remains one between a structure and a mechanism, in which obedience cannot be reduced to a loss of will.
