# Aristotelian Ethics and Well-Being: Eudaimonic Happiness in Psychology

Aristotle's view in the *Nicomachean Ethics* is that the highest good for a human being is **eudaimonia**: the flourishing achieved through the activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life, not a passing feeling. This idea became the basis for distinguishing two kinds of well-being in psychology: pleasure (*hedonia*) and flourishing (*eudaimonia*).

## Happiness in Islamic Philosophy

Islamic philosophers received this idea and translated it into the concept of *saʿāda* ("happiness"). In *Tahsil al-Saada* (*Attainment of Happiness*), al-Farabi holds that happiness is the highest perfection a human being reaches by realising his rational faculties, and that it requires the virtuous city with its theoretical and practical virtues. In *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (*Refinement of Character*), Miskawayh links happiness to the perfection of the soul through virtue, and distinguishes sensual pleasures from intellectual goods. Thus Aristotelian thought passed from Athens to Baghdad, before returning to positive psychology in the twentieth century.

## Eudaimonia versus Hedonia in Empirical Research

In 1989, in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Carol Ryff proposed a model of "psychological well-being" with six dimensions drawn from humanistic and developmental psychology (Maslow, Rogers, Erikson, Jung, Frankl, and others) and, as she later noted, resonant with Aristotelian eudaimonia: self-acceptance, positive relations, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth. In a study titled "Two Conceptions of Happiness" (1993), Alan Waterman showed that personal expressiveness, linked to eudaimonia, differs from hedonic enjoyment. In a well-known review in the *Annual Review of Psychology* (2001), Ryan and Deci set out the difference between the two traditions and built on it the theory of self-determination (autonomy, competence, and relatedness). Seligman, for his part, distinguished in *Authentic Happiness* (2002) between the pleasant life, the good life (engagement), and the meaningful life, and then formulated in *Flourish* (2011) the PERMA model: positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, and accomplishment.

## Classification of Virtues and Applications

The VIA project (Peterson and Seligman, *Character Strengths and Virtues*, 2004) drew on virtue traditions, including the Aristotelian one, to propose six core virtues and twenty-four character strengths, and turned them into a measure. The applications are broad: strengths-based counselling, psychological resilience programmes, and well-being programmes in schools and workplaces, such as the Penn Resilience Program. Studies tracing the effects of positive psychology interventions have also appeared, such as the meta-analytic review by Sin and Lyubomirsky (2009) in the *Journal of Clinical Psychology*, which found a moderate effect on well-being and depression.

## Limits of the Comparison

The first limit is that Aristotelian eudaimonia is objective: a person is flourishing even if he does not feel it, whereas psychological measurement relies on self-report, so that flourishing turns into a feeling. The second is that Aristotle requires external conditions (health, friendship, reasonable wealth, and good fortune), and holds that the life of virtue is complete only in a polis and over a full lifetime; these conditions were absent from the applications. The third is that Aristotle places the highest form of life in intellectual contemplation (Book X), which contemporary well-being interventions do not carry. The fourth is a cultural objection: the notion of individual flourishing is criticised for its bias toward Western individualism. Finally, some researchers hold that combining hedonia and eudaimonia within a single measure obscures the theoretical difference between them.
