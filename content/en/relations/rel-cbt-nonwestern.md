# Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy and Non-Western Frameworks: Importing Buddhist Mindfulness

This entry looks at the relation between cognitive-behavioural therapy (CBT) and therapeutic or contemplative frameworks from outside the Western psychological tradition. The main case is mindfulness, adopted from Buddhist meditation in the late twentieth century (see also the entry on Buddhism and the third wave of therapies); other cases include Japanese, Chinese, Māori, and Hawaiian approaches. The central question is whether such transfers constitute a respectful integration, a reduction of the source, or both.

## The Main Case: Mindfulness in CBT

In 1979 Jon Kabat-Zinn founded a stress-reduction clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center and developed Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR), an eight-week group programme using body scan, sitting meditation, and mindful movement, adapted from Zen and insight meditation practice. In the 1990s Zindel Segal, Mark Williams, and John Teasdale combined MBSR with cognitive-therapy methods to produce Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) for preventing depressive relapse (book, 2002). Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP), developed by Sarah Bowen, Neha Chawla, and G. Alan Marlatt for substance use problems, applied a similar combination with Marlatt's relapse-prevention model (book, 2011). In British guidance MBCT is recommended for people in remission from recurrent depression. Mindfulness elements also appear in ACT and DBT.

## Why the Fit Was Plausible

Several features of mindfulness practice resemble CBT techniques, which helped the integration:

- **Attention to present experience**, including monitoring thoughts and feelings.
- **Treating thoughts as mental events** rather than facts, which resembles cognitive distancing or "decentering."
- **Acceptance and non-judgment** of experience, which resembles exposure-based and acceptance-based approaches.
- **Skills practice** through structured exercises and home practice.

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Buddhist and other contemplative frameworks | CBT with mindfulness |
|---|---|---|
| Context | Religious or philosophical, with ethical commitments | Clinical psychology and medicine |
| Ultimate aim | Liberation from suffering (Buddhism) | Symptom relief, relapse prevention, improved functioning |
| View of the self | Analysis of the self as impermanent and not a fixed entity | Not a doctrinal question; individual coping and well-being |
| Training | Long-term practice, often with a teacher and community | Time-limited manualised programme |
| Evidence standard | Tradition and practice | Randomised trials, outcome measures |

## Debates

- **Is clinical mindfulness the same as Buddhist *sati*?** Bhikkhu Bodhi (2011) argues that the traditional sense of *sati* (recollection) and its place on the Eightfold Path are broader than present-moment non-judgmental awareness. Robert Sharf (2015) argues the two should not be equated. Others, including Kabat-Zinn, hold that mindfulness is a universal human capacity that the Buddhist tradition articulated.
- **Is the transfer a form of cultural extraction?** Critics see a pattern of removing a practice from its religious setting and commercialising it (Purser, *McMindfulness*, 2019). Defenders see secular translation as a way of making benefits available to people outside the tradition.
- **How strong is the evidence?** Van Dam and colleagues (2018) argued that definitions, measures, and study designs in mindfulness research often fall short and that claims outran the evidence. Support is strongest for MBCT in preventing depressive relapse and more mixed for other conditions. Adverse effects occur in a minority of participants and warrant care.

## Other Non-Western Frameworks

- **Morita therapy** (Japan): developed by the psychiatrist Shoma Morita (1874-1938), it encourages accepting anxiety as natural and acting purposefully despite it. David K. Reynolds adapted it for Western audiences as Constructive Living (1984). Its resemblance to acceptance-based therapies is often noted, though it arose independently.
- **Naikan** (Japan): a structured reflective practice associated with Yoshimoto Ishin that examines what one has received from others, what one has given, and what trouble one has caused.
- **Chinese Taoist cognitive therapy**: a cognitive psychotherapy developed by Zhang Yalin that draws on Taoist ideas.
- **Hawaiian ho'oponopono**: a traditional practice of family reconciliation, described by Mary Kawena Pukui and colleagues in *Nānā i ke Kumu* (1972), later adapted in various ways by others.
- **Te Whare Tapa Whā** (New Zealand): a Māori model of health described by Mason Durie in 1984, using the image of a four-sided house (*taha tinana*, *taha hinengaro*, *taha whānau*, *taha wairua*: physical, mental, family, and spiritual dimensions). It is used in New Zealand health policy.
- **Compassion-Focused Therapy** (Paul Gilbert, *The Compassionate Mind*, 2009) and **Hakomi** (Ron Kurtz) draw openly on Buddhist ideas, among other sources.

The Mind and Life dialogues, begun in 1987 between the Dalai Lama and scientists, helped open contemplative practice to scientific study, though they are one of several routes by which this happened.

## Limits

The relation is a documented historical transfer of techniques and ideas, not a claim of equivalence between traditions. Several of the frameworks listed have limited research support in cross-cultural trials, and evidence for each should be assessed separately. Entries here are descriptive and are not clinical guidance.
