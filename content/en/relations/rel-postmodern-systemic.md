# Postmodern Constructivism and Family Systems: Born from Opposition

Michael White's narrative therapy, developed with David Epston from the 1980s, emerged within the family therapy field and in critical dialogue with its dominant systemic and structural models. Rather than a single break, the relationship combined continuity (White drew on Bateson's ideas) with critique (of fixed structure, expert diagnosis, and the language of pathology). Constructivist and social constructionist ideas also shaped collaborative and conversational approaches (Anderson and Goolishian; Tom Andersen). This entry compares these currents with classical family systems therapy.

## Philosophical Context

- **Michel Foucault** (1926-1984): power/knowledge and the historical constitution of categories (*Surveiller et punir*, 1975). White drew explicitly on Foucault's analysis of power and knowledge and discussed it in *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* (1990).
- **Jacques Derrida** (1930-2004): deconstruction.
- **Jean-François Lyotard** (1924-1998): *La condition postmoderne* (1979), skepticism toward grand narratives.
- **Kenneth Gergen** (born 1935): "The Social Constructionist Movement in Modern Psychology" (*American Psychologist*, 1985).
- **Constructivism and social constructionism** are related but distinct: the former emphasizes how individuals construct their worlds, the latter how meaning is built in social and linguistic exchange.

## Classical Family Systems

- **Gregory Bateson**: communication, patterns, and cybernetic ideas ("Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia," 1956; *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, 1972).
- **Salvador Minuchin**: structural family therapy (*Families and Family Therapy*, 1974), in which the therapist maps boundaries and subsystems and restructures them.
- **Murray Bowen**: family systems theory (1978).
- **The Milan group** (Selvini Palazzoli and colleagues): systemic hypothesizing and circular questioning (*Paradox and Counterparadox*, 1978).

These approaches often assumed that families have patterns that the therapist can observe and change, a stance critics called "modernist."

## White and Epston

- **Michael White** (1948-2008) co-founded the Dulwich Centre in Adelaide; **David Epston** (born 1944) worked in New Zealand.
- Key texts: *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* (White and Epston, 1990); *Re-authoring Lives* (White, 1995); *Maps of Narrative Practice* (White, 2007).
- Central ideas: persons are shaped by stories; problems are externalized ("the person is not the problem; the problem is the problem"); "dominant stories" can obscure "unique outcomes" that allow alternative stories to be developed; therapy involves re-authoring.
- Sources include Bateson's distinctions (map and territory, news of difference), Foucault, and Bruner's narrative psychology.

## Points of Divergence

| Dimension | Classical family systems | Narrative / social constructionist |
|---|---|---|
| View of reality | Families have observable structures or patterns | Meanings and "realities" are constructed in language and social practice |
| Therapist | Expert who assesses and intervenes | Collaborator or curious inquirer |
| Language of problems | Dysfunction, structure, patterns | Dominant stories, discourse, externalized problems |
| Techniques | Restructuring, directives, circular questions | Externalizing conversations, re-authoring, unique outcomes |
| Role of power | Hierarchies in family organization | Power/knowledge in cultural discourses |

## Areas of Convergence

1. Both locate problems in relationships and context rather than in individual pathology alone.
2. Both are skeptical of the medical model.
3. Both attend to patterns and to resources (strengths, exceptions).
4. Systemic circularity and narrative attention to context share Bateson's ecological legacy.

## Related Developments

- **Solution-Focused Brief Therapy** (Steve de Shazer, 1940-2005, and Insoo Kim Berg, 1934-2007, Brief Family Therapy Center, Milwaukee, from 1982) developed from strategic and Ericksonian influences and uses "exceptions"; it is distinct from narrative therapy, though both share a constructivist tilt.
- **Collaborative Language Systems** (Harlene Anderson and Harold Goolishian): "Human Systems as Linguistic Systems" (1988) and the "not-knowing" stance.
- **Reflecting teams** (Tom Andersen, 1987): therapists reflect aloud their observations in front of the family.
- **Constructivist psychotherapy** (Mahoney, 1991; Neimeyer and Mahoney, 1995) applies related ideas in cognitive therapy.

## Critique

- Minuchin (1998) asked "Where is the family in narrative family therapy?" arguing that narrative approaches neglect family structure and interaction.
- Constructivists reply that structural models impose an expert's description on families.
- A deeper debate concerns whether re-narration can address material suffering, and how different stories are evaluated.
- Evidence: Solution-Focused Brief Therapy has a modest evidence base (Gingerich and Eisengart, 2000); narrative therapy has growing but more limited controlled evidence.

## Clinical Practice Today

Many family therapists integrate systemic and narrative or collaborative elements. Training programs commonly include both, and the Dulwich Centre remains a major center for narrative training.
