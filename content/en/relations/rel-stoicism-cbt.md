# Stoicism and CBT: From Epictetus to Beck

One of the best-documented cases of an ancient philosophical idea acknowledged as a precursor of modern psychotherapy. Albert Ellis (rational emotive behavior therapy, introduced in the mid-1950s) and Aaron T. Beck (cognitive therapy, developed from the 1960s) both pointed to the Stoics, and in particular to Epictetus, as forerunners. The relation is one of acknowledged inspiration and partial parallel, not of a direct and complete transmission, and the Stoic system differs from CBT in aims and metaphysics.

## The Philosophical Origin: Epictetus and the "Dichotomy of Control"

Epictetus (c. 50-c. 135 CE), a former slave who taught in Nicopolis, is known through the notes of his pupil Arrian: the *Discourses* and the *Enchiridion* (Handbook). The opening of the *Enchiridion* distinguishes what is "up to us" (judgment, impulse, desire, aversion, that is, our own activities) from what is not up to us (body, property, reputation, office). The label "dichotomy of control" is a modern one. A closely related maxim, at *Enchiridion* 5, says that people are disturbed not by things but by the judgments they form about things.

The Stoic background is the theory of emotions as judgments (associated especially with Chrysippus): passions arise from mistaken evaluations of externals as genuine goods or evils. Therapeutic practice consisted in examining one's impressions and withholding assent from false ones, set within a system of ethics, physics (a rational, providential cosmos) and logic.

## The Historical Transition

- **Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius (121-180 CE):** Marcus's *Meditations* (written in Greek as private notes) repeat the idea that distress comes from one's opinion about an event, and not from the event itself (for example *Meditations* 4.7).
- **Transmission:** Stoic texts remained known through the Renaissance and early modern periods, and Epictetus's *Enchiridion* was widely read, but the idea of a systematic psychotherapy based on it is a twentieth-century development.
- **Ellis:** he read Stoic and other philosophers as a young man and later cited Epictetus on judgments in presenting REBT (*Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy*, 1962). His ABC model (Activating event, Belief, Consequence) formalizes the idea.
- **Beck:** he developed cognitive therapy through his clinical research on depression, with influences from psychoanalysis and cognitive psychology. In *Cognitive Therapy of Depression* (Beck, Rush, Shaw and Emery, 1979), the authors acknowledge Stoic philosophers, including Epictetus, as the philosophical precursors of the approach. Beck's "automatic thoughts" and "schemas" are empirical and clinical constructs, not translations of Stoic terms.

## Essential Differences

| Dimension | Epictetus (Stoicism) | Ellis (REBT) | Beck (cognitive therapy) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Stoic philosophy, a physics of a rational cosmos | Philosophical sources (including Stoics) plus clinical practice | Clinical research on depression; cognitive psychology |
| Concept of judgment | Assent to impressions; the passions are false judgments | "Irrational beliefs" (demands, awfulizing) | Automatic thoughts and underlying schemas, with distortions |
| What is controllable | What is "up to us": judgments and choices | Beliefs about events | Interpretations; situations may be modifiable |
| Method | Examination of impressions, daily exercises | Disputation of irrational beliefs | Guided discovery, thought records, behavioral experiments |
| Aim | Virtue and a life according to nature | Emotional well-being and rational living | Symptom reduction and improved functioning |
| Metaphysics | Providential cosmos | Secular humanism | Neutral |

## What Was Preserved

1. **Mediating cognition:** emotion is influenced by how events are interpreted.
2. **Practical discrimination:** distinguishing what one can and cannot change.
3. **Re-evaluation of judgments** as a central technique.
4. **Exercises:** Stoic practices such as evening review and written reflection resemble elements of CBT (journaling, thought records), although the comparison is by analogy.

## What Was Lost or Transformed

1. **The ethical system:** Stoicism is a philosophy of virtue (wisdom, justice, courage, moderation). CBT adopted the cognitive technique without that system.
2. **The cosmic frame:** the Stoic idea of a providential rational order (*Logos*) is absent from CBT.
3. **A view of externals:** Stoics regarded external goods as "preferred indifferents," not as genuine goods; CBT stays neutral about values.
4. **Death:** Stoic texts treat death as natural and not an evil; CBT treats death anxiety as a symptom or cognitive target.
5. **Goal of the practice:** CBT aims at symptom relief and flexible functioning, whereas Stoic training aims at a transformation of character.

## Contemporary Bridges

- **Donald Robertson**: *The Philosophy of Cognitive-Behavioural Therapy: Stoicism as Rational and Cognitive Psychotherapy* (2010) examines the link in detail; his later books present Stoic practices for general readers.
- **William Irvine**: *A Guide to the Good Life: The Ancient Art of Stoic Joy* (2008).
- **Massimo Pigliucci**: *How to Be a Stoic* (2017) and *A Field Guide to a Happy Life* (2020) belong to the same popular revival.
- **Martha Nussbaum**: *The Therapy of Desire* (1994) examines Hellenistic philosophy, including Stoicism, as medical-therapeutic practice.
- The Modern Stoicism movement runs courses and events, and some studies explore Stoic interventions; the evidence base is modest compared with that for CBT.

## Mutual Criticism

- **Philosophical:** some historians of philosophy argue that modern uses detach Stoic techniques from their ethics and physics and so produce a technique rather than a philosophy of life. John Sellars's *Stoicism* (2006) offers an accessible scholarly account of the ancient doctrine against which such claims can be tested.
- **Clinical:** CBT researchers note that ancient Stoic claims were not tested empirically, and that CBT should be judged by trial evidence.
- **Third-wave:** some authors observe that acceptance-based therapies (such as ACT) share with Stoicism an attitude of accepting what cannot be controlled, while differing from Stoic and Beckian emphasis on correcting judgments.

## Clinical-Philosophical Summary

| | Stoicism | CBT |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | A life of virtue and tranquility | Reducing symptoms and improving functioning |
| Tool | Examination of impressions, daily practice | Cognitive restructuring, behavioral experiments, homework |
| Measure of success | Character and conduct | Symptom measures and relapse rates |
| Death | Natural, not an evil | Anxiety to be addressed in therapy |
| Evidence | Textual and philosophical | Clinical trials |

The relation is best described as acknowledged inspiration with substantial transformation and loss, and it should not be read as showing that CBT simply re-states Stoicism.
