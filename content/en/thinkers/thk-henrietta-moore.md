# Henrietta Moore

Henrietta L. Moore (born 1957) is a British social anthropologist and one of the leading theorists of gender in anthropology. Her early fieldwork among the Marakwet of Kenya showed how the layout of a household — where men sit, where women cook, where children sleep — encodes ideas about masculinity and femininity. From that ethnographic starting point she built a body of theory on gender, symbolism, and psychoanalysis, and in recent decades she has become a leading global thinker on prosperity, founding the Institute for Global Prosperity at University College London (UCL). She was appointed Dame Commander of the Order of the British Empire in 2016.

## Life and Career

Moore was born on 18 May 1957. She graduated from Durham University with a degree in archaeology and anthropology in 1979, then continued her studies at Newnham College, Cambridge, completing a doctorate in anthropology in 1983 with a thesis on men, women, and the organisation of domestic space among the Marakwet of Kenya. Her long-term fieldwork in Africa grounded all her later theory in detailed ethnography of gender, livelihood, and social transformation.

Her academic career moved between Britain's leading institutions. She taught at the London School of Economics, where she was director of the Gender Institute. In 2008 she succeeded Marilyn Strathern as William Wyse Professor of Social Anthropology at the University of Cambridge, holding the chair until 2014. She then moved to UCL, where she founded the Institute for Global Prosperity and holds the Chair in Culture, Philosophy and Design. She is a Fellow of the British Academy, a Fellow of the Royal Society of Arts, and an Academician of the Academy of Social Sciences.

## Key Works

Moore's first book, *Space, Text and Gender: An Anthropological Study of the Marakwet of Kenya* (1986), developed from her doctoral fieldwork and showed how gender is inscribed in the organisation of domestic space. *Feminism and Anthropology* (1988) surveyed the uneasy relationship between feminist theory and anthropology and became a standard text. *A Passion for Difference: Essays in Anthropology and Gender* (1994) collected her essays on gender, symbolism, and the body, and *Cutting Down Trees: Gender, Nutrition and Agricultural Change in the Northern Province of Zambia, 1890–1990* (1994), written with the historian Megan Vaughan, traced a century of change in women's work and nutrition in northern Zambia.

She edited two influential volumes, *The Future of Anthropological Knowledge* (1996) and *Anthropological Theory Today* (1999), and her later book *The Subject of Anthropology: Gender, Symbolism and Psychoanalysis* (2007) brought psychoanalytic theory into dialogue with the anthropology of gender and the self. In recent years her work has turned to prosperity and sustainable futures, articulated through the Institute for Global Prosperity's research on universal basic services, new economic models, and the future of cities.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Moore's central contribution is a distinctive approach to the comparative analysis of gender and sexuality. Against the assumption that "man" and "woman" are stable, universal categories, she argued that gender is produced through the interrelation of material and symbolic systems: the division of labour, the layout of space, ritual, and the body. Her Marakwet ethnography demonstrated this concretely — the house is not a neutral container for social life but a text in which gendered meanings are written and read.

She also developed an influential dialogue between anthropology and psychoanalysis. Drawing above all on Lacanian theory, she argued that psychoanalysis offers anthropology a way of thinking about the subject, desire, and identification that does not reduce the person to a reflection of social structure. Her work in this vein explores what psychoanalytic perspectives might mean for the anthropology of gender and the self.

In her later work on prosperity, Moore has challenged conventional models of economic growth, arguing that communities, businesses, and governments can only flourish by engaging with diversity, working collaboratively, and remaining within ecological limits. This work connects her early concerns — livelihood strategies, gender, and social transformation in Africa — to global questions of inequality, displacement, and the green transition.

## Influence and Critique

Moore is recognised as one of the leading theorists of gender in social anthropology, and her books have shaped feminist anthropology, the anthropology of the body, and the study of space and material culture. Her editorial work helped define the agenda of anthropological theory in the 1990s, and her move from gender theory to the study of prosperity has made her a prominent public intellectual in debates about sustainable development, universal basic services, and the future of cities.

Her work has also been debated. Some critics argue that her use of psychoanalytic theory, developed in a European clinical tradition, risks universalising a particular model of the subject, and that the turn to Lacan sits uneasily with the ethnographic commitment to local understandings of personhood. Others have questioned whether the language of prosperity can carry the critical force of her earlier work on gender and inequality, or whether it softens the analysis of power. Moore's defenders point to the continuity of her project: from the Marakwet house to the global city, she has asked how people make meaningful lives within structures they did not choose — and how those structures might be changed.
