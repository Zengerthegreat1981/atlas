# Paul Greengard

An American neuroscientist and biochemist (1925–2019), who uncovered how slow signals are transmitted between nerve cells through second messengers and protein phosphorylation. He shared the 2000 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Arvid Carlsson and Eric Kandel. His work made it possible to understand how brain drugs act inside the cell after binding to the receptor, and not only at the synapse.

## Training and Career

Greengard was born in New York in 1925 and served in the US Navy during the Second World War. He earned a bachelor's degree at Hamilton College in 1948 and a doctorate in biophysics at Johns Hopkins University in 1953, then held postdoctoral posts in Britain and at the National Institutes of Health. From 1959 to 1967 he directed biochemistry at Geigy Research Laboratories in Ardsley, New York. He was professor of pharmacology at the Albert Einstein College of Medicine and, from 1968 to 1983, professor of pharmacology and psychiatry at Yale. In 1983 he moved to Rockefeller University, where he headed the Laboratory of Molecular and Cellular Neuroscience until his death in 2019.

## Slow Signaling and Second Messengers

It had been known, since the work of Hodgkin, Huxley and Katz, that signaling between nerve cells travels within fractions of a second through ion channels that the transmitter opens. Greengard showed that there is another, slower mode that lasts from seconds to minutes, in which transmitters act through G-protein-coupled receptors that increase a second messenger inside the cell, cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). With Kebabian and Petzold in 1972 he showed that dopamine activates an enzyme that synthesizes cAMP in the caudate nucleus of the rat. With Clement-Cormier and colleagues in 1974 he showed that antipsychotics inhibit this enzyme, linking the dopamine receptor and antipsychotic drugs at the biochemical level. He argued, and supplied extensive biochemical evidence, that cAMP acts by activating protein kinase A, which adds phosphate groups to target proteins and changes their function, thereby altering the cell's excitability and its ability to release transmitter. Protein kinase A itself had been identified in other tissues; Greengard's contribution was to show how this chemistry operates at synapses.

## Phosphorylated Proteins: DARPP-32 and Synapsin

Greengard's team identified specific phosphorylated proteins that act as links between the chemical signal and the cell's response. The best known is the protein DARPP-32, described by Walaas and Greengard in 1984. It is abundant in the regions that receive dopamine and acts as a switch that integrates signals from dopamine, glutamate and serotonin inside medium spiny neurons. Later studies, including the paper by Svenningsson and colleagues in *Science* in 2003, showed that drugs that produce psychosis-like effects, such as phencyclidine and hallucinogens, act through a common pathway that includes this protein. Synapsin is a family of proteins that binds transmitter vesicles to the cytoskeleton and regulates their release. Variants in the synapsin I gene have been linked to epilepsy and, in some families, to autism spectrum disorder.

## Relevance to Psychiatry

This work helped in understanding how antipsychotics, antidepressants, stimulants and addiction drugs act, since the signal after the receptor determines the long-term effect. In 2002 Greengard co-founded Intra-Cellular Therapies to develop drugs that target these pathways. The company later developed lumateperone, which the US Food and Drug Administration approved for schizophrenia in 2019 and subsequently for depressive episodes in bipolar disorder (2021) and as an add-on to antidepressants for major depressive disorder (2025). This is an example of basic knowledge moving into treatment, although the link between the drug and the DARPP-32 pathway specifically remains under investigation.

## Awards and Support for Women in Science

Greengard used part of his Nobel Prize money to establish the Pearl Meister Greengard Prize (first awarded in 2004), which recognizes outstanding women scientists. It honors his mother, who died shortly after his birth.

## Impact

Greengard's work made slow chemical signaling a central part of neuroscience alongside the fast electrical account associated with Hodgkin, Huxley and Katz. It supplied a framework that links transmitters such as dopamine to phosphorylation inside the cell, and it gave psychopharmacology a language for what happens after a drug binds a receptor. Learning, mood and drug response are not explained by these reactions alone, but they are no longer discussed only as events at the membrane.

## Limits and Criticism

This entry is educational and not clinical advice. Greengard's work focuses on pathways in specific cells and does not by itself explain how symptoms of schizophrenia or depression arise at the level of circuits and behavior. Many experiments were conducted on rats and mice and on brain slices, and whether the results carry over to humans remains an open question. Psychiatric diagnoses also do not map onto specific signaling pathways: there is no biological test based on DARPP-32 in clinical practice, and it cannot be concluded that the observed drug effect of any medication passes through this pathway alone.
