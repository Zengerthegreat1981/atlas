# David Ricardo

**David Ricardo** was a British economist of Sephardic Jewish origin. He combined great financial success in the London bond market, which allowed him to retire early and devote himself fully to theoretical research, with being one of the most methodologically rigorous and logically abstract minds of classical political economy. With James Mill's encouragement he pushed classical political economy toward a spare deductive style that later theorists treated as a model.

## From Reader of Smith to Theoretical Rival

Ricardo entered the field of political economy after a relatively late reading of Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations*. He then engaged in a public debate over British monetary policy during the Napoleonic Wars (the "Bullionist Controversy") before moving on to formulate a complete theoretical system in his principal work, *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817). Unlike Smith's descriptive style, rich in historical examples, Ricardo adopted an abstract deductive method: he began from explicitly simplified assumptions and derived rigorously logical conclusions from them. This approach laid the foundations of what would later be known as the Ricardian school of political economy.

## The Theory of Differential Rent

Ricardo contributed profoundly to the political debate of his time over the **Corn Laws**, which imposed protective tariffs on grain imports to shield large British landowners. He developed the theory of **differential rent**: when farmers, under rising demand, are forced to cultivate less fertile marginal land, owners of the most fertile land earn a rent equal to the difference in productivity, without any additional effort on their part. Ricardo used this theory as a direct political argument. Protecting domestic grain prices raises landowners' rents at the expense of the profits of industrial capitalists and the wages of workers, which harms the national economy as a whole.

## Comparative Advantage: His Most Distinctive Contribution

In *Principles*, Ricardo presented an argument that has remained among the most enduring ideas in economics: **comparative advantage**. Through a numerical example of trade in wine and cloth between Portugal and England, he argued that specialisation and free exchange can benefit both countries even when one is more productive in every good in absolute terms, if each specialises in the good it produces at lower opportunity cost.

## Parliamentary Service and Direct Political Influence

Ricardo's influence extended beyond academic circles into direct political practice. He was elected a Member of Parliament for the House of Commons in 1819, where he defended his theoretical positions in debates on free trade, taxation and monetary policy until his relatively early death in 1823.

## The Dual Legacy: Neoclassical and Marxist

It is an irony in the reception of Ricardo's thought that it later influenced two diametrically opposed directions. Neoclassical economics took from him the rigour of the deductive method and the interest in international trade. Karl Marx, by contrast, regarded Ricardo as having taken the labour theory of value more seriously than anyone else as a scientific matter, and built his own critique of capitalism on it. Piero Sraffa later revived Ricardo's methodology in the twentieth century within the Cambridge capital controversy, affirming the continuity of his analytical legacy across ideologically competing schools.

## Personal Wealth and the Source of His Intellectual Independence

Ricardo amassed a large fortune working as a broker in the British government bond market, especially during the Napoleonic Wars, and is traditionally said to have profited from the rise in British government bond prices after the Battle of Waterloo in 1815. This wealth allowed him to retire from the stock exchange in 1814, at the age of forty-two, and devote himself fully to theoretical research and writing. This financial independence was rare among the thinkers of his time, and it enabled him to pursue his theoretical ideas to their furthest logical conclusions without regard to pressing institutional or academic interests.

## His Scientific Correspondence: The Real Laboratory of Ideas

Historians of economic thought regard Ricardo's private correspondence, especially with Thomas Malthus and James Mill, as an extremely important source for understanding the development of his ideas. These letters reveal a careful internal thought process, with hesitations and explicit admissions of theoretical difficulties that did not appear as clearly in his published texts, which seem more coherent on the surface. The correspondence was later collected in the *Works and Correspondence* edited by Piero Sraffa with the collaboration of Maurice Dobb (1951–1973).

## Social Context: A Sephardic Jew in a Protestant Society

Ricardo was born into a Sephardic Jewish family that had emigrated from Holland to England. In 1793 he married Priscilla Wilkinson, a Quaker, after a break with his Jewish family, and he later became a Unitarian. This personal path reflects the complexities of the social integration of Jews in Britain at the beginning of the nineteenth century, though it had no direct effect on his economic ideas, which remained purely secular in their analytical method.
