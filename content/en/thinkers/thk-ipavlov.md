# Ivan Pavlov

A Russian physiologist (1849–1936), Nobel laureate (1904), founder of "Classical Conditioning," and father of "Objective Psychology."

## Biography and Context

Pavlov was born in Ryazan in 1849, studied in St. Petersburg, headed the physiology department at the Institute of Experimental Medicine, and worked for decades on digestion with advanced surgical techniques that allowed him to follow glandular secretion in healthy, living dogs rather than anesthetized animals. In this connection he published *Lectures on the Work of the Main Digestive Glands* (1897). His method rested on long observation of the healthy animal, which distinguished him from the physiologists of his day, who relied on brief acute experiments.

## What He Contributed

He won the Nobel Prize in 1904 for his research on the **physiology of digestion**, not on learning. From his measurement of salivary secretion in that research he noticed that dogs salivate before the food arrives; he first called this "psychic secretion" and then devoted decades to studying it. He showed that the salivary response arises to a neutral stimulus (a tone, a metronome or a light; the bell was only one of many stimuli, contrary to the popular textbook picture) after it has been **paired** with food several times in succession. He called the acquired response the "Conditioned Reflex" and extended the law to include "extinction," "generalization" and "discrimination." He also proposed a typology of "types of nervous system" (strong and weak types), which later influenced Hans Eysenck's work on extraversion–introversion.

This appeared in his books *Conditioned Reflexes: An Investigation of the Physiological Activity of the Cerebral Cortex* (1927, in English translated by Anrep) and *Lectures on Conditioned Reflexes* (1928). As Pavlov observed of the acquired reflex mechanism (1927, p. 25):

> "The temporary nervous connection is the most universal physiological phenomenon in the animal world existing around us, and in ourselves."

He later used the same experiment to study what he called "experimental neurosis": when the difference between two stimuli the animal has learned to discriminate is narrowed, its behavior becomes disturbed and it shows signs resembling a breakdown. In his final years he spoke of the "second signaling system," that is, language as the signal of signals in the human being, a distinction that later influenced Soviet psychology.

## His Place in the Current

The "common grandfather" of all behaviorism (methodological, radical and therapeutic), although behaviorists did not accept his neurophysiological theory. A major influence on Watson, who adopted the idea of conditioning in building behaviorism after his 1913 article, and on Skinner, Wolpe and Eysenck. In Russia a whole generation of physiologists studied under him, among them some who carried his laboratory to America, such as the American W. Horsley Gantt, who worked with him and then founded a laboratory at Johns Hopkins University.

## Limits and Critique

First: what is called Soviet "Pavlovianism" is not his doing. Pavlov died in 1936, and then after his death, in **1950**, the Soviet Academy of Sciences held the joint "Pavlovian Session," at which his doctrine was imposed as official dogma on physiology and psychiatry, and dissenters were sidelined. That is, his name was used after his death as a tool for liquidating scientific opponents.

Pavlov himself was one of the most outspoken public critics of the Bolshevik regime; he wrote letters of protest to its leaders, and remained protected because the regime valued his international prestige. This is one of the clearest cases in the history of science of a scholar's legacy being seized against his declared position.

Second: scientifically, his simple model of conditioning did not survive unchanged. Rescorla (1967) showed that conditioning depends on the **contingency** between the two stimuli, not merely on their temporal pairing, and Garcia and Koelling (1966) showed that an animal learns the association of taste with nausea with an ease it does not show with other kinds of stimuli, so not all stimuli are equally associable. The four "types of nervous system" have also not proved valid as a fixed biological classification. To this is added an ethical objection: his laboratories performed repeated surgeries on many dogs, which makes his method a subject in the literature on the ethics of animal research.

## Major Works

Lectures on the Work of the Main Digestive Glands (1897) · Conditioned Reflexes (1927) · Lectures on Conditioned Reflexes (1928).
