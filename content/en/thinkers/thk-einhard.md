# Einhard

## Definition

Einhard (c. 775–840 CE) was a Frankish scholar and writer of East Frankish origin (from the Mainfeld region). He was educated at the monastery of Fulda, then joined the court of Charlemagne at Aachen, where he became one of the most prominent scholars of the "Carolingian Renaissance," as well as an architect and a trusted court adviser. He is best known for his *Life of Charlemagne* (*Vita Karoli Magni*), which is regarded as the first biography of a secular ruler written in Western Europe since the fall of the Roman Empire, and is one of the most important sources for Carolingian history on the life of the founder of the Carolingian Empire.

## Historical Context

Einhard wrote his biography in the decade following Charlemagne's death in 814, probably between 817 and 833, during the reign of Charlemagne's son and successor, Louis the Pious, at a time when the Carolingian court was undergoing a reassessment of Charlemagne's legacy amid growing political tensions among his heirs. For many years Einhard served as an intimate adviser first to Charlemagne and then to Louis the Pious, which gave him direct and personal knowledge of the emperor's daily life, habits, and family, material that was rare and not available to any other historian of that age with the same degree of closeness and personal experience.

## Main Ideas or Method

Einhard consciously followed the model of the classical Roman biography, specifically Suetonius's *Lives of the Twelve Caesars* (*De Vita Caesarum*), borrowing from it the narrative structure and sometimes entire phrases and constructions verbatim. This is a classic example of the "literary imitation" (*imitatio*) that characterized the Carolingian Renaissance in its conscious recovery of the ancient Roman heritage. Einhard divided his biography into thematic sections rather than a purely linear chronological narrative: an introduction on the origin of the Carolingian dynasty, then Charlemagne's military and political achievements, then an intimate section describing his physical appearance, eating habits, pastimes, and family relationships, and finally Charlemagne's testament and death. This structure was taken directly from the model of Suetonius.

## Major Works or Representatives

Einhard's *Life of Charlemagne* is his only famous work in the field of historiography. It enjoyed wide circulation and dense copying throughout the Middle Ages, and more than eighty manuscripts of it have survived, indicating its high reference status. Einhard is also credited, though this attribution is a matter of continuing scholarly debate, with part of the *Royal Frankish Annals* (*Annales Regni Francorum*). He also left many personal letters and another work entitled *The Translation and Miracles of the Saints Marcellinus and Peter*, which documents the transfer of holy relics to his own estate, a text that reveals an aspect of popular Carolingian piety.

## Legacy and Criticism

Einhard's biography established a literary and historiographical model that later medieval writers followed when writing the lives of kings and emperors, and it remains today the principal source on which historians of the Carolingian age rely in depicting Charlemagne's character and daily life. Contemporary critics, however, warn against Einhard's excessive reliance on Suetonius's literary model, to the point that it is sometimes difficult to distinguish between an actual realistic description and a literary borrowing copied verbatim. His close personal proximity to the court also made him inclined to exaggerate the glorification of Charlemagne and to whitewash some of the more problematic aspects of his rule, especially his bloody wars against the Saxons and the policy of forced Christianization he imposed on them.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Einhard's treatment of the classical Roman model (Suetonius) offers a striking parallel to how Arab Muslim writers handled ancient Persian literary models in writing the biographies of caliphs and kings. For example, early Arabic biographical literature was influenced by the translated Persian traditions of the "mirrors for princes" (*adab al-muluk*). In addition, the documented diplomatic relations between Charlemagne and the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid (the famous exchange of gifts that included the elephant Abu al-Abbas and the water clock) make Einhard's biography a rare direct European source for understanding the image of the Abbasid caliphate in early Western European consciousness during the Middle Ages.

## The Personal Section and Physical Description

One of the most distinctive and famous parts of the biography is the section in which Einhard describes Charlemagne's physical appearance with almost clinical precision: his tall stature, thick neck, slightly protruding belly, a voice relatively high-pitched for his large body, and his love of swimming in the hot springs of Aachen, to the extent that he built his palace nearby specifically for this purpose. Einhard also describes the simplicity of Charlemagne's everyday dress compared with the splendid appearance of the court, his relatively moderate eating habits, and his strong determination to have his sons and daughters taught reading, writing, and the noble arts, despite his own relative illiteracy in writing although he could read. These intimate human details are rarely found in the writings of early medieval historians about the rulers of their age, which explains why this text continues to be cited as a primary source in all modern studies of the actual personality of Charlemagne.
