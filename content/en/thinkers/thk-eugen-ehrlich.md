# Eugen Ehrlich

An Austrian jurist and sociologist of Jewish origin, who taught Roman law at the University of Czernowitz in the multi-ethnic region of Bukovina from the late 1890s and is regarded as one of the founding fathers of the sociology of law as an independent academic discipline. He is best known for his central concept of "living law."

## The Central Idea

In *Grundlegung der Soziologie des Rechts* (1913; translated into English as *Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law*, 1936), Ehrlich put forward a founding thesis: the "center of gravity" of legal development in any society lies neither in official legislation issued by the state nor in judicial decisions of the courts, but in society itself, in the practices, customs, contracts and actual arrangements by which people organize their daily relations (marriage, work, property, trade), regardless of what the explicitly written official law says. For this he formulated the concept of "living law" (Lebendes Recht): the set of actual rules by which people live and which they obey voluntarily in their daily lives. These rules may coincide with the state's official law or differ from it fundamentally. He based his conclusions on his direct field observation of the multi-ethnic region of Bukovina (including Romanians, Ukrainians, Germans, Jews, Poles and Armenians), where multiple customary and legal systems actually coexisted under the umbrella of a single official Austrian imperial law that did not accurately reflect this actual diversity. He argued that a genuinely sociological understanding of law requires an empirical study of this actual "living law," rather than being content with analyzing abstract official legal texts, as traditional dogmatic legal studies had customarily done.

## Life and Debates

Ehrlich was born in Czernowitz in 1862 and died in 1922. Earlier in his career he argued for "free" legal decision-making in *Freie Rechtsfindung und freie Rechtswissenschaft* (1903), within the Free Law movement. His claims were criticized by Hans Kelsen in a well-known exchange (1915), who argued that Ehrlich confused the normative science of law with the sociological study of social behavior.

## Impact

Ehrlich is a founding figure of the sociology of law as an empirical field distinct from dogmatic legal studies. His work was taken up in the United States by Roscoe Pound, and his concept of living law is a recurring reference point for later socio-legal scholarship, including work on legal pluralism and on the gap between "law in books" and "law in action," a phrase associated with Pound.
