# Confucius

Kong Qiu, known as Kongzi or "Master Kong" (traditionally 551–479 BCE), was a teacher and political thinker from the state of Lu in the Spring and Autumn period of Chinese history. He taught that a good society depends on the moral cultivation of individuals, on proper ritual and on responsible, humane government. His teaching became the foundation of what the West calls Confucianism, the most influential tradition in East Asian intellectual life.

## Life and Context

Almost everything about his life comes from later sources, above all the *Records of the Grand Historian* by Sima Qian (about 100 BCE), written four centuries after his death, along with scattered anecdotes in the *Analects*. By this tradition he was born in or near Qufu in the state of Lu, lost his father young, and rose from modest circumstances through study of ritual, music, poetry and history. He held minor office in Lu, and in middle age spent years travelling among the feudal states with a group of disciples, seeking a ruler who would apply his principles, without lasting success. He returned to Lu late in life and devoted himself to teaching and to the transmission of the classical texts. His era was marked by the decline of Zhou royal authority and by incessant warfare among states, which gave urgency to his question of how order and virtue can be restored.

## Main Ideas

Confucius did not claim to invent a new doctrine; he described himself as a transmitter of the way of the early Zhou sages. Several concepts organize his thought, as presented in the *Analects*.

- **Ren** (humaneness, benevolence): the central virtue, expressed in care for others and in a version of reciprocity (*shu*) often rendered as not imposing on others what one would not want oneself.
- **Li** (ritual, propriety): the forms of conduct through which respect and social roles are enacted, which he treated as a means of shaping character and not as empty formality.
- **Junzi** (the exemplary person): an ideal defined by moral character rather than birth.
- **Filial piety** (*xiao*) and the proper conduct of the five relationships: family virtue as the root of civic virtue.
- **Government by virtue**: rulers should lead by moral example and not by punishment alone, and the "rectification of names" requires that words and social roles correspond to reality.
- **Learning and self-cultivation**: moral development is lifelong and depends on study, reflection and imitation of worthy models.

## Sources of Our Knowledge

Confucius is not known to have authored books in the modern sense. The primary source, the *Analects* (*Lunyu*), is a collection of sayings and brief dialogues compiled by disciples and later generations over a period of time; scholars see it as having layers of different dates, with some passages probably added after his lifetime. Tradition also credited him with editing the Five Classics (the *Book of Poetry*, *Book of Documents*, *Book of Changes*, *Book of Rites* and the *Spring and Autumn Annals*), but the extent of his role is uncertain and is doubted for most of them. Later texts such as the *Mencius*, the *Xunzi*, the *Great Learning* and the *Doctrine of the Mean* record or develop the school's thought but are not by him.

## Method

His method is pedagogical and exemplary rather than systematic. The *Analects* shows him adapting his answers to the student and the occasion, leading disciples to infer a general lesson, and valuing the combination of learning and thought. He taught from texts, especially poetry and history, and from the model of the ancients, and he treated ritual practice as a way of training sensibility. He tends to describe virtues through concrete cases and persons, and not through abstract definitions, which is why scholars continue to debate whether his thought amounts to a systematic ethical theory.

## Influence and Reception

After a period of competition with other schools in the Warring States, the tradition was elevated under the Han dynasty, and from the Han period onward Confucian learning became the basis of state education and the civil service examinations in China, a role that lasted until the early twentieth century. Neo-Confucian thinkers of the Song and Ming dynasties, most notably Zhu Xi and Wang Yangming, reworked it metaphysically; the tradition spread to Korea, Japan and Vietnam. Jesuit missionaries introduced him to Europe, where his name was Latinized as "Confucius." In the twentieth century he was attacked in China, notably in the May Fourth period and the Cultural Revolution, and later reappeared in public discourse; contemporary philosophers have also developed Confucian virtue ethics and political thought.

## Criticisms and Debates

Early critics included the Mohists, who rejected elaborate ritual and partiality to kin in favour of impartial concern, and the Daoists, who saw his moral project as artificial. Modern critics have argued that the emphasis on hierarchy and obedience supported authoritarian rule and patriarchal structures, and passages on women have been widely criticized. Defenders respond that the core ideas stress reciprocal obligations and the ruler's moral accountability. Historians also debate how far the "Confucius" of the texts reflects the actual man, how much of the *Analects* is authentic, and whether "Confucianism" is best understood as a philosophy, a religion or a way of life.
