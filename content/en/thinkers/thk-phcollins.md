# Patricia Hill Collins

Patricia Hill Collins (born 1948) is an American sociologist and social theorist whose work on Black feminist thought and intersectionality has had a major influence on sociology, gender studies and race studies.

## Life and Context

Collins earned her doctorate in sociology at Brandeis University (1984) and taught at the University of Cincinnati, where she was a professor of African American studies, before moving to the University of Maryland, College Park. In 2009 she became the first Black woman to serve as president of the American Sociological Association. Her work grows out of the traditions of Black women intellectuals and activists and engages with American sociology and feminist theory.

She was born in Philadelphia. Before the doctorate she took a master's degree in teaching at Harvard and worked in schools and community education.

## Main Ideas

In *Black Feminist Thought*, Collins argued that Black women have developed a distinctive standpoint shaped by their location at intersections of race, class and gender, and that this knowledge has often been excluded from mainstream scholarship. She described the outsider-within position, in which Black women see social life from vantage points that reveal contradictions in dominant ideologies. She analyzed controlling images, such as stereotypes of the mammy or the welfare queen, that justify oppression. She proposed the matrix of domination to describe how systems of power are organized across structural, disciplinary, hegemonic and interpersonal domains. Her later work on intersectionality treats it as both an analytic tool and a form of critical social theory, linked to praxis and social justice. Among the controlling images she discusses is the matriarch, alongside the mammy and the welfare mother. In the matrix of domination, each domain of power can also be a site of resistance. *Fighting Words* (1998) asks what makes a social theory critical, and *From Black Power to Hip Hop* (2006) takes up nationalism, feminism, and youth culture.

## Key Concepts in Context

The "outsider-within" idea was first set out in her 1986 article "Learning from the Outsider Within" (*Social Problems*), which examined Black women domestic workers and academics who gain insight from being included yet never fully belonging. *Black Feminist Thought* organizes Black women's thought around themes such as self-definition, the interlocking nature of oppression, and the importance of everyday culture, family and community as sites of knowledge and resistance. Her notion of intersectionality builds on the term coined by Kimberle Crenshaw (1989, 1991), but she places more weight on systems of power (the matrix of domination) than on the position of individuals. Her writing on Black popular culture and sexuality (*Black Sexual Politics*, 2004) applies the same framework to contemporary media, family and politics. Her work also echoes W. E. B. Du Bois's idea of double consciousness, which she draws on in discussing Black intellectual traditions.

## Method

Collins draws on interpretive and historical sources, including literature, music and the writings of Black women, together with sociological analysis, and she argues for taking lived experience seriously as a source of knowledge. In *Black Feminist Thought* she sets out an alternative epistemology in which experience is a criterion of meaning, dialogue is used to assess knowledge claims, and an ethic of care is joined to an ethic of personal accountability.

## Influence and Criticism

Her work, alongside that of Kimberle Crenshaw, helped establish intersectionality as a widely used framework across the social sciences. Critics have argued that standpoint theory risks essentializing group experience, and that intersectionality can be conceptually ambiguous or difficult to operationalize in empirical research. Collins has answered that a standpoint is collective and heterogeneous rather than a single uniform experience, and that the assessment of knowledge claims remains part of the theory.
