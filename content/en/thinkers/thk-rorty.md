# Richard Rorty

An American philosopher (1931–2007), the pioneer of what has been called "Neo-Pragmatism", who taught philosophy at Princeton, then the humanities at the University of Virginia, then comparative literature at Stanford. He became famous for emerging from the heart of analytic philosophy to announce the end of its representational project, and for rereading Dewey's pragmatist heritage in the light of Heidegger, Wittgenstein and Nietzsche, thus becoming the most prominent bridge between the analytic and continental traditions in the second half of the twentieth century.

## His life and intellectual formation

Rorty was born in New York on 4 October 1931 to a family of leftist intellectuals, the son of the writer James Rorty and the writer and activist Winifred Raushenbush. He studied at the University of Chicago, where he was influenced by the climate of Richard McKeon and Charles Hartshorne, and then received his doctorate from Yale in 1956. He taught at Wellesley College, then moved to Princeton in 1961 and stayed there until 1982, the period in which he wrote his first analytic works and edited the collection *The Linguistic Turn* (1967). He then held a university professorship of the humanities at Virginia until 1998, and ended at Stanford in the Department of Comparative Literature until his retirement. He died in Palo Alto on 8 June 2007.

## What he contributed

In his book *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) he shattered the picture that modern philosophy had inherited since Descartes, Locke and Kant, namely that the mind is an inner mirror that reflects reality, and that the task of philosophy is to examine the quality of the mirror and correct its reflections. He relied on three heroes, Wittgenstein, Heidegger and Dewey, and added to them Sellars's critique of the "myth of the given" and Quine's critique of the distinction between analytic and synthetic, to conclude that knowledge is not an accurate representation but a social practice of justification that takes place within an open "conversation". From here he distinguished between "systematic" philosophy, which seeks fixed foundations, and "edifying" philosophy, which keeps the conversation going and expands the human vocabulary.

In his book *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989) he moved from the critique of knowledge to building a picture of the self and society. Language, the self and liberal society are all "contingent", resting neither on a fixed human nature nor on a transcendent authority. From this contingency he drew the figure of the "Liberal Ironist": a person who recognizes that her final vocabularies are open to revision and has no argument that would persuade every mind, but who remains committed to reducing cruelty. He divided life into two spheres: a private sphere for the creative redescription of the self, as the poet passionately engaged in self-creation does, and a public sphere guided by the idea of solidarity and the expansion of the "we" so that it includes those who used to be counted as "they". He holds that novels and literature are more capable than theoretical philosophy of driving this expansion, because they teach listening to the pain of others.

## Truth, solidarity and objectivity

In the essays *Consequences of Pragmatism* (1982) and *Objectivity, Relativism, and Truth* (1991) he defended abandoning the question "what is truth?" as correspondence to reality, and settling for asking "what can we justify to our peers?". He held that the desire for objectivity can be replaced by the desire for solidarity, and that truth is "what is good for us to believe" in a sense that does not mean manipulation but liberation from the illusion of an external observer. This position drew on him the charge of relativism, and he replied that he is "ethnocentric" in an explicit sense: we always start from our collective norms and have no neutral refuge.

## His influence on politics and literature

In *Achieving Our Country* (1998) and *Philosophy and Social Hope* (1999) he called on the American left to take pride in the tradition of reform from Walt Whitman to John Dewey, and criticized what he called the "cultural left" for its preoccupation with contempt and theorizing at the expense of concrete reformist action. In *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* he read Nabokov, Orwell and Proust philosophically, and in *Essays on Heidegger and Others* (1991) he read Heidegger, Derrida, Foucault and Davidson in a deliberately literary way, and held that philosophy is one literary genre among others. This orientation made his books a reference in literary studies and critical theory as well as in philosophy.

## His place in the current

Rorty belongs to the line of American pragmatism founded by Peirce, James and Dewey, but he reformulated it in a "post-analytic" language: from Dewey he retains the rejection of the great dualisms (subject and object, thought and reality) and abandons the terms "experience" and "nature", which he saw as metaphysical remnants. A famous debate took place between him and Hilary Putnam on realism and relativism, and he also engaged with Habermas over the possibility of founding universal rationality, and with Derrida in the reading of deconstruction. In the Arab world *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* was translated into Arabic, and the book stirred discussion among those working in epistemology, the philosophy of language and hermeneutics.

## Limits and critique

His analytic critics, such as Searle and Putnam, held that Rorty confuses a critique of a particular doctrine of representation with a rejection of the very idea of truth, and that his argument for the "end of epistemology" is stronger rhetorically than logically. His continental critics, such as Habermas, held that his sharp distinction between private and public weakens social critique because it relegates deep questions to personal taste. Some moral philosophers, among them Martha Nussbaum, took him to task because his reliance on the novel and empathy is not enough to found justice unless supported by public arguments. Describing him as an "existentialist" holds only in a limited sense; he is closer to a cultural philosopher opposed to ontology than to a traditional existentialist.
