# Bronisław Malinowski

A Polish-British anthropologist (born in Kraków in 1884, died in New Haven in 1942), regarded as a founder of intensive participant-observation fieldwork as a standard anthropological method and of the British functionalist school, alongside A. R. Radcliffe-Brown.

## The Fieldwork Turn

Because the First World War broke out while he was an Austro-Hungarian subject in Australia (an enemy state), Malinowski, then a lecturer at the London School of Economics, was unable to return to Europe and carried out fieldwork in Papua between 1914 and 1918, including two extended stays in the Trobriand Islands (1915–1916 and 1917–1918). There he lived among the population directly, learned their language, and took part in their daily lives, rather than relying on interpreters and intermediaries as was common at the time. This experience made participant observation the gold standard of ethnographic research throughout the twentieth century.

## Central Work

In *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922), Malinowski documented the "Kula Ring," a ceremonial exchange network of shell necklaces and armbands that links communities across a wide archipelago through circular routes, one in which valuables are passed on rather than kept, and which is accompanied by ordinary trade. The valuables circulate for prestige and social ties rather than direct utility. Malinowski showed that this exchange performs an essential social and political function by binding geographically distant communities into a stable, long-term network of trust and alliance. The work was later used as one of the central ethnographic references in Marcel Mauss's theory of the gift.

## Functional Theory

Malinowski extended the application of this functional model in later works such as *The Sexual Life of Savages* (1929) and *Magic, Science and Religion* (1948). He showed how Trobriand people turn to magic specifically in situations involving risk and high uncertainty, such as fishing in the open sea, while relying on purely practical technical knowledge in the safer and more predictable activities, such as fishing in calm lagoons. The link between magic and uncertainty has been much discussed in later comparative studies.

The influence of Malinowski's functional theory extended beyond British anthropology, affecting American sociology through figures such as Talcott Parsons, who had studied at the London School of Economics. Malinowski's psychological individual functionalism contrasted with the structural functionalism of his contemporary Radcliffe-Brown, who developed a rival alternative focused on the structure of social relations rather than the individual's psychological needs.

Malinowski developed a functional model linking cultural institutions (magic, myth, kinship, and ritual) to basic biological and psychological needs of the individual (food, security, and reproduction). Each cultural element serves a function in satisfying these needs or derived needs arising from them. This differs from Radcliffe-Brown's structural functionalism, which focused on the needs of the social system as a whole rather than the individual.

## Impact and Criticism

Despite the controversy aroused by his personal diaries, Malinowski remains a globally recognized founder of the modern fieldwork method in social anthropology. His works on the Trobriands (*Argonauts*, *The Sexual Life of Savages*, *Magic, Science and Religion*) are taught in every introductory anthropology program around the world as a classic model of deep, analytical descriptive ethnography.

After his death, his personal fieldwork diary (*A Diary in the Strict Sense of the Term*, 1967) was published. It revealed intense personal tensions and included derogatory remarks about the people he studied. This sparked a broad debate about the gap between the declared scientific objectivity of the field and the subjective experience of the ethnographer. It later became a key reference in the critique of classical anthropology (the crisis of representation and reflexivity).

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Malinowski conducted no fieldwork in the Arab-Islamic world, since his main research was concentrated in the Trobriand Islands in the Pacific Ocean. However, his method of "participant observation" and long-term immersion in the local population's language deeply influenced a later generation of anthropologists who studied Arab and Islamic societies in the field. Examples include Ernest Gellner in his study of the Moroccan Atlas and E. E. Evans-Pritchard in his work on the Sanusiyya of Cyrenaica. They applied the fieldwork standards associated with Malinowski (intensive, long-term fieldwork) to Arab and Islamic contexts that Malinowski himself never visited.
