# James Garbarino

An American developmental psychologist (born 1947) and one of the leading researchers on the relationship between child abuse and family and community violence on one side, and juvenile and adolescent violence on the other. He has served as a psychological expert witness in criminal trials, particularly in cases reviewing sentences imposed on juvenile offenders.

## Career

Garbarino taught at several American universities and headed the Erikson Institute for Advanced Study in Child Development in Chicago. He served as a professor of human development at Cornell University, and from 2006 he held the Maude C. Clarke Chair in Humanistic Psychology at Loyola University Chicago, where he founded the Center for the Human Rights of Children. His research focused on the "social ecology" of child and adolescent development, that is, the study of how overlapping layers of family, school, community, and culture affect the course of psychological development. In this he was influenced by the developmental psychologist Urie Bronfenbrenner, whose theory of "the ecology of human development" was presented in his book published by Harvard University Press in 1979.

## Theoretical Framework: From Abuse to a Toxic Environment

In 1977 Garbarino published "The Human Ecology of Child Maltreatment" in the *Journal of Marriage and the Family*, presenting a model in which abuse is not explained by the parent's traits alone, but by the interaction of family stress with social isolation and the absence of neighborhood support. His study with Ann Crouter in 1978, published in *Child Development*, supported this model. It compared rates of abuse among economically similar neighborhoods and found that differences followed the richness of social networks. The idea developed further in his book *Raising Children in a Socially Toxic Environment* (1995), in which he argued that poverty, violence, the media, and the breakdown of neighborhoods accumulate risks for the child. He added that children are affected not by a single factor but by the accumulation of factors, so that the more risk factors present, the greater the likelihood of behavioral problems.

## Children of War and Community Violence

Garbarino worked with Kathleen Kostelny, Nancy Dubrow, and others in conflict zones. They published *No Place to Be a Child: Growing Up in a War Zone* (1991, with Kostelny and Dubrow) and *Children in Danger: Coping with the Consequences of Community Violence* (1992, with Dubrow, Kostelny, and Carole Pardo). Together with Kostelny, he applied the "risk accumulation" model to Palestinian children in the West Bank and Gaza. In 1996 they reported in *Child Development* that behavioral problems in children were associated with the number of stressors, including political violence and family pressure, more than with any single event, and that a supportive family and connection to the community softened the impact. These studies helped direct child protection programs in areas affected by conflict, including in the Arab world, toward supporting the family and the community rather than relying only on individual treatment.

## Contributions

- ***Lost Boys*** (*Lost Boys: Why Our Sons Turn Violent and How We Can Save Them*, 1999): The book appeared in the same year as the Columbine High School massacre in April 1999, which made Garbarino a leading media and policy expert consulted on violence among adolescent boys in the United States. The book attributes some boys' tendency toward violence to the accumulation of multiple risk factors: family neglect, paternal abandonment, physical and emotional abuse, "spiritual emptiness," and a culture that normalizes violence through film, television, and video games. It draws on quantitative data alongside qualitative interviews with detained juvenile offenders and young men convicted of murder.
- **Forensic testimony**: He served as a psychological expert witness in many murder cases involving juveniles, presenting developmental-psychological assessments of how early trauma and abuse affect the maturing capacity to control behavior and make decisions, particularly in sentence review hearings for minors. He presented this experience to readers in his book *Miller's Children: Why Giving Teenage Killers a Second Chance Matters for All of Us* (2018), in which he discussed the US Supreme Court's decision in *Miller v. Alabama* (2012) on banning mandatory life imprisonment without parole for juveniles.
- **Extensive scholarly output**: He published many books on violence, childhood trauma, and youth development, including works on children of war and victims of abuse, and *See Jane Hit: Why Girls Are Growing More Violent and What We Can Do About It* (2006) on the rise in violence among girls.

## Legacy

His work helped shift the American public debate on adolescent violence from individual explanations ("innate evil") to environmental and cumulative explanations that recognize the role of abuse, neglect, and social context. This was reflected in later American court rulings that restricted life sentences and executions for minors. In *Roper v. Simmons* (2005), *Graham v. Florida* (2010), and *Miller v. Alabama* (2012), the court relied on the view that juveniles are less mature and more susceptible to environmental influence. These ideas are consistent with his argument, although he was not their only source and his direct influence on the rulings is not documented here.

## Limitations and Criticism

His explanatory framework relies mainly on the American context (wide access to firearms, media violence), and its applicability to other cultural contexts with different social systems has not been tested to the same degree. A limitation of the risk accumulation model is that it describes groups better than it predicts individuals: most children exposed to many risks do not commit violence. The 2002 Safe School Initiative, prepared by the US Secret Service and the Department of Education on perpetrators of school attacks, found that there is no single "profile" by which an attacker can be predicted. *Lost Boys* draws on samples of detained offenders and qualitative interviews, which may carry selection bias, since those studied after committing a crime do not represent those who were exposed to the same factors and did not offend. The role of expert witness in juvenile cases also raises an ethical question about balancing the scientific role with the advocacy role.
