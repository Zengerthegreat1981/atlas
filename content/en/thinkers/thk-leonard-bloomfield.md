# Leonard Bloomfield

An American linguist and a principal architect of **American structural linguistics** in the first half of the twentieth century, alongside Edward Sapir and others. His book *Language* (1933) was a standard textbook in American universities for decades.

Bloomfield was born in Chicago in 1887 and took his doctorate at the University of Chicago in 1909. He taught at the Universities of Cincinnati and Illinois, at Ohio State, at Chicago, and from 1940 at Yale. Besides Germanic philology he described Algonquian languages such as Menomini and also worked on Tagalog.

## Distributional and behaviorist method

Unlike much of the Prague Circle, Bloomfield's later method was **behaviorist**, influenced especially by the psychologist Albert Paul Weiss. Linguistic structure was to be stated from the observable **distribution** of forms (where an element can and cannot occur). Meaning was not denied, but he treated it as difficult to study directly and defined it, in *Language*, in terms of the speaker's situation and the hearer's response. The resulting practice is known as **distributional analysis**.

## Impact

Descriptive linguistics in his manner was dominant in much of American linguistics until the mid-twentieth century. Zellig Harris, among others, carried distributional method toward a more abstract formalism. Noam Chomsky, Harris's student, argued in *Syntactic Structures* (1957) and in his review of Skinner that behaviorist and purely distributional accounts did not explain linguistic creativity. That argument was a sharp break with Bloomfieldian practice.

## Immediate constituent analysis

A central analytical tool in this tradition is "immediate constituent analysis": a method for dividing the sentence gradually into successive binary constituents (not into individual words all at once). A sentence is divided, for example, into a subject phrase and a predicate phrase, and each of these is in turn divided into smaller constituents, and so on, until reaching the individual morphemes. Phrase-structure trees in generative grammar are in part descended from this kind of analysis, despite the deep theoretical break between Chomsky and the Bloomfieldians over the source of structure.

## A developmental shift: from early rationalism to strict behaviorism

An important intellectual development can be observed in Bloomfield's own career. In his early book *An Introduction to the Study of Language* (1914), he adopted a rationalist psychological framework closer to the traditions of Wundt in psychology, treating meaning as an internal mental state legitimate to study. By *Language* (1933), under the influence of American behaviorism and of Weiss, he no longer treated meaning as an inner mental state that linguistics could study directly. He still used meaning to identify forms, and he defined it as the situation of utterance plus the hearer's response. This shift reflects a broader methodological conflict that marked the entire history of American linguistics in the twentieth century, between the rationalist and behaviorist tendencies.

## Institutional impact: the Linguistic Society of America

Bloomfield was one of the founding members of the Linguistic Society of America in 1924 and a frequent contributor to its journal *Language*. This made him a central institutional figure, not only an intellectual one, in organizing the academic field of linguistics in the United States. His authoritative work helped make descriptive linguistics an academic discipline in its own right, with its own methodology, separate from the traditional historical philology that had prevailed in European universities before that.

## Method as a neutral instrument

Bloomfield wished distributional analysis to be a neutral instrument capable of describing any language in the world, free from prior cultural or historical biases, including unwritten languages that had not previously received serious linguistic study. This methodological ambition, describing each language "in its own right" on the basis of its internal data alone rather than imposing grammatical categories borrowed from Latin or Greek as traditional philology did, was a pivotal methodological contribution. It paved the way for extending descriptive linguistics to cover the languages of the world on an equal methodological footing.

## Behaviorist theory of meaning

He defined the meaning of a linguistic form as the situation in which the speaker utters it and the response it calls forth in the hearer. That stimulus-response definition leaves open how one form can be tied to very different responses as the context changes. Chomsky later made this kind of difficulty part of his case against behaviorism as an account of language, notably in the 1959 review of B. F. Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*.

## Morphology and derivation in Bloomfield

Bloomfield also offered a methodical analysis of derivational and inflectional morphology, relying on the concept of the "morpheme" as the smallest meaningful linguistic unit. He used terms still current today, such as "free form" and "bound form," to distinguish units that can stand alone in speech from those that must always attach to others (such as inflectional affixes). These terms became an essential part of the standard technical vocabulary of morphology in linguistics ever since.
