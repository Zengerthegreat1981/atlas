# Leonard Bloomfield

An American linguist regarded as the founder of **American structural linguistics** and the architect of its rigorous descriptive method in the first half of the twentieth century. His book *Language* (1933) became the standard reference for teaching linguistics in American universities for decades.

Bloomfield was born in Chicago in 1887 and took his doctorate at the University of Chicago in 1909. He taught at the Universities of Cincinnati and Illinois, at Ohio State, at Chicago, and from 1940 at Yale. Besides Germanic philology he described Algonquian languages such as Menomini and also worked on Tagalog.

## Distributional and behaviorist method

Unlike the Prague Circle, which remained closer to functional rationalism, Bloomfield adopted a strict **behaviorist** method influenced by behavioral psychology. The structure of language must be described on the basis of the observable **distribution** of linguistic elements in context (where a linguistic element can and cannot appear), without recourse to unobservable mental concepts such as "meaning" or "intention." This method became known as **distributional analysis**.

## Impact

He founded a school that dominated American linguistics until the mid-twentieth century, and his students (most prominently Zellig Harris) developed it toward a more abstract formalism. The rejection by Noam Chomsky, Harris's own student, of the limitations of the behaviorist and distributional method in explaining linguistic creativity was the starting point of the generative grammar revolution in 1957, which constituted a sharp methodological break with Bloomfield's legacy.

## Immediate constituent analysis

Among the most important analytical tools Bloomfield established is "immediate constituent analysis": a method for dividing the sentence gradually into successive binary constituents (not into individual words all at once). A sentence is divided, for example, into a subject phrase and a predicate phrase, and each of these is in turn divided into smaller constituents, and so on, until reaching the individual morphemes. Phrase-structure trees in generative grammar are in part descended from this kind of analysis, despite the deep theoretical break between Chomsky and the Bloomfieldians over the source of structure.

## A developmental shift: from early rationalism to strict behaviorism

An important intellectual development can be observed in Bloomfield's own career. In his early book *An Introduction to the Study of Language* (1914), he adopted a rationalist psychological framework closer to the traditions of Wundt in psychology, treating meaning as an internal mental state legitimate to study. By *Language* (1933), however, he had radically shifted to a strict behaviorist method that rejected the scientific study of "meaning" altogether as not amenable to direct experimental observation, under the influence of the rise of American behavioral psychology in that period. This shift reflects a broader methodological conflict that marked the entire history of American linguistics in the twentieth century, between the rationalist and behaviorist tendencies.

## Institutional impact: the Linguistic Society of America

Bloomfield was one of the founding members of the Linguistic Society of America in 1924 and a frequent contributor to its journal *Language*. This made him a central institutional figure, not only an intellectual one, in organizing the academic field of linguistics in the United States. His authoritative work helped make descriptive linguistics an academic discipline in its own right, with its own methodology, separate from the traditional historical philology that had prevailed in European universities before that.

## Method as a neutral instrument

Bloomfield wished distributional analysis to be a neutral instrument capable of describing any language in the world, free from prior cultural or historical biases, including unwritten languages that had not previously received serious linguistic study. This methodological ambition, describing each language "in its own right" on the basis of its internal data alone rather than imposing grammatical categories borrowed from Latin or Greek as traditional philology did, was a pivotal methodological contribution. It paved the way for extending descriptive linguistics to cover the languages of the world on an equal methodological footing.

## Behaviorist theory of meaning

Bloomfield offered an explicit behaviorist definition of meaning itself, defining the meaning of a form as the situation in which the speaker utters it together with the response it calls forth in the hearer (a stimulus-response model), rejecting any reference to internal mental states that cannot be directly observed. This definition, despite its methodological rigor, faced clear practical difficulties in explaining how a single word can evoke very different responses depending on context. This is one of the points that Chomsky later exploited in his decisive critique of the behaviorist method as a whole.

## Morphology and derivation in Bloomfield

Bloomfield also offered a methodical analysis of derivational and inflectional morphology, relying on the concept of the "morpheme" as the smallest meaningful linguistic unit. He used terms still current today, such as "free form" and "bound form," to distinguish units that can stand alone in speech from those that must always attach to others (such as inflectional affixes). These terms became an essential part of the standard technical vocabulary of morphology in linguistics ever since.
