# Ernst Cassirer

A German philosopher, trained in the Neo-Kantianism of the Marburg School, who then built his own system on "symbolic forms" (*symbolische Formen*). He taught at Hamburg from 1919 and served as rector of its university in 1929–1930. In 1933 he left Germany for Oxford, then Gothenburg (1935–1941), then the United States (Yale, then Columbia), where he died in New York.

## What He Gave

In the trilogy *Die Philosophie der symbolischen Formen* (1923 language; 1925 mythical thought; 1929 phenomenology of knowledge) he moved Kant's question from the conditions of scientific knowledge alone to the conditions under which meaning forms across many domains: language, myth, art, religion, and science. Each of these is an independent mode of shaping the world, not a deficient stage on the way to science. In *An Essay on Man* (1944), written in English at Yale, he condensed the system for English-speaking readers and formulated his definition of the human being as *animal symbolicum*, which is his own formulation, not that of his teachers. In *The Myth of the State*, published after his death in 1946, he applied the analysis of mythical thought to totalitarian politics.

## His Place in the Current

He grew out of the Marburg Neo-Kantianism founded by **Hermann Cohen** and **Paul Natorp**; their logical-epistemological programme was theirs, and Cassirer's contribution to it was to extend it from science to culture as a whole. His closest documented influence is **Susanne Langer**, who built *Philosophy in a New Key* (1942) on the Cassirerian concept of the symbol; **Merleau-Ponty** discusses him, together with Goldstein, in *Phénoménologie de la perception*. The library of **Aby Warburg** in Hamburg, near which he worked in the 1920s, was the material setting that supplied him with the material of mythical thought.

## The Famous Dispute

In March 1929, at **Davos** in Switzerland, he debated **Martin Heidegger** over the interpretation of Kant. Cassirer read the *Critique of Pure Reason* as a theory of the objectivity of knowledge and culture; Heidegger read it, in *Kant und das Problem der Metaphysik* published the same year, as an ontology of finitude and temporality. The debate became the conventional sign of the division of European philosophy into two paths, and it has since been read in light of what happened after 1933: Heidegger remained and joined the Nazi Party, while Cassirer, a Jew, went into exile.

## A Limit

After 1933 Cassirer wrote in exile, and *The Myth of the State* in its final part is a direct response to Nazism; reading his later works apart from this position strips them of their meaning. In the opposite direction: the attribution of representational theories in cognitive psychology after the 1960s to his influence cannot be demonstrated through a specific chain of citations, and it is therefore not claimed here.

## Life and Career

Cassirer was born in Breslau in 1874 to a prosperous Jewish family. He studied at several universities and then at Marburg under Hermann Cohen and Paul Natorp, taking his doctorate in 1899 with a thesis on Descartes's critique of mathematical and scientific knowledge. From 1906 he was a *Privatdozent* in Berlin. At Hamburg, where he became a professor in 1919, he came into contact with the Warburg library and its circle; in 1929 he became rector of the university, being among the first Jews to hold that post in Germany. After 1933 he moved to Oxford, then Gothenburg, then Yale and Columbia. He died in New York on 13 April 1945.

## From Substance to Function

Cassirer began with the question of the philosophy of science. In "The Concept of Substance and the Concept of Function" (*Substanzbegriff und Funktionsbegriff*, 1910), he argued that modern science replaces fixed substances with mathematical relations and functions, so that the scientific concept is a rule of a series rather than an abstraction from things. He then generalised this idea to culture as a whole in *Symbolic Forms*. He also wrote on "Einstein's Theory of Relativity" (1921), *Kant's Life and Thought* (1918), and *The Philosophy of the Enlightenment* (1932).

## The Relation to Psychology

In the third volume of *The Philosophy of Symbolic Forms*, Cassirer drew on the pathological cases studied by the neurologist **Kurt Goldstein**, his cousin, in disorders of aphasia, to show that the loss of symbolic capacity changes a person's relation to the whole world, not merely to speech. This line developed in developmental psychology in the work of **Heinz Werner**, who worked at Hamburg, and it appeared in Werner and Kaplan's *Symbol Formation* (Wiley, 1963). This is what makes Cassirer a bridge between philosophy and psychology: not by influencing a particular psychological school, but by making the symbol a shared object for both.

## His Major Works

*Leibniz' System in seinen wissenschaftlichen Grundlagen* (1902) · *Das Erkenntnisproblem in der Philosophie und Wissenschaft der neueren Zeit* (three volumes, 1906–1920; the fourth volume, written in exile, appeared posthumously in 1957) · *Die Philosophie der symbolischen Formen* (3 volumes, 1923–1929) · *Die Platonische Renaissance in England und die Schule von Cambridge* (1932) · *Zur Logik der Kulturwissenschaften* (1942) · *An Essay on Man* (1944) · *The Myth of the State*, published posthumously in 1946.
