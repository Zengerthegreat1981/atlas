# Kitaro Nishida

A leading modern Japanese philosopher, the central founding figure of the "Kyoto School" of philosophy, author of *An Inquiry into the Good* and originator of the "logic of place and absolute nothingness".

## Biography and Formation

Kitaro Nishida was born in 1870 in a village near Kanazawa in Ishikawa Prefecture, and studied philosophy at Tokyo Imperial University. He then taught at the Gakushuin school in Tokyo and from there moved to Kyoto University as professor of philosophy from 1910, where he remained until his retirement in 1928. In his youth he practiced Zen meditation in the temples of Kyoto, so his philosophy came out of an attempt to connect this experience with the language of the European philosophy he had received. He died in June 1945, weeks before Japan's surrender.

## What He Gave

He inaugurated a philosophical synthesis between Western philosophy (William James, Bergson, Kant, and German Idealism, then phenomenology) and the experience of Zen Buddhism; he formulated the concept of "pure experience" (Pure Experience), influenced by William James, which precedes the split of subject and object, and he developed the "logic of place" (Basho / Topos) and "absolute nothingness" (Absolute Nothingness) as an existential horizon for containing contradictions and realizing free and authentic being.

## Main Ideas

In *An Inquiry into the Good*, Nishida begins from "pure experience", that is, experience before it divides into a perceiving subject and a perceived object, like the experience of a man playing a melody absorbed in it. He holds that subject and object follow this experience and do not precede it. He then extended the idea in the "concept of place" (basho), making every existent subsist in a wider "place" that encompasses it, up to the place of "absolute nothingness", which is not encompassed by another place, and in which the true self is realized and contradictories meet in unity. He describes this unity by the formula "absolutely contradictory self-identity", that is, opposites unite in nothingness without losing their difference.

## His Place in the Current

He is often regarded as the most important modern Japanese philosopher, cited by some Japanese phenomenological psychiatrists such as Bin Kimura, and the central figure of the philosophical circle known as the Kyoto School.

## Reception and Criticism

Nishida influenced his students Nishitani, Tanabe, and Miki, and Western studies of Japanese philosophy. He is usually criticized because his language is dense and closer to esoteric discourse, and because the influence of Zen leads some to see his project as more religious than philosophical. In the war years his political writings, especially a text on the "principle of the new world order" (1943), took part in the controversy over the Kyoto School's position on Japanese nationalism and imperialism, a controversy that remains open between those who read his texts as veiled opposition and those who see them as complicit.

## Stages of Thought and the Kyoto School

Scholars divide Nishida's thought into stages: the stage of "pure experience" in *An Inquiry into the Good* (1911), then the stage of "self-consciousness" in *Intuition and Reflection in Self-Consciousness* (1917), in which he discussed Fichte and Neo-Kantianism, then the stage of "place" after 1926, in which he formulated the concept of place and its absolute nothingness, up to his writings on the philosophy of history and religion. His lifelong friend was D. T. Suzuki, who introduced Zen to the West, which helped his connection with religious and philosophical circles outside Japan.

Around him in Kyoto a circle of students and colleagues formed, among them Hajime Tanabe, Keiji Nishitani, and Miki Kiyoshi, who formed what became known as the Kyoto School, a modern Japanese philosophical school that engaged European philosophy while drawing on the heritage of East Asia.

## Absolute Nothingness and Religion

In his last writings Nishida tied the idea of absolute nothingness to the religious question, holding that the relation between the human being and the Absolute is neither a relation between two separate beings nor a total dissolution, but an "inverse correspondence": the human being is realized to the degree that he empties himself, and the Absolute manifests to the degree that it is embodied in the concrete individual. He cites the saying of the Zen master Daito Kokushi that human beings and the Buddha have been separated for thousands of years yet are not separated for a moment. In this way Nishida offers a philosophical formula for what may be called unity in difference, the formula his students would deepen in studies of the philosophy of religion.

## Limits

His contemplative and complex metaphysical philosophical language combines Western terms and advanced Buddhist ones.

## His Most Important Works

*An Inquiry into the Good* (Zen no kenkyū, 1911) · *From the Acting to the Seeing* (1927) · "Basho" ("Place", 1926) · "The Logic of the Place of Nothingness and the Religious Worldview" (1945, the last thing he completed).
