# William Easterly

**William Easterly** is an American economist who spent many years as a researcher at the World Bank before becoming one of the leading academic critics of the effectiveness of traditional foreign aid, and a professor at New York University. He is known for a direct, polemical style in confronting what he regards as excessive optimism, unsupported by evidence, among theorists of the "big push" in development.

## From inside the World Bank to a critic of it

Easterly worked for about sixteen years (1985–2001) as an economist at the World Bank, where he worked on growth and on adjustment and development lending in many countries. This direct experience led him later to a sharply critical position toward the very institution in which he had worked, after he observed, in his description, a recurring gap between the grand promises of aid programmes and their modest actual results on the ground in many of the cases he studied directly.

## The critique of the "big push" in aid

In his book *The White Man's Burden* (2006) and subsequent works, Easterly criticized an idea deeply rooted in the history of development economics: that a single large push of coordinated external aid can "break" the poverty trap and set off self-sustaining growth, as some post-war theorists proposed. Easterly argued that this idea had repeatedly failed empirically despite being applied for decades in various forms. He warned that massive aid not carefully targeted may reinforce corruption and weaken the accountability of local governments to their citizens, if those governments come to rely more on external donors than on their domestic taxpayers.

## Planners versus searchers

Easterly distinguished between two types of development policymakers: "planners," who assume sufficient prior knowledge of the complete correct solution and seek to impose it from above through grand programmes, and "searchers," who experiment with small partial solutions and learn gradually from failure and success through mechanisms closer to decentralized market trial and error. Easterly explicitly favoured the second approach, arguing that many historical aid failures stem from the dominance of the "planner" mindset, which is overconfident in its ability to predict the complex path of development in advance.

## His relationship to the randomized trial method

Although he differs in policy conclusions from Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo, Easterly intersects with them methodologically in their scepticism toward ready-made comprehensive solutions and their preference for testing specific partial policies rather than imposing grand all-encompassing theories. However, Easterly remains more sceptical even of the capacity of partial experimental evaluations to generalize their conclusions to the national macro level, warning that the success of a small programme in a specific village does not guarantee the success of a comprehensive national policy built on it.

## Critique of development expert authority

Easterly later extended his critique in his book *The Tyranny of Experts* (2014) to a broader critique of the authority that the international system grants to foreign development experts at the expense of local populations' knowledge and political rights. He argued that a purely technical focus on "technical solutions" to poverty, detached from questions of political freedom and local democratic accountability, may sustain authoritarian regimes that appear developmentally "effective" by narrow technical criteria.

## Contested reception of his arguments

Easterly's arguments sparked wide debate within the field of development economics, especially in confrontation with Jeffrey Sachs, who advocates a large and coordinated increase in international aid to break the poverty trap in the poorest countries. That dispute is treated in the debate entry dedicated to it, and neither side has settled the discussion with a final academic consensus to this day.

## General style and political orientation

Easterly is sometimes described as closer to classical economic liberalism than to the prevailing current in contemporary development economics. He tends to trust local markets and individual economic freedom more than central government planning or large-scale international coordination. This position places him in permanent intellectual confrontation with other currents in the field that see a larger role for the state and international institutions in leading the development process. This is a normative disagreement about the role of the state that goes beyond a mere empirical dispute over the effectiveness of particular programmes.

## Influence on aid policy

Easterly's arguments influenced actual policy debates within international aid institutions themselves. They contributed to wider debate about measuring the results of individual aid programmes rather than merely the volume of money disbursed. Their effect on the overall volume of aid is hard to establish, and Sachs's case for larger aid flows remained influential.
