# Alejandro Portes

A Cuban-American sociologist, a prominent theorist of "segmented assimilation," which reframed how immigrants integrate into the host society, moving away from the classical linear model.

## Historical Context

Portes was born in Havana in 1944 and emigrated to the United States in his youth in the wake of the Cuban Revolution, a direct personal experience of migration and exile that shaped his later academic interests. He received his doctorate from the University of Wisconsin-Madison taught at universities including Johns Hopkins, and later became a professor at Princeton University, where he founded the Center for Migration and Development. He developed his research in the context of the great American immigration wave after the reform of the immigration law in 1965, which ended the earlier ethnic quota system and led to an influx of immigrants from Asia and Latin America in numbers unprecedented for decades.

## Main Ideas or Method

In their landmark article "The New Second Generation" (1993), Portes and Min Zhou put forward the theory of "segmented assimilation." Contrary to the classical model, which assumed a single linear path for immigrants toward gradual integration into the white American middle class across generations (as described by the early Chicago school sociologists), they argued that the children of contemporary immigrants face multiple and divergent paths of integration according to structural conditions. One is race (non-white immigrants face structural discriminatory barriers that traditional European immigrants at the turn of the twentieth century did not face). Another is the economic context of reception (an American labor market with shrinking industrial opportunities for the middle class alongside low-wage sectoral growth, unlike the expanding industrial labor market that received the earlier European immigration waves). Residential concentration in inner cities matters too, as does the structure of the immigrant community itself (the presence of a strong supportive community network and ethnic social capital can provide an alternative route to economic success without full cultural assimilation into the American mainstream). Portes also developed the concept of "social capital" critically in his influential article "Social Capital: Its Origins and Applications in Modern Sociology" (1998), warning against the excessive and loose use of this concept in the sociological literature.

## Major Works or Representatives

Portes developed this framework with Rubén Rumbaut in *Legacies: The Story of the Immigrant Second Generation* (2001), a broad longitudinal study that followed the children of immigrants in San Diego and Miami over several years to test the predictions of segmented assimilation theory empirically. Earlier, with Kenneth Wilson (1980) and with Robert Bach in *Latin Journey* (1985), he studied Cuban immigrants in Miami as an example of an "ethnic enclave economy," in which co-ethnic employers and workers sustain an immigrant-run business sector.

## Impact and Critique

The theory of "segmented assimilation" became a central framework in contemporary American sociology of migration, and it informed discussion of the integration of immigrants' children. It was criticized for possibly exaggerating the determinism of the three assumed paths, while later data show more complex and intersecting paths that do not always match the original three-part model precisely.

## Methodological Note

The Children of Immigrants Longitudinal Study (CILS) behind *Legacies* surveyed second-generation adolescents in the San Diego and Miami/Fort Lauderdale areas in the early 1990s and followed them up in later waves, supplemented by interviews with a subsample of families.

## Additional Remark

Portes served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1999.

## Additional Concepts

With Julia Sensenbrenner, in the article "Embeddedness and Immigration" (1993), Portes formulated the concepts of "bounded solidarity" and "enforceable trust" to explain how cohesive immigrant ethnic networks work as an actual economic resource. Members of the community abide by norms of exchange and mutual help because of their shared feeling of belonging to one collective fate in relation to the host society, backed by an informal social sanction mechanism (ostracism, loss of reputation) that makes promises enforceable without need of a formal legal contract. In "The Study of Transnationalism" (1999), Portes, Luis Guarnizo and Patricia Landolt discuss "transnationalism from below" (a term associated with Michael Peter Smith and Guarnizo's 1998 volume of that name), documenting how contemporary immigrants maintain actual, continuing economic, political and social ties with their countries of origin thanks to modern transport and communication technologies, unlike older migration waves that assumed an almost complete break with the homeland after settlement.
