# Åke Hultkrantz

A Swedish historian of religions (1920–2006), professor of comparative religion at Stockholm University. He is regarded as one of the leading scholars to establish the study of Native North American religions as a comparative field within the history of religions. Previously these religions had been studied mainly within local ethnographic anthropology, without a comparative framework across tribes.

## Definition

Hultkrantz formed a rare bridge between two methodological traditions that often part ways: European comparative history of religions (concerned with building classificatory categories that cross cultures) and American field ethnographic anthropology (concerned with describing a particular society with contextual precision). He sought to show that these two methods are not contradictory, and that they can be complementary if the researcher maintains rigorous methodological control when moving from local description to comparative generalization.

## Historical Context

From local ethnography to systematic comparison: Hultkrantz began with ethnographic fieldwork (research among the Shoshone of the Wind River Reservation, Wyoming, beginning in 1948). Unlike many anthropologists who were content to describe a single tribe, however, he sought to build a comparative framework that gathers scattered ethnographic material on hundreds of Indigenous peoples of North America into coherent analytical categories (the guardian spirit, the soul's journey after death, the supreme master of animals, the healing shaman). He drew on his training in comparative history of religions in the continental European manner, which brought him into dialogue with the phenomenological approach of scholars such as Eliade, though with a field base in ethnography that Eliade lacked.

## Main Ideas and Method

One of his notable methodological contributions was linking religious structure to subsistence patterns (hunting versus agriculture). He argued that the religions of hunting peoples in North America share a single deep structure: a contractual-spiritual relationship with the "masters of animals," who grant a successful hunt in exchange for the respect of particular rituals, despite the geographic and linguistic distance between these peoples. His approach is often described as an "ecological" approach to religion (he treated religions as shaped by their environmental and economic setting). He also wrote extensively on the guardian spirit complex, the widespread pattern, discussed comparatively by Ruth Benedict in 1923, in which an individual, through a vision or dream, acquires a personal spirit helper; and on North American concepts of the soul, the subject of his doctoral dissertation *Conceptions of the Soul among North American Indians* (1953).

## Principal Works and Figures

His reference work *The Religions of the American Indians* (English translation 1979; originally in Swedish, 1967) is a comprehensive comparative survey of the religions of the Indigenous peoples of North America. His methodological views are set out in *The Study of American Indian Religions* (1983). His later book *Belief and Worship in Native North America* (1981) collected more specialized essays on topics such as shamanism, the guardian spirit and ritual.

## Impact and Criticism

He was part of a methodological debate with Vine Deloria Jr., the Native American thinker who criticized Western scholars as "outsiders" who describe religions to which they do not belong, using concepts borrowed from European classifications. Hultkrantz represented the opposing academic position, which holds that the analytical distance of the outside researcher is a methodological necessity and not a defect to be held against them, provided it is accompanied by respect for the original sources and rigorous ethnographic precision. This debate (insider versus outsider) remains an open methodological question in the study of Indigenous religions to this day.

He taught history of religions at Stockholm University and was also active in the international organisation of the field, including the International Association for the History of Religions (IAHR), which made him a bridge between European and American scholarly circles.

## His Relation to the Comparative Study of Shamanism

Hultkrantz also contributed to the debates on the study of shamanism as a comparative category. He used the term "shaman" to describe the healing ritual specialists among some Native North American peoples. Later critics of the comparative category of shamanism, such as Alice Kehoe, have argued that such generalization imposes a false similarity between widely separated practices. This disagreement is an example of the tension between Hultkrantz's generation, which inherited European classificatory tools from comparative history of religions, and the later generation that is more cautious about cross-cultural generalization.

## Synthetic Conclusion

Hultkrantz remains a major reference for researchers of Native North American religions. This is not because his classificatory framework is beyond criticism, but because he organized a vast quantity of scattered ethnographic material into a comparative structure that could be debated and questioned in the first place. That step was methodologically necessary before it could be criticized or surpassed.
