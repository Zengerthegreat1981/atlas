# Tammam Hassan

An Egyptian linguist and one of the most prominent theorists of the "descriptive approach" to the study of Arabic grammar in modern times. He sought to reread the Arabic grammatical heritage with modern linguistic tools without breaking with it.

## Academic Career

Tammam Hassan was born in 1918 in Egypt. He studied at Dar al-Ulum in Cairo and was then sent on a scholarship to the University of London, where he studied phonetics and linguistics and was influenced by the London school associated with J. R. Firth. There he came into direct contact with the methods of the Western structuralist and descriptivist schools. On his return to Egypt he worked as a professor at the Faculty of Dar al-Ulum at Cairo University, the historic institution in which many of the pioneers of the renewal of Arabic studies in the twentieth century either studied or taught. He also served as a member of the Academy of the Arabic Language in Cairo. He died in 2011, after a career spanning more than half a century.

## Intellectual Context

Hassan worked during a debate among Arabic linguists between advocates of renewing grammar and defenders of the classical tradition of Sibawayh, al-Khalil ibn Ahmad and Ibn Jinni. Ibrahim Mustafa, in *Iḥyāʾ al-naḥw* (1937), and later Shawqi Dayf, in *Tajdīd al-naḥw*, proposed substantial changes in how iʿrāb and the teaching of grammar were conceived. Hassan did not argue for replacing Arabic grammar with an imported model, and he did not treat later school formulations as beyond criticism. He tried to restate what he took to be the descriptive logic of the heritage in modern linguistic terms.

## Method of Linguistic Research (1955)

In his early book *Manahij al-Bahth fi al-Lugha* (*Methods of Linguistic Research*, 1955), written after his period of study in London, Hassan surveyed the major Western linguistic schools (Saussurean structuralism, American descriptivism in the tradition of Bloomfield, and the London school) in a critical overview addressed to the Arab reader. He compared their methods with the tools the early Arab grammarians used to study language. This book marked his methodological starting point: adopting modern linguistic analysis not as a literal importation but as a critical instrument with which to re-examine the Arabic heritage itself.

## The Seven-Part Classification of Words

Among Hassan's most notable theoretical innovations was his departure from the traditional tripartite division of the Arabic word (noun, verb, particle), which had been standard since Sibawayh. He proposed a finer division into seven categories that reflect the functional and grammatical differences among word classes that the tripartite scheme struggles to accommodate: the noun (*ism*), the adjective (*sifa*), the verb (*fi'l*), the pronoun (*damir*), the *khalifa* (a class covering exclamatory and similar forms such as verb-nouns and interjections), the adverb (*zarf*), and the particle (*ada*). Hassan held that this seven-part division was more consistent with the actual functional structure of Arabic, and that it addressed classification problems which traditional grammar had handled only through scattered partial solutions.

## The Theory of Qara'in in Syntax

In his central book *Al-Lugha al-Arabiya: Ma'naha wa Mabnaha* (*The Arabic Language: Its Meaning and Structure*, 1973), Hassan presented the theory of *qara'in* ("contextual indicators") for analysing Arabic syntactic structures. He argued that understanding grammatical meaning (such as subject or object function) does not depend on vocalic inflection (i'rab) alone, as the traditional heritage might suggest, but on the convergence of multiple indicators. These are verbal indicators (such as inflection, word order, morphological form, agreement, and linking) and semantic indicators (such as predication, specification and relation), and some of them can operate as substitutes for others. This explains how correct understanding is possible even in the absence of overt inflection, as in spontaneous speech, unvocalized texts, or colloquial dialects that have lost the inflectional system. He argued that even in classical Arabic the language did not rely on the inflectional mark alone but on a network of converging indicators.

## Al-Usul: An Epistemological Study (1981)

In his book *Al-Usul: Dirasa Ibistimulujiyya lil-Fikr al-Lughawi ʿind al-ʿArab: al-Nahw, Fiqh al-Lugha, al-Balagha* (*Al-Usul: An Epistemological Study of Arab Linguistic Thought: Grammar, Philology, Rhetoric*, 1981), Hassan moved from applied grammatical analysis to a deeper methodological examination of the history of Arabic linguistic thought itself. He asked how the early Arab grammarians, jurists, rhetoricians and philologists built their linguistic knowledge, and what epistemological foundations (analogy, transmitted usage, induction, and the causes of grammar) they relied on to justify their rulings. The book sought to ground Arabic linguistic study as an intellectual tradition with its own coherent internal logic, rather than a loose set of rules laid down arbitrarily by the early scholars. It linked the science of legal theory (usul al-fiqh) with the science of grammar in a single methodological unity that characterized classical Arab-Islamic thought.

## Impact and Criticism

Tammam Hassan had a profound influence on the renewal of academic Arabic grammatical studies in the twentieth century. He offered a model of critical dialogue between modern linguistics and the classical Arabic heritage, rather than a rupture with it or full dependence on either. His theory of qara'in and his seven-part classification of words have become key references in Arabic linguistics courses and scholarship. Some traditionalists criticized his tendency to reinterpret the heritage through modern concepts that may not accurately reflect the intentions of the early grammarians themselves. Others saw in him a successful model for modernizing the study of Arabic without breaking with its authenticity.
