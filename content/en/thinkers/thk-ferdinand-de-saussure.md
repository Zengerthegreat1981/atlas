# Ferdinand de Saussure

A Swiss linguist and professor at the University of Geneva, regarded as the founder of modern linguistics and structuralism. He began his scientific career as a researcher in historical comparative linguistics (his treatise on the vowel system in Indo-European languages, 1878). He then shifted, in his three Geneva lecture courses (1906–1911), to establishing a new method for studying language as a system rather than as history. This shift changed the course of linguistics as a whole in the twentieth century. It moved the field's attention away from the almost exclusive focus on the historical development of languages, which dominated the nineteenth century, toward the study of a language's internal structure as a system of synchronic relations at a single moment.

## The 1878 Treatise on Indo-European Vowels

In his *Mémoire sur le système primitif des voyelles dans les langues indo-européennes* (1878), Saussure treated the vowel alternations of Proto-Indo-European as a system and posited abstract elements (his "coefficients sonantiques") to account for them. After the decipherment of Hittite, the laryngeal sounds that later scholars reconstructed were widely seen as vindicating this proposal, an early instance of the systemic thinking that marks his later work.

## Central Contribution

Saussure did not publish his general theory of language himself. His students Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye compiled it from his lectures and his students' notebooks and published it after his death as *Course in General Linguistics* (1916). In this work he formulated the basic distinctions on which structural linguistics was built: the arbitrariness of the sign, langue and parole, synchrony and diachrony, and the differential value of a linguistic element within its system.

## Life and Academic Career

Saussure was born in Geneva in 1857 into a family of scholars. From an early age he showed an interest in languages, studying in Leipzig and Berlin before publishing, at the age of twenty-one, his well-regarded treatise on the original vowel system in Indo-European languages (1878). That work earned him wide respect among comparative linguists before he turned thirty. He then worked in Paris before returning to Geneva in 1891, where he spent the rest of his academic career. He died in 1913, before seeing the publication of his most famous work, which his students built from his lecture notes three years after his death.

## The Arbitrariness of the Sign

The idea of the "arbitrariness of the sign" is the cornerstone of Saussure's theory. There is no natural or necessary bond between the signifier (the sound-image of the word) and the signified (the mental concept it refers to). The choice of the sound sequence *tree* to designate that particular plant concept is a purely social convention inherited by members of the linguistic community, not a reflection of a property inherent in the thing itself. This is shown by the many entirely different words that refer to the same concept across the world's languages (English *tree*, French *arbre*, Arabic *shajara*).

## Language and Speech: Social System versus Individual Use

Saussure distinguished between *langue* ("language"), the abstract system shared socially by members of a linguistic community (the grammar and vocabulary latent in people's minds), and *parole* ("speech"), the actual individual use of this system, intermittent and varied across concrete communicative situations. Saussure held that the proper object of scientific linguistics is the study of *langue* as a disciplined social system, not the varied individual *parole*, which resists scientific generalization. This distinction was later taken up by Chomsky in an individual cognitive form, in the dichotomy of competence and performance.

## Differential Value of the Sign

Saussure proposed that the value of a linguistic element (*valeur*) is determined not by its isolated intrinsic content but by its differential position within the overall system of the language, in contrast to the neighboring and opposing elements. This is comparable to the value of a chess piece, which cannot be understood apart from its place among the other pieces and the rules of the game as a whole. This principle allowed Saussure to explain why words with similar meanings in two languages (such as French *mouton* and English *sheep*) differ in their actual value within their respective systems, even though their approximate referents are similar.

## Synchronic versus Diachronic Method

Saussure systematically separated the study of language "synchronically," meaning the description of the state of a linguistic system at a single point in time apart from its history, from studying it "diachronically," meaning tracing its development through historical time. He gave methodological priority to synchronic study, arguing that speakers are not aware of a language's history when they use it, and perceive only the system as it exists at their present moment. This, he argued, should be the first object of scientific linguistic description before explaining its historical origins.

## Influence

He is regarded as the founding father of linguistics as an independent science with its own distinctive method. He was the direct source of inspiration for the Prague Linguistic Circle (Jakobson, Trubetzkoy) in phonology, and later for French structuralism in anthropology (Lévi-Strauss), literary criticism and semiotics (Barthes). These extensions went well beyond linguistics itself. Modern comparisons (such as Abu Deeb's in 1979) have also inspired scholars to link Saussure's principle of "differential value" with the rhetorical principle of *nazm* (syntactic arrangement), older by roughly eight centuries, in the work of 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani. The aim of such comparisons is to highlight the originality of classical Arabic linguistic thought, without claiming a direct historical influence between the two projects.
