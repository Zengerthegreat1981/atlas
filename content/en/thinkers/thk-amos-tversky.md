# Amos Tversky

## Definition

Amos Tversky (1937–1996) was an Israeli-American mathematical and cognitive psychologist, a professor at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and then at Stanford University, and the historic partner of Daniel Kahneman in formulating Prospect Theory and the research program on "Heuristics and Cognitive Biases" that helped found contemporary behavioral economics.

## Historical Context

Tversky and Kahneman met at the Hebrew University in the late 1960s, at a time when classical economic decision theory assumed that the human being is a "rational agent" who maximizes his expected utility according to precise probabilistic calculations. Through early experiments on their students and colleagues, the two researchers noticed that people's intuitive judgments deviate systematically and repeatedly from this rational model, in ways that can be predicted rather than being random errors. This discovery marked the beginning of a research collaboration that lasted well over a decade, in the course of which Tversky moved to Stanford and Kahneman to the University of British Columbia (both in 1978).

## Main Ideas and Method

Tversky and Kahneman took part in discovering and documenting a number of heuristics that the human mind uses to make quick decisions under uncertainty: the "availability" heuristic, which makes events that are easier to recall from memory seem more probable than others; the "representativeness" heuristic, which judges the likelihood that something belongs to a category on the basis of its typical similarity to it rather than the actual statistical probability; and the "anchoring and adjustment" effect, in which the final judgment is influenced by a random number or starting point presented beforehand. The pair crowned this research program with the formulation of "Prospect Theory" in 1979, a mathematical model alternative to classical expected utility theory, which explains "loss aversion": the tendency of individuals to feel the pain of a loss far more than the pleasure of gaining an equal amount, which leads to systematic biases in financial and life decisions.

## Major Works and Representatives

Their landmark paper "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk" was published in the journal Econometrica in 1979, and is among the most cited papers in economics. With Paul Slovic, Kahneman and Tversky also edited the volume *Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases* (1982), which gathered foundational studies in this field; their 1974 *Science* article of the same title was an earlier landmark.

## Impact and Criticism

Tversky is considered one of the greatest experimental psychologists and makers of behavioral economics in history. He died in 1996 of metastatic skin cancer (melanoma), and his partner Daniel Kahneman received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2002, six years after his death; because the prize is not awarded posthumously, Tversky was not eligible for it, and Kahneman publicly acknowledged that Tversky would have shared it. Some researchers, most prominently Gerd Gigerenzer, criticized the "heuristics and biases" approach on the grounds that it describes intuitive heuristics as systematic errors rather than regarding them as effective adaptive mechanisms in particular environments ("ecological rationality" or the "smart heuristics" view), which provoked an extended scientific debate between the two schools, part of which was published in the journal Psychological Review in 1996.
The work of Tversky and Kahneman contributed to later research fields from behavioral finance to nudge-based public policy, which Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein built on in part through their work on heuristics and biases, and Prospect Theory is widely taught in behavioral economics and cognitive psychology as a turning point away from the classical rational model.
