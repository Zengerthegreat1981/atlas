# Marsha M. Linehan

An American clinical psychologist (born 1943 in Tulsa, Oklahoma), professor at the University of Washington in Seattle, and founder of Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), a treatment originally formulated for chronically suicidal women diagnosed with borderline personality disorder. She was the first to show in a randomized controlled trial that a psychotherapy reduces self-harming behavior in this population, which is what the older formulation meant by "the first empirically supported treatment" for borderline cases. The phrase still needs qualification: it is the first treatment demonstrated by a rigorous trial for these cases, not the only proven treatment.

## Biography: From Patient to Therapist

She studied psychology at Loyola University Chicago and received her doctorate there (1971). In 2011 she announced in The New York Times (Carey, 23 June 2011) that in her youth she had spent a long period at the Institute of Living in Hartford because of severe emotional crises and self-harming behavior, and that this experience led her to think about a treatment out of "hell" that would bring the patient to a life worth living. In her memoir "Building a Life Worth Living" (2020, Random House) she presents this biography in detail. She belongs to the Catholic Church, and received training in Zen meditation from the Benedictine monk Willigis Jäger and earned the title of Zen teacher.

## What She Gave

Dialectical Behavior Therapy. She worked in the "Suicidal Behavior Clinic" at the University of Washington, and tried standard cognitive-behavioral therapy with chronically suicidal women, finding that a pure focus on change made them feel their behavior was "wrong," so they withdrew or attacked the therapist. She therefore added the principle of acceptance and validation as a counterpart to change, and from this tension formulated the dialectical framework. She published the results of the first trial in 1991 in Archives of General Psychiatry with Armstrong, Suarez, Allmon, and Heard, and presented the treatment in the book "Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder" (1993, Guilford Press). This was followed by a 2006 trial published in the same journal that compared DBT with treatment by community experts.

The biosocial theory. It holds that emotion-regulation disorder arises from the interaction of a high emotional sensitivity and reactivity with an "invalidating" environment that denies emotions, punishes them, or oversimplifies problems. This is the ground that gives the treatment its logic.

Treatment structure. It combines individual sessions with group training in four skills: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness, with telephone consultation between sessions and a team of therapists who support one another. The skills manual was published in its second edition under the title "DBT Skills Training Handouts and Worksheets" (2015, Guilford).

Extension. The treatment expanded to include adolescents (Miller, Rathus, and Linehan 2007), substance use disorders, in which there is what is called "dialectical abstinence" (Linehan and others 1999, in the American Journal on Addictions), and eating disorders.

## Her Place in the Current

She belongs to the cognitive-behavioral tradition, and stands at the heart of the "third wave" that brought mindfulness and acceptance into this tradition. In it she combines empirical rigor with concepts drawn from Zen, and she founded the company Behavioral Tech to train therapists.

## Reception and Criticism

Meta-analytic reviews showed a moderate effect of DBT on self-harm and suicide, as in the review by Kliem, Kröger, and Kosfelder (2010) in the Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology. However, the trial by McMain and colleagues (2009) in the American Journal of Psychiatry showed that DBT did not outperform structured "general psychiatric management," which calls for caution in considering it necessarily the best. The treatment is criticized for its intensity: it requires a team, long time, and training, which limits its spread in poorer systems. Conceptually, critics hold that the "borderline" diagnosis carries stigma and gender bias, and that the treatment is built on a particular understanding of the client's "invalidating" environment that may place blame on the family. The question of the role of the treatment's components remains: is it mindfulness, the skills, the telephone consultation, or the team?

## Her Most Important Works

- "Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Chronically Parasuicidal Borderline Patients" (1991, with others).
- "Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder" (1993).
- "A Two-Year Randomized Controlled Trial and Follow-Up of DBT versus Treatment by Experts" (2006, with others).

- "Building a Life Worth Living" (2020).
