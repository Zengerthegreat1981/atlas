# Marsha M. Linehan

An American clinical psychologist (born 1943 in Tulsa, Oklahoma; died in Seattle on 10 November 2026, aged 83), professor at the University of Washington in Seattle, and founder of Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), a treatment originally formulated for chronically suicidal women diagnosed with borderline personality disorder. Her 1991 randomized controlled trial was among the first to show that a psychotherapy reduces self-harming behavior in this population. DBT is one of the best-supported psychological treatments for borderline personality disorder and chronic suicidal behavior, but it is one of several with trial support for these cases, not the only proven treatment, and effects on some outcomes are modest and trial quality varies.

## Biography: From Patient to Therapist

She studied psychology at Loyola University Chicago and received her doctorate there, with clinical training (1971). She taught at the Catholic University of America from 1973 to 1977 and joined the University of Washington in 1977, where she spent decades developing DBT and testing it empirically, retiring in 2019. In 2011 she announced in The New York Times (Carey, 23 June 2011) that in her youth she had spent a long period at the Institute of Living in Hartford because of severe emotional crises and self-harming behavior, beginning at the age of seventeen; she had been diagnosed with schizophrenia at the time and later said her difficulties were more consistent with borderline personality disorder. She said that this experience led her to think about a treatment out of "hell" that would bring the patient to a life worth living. In her memoir "Building a Life Worth Living" (2020, Random House) she presents this biography in detail. The disclosure drew wide attention as a rare case of a therapist who developed a treatment for a disorder she had herself experienced. She belongs to the Catholic Church, and received training in Zen meditation from the Benedictine monk Willigis Jäger and earned the title of Zen teacher.

## What She Gave

Dialectical Behavior Therapy. She worked in her research clinic at the University of Washington, and tried standard cognitive-behavioral therapy with chronically suicidal women, finding that a pure focus on change made them feel their behavior was "wrong," so they withdrew or attacked the therapist. She therefore added the principle of acceptance and validation as a counterpart to change, and from this tension formulated the dialectical framework, developed during the 1980s and early 1990s, which gives the therapy its name. She published the results of the first trial in 1991 in Archives of General Psychiatry with Armstrong, Suarez, Allmon, and Heard, reporting fewer parasuicidal acts and less treatment dropout than treatment as usual among women with borderline personality disorder, and presented the treatment in the book "Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder" (1993, Guilford Press). This was followed by a 2006 trial published in the same journal that compared DBT with treatment by community experts.

The biosocial theory. It holds that emotion-regulation disorder arises from the interaction of a high emotional sensitivity and reactivity with an "invalidating" environment that denies emotions, punishes them, or oversimplifies problems. This is the ground that gives the treatment its logic.

Treatment structure. It combines individual sessions with group training in four skills: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness, with telephone consultation between sessions and a team of therapists who support one another. The second edition of the skills training manual appeared with its companion "DBT Skills Training Handouts and Worksheets" (2015, Guilford).

Extension. The treatment expanded to include adolescents (Miller, Rathus, and Linehan 2007), substance use disorders, in which there is what is called "dialectical abstinence" (Linehan and others 1999, in the American Journal on Addictions), and eating disorders, although the evidence for these adaptations is less extensive than for borderline personality disorder.

## Her Place in the Current

She belongs to the cognitive-behavioral tradition, and stands at the heart of the "third wave" that brought mindfulness and acceptance into this tradition. In it she combines empirical rigor with concepts drawn from Zen, and she founded the company Behavioral Tech to train therapists.

## Reception and Criticism

Meta-analytic reviews showed a moderate effect of DBT on self-harm and suicide, as in the review by Kliem, Kröger, and Kosfelder (2010) in the Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology. However, the trial by McMain and colleagues (2009) in the American Journal of Psychiatry showed that DBT did not outperform structured "general psychiatric management," which calls for caution in considering it necessarily the best. The treatment is criticized for its intensity: it requires a team, long time, and training, which limits its spread in poorer systems. Most of the evidence for its effectiveness also comes from Western samples. Conceptually, critics hold that the "borderline" diagnosis carries stigma and gender bias, and that the treatment is built on a particular understanding of the client's "invalidating" environment that may place blame on the family. The question of the role of the treatment's components remains: is it mindfulness, the skills, the telephone consultation, or the team? This entry is educational and not clinical advice.
