# Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma

**Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma**, of Muzayna (the dates c. 520–609 CE are only a conventional estimate), is one of the leading poets of the Jahiliyya and the author of one of the seven **Mu'allaqat**. Later critics valued that ode for its moral sentences. They counted him among the poets of the *hawliyyat*, the "annual odes," said to have been revised for a long time before they were recited, and they called Zuhayr, al-Hutay'a and others *abid al-shi'r*, "slaves of poetry," for the care of the craft.

## Context and Biography

He was of **Muzayna** by descent and lived among **Ghatafan**, in the orbit of Dhubyan. The tradition makes his father a poet and his maternal uncle, Bashama ibn al-Ghadir, a poet, and it makes Zuhayr the transmitter (*rawi*) of Aws ibn Hajar. Some accounts add that Aws married Zuhayr's mother. His sons **Ka'b ibn Zuhayr** and **Bujayr ibn Zuhayr** were poets, as were grandsons, including Uqba, known as al-Mudarrab. His pupil al-Hutay'a carried the same careful manner. Ka'b's ode *Banat Su'ad*, recited before the Prophet Muhammad when Ka'b came to make peace, is a famous story of the passage from Jahili poetry to the Prophet's court. The gift of the mantle (*burda*) in that story has the shape of a later tale. A report preserved by Ibn Qutayba and others has Umar ibn al-Khattab prefer Zuhayr, because he did not contort his speech, did not chase rare words, and did not praise a man except for what was in him.

## Main Contribution

Zuhayr's famous Mu'allaqa revolves around **praise of the peace treaty of Dahis and al-Ghabra'**, the long war that broke out between the tribes of **'Abs and Dhubyan** and exhausted them for decades. In it Zuhayr praises the two men who bore the burden of the peace and its blood-money, Harith ibn 'Awf and Harim ibn Sinan. The poem offers a profound moral reflection on **the consequences of war**. It compares war to a millstone that grinds those who set it turning, and it calls for peace and the keeping of covenants. Its tone is wise, which distinguishes it from the boasting and pure tribal zeal prevalent among many of his contemporaries.

## Main Features

- **Moral wisdom**: His poetry is known for its many proverbs and maxims drawn from the experience of a long life, rather than from mere tribal boasting.
- **Craft and revision**: The early literary sources, such as Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi, credit Zuhayr with long deliberation in composing his poems. It was said that he would spend a full year revising a single ode, which distinguished him from those who improvised verse.
- **Moderation regarding war**: Unlike a poetic tradition that glorified raiding, his Mu'allaqa explicitly calls for setting aside war and valuing the price of peace.

## Structure and Meanings of the Mu'allaqa

The ode opens at an abandoned camp, *a-min Ummi Awfa dimnatun lam takallami*, "Is it the trace of Umm Awfa's camp that will not speak?" It praises al-Harith ibn Awf and Harim ibn Sinan, who are said to have paid, from their own wealth, the blood-money that ended the fighting. At the centre is a warning against taking the war up again. The poet says his hearers have tasted it, and that what he says is not hearsay: war grinds like a millstone, then conceives, bears and suckles further harm. The closing sentences turn the story into maxims. One of them says, in substance, that whoever does not conciliate in many affairs will be bitten by fangs and trampled by hooves. The Arabic verb is *yusani'*, to deal tactfully, not to flatter. Another famous line of his, from the closing part of the same ode, says that he is weary of the burdens of life, and that whoever lives eighty years will grow weary.

## The Hawliyyat and the Craft of Poetry

The critics called Zuhayr and al-Hutay'a, and sometimes Aws ibn Hajar and Ka'b, "slaves of poetry." A well-known report says that Zuhayr spent four months composing an ode, four months revising it and four months showing it to his companions, so that a poem took a year (*hawl*) and the odes were called *hawliyyat*. The anecdote is early evidence that critics distinguished natural gift (*tab'*) from craft (*san'a*). Ibn Qutayba and al-Qadi al-Jurjani later wrote on that distinction. They did not found it on this story alone. The chain Aws, Zuhayr, al-Hutay'a is a traditional account of apprenticeship, not a documented school.

## His Place in the Jahiliyya and Islam

Ibn Sallam places Zuhayr in the first class of pre-Islamic poets, with Imru' al-Qays, al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani and al-A'sha. In the usual order of that class he stands third, after Imru' al-Qays and al-Nabigha and before al-A'sha. The report about Umar's preference has already been noted. The break between the Jahiliyya and Islam in the family is marked by Ka'b, who is said to have satirized the Prophet and then to have come to him with *Banat Su'ad*. Zuhayr himself, on the traditional chronology, died before that scene.

## Limits of the Picture

The poems give us no more than the transmitters provide. Some of the maxims attributed to Zuhayr may belong to the general stock of proverbial wisdom that was credited to the most famous speakers of such sayings. The precise chronology of the War of Dahis and al-Ghabra' is undetermined, so the dating of its years stays approximate. He should not be turned into a peace advocate in the modern sense. The praise here is framed within the context of blood-money and tribal sureties (*hamala*), not within a rejection of violence in absolute terms.

## Impact

Zuhayr's Mu'allaqa is still studied today as a model of pre-Islamic poetic wisdom. The poetic fame of his family, especially his son Ka'b's ode, has tied his name to the historical moment of transition from the Jahiliyya to Islam, although Zuhayr himself died shortly before the rise of Islam or at its outset, and did not live to see his sons embrace it.
