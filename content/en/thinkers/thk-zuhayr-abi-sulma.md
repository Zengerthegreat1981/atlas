# Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma

**Zuhayr ibn Abi Sulma Rabi'a al-Muzani** (c. 520–609 CE) was one of the greatest poets of the pre-Islamic (Jahiliyya) age, and the author of one of the seven famous **Mu'allaqat**. That ode is especially esteemed among Arab men of letters for its wisdom and moral maturity. The ancient critics counted him, together with others, among the *ashab al-hawliyyat* ("authors of the annual poems") who revised their poems for long periods before reciting them. They also called such poets, for the precision of their craft, *abid al-shi'r* ("slaves of poetry").

## Context and Biography

Zuhayr belonged by descent to the tribe of **Muzayna**, but grew up and lived among the tribe of **Ghatafan**, and inherited poetry from his family. His father was a poet, his maternal uncle Bashama ibn al-Ghadir was a poet, and his two sons, **Ka'b ibn Zuhayr** and **Bujayr ibn Zuhayr**, were also poets. Ka'b later became famous for his ode "Banat Su'ad", which he recited in the presence of the Prophet Muhammad when he declared his conversion. This ode became one of the best-known moments of transition from pre-Islamic to Islamic poetry. According to the sources, Zuhayr was also the favourite poet of the caliph **'Umar ibn al-Khattab**, because of the wisdom and balance of his verse.

## Main Contribution

Zuhayr's famous Mu'allaqa revolves around **praise of the peace treaty of Dahis and al-Ghabra'**, the long war that broke out between the tribes of **'Abs and Dhubyan** and exhausted them for decades. In it Zuhayr praises the two men who bore the burden of the peace and its blood-money, Harith ibn 'Awf and Harim ibn Sinan. The poem offers a profound moral reflection on **the consequences of war**. It compares war to a millstone that grinds those who set it turning, and it calls for peace and the keeping of covenants. Its tone is wise, which distinguishes it from the boasting and pure tribal zeal prevalent among many of his contemporaries.

## Main Features

- **Moral wisdom**: His poetry is known for its many proverbs and maxims drawn from the experience of a long life, rather than from mere tribal boasting.
- **Craft and revision**: The early literary sources, such as Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi, credit Zuhayr with long deliberation in composing his poems. It was said that he would spend a full year revising a single ode, which distinguished him from those who improvised verse.
- **Moderation regarding war**: Unlike a poetic tradition that glorified raiding, his Mu'allaqa explicitly calls for setting aside war and valuing the price of peace.

## Structure and Meanings of the Mu'allaqa

The ode opens with the poet pausing at the abandoned campsite, in its famous first line: "Is it the abandoned campsite of Umm Awfa that no longer speaks?" He then describes the litters and the dwellings in precise detail, and moves on to praise al-Harith ibn 'Awf and Harim ibn Sinan, who sought reconciliation and paid the blood-money for the slain from their own wealth. At the heart of the poem is a warning against renewing the war, which he describes as something you have tasted, and of which his account is no guesswork. It grinds you as a millstone grinds with its load, then gives birth, and what it bears is nursed to maturity. The battle in this image thus becomes a mill that breeds evil. The poem closes with verses of wisdom, among them the saying that whoever does not flatter in many matters will be bitten by fangs and trampled underfoot, and that whoever keeps faith is not blamed, while whoever turns his heart toward righteousness finds peace. These verses show the poet's inclination toward moral generalization and toward turning experience into rules.

## The Hawliyyat and the Craft of Poetry

The ancients called Zuhayr, al-Hutay'a, Aws ibn Hajar and Ka'b, Zuhayr's son, "slaves of poetry". They said that Zuhayr made his ode in four months, refined it in four, and presented it to his companions in four, so that his poems were called *al-hawliyyat* (the annual poems). This description is an early piece of evidence for Arab literary awareness of the difference between natural gift (*tab'*) and craft (*san'a*), a distinction on which Ibn Qutayba and al-Qadi al-Jurjani built their accounts of poetic craft. The report on Aws ibn Hajar shows that Zuhayr learned his craft within a continuous poetic school: Aws was his stepfather, and his maternal uncle Bashama was a poet. Zuhayr passed the craft on to his sons Ka'b and Bujayr, and it reached his grandson 'Uqba ibn Ka'b.

## His Place in the Jahiliyya and Islam

Ibn Sallam places Zuhayr in the first rank of the masters of pre-Islamic poetry, after Imru' al-Qays, al-Nabigha and al-A'sha. Ibn Qutayba reports that 'Umar ibn al-Khattab asked Ibn 'Abbas who was the best of the poets, and that he named Zuhayr, because he did not contort speech, did not pursue its obscure words, and did not praise a man except for what was in him. One of his best-known verses indicating his old age is: "I am weary of life's burdens, and whoever lives eighty years will surely grow weary." The division between the Jahiliyya and Islam in his household is marked by his son Ka'b, who first satirized the Prophet and then came to him repentant with the ode "Banat Su'ad". The Prophet then clothed him with his mantle (*burda*), in a well-known narrative that bears some traces of storytelling.

## Limits of the Picture

The poems give us no more than the transmitters provide. Some of the maxims attributed to Zuhayr may belong to the general stock of proverbial wisdom that was credited to the most famous speakers of such sayings. The precise chronology of the War of Dahis and al-Ghabra' is undetermined, which is why we content ourselves with an approximate dating of its years. Caution is also required against turning Zuhayr into a peace advocate in the modern sense. The praise here is framed within the context of blood-money and tribal sureties (*hamala*), not within a rejection of violence in absolute terms.

## Impact

Zuhayr's Mu'allaqa is still studied today as a model of pre-Islamic poetic wisdom. The poetic fame of his family, especially his son Ka'b's ode, has tied his name to the historical moment of transition from the Jahiliyya to Islam, although Zuhayr himself died shortly before the rise of Islam or at its outset, and did not live to see his sons embrace it.
