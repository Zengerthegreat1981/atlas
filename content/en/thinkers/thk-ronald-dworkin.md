# Ronald Dworkin

An American jurist and philosopher of law (1931–2013) who worked as a professor at the universities of Oxford and New York, and who succeeded **H.L.A. Hart** in the chair of jurisprudence at Oxford. He was the most prominent critic of modern legal positivism in the second half of the twentieth century.

## Dworkin's Central Objection to Hart: Rules Are Not Enough

In his early essay "The Model of Rules" (1967, reprinted in *Taking Rights Seriously*, 1977), Dworkin argued that Hart's theory, which reduces law to a system of **rules** applied in an all-or-nothing manner, cannot explain **hard cases**. In such cases judges draw on **principles** (such as the principle that no one may profit from his own wrong). These principles are not applied with all-or-nothing logic but are **weighed** against one another.

## The Alternative: Law as Constructive Interpretation

Dworkin later developed a complete theory, which he called **law as integrity**, in *Law's Empire* (1986). He holds that determining what the law says in a particular case is a **constructive interpretive** exercise. It is neither the discovery of a prior truth nor a free invention by the judge, but an attempt to offer the best possible moral and political justification that fits, and justifies, the prior legal practice at the same time.

## The Dialogue with Hart

Hart himself wrote an incomplete reply to Dworkin, published after his death as a **Postscript** to the second edition of *The Concept of Law* (1994). In it he reaffirmed the separability of law and morality in the face of Dworkin's objection. See **the Hart–Dworkin debate**. Hart there accepted that a rule of recognition may incorporate moral criteria as a condition of legal validity (a position now usually called inclusive or soft positivism, as opposed to the exclusive positivism of Joseph Raz, which denies this), and held that Dworkin's objection does not touch that version. Dworkin's later reply, in chapters of *Justice in Robes* (2006), rejected this move, arguing that a positivism that admits moral criteria into the grounds of law loses its distinctive content. Dworkin's essay "Is There Really No Right Answer in Hard Cases?" (1978; reprinted in *A Matter of Principle*, 1985) belongs to the earlier phase of the debate and concerns the right-answer thesis.

## The Right Answer Thesis

One of Dworkin's most contested claims is that every legal case, however difficult or ambiguous it may seem, has **one right answer**, which is discovered by the ideal judge whom Dworkin calls **Hercules**: a judge with superhuman ability to grasp the whole history and principles of the legal system. Hercules builds a single consistent interpretive theory that justifies, as well as possible, every precedent and every valid statutory rule in force, and then derives from it the correct resolution of the case before him. This contradicts the view Dworkin attributed to positivists such as Hart, that in hard cases the law runs out and judges exercise **discretion**, in effect making new law by reference to criteria external to the existing law, such as public policy.

## The Theory of Rights as "Trumps"

In *Taking Rights Seriously* (1977), Dworkin offers an influential metaphor: individual rights are **trumps** against arguments based on collective welfare or **utilitarian calculations**. Even if a policy maximizes overall social welfare, it may not override a basic individual right such as freedom of expression or equality, because rights by definition operate as a constraint on calculations of collective benefit, not as an element weighed within them.

## Impact and Criticism

Dworkin's work had a profound influence on Anglo-American philosophy of law and comparative constitutional law. It reshaped the debate over judicial interpretation and the normative role of the judge, moving beyond the purely mechanical picture of the separation between making law and applying it. His theory, however, faced criticism from several directions. Positivists regarded the idea of "one right answer" as idealistic and unrealistic, failing to reflect the actual nature of litigation. Legal realists and scholars in critical studies saw in "Hercules" an exaggerated portrayal of judicial neutrality that in practice conceals political and ideological choices. Critics of law as integrity asked whether a single coherent legal tradition actually exists and can be verified historically in legal systems with multiple sources and internal contradictions.

## Constitutional Interpretation: The Moral Reading

Dworkin applies his general interpretive method to constitutional law in what he calls the **moral reading** of the Constitution. He holds that vague constitutional phrases such as "equal protection" or "due process of law" are not a mandate for the judge to apply the narrow historical intentions of the framers. They are a commitment to abstract moral and political principles (an argument developed in *Freedom's Law*, 1996) (such as equality and dignity). The judge must work out the best conception of these principles in light of accumulated constitutional practice, not in light of the specific applications the drafters imagined at the time of drafting. This position places him in direct confrontation with the **originalism** school, represented by Justice Antonin Scalia. Dworkin holds that fidelity to the constitutional text itself requires interpreting it through its general principles rather than through its drafters' narrow expectations.

## Broader Philosophical and Moral Work

Dworkin's contributions were not confined to the philosophy of law. In *Sovereign Virtue* (2000), he developed a theory of distributive justice based on the principle of **equality of resources**. He used an innovative analytical tool, the "envy test," within a hypothetical auction, to determine a fair distribution of initial resources, with individuals remaining responsible for the consequences of their subsequent free choices. This position roughly parallels John Rawls's theory in *A Theory of Justice*, but differs from it in the proposed distribution mechanism. Dworkin also wrote in *Life's Dominion* (1993) about abortion and euthanasia from the perspective of the sacred value of human life, apart from the question of the moral personhood of the fetus. In *Justice for Hedgehogs* (2011), he sought to unify his theories of ethics and law within a single philosophical vision, which holds that values (truth, justice, morality) are structurally interconnected and do not conflict when properly understood.
