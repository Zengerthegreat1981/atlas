# Zora Neale Hurston

Zora Neale Hurston (1891–1960) was an American anthropologist, folklorist, and novelist whose fieldwork among Black communities in the American South and the Caribbean produced some of the most vivid ethnography of the twentieth century — and whose fiction, above all *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937), made her one of the central figures of American literature. Trained by Franz Boas at Barnard and Columbia, she combined rigorous ethnographic method with a storyteller's ear, recording folktales, sermons, songs, and hoodoo practices that other researchers had overlooked. Neglected in her later years, she was rediscovered in the 1970s and is now recognised as a founder of both Black anthropology and African American literature.

## Life and Career

Hurston was born on 7 January 1891 in Notasulga, Alabama, and grew up in Eatonville, Florida, the first incorporated all-Black town in the United States, where her father served as mayor and preacher. She left home as a teenager, worked as a maid and a waitress, and eventually made her way to Howard University and then to Barnard College in New York, where she studied anthropology under Franz Boas and graduated in 1928. Boas arranged a fellowship for her to do graduate work at Columbia, and she carried out fieldwork in Florida, Louisiana, Alabama, and later in Haiti and Jamaica, collecting folklore with a tape recorder and a notebook. She was part of the Harlem Renaissance, publishing stories and plays alongside Langston Hughes, with whom she collaborated on the play *Mule Bone* (1931). Her most productive decade was the 1930s: she published a novel, a folklore collection, and a second novel in quick succession, while working for the Federal Writers' Project. Her later years were difficult: her books went out of print, she worked as a maid and a librarian, and she died in poverty on 28 January 1960 in Fort Pierce, Florida, buried in an unmarked grave. In 1973 the novelist Alice Walker travelled to Florida, found the grave, and placed a headstone inscribed "A Genius of the South."

## Key Works

*Jonah's Gourd Vine* (1934), her first novel, drew on her parents' lives in Eatonville. *Mules and Men* (1935) is her first folklore collection, recording the tales, songs, and sermons of Black workers in Florida lumber camps and of hoodoo practitioners in New Orleans — a landmark of American ethnography. *Their Eyes Were Watching God* (1937), her masterpiece, tells the story of Janie Crawford's search for love and selfhood in the Black South; it is now regarded as one of the great American novels. *Tell My Horse: Voodoo and Life in Haiti and Jamaica* (1938) reports her Caribbean fieldwork, including her controversial account of voodoo ritual and the zombi. *Dust Tracks on a Road* (1942) is her autobiography, and *Seraph on the Suwanee* (1948) her last novel. Her essays include "Characteristics of Negro Expression" (1934), a classic analysis of Black vernacular aesthetics, and "The 'Pet Negro' System" (1943), on the patronage relations of the Jim Crow South.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Hurston's central contribution was to treat Black folk culture as a complete and sophisticated world, worthy of study on its own terms rather than as a survival of African custom or a defective version of white culture. Where earlier anthropology had often treated folklore as raw material for theory, Hurston presented it as literature: her collections preserve the voices of her informants — their timing, their humour, their metaphors — rather than reducing them to data. Her ethnography was inseparable from her fiction: *Their Eyes Were Watching God* is built from the speech, sermons, and storytelling practices she recorded in the field, and its heroine's journey is an ethnographic argument about Black women's self-determination. She also developed an influential account of Black expressive culture, arguing in "Characteristics of Negro Expression" that Black speech is dramatic, metaphorical, and performative — a theory of language that anticipated later sociolinguistics. Her work on hoodoo and voodoo treated African diasporic religion as a coherent system of knowledge and power, not as superstition. In all of this she practised the cultural relativism she learned from Boas, but with a difference: she wrote from inside the culture she studied, as a Black Southern woman, and her authority came from belonging as well as from training.

## Influence and Critique

Hurston's influence is now enormous, in two fields at once. In literature, her rediscovery by Alice Walker — whose 1975 essay "In Search of Zora Neale Hurston" launched the revival — restored her to the canon, and *Their Eyes Were Watching God* is taught in schools and universities across the English-speaking world. In anthropology, she is recognised as a pioneer of Black and feminist ethnography, of the ethnography of the African diaspora, and of writing that refuses the boundary between ethnography and literature. Her method — the participant-observer who is also a native, a storyteller, and a performer — anticipated debates about positionality and reflexivity by half a century. Critics have noted tensions in her work: her contemporaries in the 1930s sometimes faulted her for avoiding direct protest against racism, and her 1955 essay opposing school desegregation, "Court Order Can't Make Races Mix," remains controversial. Scholars have also debated the accuracy of her Haitian material and the line between ethnography and invention in her folklore collections. Yet the judgement of later generations is clear: Hurston recorded a world that would otherwise have gone unrecorded, and she did so in prose that remains unmatched in American anthropology.
