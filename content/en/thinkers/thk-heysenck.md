# Hans J. Eysenck

A British psychologist (German-British, 1916–1997) at the Institute of Psychiatry in London (IOP), credited with building "behavior therapy" as an academic movement, and a leading figure in the study of personality and individual differences in Britain. He was born in Berlin and left Germany as a young man in the 1930s because of Nazism, received his doctorate from the University of London, worked at the Maudsley Hospital, and built at the IOP a department of psychology that became one of the most active centers of research on personality and therapy in the world.

## What He Contributed

In *Behaviour Therapy and the Neuroses* (1960, as editor), Eysenck brought together the applied trends of learning theory into a single movement that he called "behavior therapy," following Wolpe's announcement in 1958. With Stanley Rachman he founded the journal *Behaviour Research and Therapy* (1963), which became a leading forum of behavior therapy. The theoretical premise of this work pictured neurosis as behavior learned through conditioning, along Pavlov's line, and so to be treated by reconditioning rather than by penetrating supposed depths.

Before all that, in 1952 he published his article "The Effects of Psychotherapy: An Evaluation" in the *Journal of Consulting Psychology*, in which he argued, from a review of available data, that the rate of improvement among treated neurotic patients did not exceed that of those who improve spontaneously without treatment; he estimated improvement at about 44% after psychoanalysis and 64% after eclectic therapy, against about 72% among those who received no specialized treatment. It was later shown that the article was methodologically flawed in many places: his classification of improvement was biased against dynamic therapy, and later re-examinations of the evidence, such as Bergin's review of 1971, disputed its conclusion; the most famous later rebuttal is Smith and Glass's 1977 meta-analysis of 375 outcome studies, which found that treated clients on average fared better than untreated ones. Yet it is among the most influential pieces ever written in the history of psychotherapy: from it the whole field of "outcome research" was born, because therapeutic schools were forced for the first time to prove that they work.

In the psychology of individual differences he developed the three-dimensional **PEN model**: extraversion versus introversion (Extraversion), neuroticism (Neuroticism), and psychoticism (Psychoticism), in works beginning with *Dimensions of Personality* (1947) and culminating in *The Biological Basis of Personality* (1967). He insisted that these dimensions are not merely statistical descriptions but have a biological basis. In *The Biological Basis of Personality* (1967) he proposed that introverts have higher baseline cortical arousal than extraverts. According to this hypothesis, the introvert is naturally higher in cortical arousal and so avoids excessive stimulation, while the extravert is lower and so seeks it. This link between the statistical factor and physiology is what distinguishes his project from Cattell's. He devised his own standard instruments (the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire, EPQ), which spread through international research.

## His Place in the Current

A leading British figure in behavior therapy, who built a "movement" out of Wolpe's individual work. His London school intersected with American learning theory, but with an orientation more inclined to inherited differences among individuals; whereas Skinner held that the environment makes behavior, Eysenck held that the individual's inherited temperament determines in the first place his readiness for conditioning. He thereby explained why some individuals acquire fears and neuroses more easily than others.

## Scientific Reception

His model produced a fruitful scientific controversy: later research largely replaced it with the Big Five model, in which "psychoticism" is often seen as a mixture of low agreeableness and low conscientiousness, and this dimension is, in his scheme, the least stable of the dimensions and the least clear in meaning. The dimensions of extraversion and neuroticism remained among the most solid findings of personality science, and modern models agree on them. In this sense his influence remains stronger in method than in the final form of the model.

## Limitation

Eysenck's record is heavier than his contribution. In *Race, Intelligence and Education* (1971) he argued that the difference between ethnic groups in intelligence scores is partly hereditary, provoking a storm that lasted decades.

But the more serious matter came after his death: his joint works with Ronald Grossarth-Maticek, which claimed that a "cancer-prone personality" predicts disease more strongly than smoking itself (and the tobacco industry funded part of them), were subjected to a formal inquiry at King's College London (an independent committee, report 2019), which found **26 papers** "unsafe" and recommended their retraction. In February 2020 two journals, *Psychological Reports* and *Perceptual and Motor Skills*, retracted 13 of Eysenck's papers (not all of them from the list of 26) and placed 61 others under "Expressions of Concern" instead of full retraction, which researchers (among them David Marks and Roderick Buchanan) criticized as insufficient, arguing that the number of unreliable publications may be larger. It is among the largest research-integrity cases in the history of psychology.

His son Michael Eysenck is a well-known cognitive psychologist, but he worked on an independent line, not as an extension of his father's project.

## Major Works

*Dimensions of Personality* (1947) · "The Effects of Psychotherapy: An Evaluation" (1952) · *Uses and Abuses of Psychology* (1953) · *The Structure of Human Personality* (1953) · *Behaviour Therapy and the Neuroses* (1960, as editor) · *The Biological Basis of Personality* (1967).
