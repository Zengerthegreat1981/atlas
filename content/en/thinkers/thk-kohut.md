# Heinz Kohut

An Austrian-American physician and psychoanalyst, president of the American Psychoanalytic Association (1964–1965) and vice president of the International Psychoanalytical Association (1965). He was a training analyst and supervisor at the Chicago Institute for Psychoanalysis and a person of great influence in its development. He is the founding father of "self psychology."

## What He Contributed

- **Empathy as an instrument of observation** (1959): his point of departure, and the earliest of his papers to have an impact. He asked: **what defines a field of science?** And he answered: **its method of observation.** What distinguishes psychoanalysis is that its data cannot be reached by the senses from outside, but through **vicarious introspection**: the analyst knows the inner state by sensing it from within the patient's position. Accordingly, whatever cannot be reached by this method (the nerves, measured behavior) lies outside the bounds of the field, however true it may be. He stressed that for him empathy is **a neutral cognitive instrument** and not a moral virtue: it can be used to inflict harm as well as to heal.
- **Narcissism as an independent line of development**: he overturned the prevailing view. It was settled that narcissism is a primitive stage to be passed beyond toward object love, so that whoever remained in it was arrested. Kohut said instead that it is **a line that develops in its own right** from its crude form to its mature forms: ambition, ideals, a sense of humor, acceptance of mortality, and creativity. The trouble, then, is not that a person is narcissistic, but that his or her narcissism **has not matured**.
- **Selfobjects**: his most precise concept and the hardest to translate. The other here is not an independent person but is **experienced as a part of one's own self and a function within it**, as an arm is experienced rather than as a stranger is experienced. There are three needs: **mirroring** (to be seen and delighted in, so that one's sense of worth forms), **idealizing** (to find a calm strength to lean on, and so to be soothed), and **twinship** (to find someone who resembles one, and so to feel that one belongs to the human kind). His thesis is that these needs **do not end with maturity**: the adult needs them to the end of life as he or she needs oxygen, and this is his fundamental disagreement with the view of independence as the goal of development.
- **Transmuting internalization**: his mechanism of growth, whose core is **optimal frustration**. What heals the child (and the patient) is not being fully satisfied, but being failed to a degree that is **bearable** and repeated, so that little by little the child builds within the function that was formerly drawn from outside. The psychic structure is thus built from **small gaps in responsiveness**, neither from its perfection nor from its absence.
- **Narcissistic rage** (1972): he distinguished it sharply from ordinary aggression. Ordinary aggression has an aim and subsides when it is reached; narcissistic rage, by contrast, is a response to **a wound to the self**, and its features are: it is **neither forgotten nor forgiven**, it seeks not victory but **the erasure of the offender**, it takes possession of its bearer so that his or her life is reorganized around it, and it produces a tight rational justification that makes it appear a matter of principle. From this concept were later read the phenomena of revenge, destructive leadership, and violence after humiliation.
- **Guilty Man and Tragic Man**: the most concise formulation of what he changed. Freud's human being is **guilty**: torn between impulse and prohibition, and treatment makes him able to bear his conflict. Kohut's human being is **tragic**: he fails not in restraining his drives but in **realizing the program of his self**, and dies with what was most important in him unlived. The great suffering for Kohut is not guilt but **emptiness and disintegration**.

## His Place in the Movement

The founder of the **Chicago school of self psychology**, and one of the deepest shifts in psychoanalysis in the second half of the twentieth century. His emphasis on empathy and responsiveness influenced later relational and intersubjective currents in American psychoanalysis.

His position is understood through his dispute with **Otto Kernberg**, the debate that organized the field for thirty years: both worked on narcissism, and each arrived at the opposite of what the other reached. For Kohut, narcissism is a **deficit** in an early response, so its remedy is **empathy**, which allows growth to resume; for Kernberg it is a **defensive structure** against primary aggression and envy, so its remedy is **confrontation**. For Kohut aggression is **always secondary**, the product of a wound rather than an innate drive, and this is the issue on which the field split.

A striking point in his biography is that **"The Two Analyses of Mr Z"** (1979), his most famous paper, in which he presents a patient he analyzed twice (once by the classical method, when he seemed to succeed though he did not, and once by the method of self psychology), was shown later, from the biography written by Charles Strozier, most probably to be **his own analysis of himself** presented in the third person.

## Limits
Three objections to Kohut:

**First: collusion with grandiosity.** This is the objection of Kernberg and those with him: that refraining from confronting exploitation, contempt, and envy in the name of empathy leaves the narcissistic structure intact, so the patient leaves more comfortable while nothing may change in his or her relations with other people. Kohut's followers reply that early confrontation reopens the very same wound so that the patient shuts down, and that confrontation becomes possible **after** the alliance has settled, not before.

**Second: an unfalsifiable method.** If empathy is the instrument of observation, by what is it judged that an analyst erred in sensing? There is no criterion outside the sensing itself. Critics have noted that this makes believing the patient's description of his or her wound a rule not open to revision.

**Third: developmental evidence.** His claim that the three selfobject needs operate from the first months, and that their disturbance produces a particular narcissistic structure, **has not been tested** in developmental research. Attachment researchers note that his concepts overlap with what has actually been measured (parental responsiveness, attunement) without having been formulated in a measurable form.

What has passed from Kohut into general practice, however, is not his theory but his **stance**: that a symptom is to be understood first from the position of the one who bears it, and that the therapist be asked about his or her share in a break in communication before naming it the patient's resistance.

## His Most Important Works
