# Robert A. Orsi

An American religious scholar (born 1953), Professor of Religious Studies and History at Northwestern University, where he holds the Grace Craddock Nagle Chair in Catholic Studies, after earlier posts at Indiana University and Harvard Divinity School. He is regarded as a leading figure in the development of the "lived religion" approach in contemporary American religious studies. This is a methodological shift from the study of official texts and doctrines to the field study of the everyday religious practice of ordinary people.

## *The Madonna of 115th Street* (1985)

In this foundational ethnographic-historical study (Yale University Press), Orsi documented the annual devotional rituals of Italian Catholic immigrants in East Harlem, New York, toward a statue of the Virgin Mary (Our Lady of Mount Carmel church). He focused on the details of bodily and popular practice, such as crawling on the knees, candles and vows, which are often neglected or treated with disdain in official theological studies of Catholicism. He showed how ordinary believers produce a complex religious meaning in their daily practices, either apart from the official doctrine of the church institution or through continuous negotiation with it.

## *Thank You, St. Jude* and *Between Heaven and Earth*

Orsi turned to the study of American Catholic women in the twentieth century in *Thank You, St. Jude: Women's Devotion to the Patron Saint of Hopeless Causes* (Yale University Press, 1996), in which he drew on letters of thanks and other sources to show how women shape their relationship with the saint in the face of illness and poverty. In *Between Heaven and Earth: The Religious Worlds People Make and the Scholars Who Study Them* (Princeton University Press, 2005), he theorised his method: for him religion is a set of "relations" between human beings and sacred beings, understood within the "presence" that believers experience as real, and the scholar must take it seriously without reducing it. He developed this further in *History and Presence* (Harvard University Press, 2016), in which he criticised academic history for excluding from its account whatever the contemporary "present" does not acknowledge.

## The Method of "Lived Religion"

Orsi's method rests on a central principle: the actual religion people live is not necessarily identical to what official doctrinal texts state or what theologians describe. It should be studied in the field as it is practised, in homes, streets, bodies and daily relationships, not only as it is written in sacred books or institutional documents. This requires ethnographic tools (participant observation, interviews and oral archives), closer to field anthropology than to intellectual history or comparative theology. *Lived Religion in America: Toward a History of Practice* (Princeton University Press, 1997), edited by David D. Hall and including an essay by Orsi, was an important statement of the approach. Orsi also edited *Gods of the City: Religion and the American Urban Landscape* (Indiana University Press, 1999).

## Lived Religion in Islam: Festivals and Visitation

Orsi's approach finds an echo in studies of lived Islam. Samuli Schielke, in *The Perils of Joy: Contesting Mulid Festivals in Contemporary Egypt* (Syracuse University Press, 2012), studied the festivals (*mawalid*) of saints in Egypt, and showed how physical joy, devotion and commerce coexist in them, and how reformists who regard them as heretical criticise them. Valerie Hoffman, in *Sufism, Mystics, and Saints in Modern Egypt* (University of South Carolina Press, 1995), wrote on popular Sufi experience. The Islamic tradition also contains an old debate between popular practice and the position of the jurists. Ibn Taymiyya, in *Iqtida' al-Sirat al-Mustaqim* (fourteenth century CE), criticised many popular celebrations and the visitation of graves, while other scholars defended them. Islam thus offers an example of a debate between "official religion" and "lived religion" that parallels what Orsi studied. Talal Asad, in "The Idea of an Anthropology of Islam" (1986), called for viewing Islam as a "discursive tradition," neither a collection of separate ideas nor of separate practices. This position intersects with Orsi's interest in practice, while differing from it in its focus on power and discourse.

## Impact and Criticism

Orsi's approach has helped reorient American religious studies away from the exclusive focus on the official doctrine of the major traditions, chiefly Protestant Christianity, toward the actual diversity of popular religious practice across classes, ethnicities and immigrant groups. "Lived religion" has become a widely used methodological approach in the field. In contrast, Meredith McGuire, in *Lived Religion: Faith and Practice in Everyday Life* (Oxford University Press, 2008), offered a different sociological formulation focusing on the body and material culture. The concept has been criticised as vague, since it is difficult to determine what does not count as "lived," and as risking a sanctification of the popular at the expense of understanding the role of institutions and power. Orsi himself discussed the limits of the idea in his lecture "Is the Study of Lived Religion Irrelevant to the World We Live In?" (*Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion*, 2003).
