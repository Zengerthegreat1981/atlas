# Heidi A. Campbell

An American scholar, professor of communication at Texas A&M University and director of the Network for New Media, Religion and Digital Culture Studies. She is regarded as a leading figure in developing the field of "Digital Religion Studies" as an independent research area within media and religion studies.

## From a Single Case Study to a Comparative Framework

Campbell began her research with doctoral work, completed in the early 2000s, on Christian groups communicating online through email. It was later published as *Exploring Religious Community Online* (2005). She then broadened this initial interest in her book *When Religion Meets New Media* (2010), moving from the study of a single case to a comparative analytical framework across several religious traditions (Orthodox Judaism, Islam and Christianity). In this book she set out the "religious-social shaping of technology" approach; the concept of "networked religion" was developed in her later writing.

## Method: Rejecting Technological Determinism

Methodologically, Campbell rejects the common assumption that digital technologies "impose" a religious transformation in a deterministic way, regardless of context. She draws on the approach of the "social shaping of technology" borrowed from science and technology studies. Each religious community negotiates the new technology according to its religious authority, its normative texts and its particular history, so that it adopts, rejects or adapts it, partly or fully, in ways specific to itself, rather than responding in a uniform mechanical way.

## The Religious-Social Shaping of Technology

Campbell's framework (the "religious-social shaping of technology", RSST) examines how a religious community negotiates a new technology through four dimensions: the community's history and tradition; its core beliefs and patterns of practice; the process of negotiation by which it decides whether to adopt, reject or adapt the technology; and the communal framing and discourse through which it justifies that decision. She applied this framework to diverse cases, including Orthodox Jewish communities' regulated use of the internet, Muslim debates and religious rulings about online media, and Christian uses of digital platforms.

## Later Projects

Campbell extended her research project by editing additional collective volumes, among them *Digital Religion: Understanding Religious Practice in New Media Worlds* (2013; a revised second edition, co-edited with Ruth Tsuria, appeared in 2021), which gathered contributions from multiple researchers to map the field of digital religion comprehensively. She also undertook a joint project with Gregory Grieve on religion and digital games (wrk-playing-with-religion-in-digital-games-campbell-grieve). She directs the "Network for New Media, Religion and Digital Culture Studies", an international research platform connecting scholars working in this emerging field.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

In her work, Campbell devotes direct attention to how Islamic religious authorities deal with digital technologies. Her treatment of Islam in *When Religion Meets New Media* and related writing looks at how Muslim religious authorities and communities respond to internet use, including online religious rulings. A recurring point is that Islam lacks a single central clerical hierarchy, so responses to digital media tend to be plural and dispersed, in contrast to more centralised structures in some other traditions. This is a general observation, not a systematic survey of Islamic jurisprudence on digital technology.

## Academic Standing

Campbell is a professor of communication at Texas A&M University and a prominent scholar of digital religion.

## Further Works

Campbell also published *Digital Judaism: Jewish Negotiations with Digital Media and Culture* (2015), an edited volume that examines how different Jewish communities engage with digital media and culture, extending her framework to a single tradition in greater depth.

## Closing Assessment

Campbell's work has helped make digital religion an established sub-field of media and religion studies, and her central point is that technology does not impose a uniform religiosity: each community negotiates it in light of its own tradition.
