# Eugène Minkowski

Eugène Minkowski (1885–1972) was a Russian-born, French-based psychiatrist and one of the founders of phenomenological psychopathology. He is best known for his analysis of lived time and of the loss of "vital contact with reality" in schizophrenia, and he is less widely read in Arabic than other figures of the phenomenological and existential tradition.

## Life and Formation

Minkowski was born in 1885 in Saint Petersburg. He studied medicine in Germany and Switzerland and trained in psychiatry in Zurich under Eugen Bleuler, the psychiatrist who introduced the terms "schizophrenia" and "autism." He settled in France and worked as a psychiatrist in Paris, where he took part in the circle around the journal *L'Évolution psychiatrique*, which brought together French psychiatrists open to psychoanalysis and phenomenology. He died in Paris in 1972.

## What He Gave

His central concept is **lived time** (*le temps vécu*): the human being does not live in measured clock time but in a time that has direction, extension, rhythm and a stretch toward the future, and mental illness can disturb this structure. Influenced by Bergson's notion of duration and the *élan vital*, and drawn increasingly to phenomenology, he argued that psychiatry should describe the person's experienced world and not merely list symptoms.

He reinterpreted Bleuler's idea of schizophrenic "autism" as a **loss of vital contact with reality** (*perte du contact vital avec la réalité*): the patient may retain intellectual capacities but loses the immediate, lived attunement to the world and to others. In the same line he described **morbid rationalism** and **morbid geometrism**, in which a rigid, abstract and spatialised reasoning replaces ordinary lived familiarity with things.

## His Examples

In depression, as he described it, the future closes and the past grows heavy, so the present becomes a wall. In some forms of schizophrenia the vital contact with reality is severed, so that things lose their familiarity and the person is left with reasoning but little living attunement. These are phenomenological descriptions of experience, not diagnostic criteria.

## Why It Matters

He provides a model of what phenomenology can do in psychiatry: a description of the patient's lived world that is more precise than a symptom list and can be learned and applied by clinicians. His analyses of time were taken up by later phenomenological psychiatrists, including Erwin Straus, and by recent work such as Thomas Fuchs's on temporality and psychopathology.

## Limits

His writing is descriptive and clinical rather than systematic, and his claims rest on case observation, not controlled research. The concepts of vital contact and lived time have also been criticised as difficult to operationalise empirically, though they remain influential in qualitative and phenomenological work.

## His Major Works

*La Schizophrénie: Psychopathologie des schizoïdes et des schizophrènes* (1927) · *Le Temps vécu: Études phénoménologiques et psychopathologiques* (1933; English: *Lived Time*, 1970) · *Vers une cosmologie* (1936) · *Traité de psychopathologie* (1966)
