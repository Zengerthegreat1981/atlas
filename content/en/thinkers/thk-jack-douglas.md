# Jack D. Douglas

An American sociologist, one of the founders of "existential sociology", a critical current that rejected the abstraction of functionalist and positivist sociology from the actual lived subjective experience of the social actor.

## The Central Idea

In his study *The Social Meanings of Suicide* (1967), Douglas argued that the official suicide statistics on which Durkheim relied are not a solid objective datum but the product of social classifications and negotiations carried out by investigating officials and forensic examiners, and he called for a method that restores the standing of the subjective meanings that actors give to their actions. Later, with John Johnson in *Existential Sociology* (1977), he developed a framework that rejects the assumption of the abstract "rational actor" or the rigid "social role", focusing instead on raw existential experience (anxiety, pain, pleasure, chaos) that individuals actually live in facing the social world, and which precedes any prior theoretical classification of it.

## Historical Context

Jack Douglas developed his analysis in an American academic context dominated in the 1960s by the strict statistical positivism inherited from Émile Durkheim, who in *Suicide* (1897) relied on comparative official statistics across European countries to build a purely structural sociological explanation of the phenomenon of suicide, regarding these statistics as a solid objective datum that can be explained without the need to understand the subjective meaning that each individual gives to his or her act. Douglas's critique was influenced by the traditions of phenomenology (Alfred Schutz) and ethnomethodology (Harold Garfinkel), which doubted the presumed "objective" nature of any official statistic or social classification.

## Main Ideas and Method

Douglas showed in the field how a death certificate and the classification of a particular case as "suicide" are constructed through a complex social negotiating process in which the coroner, the police and sometimes the family and the insurance investigator take part, influenced by non-objective factors (such as the religious and social stigma attached to suicide in some cultures, or considerations related to life insurance policies), which makes the official "suicide rate" a partial reflection of institutional classification practices as much as it is a reflection of the actual phenomenon itself. Later, with John Johnson, he developed the "existential sociology" framework, which rejected the abstract "rational actor" assumed by functionalist theory and the rigid "social role" of traditional symbolic interactionism, focusing instead on raw existential dimensions such as anxiety, pain, pleasure and chaos that individuals actually live in facing the social world.

## Prominent Works and Representatives

His book *The Social Meanings of Suicide* (1967) represents the first founding work, followed by his jointly edited book with John Johnson, *Existential Sociology* (1977), which gathered multiple contributions from this emerging current. Douglas engaged critically with Émile Durkheim, author of the classic study of suicide, and with Harold Garfinkel, founder of ethnomethodology, who shared his interest in deconstructing apparently "objective" official classifications.

## Impact and Criticism

This current paved the way for later currents in the sociology of emotion, the body and lived experience, and for the critique of the cold, detached style of writing in the social sciences. However, Douglas's critique of official statistics was in turn criticized on the view that total scepticism about the reliability of statistical data may make any broad systematic comparative research difficult, and that some aspects of the classification process (such as determining the medical cause of death) remain objectively verifiable despite the existence of a social negotiating margin around it. His most prominent contribution remains his early methodological critique of the reliability of official statistics and his co-founding of the existential current in American sociology.

## Later Work and Legacy

Douglas worked as a professor at the University of California, San Diego. His later work included *Investigative Social Research* (1976), studies of deviance and of the welfare state, and, with Paul Rasmussen and Carol Ann Flanagan, *The Nude Beach* (1977), a field study of nude beaches. Douglas is remembered today as one of the early voices that pushed American sociology away from excessive positivist methodological certainty toward a deeper awareness of the fragility of every social classification, however official or objective it may appear on its face. His study of suicide is often discussed alongside Durkheim's as a contrasting methodological position on the limits of official statistics.
