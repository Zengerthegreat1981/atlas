# Boaventura de Sousa Santos

Boaventura de Sousa Santos (born 1940) is a Portuguese sociologist and legal scholar, long associated with the University of Coimbra, whose work argues for "epistemologies of the South" and a broader recognition of knowledges produced outside the Western academic canon. He is a leading figure in global-South critical social theory and in discussions around the World Social Forum.

## Life and Context

Born in Coimbra in 1940, he studied law there and obtained a doctorate in the sociology of law at Yale. He taught at Coimbra, held a long-term appointment at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, and founded the Centre for Social Studies at Coimbra. His early field research on informal justice in a Rio de Janeiro favela (the "Pasargada" study) shaped his sociology of law, and his later work addressed globalization, democracy, human rights and Latin American social movements.

## Main Ideas

- Abyssal thinking: modern Western thought divides the world by an invisible line, making the knowledge and experience of colonized peoples non-existent or invalid.
- Epistemologies of the South: knowledges born in struggles against capitalism, colonialism and patriarchy deserve recognition as knowledge in their own right.
- Sociology of absences and of emergences: research should reveal what is actively produced as non-existent and identify possibilities emerging in the present.
- Ecology of knowledges: different forms of knowledge, scientific and non-scientific, should interact on the basis of equality rather than hierarchy.
- Subaltern cosmopolitanism and "interlegality": law operates through overlapping legal orders at local, national and global scales.

## Major Works

- Toward a New Common Sense: Law, Science and Politics in the Paradigmatic Transition (1995)
- Another Knowledge Is Possible (edited, 2007)
- Epistemologies of the South: Justice Against Epistemicide (2014)
- The End of the Cognitive Empire (2018)

## Method

His approach joins socio-legal empirical research with critical social theory, and he advocates an engaged, activist-oriented scholarship that learns from movements rather than only studying them.

## Influence and Criticism

His concepts are widely used in decolonial studies, legal sociology and discussions of social movements in Latin America, Africa and Europe. Critics argue that his categories risk romanticizing popular knowledge, that "the South" is defined loosely, and that his broad syntheses sit uneasily with detailed empirical evidence.
