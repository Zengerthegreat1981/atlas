# Joseph Schumpeter

**Joseph Schumpeter** was an Austrian-American economist and one of the most original political economists of the twentieth century. He offered an alternative vision of the nature of capitalism that highlights innovation and entrepreneurship as the central engine of economic change, in contrast to the prevailing emphasis at the time on static equilibrium or the management of aggregate demand.

## From the Austrian Ministry of Finance to Harvard

Schumpeter was born in Moravia (then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire). He studied law and economics at the University of Vienna, where he attended the seminar of Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, one of the leading figures of Austrian marginalism. He served briefly as Austrian Minister of Finance in 1919, under extremely difficult conditions following the First World War. He then worked as a banker, taught at the University of Bonn, and from 1932 until his death was a professor at Harvard University in the United States.

## The Entrepreneur and New Combinations

In his early book *The Theory of Economic Development* (1911), Schumpeter drew a fundamental distinction between a "static" economy that repeats itself without essential change, and genuine **"development"**, which is driven by the **entrepreneur** through the execution of "new combinations" of existing factors of production: a new product, a new method of production, a new market, or a new form of industrial organisation. Later commentators distinguish an earlier "Mark I" Schumpeter, who stressed the individual entrepreneur, from a later "Mark II" Schumpeter, who stressed innovation within large firms' research departments. The entrepreneur is not merely a manager who improves efficiency within existing limits. He deliberately breaks the existing equilibrium, earning temporary extraordinary profit before competitors imitate him and dissipate this extraordinary profit, which then begins a new cycle of innovation.

## Creative Destruction

In his American book *Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy* (1942), Schumpeter developed his most famous concept: **"creative destruction"**. He described the essence of capitalist dynamism as successive waves of radical technological innovation that wholly destroy existing industries, skills and firms even as they create new ones. He argued that even the temporary monopoly arising from a successful innovation may not be an obstacle to long-run dynamic efficiency, but rather an incentive to invest in the next innovation, which will in turn destroy it.

## Will Capitalism Collapse from Its Own Success?

In the same book, Schumpeter advanced a controversial thesis: that capitalism, despite its striking economic success, carries the seeds of its own erosion. The bureaucracy of large corporations weakens the spirit of individual risk-taking entrepreneurship, and a critical intellectual class arises from within its own institutions (universities and a press financed by the fruits of capitalism itself). This gradually paves the way for forms of bureaucratic administrative "socialism", which, in his view, could nonetheless be "democratic" rather than totalitarian. This was offered as a diagnosis of long-run tendencies, not as a prediction he welcomed, and capitalism has not evolved into administered socialism in the form he anticipated.

## Analysis of Business Cycles

Schumpeter was also concerned with explaining the economic cycle as a phenomenon composed of overlapping waves of innovation of varying lengths. This included long waves that he linked to what the Russian economist Nikolai Kondratiev had described. He set this out in his two-volume book *Business Cycles* (1939), although this work remained less influential than his other books.

## Standing and Legacy

For decades Schumpeter remained in the shadow of the Keynesian model's dominance over macroeconomics. However, his ideas on innovation, entrepreneurship and creative destruction returned to become a basic reference in the economics of growth, innovation and entrepreneurship from the late twentieth century onward. Richard Nelson and Sidney Winter later expanded them within a rigorous analytical framework known as evolutionary economics.

## Personality and Rivalry with Keynes

Schumpeter was known for a flamboyant personality. He lived in the long shadow of his contemporary rival John Maynard Keynes, whose macroeconomic model of demand management dominated the global economic scene for decades after the Second World War. Schumpeter's ideas on entrepreneurship and innovation remained less directly influential on official economic policy during his lifetime, before experiencing a broad academic revival from the late twentieth century onward, as interest grew in the economics of innovation and technological growth.

## A Long-Term Historical View of Capitalism

Schumpeter also distinguished himself by insisting on analysing capitalism over a long historical horizon spanning decades and centuries, rather than through a short-term macroeconomic analysis focused on the fluctuations of a year or two, as Keynesian economics did. This led him to consider questions about the fate of the capitalist system as a whole as a civilisation, not merely the management of its short-term economic cycle. This is a relatively rare perspective among twentieth-century economists, most of whom focused on short-term analytical tools.
