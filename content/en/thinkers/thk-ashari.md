# Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari

Founder of the Ash'ari "science of kalam," one of the most influential theological currents in Sunni Islam. He was born in Basra and, according to the traditional biographies, studied with the leading Mu'tazili Abu 'Ali al-Jubba'i until about the age of forty, and then publicly announced his repentance from the Mu'tazila in the mosque of Basra (traditionally dated about 913 CE).

## What He Contributed

- **"Al-Ibana 'an Usul al-Diyana"**: a concise exposition of his creed in the manner of the people of hadith and Ahmad ibn Hanbal, directed against the Mu'tazila and other opponents.
- **"Maqalat al-Islamiyyin"**: a historical exposition of the doctrines of the Islamic sects (important for contemporary historians of religion).
- **"Al-Luma'"**: a treatise on the fundamentals of kalam, treating topics such as the Qur'an, faith, the promise and the threat, and the names and attributes.
- **The doctrine of attributes (Khabariyyat)**: the reported attributes (the hand, the face, the istiwa') are "established" for God without "how" (La Kayfa) — neither anthropomorphism nor divesting.
- **The theory of acquisition (Kasb)**: God alone is the "creator" of acts, and the human being is their "acquirer" — that is, his act is joined to a created power that God creates in him, so that choice is established for him without contending with God over creation.

## His Place in the Tradition

- **Founder of Ash'arism**, which became the dominant theology among many Shafi'i and Maliki scholars, while the parallel school of al-Maturidi (d. 944) prevailed among the Hanafis; most Hanbalis rejected both.
- **His relation to the Mu'tazila**: a "graduate" of the Mu'tazili school, he used their tools against them.
- **Adversaries**: the Mu'tazila, and the anthropomorphists (mushabbiha) among the Hanbalis.

## His Most Important Works

- Al-Ibana 'an Usul al-Diyana
- Maqalat al-Islamiyyin
- Al-Luma' fi al-Radd 'ala Ahl al-Zaygh wa al-Bida'
- Risala ila Ahl al-Thaghr (Epistle to the People of the Frontier)

## Upbringing and Conversion

Abu al-Hasan 'Ali ibn Isma'il al-Ash'ari was born in Basra in 260 AH (about 874 CE), and traces his lineage to the Companion Abu Musa al-Ash'ari. The biographical dictionaries mention that he took hadith and fiqh from Zakariyya al-Saji in Basra, and then attached himself to Abu 'Ali al-Jubba'i, the master of the Mu'tazila of Basra, and took Mu'tazili kalam from him until about the age of forty, and used to debate on his behalf. The biographical dictionaries, such as Ibn 'Asakir's "Tabyin Kadhib al-Muftari" and al-Subki's "Tabaqat al-Shafi'iyya al-Kubra," mention that in about 300 AH (about 913 CE) he mounted the pulpit in the great mosque of Basra and announced his return from I'tizal. These books relate the story of his debate with al-Jubba'i on the question of the salutary and the more salutary (the three brothers), a traditional account that cannot be documented from a contemporary source.

He then moved to Baghdad and remained there until his death in 324 AH (about 935 or 936 CE), and was buried between al-Karkh and Bab al-Basra. Ibn 'Asakir reports that he wrote some three hundred works, of which only a few survive.

## Al-Ash'ari's Sources and Method

Al-Ash'ari benefited from the tradition of 'Abd Allah ibn Sa'id ibn Kullab (d. after 240 AH), who established a middle doctrine: he affirms God's eternal attributes contrary to the Mu'tazila, and uses rational argument contrary to the people of hadith, who disliked kalam. For this reason it has been said that al-Ash'ari in his final phase followed the path of the Kullabiyya, and added to them the tools of disputation he had learned from I'tizal. In the "Ibana" (whose attribution and relation to his other works are discussed by scholars) his affiliation with Ahmad ibn Hanbal and the people of hadith appears clearly, while in the "Luma'" his finer kalam craft in argumentation appears.

Among his most prominent positions: that the Qur'an is the eternal, uncreated speech of God; that seeing God with the eyes in the hereafter is rationally possible and actual by transmitted report; that the acts of servants are created by God and acquired by the servant; and that reason does not obligate anything upon God and does not by itself deem things good or evil, so that the good is what revelation deems good. The latter was set up in opposition to the Mu'tazili view of rational deeming good and evil.

## Influence and Reception

A generation of theologians studied under his followers: al-Qadi Abu Bakr al-Baqillani (d. 403 AH), who set up for his doctrine the theory of the indivisible atom and the accident; Abu Bakr ibn Furak, who compiled "Mujarrad Maqalat al-Shaykh Abi al-Hasan"; Abu al-Ma'ali al-Juwayni; and then Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, who introduced Aristotelian logic into kalam. The doctrine then spread with the support of the Nizamiyya schools in the fifth century AH and then through the commentaries of Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, al-Iji, and al-Taftazani, until it became the dominant tendency at al-Azhar and many Sunni schools. Among modern studies, orientalists took an interest in his treatises, and Hellmut Ritter issued an edition of "Maqalat al-Islamiyyin" (1929–1933).

## Limits and Criticism

The Hanbalis in Baghdad objected to the methods of kalam as a whole, and Ibn Taymiyya criticized what he called al-Ash'ari's being influenced by the Kullabiyya and the Mu'tazila in the questions of attributes and speech, and many of the later Ash'aris disowned Abu al-Hasan's views in the "Ibana." On the other hand, disagreement persists among scholars over attributing the details of the three phases (Mu'tazili, Kullabi, Hanbali) to al-Ash'ari, a late account from the books of the classes (tabaqat) that has no check from his own texts. Scholars, among them Richard Frank and George Makdisi, also note that later Ash'arism is quite different from the founder's own statements.
