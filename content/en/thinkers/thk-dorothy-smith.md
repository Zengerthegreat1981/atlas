# Dorothy Smith

Dorothy E. Smith (1926-2022) was a British-born Canadian sociologist who developed feminist standpoint theory and institutional ethnography, a method for exploring how everyday life is organised by large-scale institutional processes.

## Life and Context

Born in England, Smith studied at the London School of Economics and received her doctorate at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1963. She taught at the University of British Columbia, where she was involved in the early women's movement, and later at the Ontario Institute for Studies in Education at the University of Toronto. Her outlook was shaped by Marx, Mead, Schutz, ethnomethodology and the women's movement.

## Main Ideas

- **Standpoint of women.** In "Women's Perspective as a Radical Critique of Sociology" (1974), Smith argued that conventional sociology had been created from men's positions, in a world where women's unpaid work supports the "abstract" work of institutions. She proposed starting inquiry from the everyday world of women.
- **Bifurcation of consciousness.** She described the split between the lived, bodily experience of everyday life and the abstract, conceptual mode of professional and institutional work.
- **Ruling relations.** She used this term for the textually mediated and extra-local forms of organisation, such as bureaucracy, law and science, that coordinate people's activities.
- **Texts as organisers.** Documents, forms and categories carry institutional power across time and place, and she paid close attention to them.

## Method

Institutional ethnography starts from people's actual experience in a setting, then traces how their activities are linked to institutional texts and procedures. Its aim is to explain the social relations that shape experience, not to impose theoretical categories.

## Case Studies

In an article first widely read in the 1970s, titled *K is mentally ill*, Smith analysed an interview account of how a young woman came to be defined as ill by her friends. She showed how the telling selected and ordered incidents so that K's conduct appeared to break rules without good reason, while other readings receded. The point was not to diagnose or clear K but to expose how a factual account is organised. In research later published as *Mothering for Schooling* (2005), Alison Griffith and Smith began from their experience as single parents and interviewed mothers about the work of preparing children for school. Getting children fed, dressed, on time, and ready to learn, and supplementing classroom teaching, supported what schools could accomplish. Schools' expectations assumed time and resources that families possessed unequally, which could reproduce class differences even when every mother was concerned for her child. Both studies move from a particular account or activity to the wider relations organising it.

## Contemporary Empirical Examples

Institutional ethnography has been used in health research. Janet Rankin and Marie Campbell's *Managing to Nurse* (2006) examined Canadian hospital work during managerial reform. Starting from nurses' daily activities, it traced how forms, information systems, and efficiency measures, such as those governing admission and discharge, reorganised attention and judgement. What counted as effective management in records could diverge from what nurses saw patients needing. The study does not show that all measurement harms care; its strength is the tracing of specific connections between texts and work in particular settings. Researchers using the method can ask what people do, which documents they must activate, who elsewhere relies on those documents, and which parts of experience the institutional categories leave out.

## Related Concepts

Related concepts include standpoint, institutional ethnography, ruling relations, bifurcation of consciousness, unpaid work, reflexivity, ethnomethodology, bureaucracy, and textual mediation. Comparisons with Patricia Hill Collins and Sandra Harding clarify different forms of standpoint theory, while Weber on bureaucracy and Foucault on discourse offer other accounts of organised power. Marx informs Smith's insistence on beginning with the activities of actual people, and Schutz and ethnomethodology her attention to how everyday sense is accomplished. The concept of work is deliberately broad, covering effort and skill that institutions rely on without paying or recording it. This links institutional ethnography to research on care, emotional labour, and the gendered division of labour.

## Influence and Criticism

Smith's approach has been applied widely in nursing, education, social work and policy research. Critics, including Black feminist and postmodern scholars, argued that a single "women's standpoint" risks overlooking differences of race, class and sexuality, and some say the method lacks clear rules of evidence.
