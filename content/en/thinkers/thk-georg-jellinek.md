# Georg Jellinek

**Georg Jellinek** was a German public-law scholar and professor at the University of Heidelberg. He is a leading theorist of the **state and of general public legal personality** in the German positivist tradition. He framed a systematic theoretical account of the state that remained a basic reference for decades, before the later pure positivism of Hans Kelsen went beyond it and engaged with it critically.

## The Three-Element Theory of the State

In his central work *Allgemeine Staatslehre* (General Theory of the State, 1900), Jellinek established what is known as the **three-element theory** (*Drei-Elemente-Lehre*): the state is a legal entity formed of three indispensable elements, namely territory (*Staatsgebiet*), people (*Staatsvolk*), and organized political authority (*Staatsgewalt*). This analytical definition, which separates the state from any particular moral or teleological content, became a normative basis repeatedly used in public international law to determine the conditions for the existence of a state. Similar criteria appear in the Montevideo Convention of 1933 on the Rights and Duties of States.

## Methodological Duality: The State Between Social Fact and Legal Concept

Jellinek distinguishes between two complementary approaches to studying the state. The **social approach** (*Soziale Staatslehre*) examines the state as a real social and political phenomenon. The **legal approach** (*Rechtliche Staatslehre*) treats it as a **juristic person** (*juristische Person*) bearing rights and obligations within the legal system. This methodological duality, known as the **two-sides theory** (*Zwei-Seiten-Theorie*), allowed Jellinek to avoid the pure positivist reduction that Kelsen later developed, in which the state dissolves entirely into the system of legal norms with no social remainder, while retaining strict legal analytical precision.

## The Normative Force of the Factual

One of Jellinek's influential theses is the idea of the **normative force of the factual** (*Normative Kraft des Faktischen*). When a particular factual situation becomes stable over time (such as a political regime arising from a revolution or a coup, or a repeated administrative practice), it gradually acquires, through its continued and actual stability, a normative force that leads actors to treat it as a binding fact, regardless of its original legitimacy. This idea influenced debates in international law on the recognition of de facto governments, and in constitutional legitimacy when political systems change.

## The Legal Personality of the State and General Public Rights

Jellinek also developed an influential theory of **subjective public rights** (*subjektive öffentliche Rechte*). He classified the individual's relationship with the state into four legal statuses: the passive status, the active status, the negative (liberty) status, and the positive status. This classification influenced the development of the theory of fundamental constitutional rights in German and European law, and the later debate over the nature of the rights an individual holds against the state as a general legal person with a single will independent of the individuals who govern it.

## Impact and Criticism

Jellinek's theory of the state formed the foundation for a whole generation of German and European public-law scholars before it was challenged by pure Kelsenian positivism, which rejected Jellinek's methodological duality as an inconsistent mixture of the descriptive social method and the normative legal method. Nevertheless, the three-element theory remained a practical standard for determining the existence of states in contemporary international law, including in debates over the recognition of newly emerging political entities.

## Intellectual Background: Between Vienna and Heidelberg

Jellinek was born in Leipzig in 1851, the son of the Vienna rabbi Adolf Jellinek, and grew up in Vienna. He studied law and philosophy at Vienna, Heidelberg and Leipzig, taught at Vienna from 1879 and at Basel from 1889, and became professor at Heidelberg in 1891, where he died in 1911. His work stands between legal doctrine and the social sciences emerging in Germany at the end of the nineteenth century. This intermediate intellectual position, between pure legal philosophy and political sociology, enabled him to develop his dual method, the "two-sides theory," which refuses to reduce the state to either perspective alone. Later students, by contrast, tried to settle the tension in favor of one side with greater rigor.

## Constitutional Change and Constitutional Mutation

In *Verfassungsänderung und Verfassungswandlung* (1906) Jellinek distinguished formal amendment of a constitution's text from informal constitutional transformation (*Verfassungswandlung*) brought about by practice, convention or interpretation without any change to the text. This is the context in which his thesis on the normative force of the factual is often discussed.

## Relation to Kelsen

Kelsen criticized Jellinek's two-sides theory, most directly in *Der soziologische und der juristische Staatsbegriff* (1922), arguing that a legal science cannot combine a sociological and a juristic concept of the state and that the state, juristically considered, is identical with the legal order.

## His Role in the Emergence of Constitutional Law as an Independent Discipline

Jellinek is credited with a major role in separating the general **theory of the state** (*Allgemeine Staatslehre*) from both abstract political philosophy and narrow positive constitutional law that merely interprets the text of a single constitution. He sought to build a general theoretical framework applicable to analyzing any state in any political system, independent of the peculiarities of one national constitution. This made his book a reference taught in theory-of-the-state curricula across continental Europe, regardless of the differing national constitutional systems of its students.
