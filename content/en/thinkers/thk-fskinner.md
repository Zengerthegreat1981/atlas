# B. F. Skinner

An American psychologist, founder of "radical behaviorism" (Radical Behaviorism), and father of "operant conditioning" (Operant Conditioning) and the "experimental analysis of behavior" (EAB).

## What He Contributed

In *The Behavior of Organisms* (1938) he formulated "operant conditioning": "operant" behavior (which operates on the environment) is shaped by its consequences: positive/negative reinforcement, punishment. He also built the "Skinner Box" (1930s) to study rats and pigeons in controlled environments. With C. Ferster, he worked out the "schedules of reinforcement" (Schedules, 1957). He also carried out major applied projects: the "Teaching Machine" (1950s), "Programmed Instruction," and *Walden Two* (1948), an imaginary society built on behavioral engineering. He formulated "radical behaviorism" (1945): all "behavior" (public and private) is subject to scientific laws, including "private behavior" (thoughts, feelings).

## His Place Within the Current

The founder of behaviorism in its radical form, and the source of two distinct branches that came out of him: the "experimental analysis of behavior" (EAB) in the laboratory, and "applied behavior analysis" (ABA) in the clinic and the school. He departs from Watson on a fundamental point: he did not deny the existence of thoughts and feelings, but counted them as private behavior subject to the same laws, which is why he called his doctrine "radical," to distinguish it from methodological behaviorism, which excludes the inner from study altogether.

## Limitation
The greatest scientific objection raised against him is Noam Chomsky's critique of his book *Verbal Behavior* (1959 review): that reinforcement and shaping do not explain the child's ability to produce sentences it has never heard, and that the vocabulary of behaviorism ("stimulus," "response") loses all specificity when transferred to language. That review is regarded as one of the most influential texts in the field's shift toward cognitivism.

There is also a common myth that trails his name: that he raised his daughter Deborah in a "Skinner Box" and she became psychotic and killed herself. None of that is true. The "Air Crib" was a temperature-controlled crib, not an experimental apparatus, and Deborah Skinner Buzan herself responded publicly to the story while alive, working as an artist in London. The conflation between her and "Little Albert" in Watson's experiment is groundless.

## His Most Important Works

*The Behavior of Organisms* (1938) · *Walden Two* (1948) · *Science and Human Behavior* (1953) · *Schedules of Reinforcement* (1957, with Charles Ferster) · *Verbal Behavior* (1957) · *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* (1971) · his autobiography in three volumes (1976–1983).

## Biography and Course of Life

Skinner was born in 1904 in the state of Pennsylvania, studied English literature at Hamilton College and tried writing before moving to psychology at Harvard, where he earned his doctorate in 1931. He then worked at the University of Minnesota and Indiana University, and returned to Harvard in 1948 and remained there until his retirement in the mid-1970s. He died in 1990. During the years of the Second World War he carried out a project to train pigeons to guide missiles; the project was never used in practice, but it revealed his belief that complex behavior can be built step by step through reinforcement. His literary background contributed to his evangelizing tone: he wrote the novel *Walden Two* and then books in readable language for the educated public.

## The Three-Term Contingency and Shaping

What he offered can be summarized in the analysis of what he calls the "three-term contingency": a discriminative stimulus that precedes the response, a response that operates on the environment, and a consequence that follows it and modifies the probability of its recurrence. He does not view reinforcement as an intrinsic reward but as a functional relation: anything that increases the probability of behavior is called a reinforcer. From this framework came "shaping" (shaping), that is, reinforcing successive approximations of the desired behavior until a behavior that did not previously exist is formed. Among his most prominent discoveries is the effect of schedules of reinforcement: the irregular intermittent schedule produces behavior that is steadier and more resistant to extinction than continuous reinforcement, an important finding for understanding persistence and even gambling addiction.

## The 1945 Article and the Definition of the Inner

He formulated his philosophical position in his article "The Operational Analysis of Psychological Terms" (1945), in which he refused to place the inner outside science, but also refused to make it a cause: feelings and thoughts are behavior that occurs under the skin and in turn needs to be explained by a history of reinforcement, and must not be used as an explanation of overt behavior. He therefore counted expressions such as "drive" and "intention" as names for what needs explaining, not for what explains. In his article "Selection by Consequences" (1981) he held that behavior selects as nature selects species, and that culture itself selects its practices by their consequences, thus extending the logic of reinforcement to the level of society.

## The Applied Legacy

Behavior analysis moved from Skinner's laboratory to several applied fields. In education he produced teaching machines and programmed instruction, ideas that recurred later in educational software. In therapy, applied behavior analysis led to programs for training children with autism, the most famous of whose results is Lovaas's 1987 study, which later aroused sharp controversy over its criteria and over the nature of its goals. Some of his principles moved into classroom management, organizational work, and addiction treatment, and "contingency management" became one of the strongest evidence-supported interventions for substance use disorders.

## Additional Limits and Criticism

In addition to Chomsky's review, an empirical critique appeared from within the tradition itself: Keller and Marian Breland in 1961, in the article "The Misbehavior of Organisms," showed that animals revert to instinctive behaviors that conflict with what is required of them despite reinforcement, and research by Garcia and Koelling on taste aversion showed that association is not equal between every stimulus and every consequence. On the ethical level, the book *Beyond Freedom and Dignity* provoked objection to the call for engineering social behavior, and to Skinner's skepticism toward the concepts of will and responsibility. His response was that control exists in all cases, and that the question is who exercises it and to what end.
