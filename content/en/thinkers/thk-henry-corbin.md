# Henry Corbin

A French philosopher and orientalist (1903–1978). He was an early translator of Martin Heidegger into French (notably *Qu'est-ce que la métaphysique?*, 1938). His academic path then turned entirely toward Iranian Islamic philosophy under the influence of his teacher Louis Massignon (thk-louis-massignon), who introduced him to Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi (thk-sohrevardi) in Paris in the late 1920s, giving him a lithographed edition of his principal work, *Hikmat al-Ishraq*. Corbin later pursued the study of Suhrawardi manuscripts during his stay at the French Institute in Istanbul (1939–1945). In 1945 he moved to Tehran, where he founded the Department of Iranology of the Franco-Iranian Institute (1946). In 1954 he succeeded Massignon as director of studies for Islam and the religions of the Arab lands at the École pratique des hautes études in Paris.

## The Academic Project: "Iranian Islam"

Corbin devoted his vast academic project, *En Islam iranien: aspects spirituels et philosophiques* (wrk-en-islam-iranien-corbin, four volumes, 1971–1972), to reconstructing the history of late Iranian Islamic philosophy: the Illuminationism of Suhrawardi, Ibn Arabi, and Imami Shi'ism's esoteric tradition, leading to the Transcendent Wisdom of Mulla Sadra (sch-transcendent-theosophy). He presented these as a living, continuous philosophical and spiritual tradition rather than a merely historical subject that had ended. This contradicted the orientalist narrative of his time, which regarded Islamic philosophy as having ended with the death of Ibn Rushd (Averroes).

## Method: Symbolic Phenomenology and the "Imaginal World"

Corbin developed the concept of the *mundus imaginalis* (the imaginal world) to describe an intermediate realm between the sensible and the intelligible, in which spiritual visions move according to the Illuminationists and Ibn Arabi. He borrowed tools from Heidegger's phenomenology and from Jungian analytical psychology (the archetypes) to interpret this tradition. This methodological stance approaches, as with Schimmel (thk-annemarie-schimmel) and Massignon before him, a deep hermeneutic sympathy rather than strict academic descriptive neutrality. It has been criticised, for example in Steven Wasserstrom's *Religion after Religion* (1999), which places Corbin alongside Eliade and Scholem in the Eranos circle, as projecting modern Western frameworks onto classical Islamic texts. Corbin and Eliade both took part in the Eranos conferences and shared an interest in imagination and symbol, though their methods differ.

## From Heidegger to Suhrawardi

Corbin began his intellectual career as a translator of Martin Heidegger into French in the 1930s, which gave him precise phenomenological tools that he would later apply to reading Islamic texts. However, his encounter with the manuscripts of Suhrawardi, through the guidance of Louis Massignon, transformed his path entirely. He published the first critical edition of Suhrawardi's philosophical texts (*Opera Metaphysica et Mystica*, vol. 1, 1945), thereby beginning his lifelong project of reviving Illuminationism as a contemporary academic field, after centuries of relative neglect in European orientalism. European orientalism had traditionally focused on the Peripatetic philosophy (Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd).

## "The Imaginal World" and Ibn Arabi

Corbin applied his central concept, the "imaginal world" (*mundus imaginalis*), in particular in his study of Ibn Arabi, *Creative Imagination in the Sufism of Ibn 'Arabi* (French version 1958). There he read Ibn Arabi's theory of "imagination" (the absolute imagination in which the divine realities appear in symbolic images) as an accurate description of a real intermediate ontological world, rather than a mere poetic metaphor or a subjective psychological faculty. Corbin regarded this "imaginal world" as the key to understanding the spiritual experience of the Sufis and the Illuminationists, going beyond the modern rationalist reduction that confines reality to the sensible and the intelligible alone.

## Standing and Reception

Corbin founded the series "Bibliothèque iranienne" (with the Franco-Iranian Institute) for the critical editing of Iranian-Islamic philosophical texts. He had a significant influence on the field of French and international Iranian and Islamic studies. His method, however, remains a subject of continuing debate between those who regard it as a fruitful hermeneutic recovery of a neglected tradition and those who see in it a modern Western projection (Jungian and Heideggerian) onto classical Islamic texts with their own particular historical and theological context.

## Deep Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Corbin is notable among twentieth-century orientalists for the depth of his actual engagement with the original Arabic and Persian Islamic texts, rather than with intermediary translations alone. He himself edited previously unpublished Arabic and Persian philosophical texts (such as works by Suhrawardi and Mulla Sadra Shirazi). In doing so he established a continuing French research tradition in Iranian Islamic philosophy, which has continued through his students and successors at the École pratique des hautes études. Together with Louis Massignon, Corbin is considered an important French academic bridge between the Islamic philosophical and Sufi heritage and modern European thought in the twentieth century.

## Institutional Legacy

The "Bibliothèque iranienne" contributed to publishing many critical editions of Persian and Arabic texts, which gives Corbin's influence an institutional dimension beyond his own interpretive writing.

## His Final Years

From 1949 Corbin was a regular participant in the Eranos conferences at Ascona, and in later years he helped found the Université Saint-Jean de Jérusalem, an intellectual forum. He died in Paris in 1978.
