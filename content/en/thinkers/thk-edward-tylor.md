# Edward Burnett Tylor

An English anthropologist considered one of the founders of academic cultural anthropology in Britain. He was knighted in 1912 and held the first chair of anthropology at the University of Oxford (1896). In his book *Primitive Culture* (1871), he offered one of the earliest systematic anthropological definitions of the concept of "culture": "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society."

## Central Idea

Tylor adopted a unilinear evolutionary model, which holds that all human societies pass through successive stages from "savagery" to "barbarism" and then "civilization." The differences between contemporary cultures, in his view, reflect different positions on the same evolutionary scale rather than separate systems. He developed the concept of "survivals": customs and beliefs that persist in a later society after losing their original function, serving as a fossil-like trace of an earlier evolutionary stage. He also proposed a theory of the origin of religion known as "animism," the belief in spiritual beings, which he regarded as the simplest and oldest form of religion, arising from humanity's attempts to explain phenomena such as dreams and death.

## Impact and Critique

Tylor's unilinear evolutionary model was sharply criticized by the next generation of anthropologists (especially Franz Boas and his students), who accused it of Eurocentrism and of ranking non-European peoples in lower tiers on the basis of superficial comparisons rather than fieldwork. Nevertheless, his definition of culture remains one of the most influential definitions in the history of the discipline.

## His Statistical Comparative Method

In his well-known article "On a Method of Investigating the Development of Institutions" (1889), Tylor devised an early statistical method known as "adhesions" for studying how particular social institutions recur together statistically across a broad sample of societies around the world (for example, the association between post-marital residence customs and avoidance practices such as mother-in-law avoidance). This was a pioneering attempt to subject anthropological hypotheses to systematic quantitative testing, and it is regarded as one of the earliest applications of comparative statistics across cultures in the social sciences.

## Animism and the Origin of Religion

In his theory of "animism," Tylor argued that the idea of the soul arose from early humans' attempts to explain phenomena such as dreams, unconsciousness, and death. From these experiences he inferred the existence of a "soul" or "spirit" separate from the body, which could leave it temporarily (as in dreams) or permanently (as in death). He held that this basic idea gradually developed into more complex beliefs about spirits and gods throughout human history, in a linear evolutionary model of religion that later influenced James Frazer.

## Institutional Position at Oxford

As the first holder of an academic chair in anthropology in Britain, Tylor helped establish anthropology as an independent university discipline with its own institutional standing, moving beyond its earlier status as a pastime for collectors of artifacts and travelers. He was keeper of the Oxford University Museum from 1883 and was associated with the university's anthropological collections, which became the Pitt Rivers Museum, one of the most important ethnographic museums in the world.

## Quaker Upbringing and Its Influence

Tylor was born in London in 1832 into a Quaker family. Because of the restrictions imposed on non-Anglicans in his era, he did not attend a conventional university, and he worked in the family business before travelling. A journey to Cuba and Mexico in 1855–1856, partly in the company of Henry Christy, was a turning point in his interest in non-European cultures and led to his book *Anahuac* (1861). This unconventional path reflects how early academic anthropology emerged from amateurs and travelers before becoming a formal university discipline, partly through his own later efforts.

## Role in Founding Learned Societies

Tylor was an active member of the Anthropological Institute of Great Britain and Ireland and served as its president. He also took part in the committee of the British Association for the Advancement of Science that produced *Notes and Queries on Anthropology* (first edition 1874), a guide intended to standardize how travelers, missionaries, and colonial administrators recorded ethnographic information.

## Influence on the Comparative Study of Religion

Tylor's evolutionary model paved the way for a generation of comparative religion scholars, foremost among them Sir James Frazer in *The Golden Bough*, to apply a similar method in tracing the "evolution" of religious rites and beliefs across multiple cultures. The entire evolutionary trend was later subjected to a radical critical review by functionalist anthropology and cultural relativism in the twentieth century.

## Conclusion

Tylor remains a founding father of British academic cultural anthropology. His famous definition of culture is still a starting point for every contemporary academic discussion of this central concept, even after later anthropology moved beyond his unilinear evolutionary model through a decisive cultural-relativist critique.
