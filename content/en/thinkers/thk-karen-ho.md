# Karen Ho

Karen Ho is an American anthropologist best known for turning ethnography on Wall Street itself. In her study *Liquidated: An Ethnography of Wall Street* (2009), she showed how the everyday culture of investment bankers — their insecurity, their prestige hierarchies, and their faith in shareholder value — helped produce the financial crisis of 2008. Her work helped bring the anthropology of finance to prominence and showed that ethnography could explain global economic events by studying the people at the top rather than only those affected by their decisions.

## Life and Career

Ho studied at Stanford University and then entered the doctoral programme in anthropology at Princeton University.

While a graduate student at Princeton in the mid-1990s, Ho became fascinated by the wave of corporate downsizing then sweeping the United States, above all the mass layoffs at large firms such as AT&T. Rather than studying the laid-off workers, she decided to study the people doing the laying off. She interviewed for jobs with the banks recruiting on Princeton's campus and took a leave from her studies in 1996 to work as an internal management consultant at Bankers Trust, later part of Deutsche Bank. She stayed for about a year, until her own work group was downsized — an experience that gave her first-hand knowledge of the insecurity she would later analyse. Returning to Princeton, she conducted extensive interviews with Wall Street employees and observed bankers at work and at industry gatherings, and completed her doctorate in 2003 with a dissertation on Wall Street.

Ho is on the faculty of the University of Minnesota, where she teaches anthropology.

## Key Works

Ho's central work is *Liquidated: An Ethnography of Wall Street* (2009), published by Duke University Press just after the global financial crisis, which gave it an unusually wide public audience. The book grew out of her doctoral research at Princeton. An earlier article, "Situating Global Capitalisms: A View from Wall Street Investment Banks" (*Cultural Anthropology*, 2005), laid out the core argument, and "Disciplining Investment Bankers, Disciplining the Economy: Wall Street's Institutional Culture of Crisis and the Downsizing of Corporate America" (2009) extended it to the history of corporate restructuring.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Ho's most influential concept is the "culture of liquidity": the idea that investment banks are organised around constant restructuring, frequent lay-offs, and the treatment of firms — including their own — as assets to be bought, sold, and stripped down. Bankers, she argues, experience this insecurity as a productive challenge and a mark of elite character, because their own wealth cushions them from its worst effects. They then generalise that experience into a model of how the whole economy should be run, presenting downsizing and shareholder value as efficient and inevitable.

This argument reverses the usual direction of the anthropology of capitalism. Instead of studying only the poor and the marginalised, Ho practised what anthropologists call "studying up": ethnography of powerful institutions and elites, conducted from inside. Her insider position — a former Bankers Trust employee who knew the language, the recruiting rituals, and the social codes of the banks — gave her access to a famously secretive world, while her critical distance allowed her to show how bankers' self-understanding shaped corporate America far beyond Wall Street.

Ho also argued that the culture of liquidity helps explain the financial crisis. Because bankers are rewarded for short-term deals and move frequently between firms, they have little incentive to care about the long-term health of the companies they restructure. The crisis, in her account, was not an accident or a technical failure but the predictable outcome of an institutional culture that treats instability as a virtue.

## Influence and Critique

*Liquidated* is a prominent work in the anthropology of financial markets, alongside other ethnographies of traders and trading floors. It is widely taught in courses on economic anthropology and the sociology of finance, and frequently cited in public debate about the causes of the 2008 crisis. Ho's work also connects the anthropology of finance to feminist theory, notably through the "Gens" manifesto, co-authored with Laura Bear, Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing, and Sylvia Yanagisako (2015), which argues that capitalism is generated through gender, race, kinship, and sexuality rather than standing apart from them.

The book has been praised above all for its methodological daring: gaining sustained access to one of the most closed institutions in American life and turning that access into rigorous analysis. Some colleagues have noted that her fieldwork sample was necessarily limited in time and in the range of firms she could observe, given the secrecy of the setting, and that a study centred on elite bankers can say less about the workers and communities on the receiving end of restructuring. Her position as a former insider also raises the question, familiar from reflexive ethnography, of the ethnographer's distance from a world she once inhabited.
