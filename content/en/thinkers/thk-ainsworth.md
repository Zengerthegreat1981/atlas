# Mary Ainsworth

An American psychologist, and a leading figure in giving attachment theory its empirical, measurement-based foundation. **The theory itself is John Bowlby's**, formulated in his trilogy *Attachment and Loss*; Ainsworth's distinctive share is **two longitudinal field studies**, **a laboratory measurement procedure**, and **a classification** by which differences among children are measured.

## What She Contributed

### The Ganda Study (1954–1955)

After working with Bowlby's research group at the Tavistock Clinic in London from 1950, Ainsworth moved to Uganda, where she observed a small sample of mothers and infants from the Ganda people in villages near Kampala. She published the results in ***Infancy in Uganda: Infant Care and the Growth of Love* (1967)**, in which she linked **the mother's sensitivity to her child's signals** with the child's behavior at separation and reunion, the independent variable in all her later work.

### The Baltimore Study (1963–1967)

A longitudinal study using home observation in Baltimore, with extended visits during the child's first year of life, again on a small sample of about two dozen families. From this same sample came the data on which the classification was built, published together with the laboratory procedure in *Patterns of Attachment* (1978).

### The Strange Situation Procedure (1969–1970)

A laboratory protocol of eight brief scenes (about twenty minutes), presented first in **Ainsworth & Wittig (1969)** and then in **Ainsworth & Bell, *Child Development* 41 (1970)**: mother and child in an unfamiliar room, then a stranger enters, then **two separations** and **two reunions**. The criterion on which she built the classification is not the child's crying at separation but **its behavior at reunion**.

### The Three-Way Classification

- **B (secure)**: seeks the mother on her return, accepts her soothing, returns to exploration.
- **A (avoidant)**: does not seek her on return, acts as if she were not there.
- **C (resistant/anxious)**: seeks her and then refuses soothing, both afraid and angry.

### The Fourth Pattern Is Not Hers

**Pattern D (disorganized)** was added by **Mary Main** with **Judith Solomon** in **1986**, after Ainsworth and outside her three-way classification. The extension of the model to romantic relationships among adults was the work of **Cindy Hazan and Phillip Shaver (1987)**, without her participation.

## Her Place in the Current

- **With Bowlby**: not a disciple who transmits, but the measurement partner in a partnership: Bowlby supplied the theoretical framework, and she supplied the instrument, the sample, and the classification.
- **Versus Harlow**: Harlow worked on monkeys and Ainsworth on humans; both put emotional contact, not feeding, at the center of the bond.
- **Versus Piaget**: Piaget worked on cognitive structure, and she on the emotional bond in the first year.

## Her Most Important Works

- ***Infancy in Uganda: Infant Care and the Growth of Love*** (1967).
- **"Attachment, exploration, and separation"** with Silvia Bell, *Child Development* 41 (1970).
- ***Patterns of Attachment: A Psychological Study of the Strange Situation*** (1978), co-authored with **Blehar, Waters, and Wall**.
- The chapter **"Attachments and other affectional bonds across the life cycle"** in *Attachment Across the Life Cycle* (1991), edited by Parkes, Stevenson-Hinde, and Marris.

## Limitations

The first limitation is that what the procedure measures is **a twenty-minute laboratory episode** in an unfamiliar room, not the bond itself or its course. The second is the cross-cultural debate over the validity of the classification outside American samples: a German sample in Bielefeld (Grossmann and colleagues, 1985) produced a much higher proportion of avoidance than the American distribution, and a Japanese sample in Sapporo (Miyake, Chen, and Campos, 1985) produced a higher proportion of resistance. The open question is whether this is a difference in attachment or in the meaning of the laboratory separation itself. The meta-analysis that pooled thirty-two samples in eight countries (van IJzendoorn and Kroonenberg, 1988) found that variation **within** a single country was greater than variation between countries.
