# Walter Eucken

**Walter Eucken** was a German economist and, with the lawyer Franz Böhm, a principal figure of the Freiburg School and of **ordoliberalism**. He developed a theoretical framework that combines the freedom of competitive markets with the necessity of a legal and institutional framework designed carefully by the state, which protects the structure of competition from internal erosion through the formation of private cartels and monopolies. In this way he distinguished himself from both classical laissez-faire liberalism and centrally planned socialism.

## Two failed experiences that drove theorizing

Eucken, a professor at the University of Freiburg from 1927, lived through two German experiences that were his direct intellectual motivation: the formation of huge industrial cartels in Germany in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, under the cover of a formal economic liberalism that allowed genuine competition to be undermined from within; and then the totalitarian economic planning of Nazi Germany, whose rise Eucken witnessed as a critic. He remained at Freiburg and took part in the Freiburg discussion circles of dissenting academics, working within the constraints of academic life under an authoritarian regime.

## The founding principles of the economic order

In his principal work, *Die Grundlagen der Nationalökonomie* (1940), and the posthumously published *Grundsätze der Wirtschaftspolitik* (1952), Eucken set out "constituent principles" that he held a competitive order requires. The central one is a functioning price system under competition. With it he listed stability of the currency; open markets (freedom of entry); private property; freedom of contract; liability, meaning that decision-makers bear the losses of their choices; and a constancy of economic policy sufficient for private planning. He treated anti-monopoly policy as a separate "regulating" principle needed because competition does not maintain itself.

## Criticism of the old formal liberalism

Eucken explicitly distinguished his analysis from the old German economic liberalism, which had been content to demand that the state remain neutral and refrain from intervention in a formal sense, without sufficient attention to the fact that this formal neutrality could leave the field open to large private economic forces to undermine real competition through monopoly agreements. He insisted that the state must play an active and continuous role in protecting the competitive "order" (*Ordo*) itself, not merely standing passively neutral toward it.

## The Freiburg circle as an intellectual school

Together with lawyers he worked with, such as Franz Böhm, Eucken formed a research circle known as the "Freiburg School", which combined economics with constitutional law in an interdisciplinary approach that was unusual at the time. It held that sound economic policy cannot be separated from a carefully designed constitutional legal framework that protects the structure of competition over the long term, rather than being separate technical economic policies detached from the state's general legal framework.

## Early death before the full effect of his work

Eucken died in London on 20 March 1950, while lecturing at the London School of Economics, less than two years after the West German currency reform of June 1948. Ludwig Erhard directed that reform and the move to a market economy in the Bizone. Ordoliberal ideas were one source of what Erhard and Alfred Müller-Armack called the social market economy. Eucken did not live to see the rapid West German growth of the 1950s.

## Lasting institutional influence

Eucken's ideas left a direct institutional imprint in the German Act against Restraints of Competition (passed in 1957 and in force from 1958), which led to the creation of the Federal Cartel Office, and he is often cited as an influence on later European competition policy. The Walter Eucken Institut, a research institute bearing his name, was established in Freiburg in 1954 and has continued developing the ordoliberal tradition for decades after his death.

## A distinction from deeper Austrian skepticism

Historians of economic thought distinguish Eucken's position from that of his colleagues in the Mont Pèlerin Society who belonged to the Austrian tradition (Hayek and Mises). While Eucken shared the general diagnosis of the danger of totalitarian central planning, he remained more confident than the Austrians that active and continuous government intervention is needed to protect the structure of competition itself. He regarded reliance on spontaneous competition alone as insufficient against monopoly power unless supported by a strict legal framework.

## A method combining theory and history

Methodologically, Eucken tried to combine abstract theoretical analysis with comparative historical observation of actual economic systems. He rejected both pure mathematical abstraction detached from institutional reality and descriptive history lacking an organized analytical framework, in an attempt to build a method for the comparative study of economic systems.

## Influence on the design of the eurozone

Ordoliberal arguments about currency stability and fiscal discipline were revisited in the debates surrounding the euro crisis after 2010. Some observers saw the strict German policy toward member states burdened by debt as reflecting the continuing influence of the original ordoliberal logic, which ties long-term economic stability to strict fiscal discipline and full responsibility for each party's commitments, rather than to repeated rescue interventions that might distort future incentives for fiscal discipline.

## A legacy beyond German borders

Eucken's influence went beyond the narrow German context to become a reference in the design of competition policy across several European economies, and ordoliberal ideas are often said to have informed European Union rules on competition and state aid, though how direct this influence was is debated.
