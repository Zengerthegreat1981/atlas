# Paul Farmer

An American physician and medical anthropologist (1959–2022), one of the most prominent founders of contemporary **critical medical anthropology**, and the leading popularizer, in medicine and public health, of the concept of **structural violence**, which became a central analytical tool in studies of global health, poverty and disease. Born in Massachusetts, he grew up in relative poverty in Florida (his family lived for a time in a converted bus and later on a boat), then studied medical anthropology at Duke University, and earned both a PhD in anthropology and an MD from Harvard. He died in Rwanda in February 2022, reportedly of an acute cardiac event.

## Contributions

### The Concept of Structural Violence

Drawing on his long fieldwork in Haiti, Farmer adapted and developed the concept of **structural violence** (coined by Johan Galtung): the harm inflicted on individuals by **social, economic and political structures** that are not directly visible, such as poverty, racism and the unequal distribution of health resources, rather than a direct act of individual violence. He argued that epidemics (tuberculosis, AIDS) in poor societies are not "biological bad luck" but the direct product of these structures, and that ignoring this dimension in global health policy reproduces inequality instead of addressing it. He developed this framework in detail in *Infections and Inequalities: The Modern Plagues* (1999) and *Pathologies of Power* (2003).

### Partners In Health

Together with Ophelia Dahl, Jim Yong Kim, Todd McCormack and Thomas J. White, he co-founded **Partners In Health** in 1987, building on the clinic Zanmi Lasante that he had helped establish in Cange, Haiti, in the mid-1980s. Through it he applied a practical model based on providing free and comprehensive health care to the poor, including combined treatment of drug-resistant tuberculosis and AIDS in poor communities, in opposition to the prevailing view at the time that such treatment was "economically unworkable" in poor countries. His experience challenged that assumption, and it later informed World Health Organization policy.

### *AIDS and Accusation* (1992)

In his first ethnographic book, a study of a Haitian village, Farmer analysed how epidemics are interpreted locally through cultural frameworks (sorcery, mutual accusation between neighbours), which are themselves shaped by the history of colonialism and structural poverty, linking the local cultural explanation to the macro political-economic analysis.

## Impact

- The concept of structural violence reshaped medical anthropology and global public health, and became a standard reference in studies of the "social determinants of health".
- The experience of Partners In Health demonstrated in practice that complex chronic diseases can be treated in the poorest settings, and is often cited as an influence on the global-health funding debates of the 2000s, including around the Global Fund to Fight AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria.
- Journalist Tracy Kidder's book *Mountains Beyond Mountains* (2003) gave wide coverage to his work and made it known to a broad public.

## Limitations

- Some health economists criticised Farmer's model as costly and difficult to scale beyond small projects funded generously, compared with national public health systems.
- Critics from within anthropology have sometimes argued that the combination of his roles (physician, researcher and executive director of a major organisation) makes it difficult to separate academic analysis from political advocacy.

## Intellectual Background: From Galtung to Liberation Theology

Farmer borrowed the term structural violence from the Norwegian peace researcher Johan Galtung in his article "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research" (*Journal of Peace Research*, 1969), but filled it with clinical and ethnographic content. His work was influenced by Latin American liberation theology and by Gustavo Gutiérrez's principle of the preferential option for the poor, and he formulated a conception of medicine as a commitment to those abandoned by systems. This is consistent with what he set out in *The Uses of Haiti* (Common Courage Press, 1994), where he argued that the Western view of epidemics in Haiti ignores the history of colonialism, debt and American intervention.

## Drug-Resistant Tuberculosis and Community-Based Treatment

In the Cange project, and with the participation of physicians such as Jim Yong Kim and Salmaan Keshavjee, Partners In Health developed a model that relies on community health workers who visit patients in their homes to monitor the taking of medication, with nutritional support and transport. This model was applied in Lima, Peru, from the mid-1990s, where multidrug-resistant tuberculosis was treated with good cure rates, despite objections from some global health experts about its cost. Following this, the World Health Organization revised its guidance on the management of drug-resistant tuberculosis in low-income settings. Farmer set out the theoretical basis of this work in his article "Structural Violence and Clinical Medicine" (*PLoS Medicine*, 2006), written with colleagues, in which he argues that a physician cannot separate diagnosis from the conditions that produce the disease.

## Recent Works

He wrote *Haiti After the Earthquake* (PublicAffairs, 2011), about the 2010 earthquake and the reconstruction efforts, and *Fevers, Feuds, and Diamonds: Ebola and the Ravages of History* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2020), about the Ebola epidemic in West Africa and its roots in the history of colonialism and weak health institutions. He collaborated with Jim Kim and Arthur Kleinman on *Reimagining Global Health* (University of California Press, 2013).

## Questions of Equity

Debate has arisen over whether the model of exceptional funding can be available to everyone, and over the effect on the independence of local health systems of an organisation that depends on donors from abroad. Critics in Haiti have accused some international relief projects, though not necessarily Farmer himself, of weakening state institutions by creating parallel services. This is a question that remains open.
