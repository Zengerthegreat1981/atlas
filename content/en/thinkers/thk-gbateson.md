# Gregory Bateson

Gregory Bateson (1904–1980) was a British-born anthropologist, social scientist, linguist, and cybernetician whose work crossed anthropology, psychology, communication theory, and ecology. The son of the geneticist William Bateson, he began as an ethnographer of ritual and social structure in New Guinea and Bali and ended as one of the founders of systems thinking, best known for the double-bind theory of schizophrenia, for his account of schismogenesis, and for his later synthesis of mind and nature in *Steps to an Ecology of Mind* (1972) and *Mind and Nature* (1979). Few twentieth-century anthropologists have been cited so widely outside their own discipline, by family therapists, communication theorists, ecologists, and philosophers alike.

## Life and Career

Bateson was born on 9 May 1904 in Grantchester, near Cambridge, England, the youngest son of William Bateson and Caroline Beatrice Durham. He studied zoology at St John's College, Cambridge, before turning to anthropology under A. C. Haddon, and he carried out fieldwork among the Iatmul people of the Sepik River in New Guinea in the late 1920s and early 1930s. His first major book, *Naven* (1936), was an ethnography of a ritual among the Iatmul in which men dressed as women and women as men. Relatives, notably a mother's brother, mark a young person's achievements through that reversal of gendered dress and conduct. Bateson read the rite through social structure, through emotional style, which he called ethos, and through cultural logic, which he called eidos, and the book openly questions the standing of its own categories. It already displayed the distinctive Batesonian move: treating a ceremony not as an isolated custom but as a window onto the whole pattern of social relations.

In 1936 he married the anthropologist Margaret Mead, and the two worked together in Bali, where they pioneered the use of photography and film in ethnography, publishing *Balinese Character* (1942). They recorded posture, play, teaching, trance, and parent–child interaction, and arranged photographs so that the images carried part of the argument. Later readers have questioned the book's broad psychological generalisations about Balinese character. After the Second World War, during which he worked for the Office of Strategic Services in South-East Asia, Bateson settled in the United States. He never held a conventional senior university chair for long; instead he worked at the Veterans Administration Hospital in Palo Alto, at Stanford, at the Oceanic Institute in Hawaii, and finally at the University of California, Santa Cruz, where he taught in the 1970s. He died in San Francisco on 4 July 1980. His career was deliberately peripatetic, moving between anthropology, psychiatry, and the emerging science of cybernetics, and he was a central figure at the Macy Conferences on cybernetics in the late 1940s and early 1950s.

## Key Works

- *Naven: A Survey of the Problems Suggested by a Composite Picture of the Culture of a New Guinea Tribe Drawn from Three Points of View* (1936; 2nd edition 1958).
- *Balinese Character: A Photographic Analysis* (1942), with Margaret Mead.
- "A Theory of Play and Fantasy" (1955), the essay that introduced the idea of metacommunication and the framing of messages. It argued that animals and people exchange signals about how other signals should be taken.
- *Communication: The Social Matrix of Psychiatry* (1951), with Jurgen Ruesch.
- "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia" (1956), with Don D. Jackson, Jay Haley, and John H. Weakland, presenting the double-bind hypothesis.
- *Steps to an Ecology of Mind: Collected Essays in Anthropology, Psychiatry, Evolution, and Epistemology* (1972).
- *Mind and Nature: A Necessary Unity* (1979).
- *Angels Fear: Towards an Epistemology of the Sacred* (1987), completed after his death by his daughter Mary Catherine Bateson.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Bateson's most influential anthropological concept was schismogenesis, developed in *Naven*: the idea that social interaction can escalate through feedback, as when boasting provokes more boasting (symmetrical schismogenesis) or dominance provokes submission, which provokes further dominance (complementary schismogenesis). The concept gave anthropology a dynamic, relational vocabulary for conflict and ritual, and it anticipated later systems theory.

His second great contribution came from psychiatry. The double-bind theory proposed that schizophrenia could be understood, at least in part, as a response to repeated communication in which a person receives two contradictory injunctions at different logical levels — for example, a mother who says "come closer" while her body language says "go away" — and is forbidden to comment on the contradiction, inside a relationship the person cannot readily leave. The theory was less a claim about a single cause than a demonstration that mental illness could be analysed as a disorder of communication within a family system, and it helped to found family therapy. Because it placed the pattern in family communication, it could also be used to blame relatives.

Underlying both ideas was Bateson's lifelong concern with what he called the "pattern which connects": the proposition that mind is not confined to the brain but is immanent in systems of interaction, in organisms and ecosystems alike. In *Mind and Nature* he argued that the same formal patterns — difference, feedback, recursion — govern biological evolution and human learning, and he treated information as a difference that produces a further difference in a system. He criticised action that maximises one variable while ignoring the circuit that sustains it. This "ecology of mind" made him an early voice of ecological thinking, arguing that the Cartesian split between mind and nature was itself a dangerous epistemological error.

## Influence and Critique

Bateson's influence has been enormous and diffuse. Family therapists adopted the double bind and the concept of the paradoxical injunction; communication theorists took up his analysis of frames and metacommunication; and ecologists and systems theorists cite his warning that a society which treats nature as a mere resource will destroy the systems on which it depends. The posthumous *Angels Fear*, completed by his daughter Mary Catherine Bateson, extended his ideas to questions of religion and the sacred; her memoir *With a Daughter's Eye* (1984) describes his life and thought alongside Mead's.

Critics have noted the limits of his work. The double-bind theory, influential as a therapeutic metaphor, was never confirmed as an explanation of schizophrenia, and later research emphasised genetic and neurochemical factors. Anthropologists have also observed that *Naven*, for all its brilliance, treated Iatmul society through a formal model that paid limited attention to history and colonial change. Bateson's writing, too, was famously elusive, moving between anecdote, mathematics, and aphorism in ways that some readers found inspiring and others found frustratingly unsystematic. Yet his central insight — that human problems must be understood as patterns of relationship rather than as properties of isolated individuals — has outlasted these criticisms and remains a living presence in anthropology, therapy, and ecological thought.
