# Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi

An American-Hungarian psychologist (1934–2021), one of the founders of positive psychology together with Martin Seligman, and the best-known theorist of the concept of **"flow"**, the optimal state of absorption in an activity. He died on 20 October 2021.

## Biography and Formation

He was born on 29 September 1934 in the city of Fiume (today Rijeka) on the Adriatic coast, to a Hungarian family whose father worked in the diplomatic service. As a child he witnessed the devastation of the Second World War and the collapse of the social order in Central Europe that followed. He used to say that what struck him was that adults whose lives had been stable fell apart quickly when they lost their jobs and status, while some of them kept a measure of calm, and so he asked what makes life worth living. He then emigrated to the United States in 1956 and studied at the University of Chicago, where he received his bachelor's degree in 1960 and his doctorate in psychology in 1965. He worked as a professor at Lake Forest College and then at the University of Chicago, where he chaired the Department of Psychology, before moving in 1999 to Claremont Graduate University in California, where he founded the Quality of Life Research Center and remained for decades.

## Scientific Context

In the mid-twentieth century psychology was preoccupied with disorder or with conditioning, and human intrinsic motivation had little presence in the literature. In his early studies of artists, among them *The Creative Vision* (with Getzels, Wiley, 1976) on students at the Art Institute of Chicago, Csikszentmihalyi found that some of them became absorbed in their work without any external reward. This observation was the starting point for the idea of intrinsic motivation. Maslow's work on "peak experiences" was a close reference, but he saw peak experiences as descriptive and rare, and as needing systematic investigation in daily life.

## What He Contributed

### Flow Theory

Csikszentmihalyi developed the concept of flow to describe the mental state in which a person feels complete absorption in an activity, deep enjoyment, a diminished awareness of the self, a distorted sense of time, and intrinsic motivation (acting for its own sake, not for an external reward). He first presented it in *Beyond Boredom and Anxiety* (Jossey-Bass, 1975) and then to the general public in *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (Harper & Row, 1990).

### Conditions of Flow

The model specifies basic conditions: a clear goal for the action, immediate feedback on performance, and a balance between challenge and skill. If the challenge far exceeds the skill, anxiety results; if it is far below the skill, boredom results; and flow occurs at the balance. This diagram was later revised from three channels (anxiety, boredom, flow) into a more refined model that adds states such as relaxation, control, apathy, and arousal.

### The Autotelic Personality

He coined the concept of the **autotelic personality**, from the Greek *autos* (self) and *telos* (goal): a person who finds enjoyment in the activity itself, chooses activities that challenge his abilities, focuses on the present, and feels in control.

### The Experience Sampling Method

With Larson and Prescott in the late 1970s he devised the Experience Sampling Method (ESM), an approach in which participants are given a pager that sounds at random times during the day, and each one records at the signal what he is doing, with whom, what he feels, and the level of challenge and skill. The early results were presented on adolescents in "The ecology of adolescent activity and experience" (Journal of Youth and Adolescence, 1977), and then in a study with LeFevre on optimal experience in work and leisure (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1989), which found that workers experience flow at work more than in their free time, yet say they would prefer to be at leisure. ESM made flow measurable in daily life after it had been a qualitative description.

### Creativity and Good Work

In *Creativity: Flow and the Psychology of Discovery and Invention* (HarperCollins, 1996) Csikszentmihalyi presented the **systems model** of creativity: that it is not a trait of the individual alone but an interaction among three elements: the person, the domain (rules and practices), and the field (the experts who judge). In *Good Work* (with Howard Gardner and William Damon, Basic Books, 2001) he studied professionals who combine excellence with ethical responsibility, and in *Good Business: Leadership, Flow, and the Making of Meaning* (Viking, 2003) he applied the idea to leadership and organizations.

## His Place in the Field

- **Versus Maslow**: he continued Maslow's interest in peak experiences but gave them an empirical foundation.
- **Versus Seligman**: he focused on happiness in the activity and on momentary experience, while Seligman focused on optimism, meaning, and flourishing. Together they wrote the founding article "Positive psychology: An introduction" in American Psychologist in 2000.
- **Versus psychoanalysis**: he studied health and excellence rather than disorder.

## Impact and Criticism

Flow theory influenced education, game design, work environments, sport, and music. Criticism concerns three matters: that flow is mostly measured by self-report, which is affected by expectation; that its conceptual boundaries are unclear among absorption, enjoyment, concentration, and competence; and that the flow state can accompany harmful activities such as gambling and compulsive gaming, so that it is not sufficient by itself as a criterion of the good. He himself acknowledged that the ethical value of an activity requires another criterion.
