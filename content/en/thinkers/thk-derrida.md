# Jacques Derrida

A French philosopher born in Algeria, founder of the current of Deconstruction and one of the most influential philosophers of the second half of the twentieth century; he re-questioned the history of Western metaphysics and deconstructed the oppositional binaries that found philosophical and linguistic thought.

## His Contribution

- **Deconstruction**: a critical method that unsettles the stability of meaning and of philosophical texts, revealing the contradictions and implicit assumptions on which the traditional hierarchical binaries rest (such as: reason/body, presence/absence, nature/culture).
- **Deferral and difference (Différance)**: he coined the term Différance to signify the double sense of spatial "difference" and temporal "deferral" of meaning, showing that meaning is never present complete at any moment but remains deferred across an infinite network of references.
- **The critique of logocentrism (Logocentrism)**: he criticized Western philosophy's preference for speech and the living voice over writing, and its assumption that direct access to truth is possible without linguistic and semiotic mediation.
- **Questioning psychoanalysis and the archive**: he engaged the legacy of Freud and Lacan in his writings on the unconscious, memory, and the concept of "archive fever" (Mal d'archive), showing that the unconscious functions as a structure of writing and an indelible trace.

## His Place in the Tradition

Derrida represents the cornerstone of the current of deconstruction and poststructuralism; philosophers of ethics and politics, feminist theory and cultural studies have drawn on his method, and some writers in psychology and narrative therapy have drawn on it in criticising closed concepts of personality and dominant narratives.

## Limit

Derrida's thought stands at the heart of critical continental philosophy and the philosophy of language; his texts are extremely complex and terminologically abstract, and his influence on psychology is mediating and conceptual, not that of a direct clinical practitioner.

## Biography

Jacques Derrida was born on 15 July 1930 in El Biar, Algiers, to an Algerian Jewish family, and lived his childhood under the Vichy laws that expelled him from school for a short time in 1942, an experience that returned in his book *Monolingualism of the Other* (1996). He moved to France in 1949, entered the École Normale Supérieure in Paris in 1952, passed the agrégation in 1956, and then spent a year at Harvard University. He performed military service as a teacher in Algeria, worked as an assistant at the Sorbonne (1960–1964), then as a lecturer at the École Normale Supérieure until 1984, then as director of studies at the École des Hautes Études en Sciences Sociales, and was a visiting professor at American universities including Johns Hopkins, Yale and the University of California, Irvine. He died in Paris on 9 October 2004.

## His Philosophical Beginnings

Derrida began with a critique of Husserl's phenomenology, translating and introducing "The Origin of Geometry" (1962), and writing *Speech and Phenomena* (1967), in which he showed that Husserl prefers the presence of the inner voice to the written sign, and so falls into what he called the "metaphysics of presence." In the lecture "Structure, Sign and Play in the Discourse of the Human Sciences" at Johns Hopkins in 1966 he presented his critique of the center in structuralist discourse. In *Of Grammatology* (1967) he read Saussure, Rousseau and Lévi-Strauss to show that writing is not a secondary recording of speech but an original trace that makes meaning possible. After that his works diversified to include *Dissemination* and *Margins* (1972), *Glas* (1974), and *The Post Card* (1980), and then later works on politics and ethics, among them *Specters of Marx* (1993), *Politics of Friendship* (1994) and "Force of Law," and works on hospitality, forgiveness and justice.

## Deconstruction as Reading

Derrida does not present deconstruction as a method that can be applied mechanically, but as a practice of reading that traces in the text the moments in which it works against what it declares, so that the traditional binaries such as speech/writing and nature/culture appear unable to fix themselves. He insists that deconstruction is not demolition but attention to the structure of the text that allowed it to be. He treated the question of justice as that which is not deconstructible, a question to which he devoted his lecture "Force of Law: The Mystical Foundation of Authority," delivered at the Cardozo School of Law in 1989.

## Influence and Reception

He influenced American literary criticism after his lecture, and feminist studies, postcolonial studies, architecture, legal studies and art. In the Arab world the schools of modern criticism took him up under the name "al-tafkikiyya" or "al-tashrihiyya," among them Abdullah al-Ghadhdhami in *al-Khati'a wa-l-Takfir* (1985), and Kazim Jihad translated *Writing and Difference* into Arabic (1988).

## Limits and Critique

John Searle, in his controversy with Derrida over speech act theory, held that Derrida misunderstood Austin, and Derrida replied in "Signature Event Context" and then in "Limited Inc" (1988). Jürgen Habermas, in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985), criticized the erasure of the boundaries between philosophy and literature. The controversy escalated in 1992 when analytic philosophers objected to the University of Cambridge awarding Derrida an honorary doctorate, regarding his work as linguistic nonsense, while the university voted by a majority in his favor. He was also accused of relativism and of difficulty of style, and of abolishing the possibility of truth, a charge denied by his defenders, who read his work as a critique of the structure of truth and not its negation.
