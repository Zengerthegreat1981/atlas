# Johann Gottlieb Fichte

A leading first-generation philosopher of **German Idealism** (Deutscher Idealismus), who developed the Kantian theory of knowledge in the direction of the **I** (Das Ich) as the first principle. Hegel read him as the one who pushed the principle of subjectivity to its extreme, and for precisely that reason held that his philosophy stops at a finite "I" that does not reach the Absolute. He was born in Rammenau in Saxony on 19 May 1762 to a poor family of artisans, and died in Berlin on 29 January 1814 of typhus, caught from his wife, a nurse.

## Biography and Formation

He studied theology in Jena and Leipzig with the help of a noble patron, and worked as a private tutor. He read Kant in 1790, which had a decisive effect on his life, and he traveled to Königsberg to meet Kant and wrote *Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation* (1792). It was published without its author's name and people thought it was by Kant, until Kant made the matter known and Fichte's fame spread. He was appointed professor at Jena in 1794, where he delivered the "Lectures on the Vocation of the Scholar" and founded the "Science of Knowledge." In 1798 his opponents accused him of atheism because of an essay on "the divine governance of the world," and he was forced to leave his post in 1799 and moved to Berlin. In 1807–1808 he delivered the "Addresses to the German Nation" in Berlin, which was occupied by Napoleon, and in 1811 he was elected the first elected rector of the new University of Berlin.

## What He Gave

### The "Science of Knowledge" (Wissenschaftslehre, 1794)

The principles of the Science of Knowledge (Wissenschaftslehre): all knowledge begins from the **I** (Ich), which posits itself (**I = I**), then posits the **Not-I** (Nicht-Ich) as a necessary counterpart. The third principle unites the two: "I posit in the I a divisible I in opposition to a divisible Not-I," that is, the subject and the world each determine the other's limit within one consciousness. This threefold structure is, for him, the structure of all knowledge.

By it he wanted to go beyond the Kantian "thing in itself," which he saw as a contradiction, so he made the act (Tathandlung), not the fact (Tatsache), the origin of philosophy: the I is not a fixed substance but a free act of self-positing. His thought divided into phases: the first "Science of Knowledge" in Jena, then later formulations including the "Science of Knowledge in the New Method" (1796–1799), then the Berlin phase, in which he leaned toward the concept of the Absolute and the divine life, as in *The Way Towards the Blessed Life* (1806).

## Ethics and Natural Right

In *Foundations of Natural Right* (Grundlage des Naturrechts, 1796–1797) and *The System of Ethics* (System der Sittenlehre, 1798), Fichte formulated the concept of **mutual recognition** (Anerkennung): a subject does not become free by itself, but when it is called to freedom by another subject that voluntarily limits itself before it, which he called "summoning." **Freedom** is not an individual possession but a **relation between subjects**, and this is the idea that Hegel later took up and on which he built the dialectic of master and slave.

## Politics and the Nation

In *The Closed Commercial State* (1800) he called for a state that regulates the economy to guarantee every citizen's right to work and to live. In the *Addresses to the German Nation* (1808) he held that a new national education is the path to the nation's revival after the defeat by Napoleon, and that the German language carries a living spirit. These addresses became a reference for German nationalism in the nineteenth century, and were later misused in narrow contexts.

## Influence

- **Schelling**: began as a young follower and then colleague of Fichte in Jena, then developed toward the philosophy of nature.
- **Hegel**: criticized Fichte from the perspective of the objective Absolute, holding that Fichte's I remains finite.
- **Early Romanticism**: Novalis, Friedrich Schlegel, and Hölderlin took up his principle of the "act of positing" and transformed it from an epistemic foundation into an aesthetic and poetic one.
- **Karl Christian Friedrich Krause**: his student, who proceeded from the principle of the I to his doctrine of "panentheism" (the unity of being in God), a doctrine that through his Spanish students became a fully developed political and educational current in Spain (Krausismo).
- In contemporary philosophy: studies in Germany and America have returned to his concept of recognition, and Axel Honneth revived the idea in *The Struggle for Recognition* (1992).

## Principal Works

- **Versuch einer Kritik aller Offenbarung** (1792): Attempt at a Critique of All Revelation.
- **Grundlage der gesamten Wissenschaftslehre** (1794): Foundations of the Entire Science of Knowledge.
- **Grundlage des Naturrechts** (1796-1797): Foundations of Natural Right.
- **System der Sittenlehre** (1798): The System of Ethics.
- **Der geschlossene Handelsstaat** (1800): The Closed Commercial State.
- **Reden an die deutsche Nation** (1808): Addresses to the German Nation.

## Limits and Criticism

Jacobi wrote to Fichte in 1799 that his idealism ends in "nihilism" because the I dissolves the world into itself. Schelling and Hegel held that the finite I is unable to explain nature and the Absolute. Fichte's difficult expressions and the changing formulations of his theory burdened the work of his commentators. On the political side, some of his writings carry an excessive nationalist tendency and anti-Jewish statements in the 1793 pamphlet on the French Revolution, and critics point to an authoritarian tendency in *The Closed Commercial State* that restricts economic freedom in the name of justice.
