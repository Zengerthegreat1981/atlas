# John Rawls

An American political philosopher, born in Baltimore in 1921 and died in 2002 in Massachusetts. He was a professor at Harvard University from 1962 until his retirement from teaching in 1991, and is regarded as one of the most important political philosophers of the twentieth century and a leading modern theorist of the social contract tradition.

## Origins and Formation

Rawls studied at Princeton University, graduating in 1943, then served as a soldier in the Pacific during the Second World War, an experience that left a deep moral mark on him. He then returned to Princeton and obtained his doctorate in 1950 with a dissertation on the grounds of ethical knowledge, then taught at Princeton, Cornell University and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and moved to Harvard in 1962. In his two articles "Two Concepts of Rules" (1955) and "Justice as Fairness" (1958) the first seeds of his project appeared.

Rawls did not write much before "A Theory of Justice," but he kept refining his ideas for nearly a decade, and published the book in 1971, which became a landmark of Anglo-American political philosophy in the twentieth century, since it helped restore to it a standing that had weakened in the face of analytic and linguistic philosophy, and put social justice at the head of philosophical discussion.

## What He Offered

He re-grounded the social contract on contemporary Kantian foundations through the "Original Position" and the "Veil of Ignorance," in which hypothetical individuals — who do not know their social, economic or religious positions — choose the principles of justice that will regulate the basic structure of society. Rawls concluded with two principles: (1) broad equality in the basic liberties, and (2) the difference principle, which permits social and economic inequalities on condition that they benefit the least advantaged and are attached to positions open to all, under the principle of "Justice as Fairness."

## Structure of the Theory

Rawls's theory begins from a question about the principles that free and equal people would accept if they chose them apart from their private interests. For this reason he imagines the "Original Position" in which the parties choose behind a "veil of ignorance," so that they do not know their sex, class, talents or their particular conception of the good. He holds that they would choose, first, the principle of equal basic liberties, which has priority over the others; second, the principle of fair equality of opportunity; and third, the difference principle, which accepts economic inequality if it is in the interest of the least advantaged. To justify this he uses the method of "reflective equilibrium," in which he balances general principles against our settled judgments in particular cases and adjusts each in the light of the other.

## From the Theory to Political Liberalism

After the criticism of the communitarians and others, Rawls came to see that "A Theory of Justice" had assumed that citizens share a comprehensive philosophical conception, an unrealistic assumption in a pluralist society in which opposing religions and philosophies compete. In "Political Liberalism" (1993) he therefore re-presented justice as fairness as a "political" conception, not a comprehensive doctrine, on which the adherents of various doctrines can converge in an "overlapping consensus," and which can be discussed in public life through public reason. In "The Law of Peoples" (1999) he extended the idea to international relations, outlining principles governing liberal peoples and decent peoples, proposing that other states respect basic human rights without being alike in their systems.

## Impact

Rawls became a major reference point for contemporary political philosophy, whether by support or by opposition. His ideas contributed to the discussion about the welfare state, education, health care, justice between generations and global justice. His ideas also appeared in Arabic writings on pluralism, citizenship and the social contract, and the idea of public reason was compared with discussions of the public sphere.

## His Place in the Current

Rawls is a central figure of what became known as "contemporary political liberalism," and influenced a generation of theorists in law, ethics and public policy. His later exchanges with the communitarians (Sandel, MacIntyre, Walzer) and the libertarians (Nozick) formed an entire decade of heated debate in Anglo-American philosophy.

## Limits of the Project and Its Critique

Rawls's later defence in "Political Liberalism" (1993) of the "theory of overlapping consensus" did not answer all the criticisms of the communitarians, who saw in it an irredeemable abstraction of the concept of a self isolated from its ends. Robert Nozick in "Anarchy, State, and Utopia" (1974) attacked the difference principle from the perspective of the right to property; Susan Okin criticized Rawls's neglect of the family as a basic institution that produces injustice between the sexes; and others faulted him for confining justice within the framework of the nation-state. Amartya Sen held in "The Idea of Justice" (2009) that the search for a perfectly just society does not help much in addressing existing injustice, and that comparison among real cases is more useful.
