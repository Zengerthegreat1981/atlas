# Hersch Lauterpacht

**Hersch Lauterpacht** was a jurist of international law, born in 1897 in Zolkiew, Austrian Galicia (now Zhovkva, Ukraine), to a Jewish family. He studied law in Vienna, where Hans Kelsen was among his teachers, then moved to Britain in 1923, studied at the London School of Economics, and became Whewell Professor of International Law at Cambridge (1938-55) and later a judge of the International Court of Justice (1955-60). He is a leading theorist of international law in the twentieth century and an early advocate in linking international law to human rights.

## Critique of Voluntarist Positivism: International Law as an Integrated System

Against the traditional voluntarist positivism, which confines international law to the will of states expressed explicitly in treaties and custom, leaving broad gaps in which states are "free" and bound by nothing, Lauterpacht argues in *The Function of Law in the International Community* (1933) that this view undermines the very idea of international law. Any genuine legal system must be **logically complete** (*Completeness of the Legal Order*): a judge or arbitrator must be able to offer a solution to any dispute submitted, even by resorting to the general principles of law, rather than declaring a "legal vacuum" that leaves the dispute unresolved.

## Compulsory Adjudication and the Judicial Function

Lauterpacht strongly defends the expansion of **compulsory jurisdiction** of international courts. He opposes the view that the large number of "political" disputes that cannot be decided judicially justifies leaving broad areas outside the reach of law. He holds that the distinction between "legal" and "political" disputes is often exaggerated for political purposes that serve powerful states wishing to escape judicial accountability. In his view, expanding compulsory international adjudication is the only practical path to building an international order that is genuinely based on the rule of law rather than on the bare balance of power.

## Human Rights as a Subject of International Law

Lauterpacht was among the first to call for including **individual human rights** among the direct subjects of international law, at a time when traditional international law treated individuals as "objects" rather than "persons" in this law, since international legal relations were confined to relations between states. In *An International Bill of the Rights of Man* (1945) he called for a binding international document on human rights. The book anticipated themes of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), though its direct influence on the Declaration's drafting is debated.

## Role in the Nuremberg Prosecutions

Lauterpacht advised the British prosecution team at Nuremberg and contributed to the legal arguments of the chief British prosecutor, Hartley Shawcross. He is credited with urging the inclusion of **crimes against humanity**, as crimes for which individuals are directly accountable under international law regardless of the sovereignty of the state in whose name they were committed. His role was largely behind the scenes.

## Impact and Criticism

Lauterpacht's project represented one of the strongest liberal responses to political realism, which regards international law as a mere cover for the balance of power. It helped establish an intellectual tradition that links the legitimacy of the international order to how well it respects the rights of individuals, not only the interests of states. Later critics, foremost among them Martti Koskenniemi in his analysis of the structure of international legal discourse, have objected that his optimism about achieving the "logical completeness" of the international legal order through judicial expansion underestimates the deep structural tensions between the demand for state sovereignty and the demand for international legal governance. This tension is not resolved simply by expanding the jurisdiction of courts.

## From Vienna to Cambridge: An Intellectual Path Between Positivism and Liberalism

Lauterpacht received his early academic training in Vienna, where he was influenced by Hans Kelsen's analytical rigor in analyzing the structure of law and by the thesis that international and national law form one system. Unlike Kelsen, who limited himself to analyzing the formal validity of rules apart from their purposes, however, Lauterpacht directed his work toward a clear normative liberal project: expanding the reach of law and reducing the room for unrestricted politics in international relations. This blend of Kelsenian positivist rigor and liberal purposiveness distinguishes his project from both his teacher Kelsen and from contemporary political realists, who regarded his ambition for a "complete international law" as excessive and unrealistic optimism.

## His Revision of Oppenheim's Treatise

Among his practical and influential contributions was his editorship of later editions (from the fifth, 1935, to the eighth, 1955) of Lassa Oppenheim's *International Law: A Treatise* after Oppenheim's death in 1919. He introduced substantial revisions reflecting the developments of the League of Nations and then the United Nations, and expanded the protection of individuals in international law. He thereby transformed this classic, strictly positivist reference into a text more open to the human and institutional dimensions of modern international law. In this way he reflected his own intellectual project from within a tradition founded by an earlier jurist with a different method.

## Judicial Practice at the International Court of Justice

Lauterpacht crowned his career by being elected a judge of the International Court of Justice in 1955. From that judicial position he sought to translate his theoretical views on the completeness of the international legal order into practice. In his individual opinions, for example in *Certain Norwegian Loans* (1957), where he argued that a self-judging reservation to a state's acceptance of the Court's jurisdiction was invalid, he insisted that the Court should apply the law even in politically sensitive cases, rather than invoking its vagueness or "political" character to avoid deciding them. He thus remained faithful, until the end of his professional life, to the central thesis he had defended academically since the 1930s.
