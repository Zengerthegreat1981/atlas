# Ahmad ibn Hanbal

A Baghdadi hadith scholar and jurist, eponym of the Hanbali school, one of the four major Sunni schools of law, and author of the **Musnad**, one of the largest collections of Prophetic hadith, arranged by the names of the Companions who transmitted them. He is historically known for his steadfastness in refusing to say that the Qur'an was created, during the **mihna** (inquisition) imposed by the Abbasid state under al-Ma'mun, al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq. Among the Sunnis he became a symbol of resistance to forcing a theological doctrine by political power.

## Biography

Ahmad ibn Hanbal was born in Baghdad and grew up there as an orphan. He devoted his life to seeking Prophetic hadith. He travelled to the Hijaz, Yemen, Syria and Iraq to collect and hear it from dozens of teachers, and early in his scholarly life he studied for a time with al-Shafi'i in Baghdad. He was known for his great piety and asceticism, and for refusing to accept the rulers' gifts.

When the caliph al-Ma'mun declared in 218 AH the imposition of the doctrine that the Qur'an was created as an official state creed (completing an official Mu'tazili orientation), and ordered the examination of jurists and hadith scholars on it, Ibn Hanbal refused to submit. He was imprisoned and flogged under the caliph al-Mu'tasim (al-Ma'mun's successor) without retracting. The inquisition continued under al-Wathiq, until the caliph al-Mutawakkil ended it (c. 234 AH) by abolishing the official creed and releasing the detainees. Ahmad ibn Hanbal died in Baghdad in 241 AH, and vast crowds attended his funeral, reflecting the popular standing that the inquisition had consolidated.

## What he gave

### The Musnad: collecting hadith in the order of transmitters

The **Musnad** contains tens of thousands of hadiths (commonly estimated at roughly 30,000, with the figure varying by edition and by the counting of repetitions) arranged by the Companion who transmitted them, not by legal subject (unlike the later Sunan books). This makes it closer to a comprehensive documentary archive than to an organized legal compendium for direct use. Ahmad ibn Hanbal collected it from the abundance of what he heard on his long journeys. His son 'Abd Allah ibn Ahmad edited the final version in circulation and added some supplements to it after his father's death; the exact extent of these additions (*zawa'id*) remains a matter of scholarly study.

### His position on the createdness of the Qur'an

Ibn Hanbal's categorical refusal to say that the Qur'an was created during the mihna was both a doctrinal and a methodological stance. It was a refusal to subject the text of revelation to Mu'tazili theological propositions (that everything other than God is created, and therefore God's speech, the Qur'an, is created and not eternal). At the same time, it was a refusal of the principle of imposing a particular doctrine by sultanic force on jurists and hadith scholars. This position later nourished the Hanbali school's method in creed, based on accepting the texts of revelation as they came, without delving into detailed theological interpretations of God's attributes. This method is known as "affirmation without modality" (ithbat bila takyif).

### His creedal method: accepting the text without theological interpretation

Ibn Hanbal's position on the createdness of the Qur'an crystallized into a broader creedal principle, which he applied to the general body of texts on God's attributes in the Qur'an and hadith. The principle is to accept the words of revelation as they came (such as "hand", "istiwa'" and "face") without negating their meaning, without interpreting them into figurative meanings by the method of the theologians (such as the later Mu'tazila and Ash'ari), and without likening God's attributes to those of created beings. This method, later known as the creed of the Salaf regarding the attributes, became a distinctive feature of the Hanbali creedal school. It distinguishes the school from other theological schools (the Mu'tazila, the Ash'ari and the Maturidi), which developed more complex interpretive tools for dealing with ambiguous texts.

## Impact

The mihna made Ahmad ibn Hanbal a central symbol in the Sunni memory of resistance to the politicization of creed. His account of it, transmitted by historians such as al-Tabari and by later biographers, shaped the image of the "steadfast traditionist" facing political authority. The Hanbali school spread less widely geographically than the other three schools, but remained alive in Iraq, Najd and Syria. It later became the legal basis on which Ibn Taymiyya and his students built their juristic efforts, and then the Wahhabi movement in the Arabian Peninsula from the eighteenth century. It is today the official school of Saudi Arabia.

The Musnad remained a basic hadith reference that scholars consult, despite not reaching the rank of the two Sahihs (of al-Bukhari and Muslim) in acceptance criteria, since it includes weak-chain hadiths alongside the sound ones.

## Limitation

Unlike hadith books arranged by legal chapters, the arrangement of the Musnad by transmitter names made its direct use in legal research difficult. This led later hadith scholars to produce alternative subject indexes and arrangements. Ibn Hanbal also did not require that everything he transmitted meet a single strict standard of soundness. The Musnad includes hadiths of varying strength, so it requires later critical work and chain analysis (as in twentieth-century editions) to distinguish sound from weak hadiths one by one.

As for the historical account of the mihna itself, although the sources agree on its broad outlines, they differ in detail depending on the source (Sunni or Mu'tazili in orientation). This calls for methodological caution in reading it as a single narrative fully agreed upon by all available historical sources.

### His relation to Malik, al-Shafi'i and Abu Hanifa

In his youth in Baghdad, Ahmad ibn Hanbal associated with the circle of Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i, narrated from him, and attended him for a period before al-Shafi'i moved to Egypt. His scholarly formation was much broader than this, however, as he heard from dozens of teachers during his travels. He had no direct discipleship to Malik ibn Anas (who died in 179 AH, when Ahmad was about fifteen), nor to Abu Hanifa (who died in 150 AH, fourteen years before Ahmad's birth). Yet his legal school arose in an implicit dialogue with the three schools that preceded it. Hanbalism represented the most textualist of the four major schools, and the least expansive in opinion (ra'y) and abstract analogy. It occupied a position roughly at the far end of this methodological spectrum from Abu Hanifa's method.

## Major works

- **al-Musnad**: one of the largest collections of Prophetic hadith, arranged by the names of the Companion transmitters.
- Scattered legal and creedal questions, recorded by his students and attributed to him. He did not himself compose them in a single independent comprehensive legal book, unlike Malik and al-Shafi'i.
- **Al-'Aqida** (brief creedal texts attributed to him, recorded and transmitted by later students such as Abu Bakr al-Khallal in *Kitab al-Sunna*).

### His image in later Sunni memory

Ahmad ibn Hanbal's standing in later popular Sunni consciousness went beyond his role as the eponymous jurist of a school. He became a symbolic figure as "imam of the People of the Sunna and the Community", facing the theological strife. This image was consolidated by later biographical writings, such as Ibn al-Jawzi's *Manaqib Ahmad ibn Hanbal*, which portrayed him as a model of steadfastness on religious principle in the face of direct political pressure. This symbolic image, though largely built on well-documented historical events (his imprisonment and flogging under al-Mu'tasim), has had devotional elements and narrative exaggerations added over the centuries. Contemporary historians distinguish these from the core of events documented in multiple independent sources.
