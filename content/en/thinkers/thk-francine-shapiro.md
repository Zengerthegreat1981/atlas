# Francine Shapiro

An American psychologist, discoverer and developer of **Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing** (EMDR), a therapeutic method that appears among the treatments recommended for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) in the World Health Organization's 2013 guidance and in a number of national guidelines. She studied English literature, receiving a master's degree in it, and then obtained her doctorate in psychology from a professional school of psychology rather than a conventional university department, a point some critics have raised. She died in 2019.

## What She Contributed

Shapiro discovered the methodology in 1987, by her own account, during a walk in a park, when she noticed that recalling a disturbing problem while her eyes moved back and forth reduced the intensity of the disturbing feeling. On this observation she built a structured therapeutic protocol of eight phases: **(1) history taking**, **(2) preparation** (preparing the client for the concept of processing and building trust), **(3) assessment** (identifying the target memory, the negative image, and the negative belief), **(4) desensitization** with eye movements, tapping, or bilateral tones, **(5) installation** (strengthening the alternative positive belief), **(6) body scan** (searching for residual physical tension), **(7) closure** (ensuring stability before the end of the session), **(8) reevaluation** in subsequent sessions.

The theoretical hypothesis: the "Adaptive Information Processing model" holds that trauma symptoms are the product of dysfunctional neural storage, and that bilateral stimulation (Bilateral Stimulation), through eye movements, tapping, and tones, reactivates the natural memory-processing mechanisms so that the traumatic memory is integrated into the healthy memory network. As Shapiro formulated the core premise in *Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing* (1995):

> "The information processing model posits that there is an innate system in all of us that is physiologically geared to process information to a state of mental health."

Her founding book *Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing: Basic Principles, Protocols, and Procedures* (1995, later editions 2001, 2018) is the global reference. EMDR has also been applied, with a much thinner evidence base, to other problems such as anxiety, depression, bereavement, and complex trauma.

## Her Place Within the Current

EMDR intersects with cognitive behavioral therapy (Beck, Foa) and with prolonged exposure therapy, but it distinguishes itself with a different neurocognitive hypothesis. The World Health Organization (WHO) included it among the recommended psychological therapies for PTSD in 2013. The EMDR International Association (EMDRIA) was established in 1995 to regulate training and promote research. Neuroimaging studies of EMDR have been few and small, and their findings are not settled. She was influenced by the work of Joseph Wolpe on systematic desensitization (Systematic Desensitization), from which she borrowed the Subjective Units of Distress (SUD) scale, by which the patient rates the intensity of his or her discomfort from zero to ten, an essential element of her protocol. She is regarded as one of those who contributed to turning "trauma therapy" from a subspecialty into an independent clinical field since the 1990s.

## Limitation
It is listed here as a controversial treatment within trauma therapy circles: supporters regard it as a revolution, and opponents doubt the specificity of bilateral stimulation and hold that the effectiveness may be the result of exposure to the memory and not of the eye movement. Reviews of the evidence, including analyses by Richard McNally, have argued that "dismantling" trials, which compare the full protocol with a version lacking eye movements, generally find little or no added benefit from the eye movements, which suggests that structured exposure to the memory may account for much of the effect. Her work also focused on the procedural dimension more than the philosophical dimension. Other criticisms directed at EMDR include the lack of distinction between what is "practical" (works with many patients) and what is "theoretically understood" (the neural explanation is unsettled), and its limitation to short-term effect in some studies. Nevertheless, randomized trials have shown it to be effective for PTSD compared with waiting-list control, and guidelines list it among the recommended trauma-focused treatments.

## Her Impact on the Current

Since the 1990s, Shapiro has influenced the trauma therapy movement in several ways: 1) she showed that structured, repeatable protocols can achieve results in fewer sessions than traditional treatments, 2) she pushed toward new neuroscience research on the processing of traumatic memory, 3) she widened the scope of trauma therapy to include different age groups and multiple cultures, 4) she influenced practitioners working with anxiety and other conditions beyond PTSD.

## Her Most Important Works

- *Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing: Basic Principles, Protocols, and Procedures* (1995, third edition 2018).
- *Getting Past Your Past* (2012), a book aimed at the general public.
- *EMDR as an Integrative Psychotherapy Approach* (2002, editor).
