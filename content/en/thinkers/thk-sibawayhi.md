# Sibawayhi

Abu Bishr Amr ibn Uthman ibn Qanbar, known as **Sibawayhi**, was a grammarian of Persian origin (born in the district of Shiraz in Fars around 760 CE; the year is approximate). He settled in **Basra** and studied under its leading scholars, most prominently **al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi**, the founder of the science of prosody and the figure traditionally credited with the lexicon *al-'Ayn*, and also under Yunus ibn Habib and 'Isa ibn 'Umar. Sibawayhi is considered the author of **the first comprehensive systematic grammar of the Arabic language** and of the most famous book in the history of Arabic grammar. It is known in short as *al-Kitab* (The Book), a name that became his title without the need for any other.

## Contributions

*al-Kitab* is the earliest surviving comprehensive Arabic grammatical work, running to five volumes in the standard modern edition. It combines **syntax** (the structure of the sentence and the inflection of words), **morphology** (the structure of the word) and observations on **sounds** (the articulation points and qualities of letters). Sibawayhi drew his material from three sources: **the Holy Quran**, **pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry**, and **the speech of eloquent Arabs (a'rab)** whom he met and whose language he cited directly. In this way he made induction from the mouths of native speakers a methodological pillar, not merely a supplement to written texts.

Sibawayhi used and consolidated a technical vocabulary that Arabic grammar still uses today: **the governing and the governed (al-'amil and al-ma'mul)**, **the subject and the direct object (al-fa'il and al-maf'ul bihi)**, and the division of words into **noun, verb and particle**. He also analyzed **the grammatical cause ('illa)**, explaining the reason for inflection and not merely describing it, which made his book closer to a **linguistic theory** than to a list of rules. He discussed disputed questions that later became the subject of disagreement between the **Basra** and **Kufa** grammatical schools, which emerged after him around his method.

Sibawayhi died relatively young, in the 790s CE (the sources give 177 AH / 793 CE, 180 AH / 796 CE and other years), and the *Kitab* was transmitted after him; his colleague and pupil **al-Akhfash al-Awsat** is reported to have been the first to study it with him and to have transmitted and explained it. A well-known story, whose historicity is uncertain, tells that Sibawayhi lost a debate with the Kufan grammarian al-Kisa'i in Baghdad (the so-called *mas'ala zunburiyya*, on the wasp and the scorpion), and left for Persia.

The closing chapters of the *Kitab* also treat phonetics, classifying the letters by their points of articulation (*makharij*) and describing assimilation (*idgham*), which later made the work a source for the history of Arabic phonology.

## Influence

*al-Kitab* became the reference on which the **Basran** school of grammar was built, in opposition to the **Kufan** school. Many later grammarians commented on it, among them al-Mazini and al-Sirafi. It remained a subject of academic study and edition into the modern era, and it is a primary source for understanding the **structure of classical Arabic** in the first and second centuries AH. Its method of linguistic induction is regarded by many historians of linguistics as an early and rigorous descriptive approach, although the work also contains normative and theoretical argument.

## Caveats

*al-Kitab* is highly dense and terse, to a degree that made it inaccessible to many readers without an intermediary explanation, which is why an entire industry of commentaries arose. Moreover, the biography of Sibawayhi relies on later sources (such as al-Sirafi and Yaqut al-Hamawi) that conflict in their details of his birth, his death and the reasons for his departure from Baghdad.

## Key Works

- **al-Kitab** (Sibawayhi did not give it this name; it became known by it later)
