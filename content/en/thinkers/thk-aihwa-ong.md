# Aihwa Ong

Aihwa Ong (born 1950) is a Malaysian-born American anthropologist at the University of California, Berkeley, and one of the most influential theorists of globalisation, citizenship, and neoliberalism. Across several books she combined close ethnographic fieldwork with critical theory, moving from factory women in Malaysia to transnational Chinese elites, Southeast Asian refugees in California, and the biotechnology hubs of Singapore. Her concepts of "flexible citizenship" and "neoliberalism as exception" reshaped how anthropologists, geographers, and migration scholars think about sovereignty and belonging in a globalised world.

## Life and Career

Ong was born in 1950 in Malaysia (then the Federation of Malaya) into an ethnic Chinese family. She moved to the United States for her higher education and received her doctorate in anthropology from Columbia University. She joined the Department of Anthropology at the University of California, Berkeley, in the 1980s, where she has held the Robert H. Lowie Distinguished Chair in Anthropology.

Her career unfolded alongside the transformation of Southeast Asia: the industrialisation of Malaysia, the rise of the East Asian economies, the movement of refugees after the wars in Indochina, and the emergence of Singapore and China as centres of biotechnology. Each of these changes became the subject of her research, and her work is unusual in the way it moves between fine-grained ethnography and large-scale theorising about global capitalism.

## Key Works

Ong's first book, *Spirits of Resistance and Capitalist Discipline: Factory Women in Malaysia* (1987), grew from fieldwork among Malay women working in electronics factories in free-trade zones. It became a classic of the anthropology of labour and gender. Her best-known book, *Flexible Citizenship: The Cultural Logics of Transnationality* (1999), studied wealthy ethnic Chinese families who accumulated multiple passports and spread family members across several countries. *Buddha Is Hiding: Refugees, Citizenship, the New America* (2003) turned to Cambodian refugees in California and the American welfare and citizenship regimes they encountered.

Her most important theoretical work, *Neoliberalism as Exception: Mutations in Citizenship and Sovereignty* (2006), argued that neoliberalism is not a uniform global system but a flexible technique of governing applied selectively, creating zones of partial sovereignty within single states. She co-edited *Global Assemblages: Technology, Politics, and Ethics as Anthropological Problems* (2005) with Stephen J. Collier, and her later book *Fungible Life: Experiment in the Asian City of Life* (2016) studied biomedical research at Biopolis in Singapore.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

In *Spirits of Resistance*, Ong analysed episodes of collective spirit possession on Malaysian factory floors not as superstition but as a form of symbolic resistance: a bodily idiom through which young women newly arrived from villages protested the unfamiliar physical and temporal discipline of industrial capitalism. The argument showed how cultural idioms could carry political meaning without taking the form of open rebellion, and it became a model for the anthropology of work and gender.

*Flexible Citizenship* introduced the concept for which Ong is best known. Transnational elites, she argued, treat citizenship instrumentally, accumulating passports and residences and distributing family members across countries — the so-called "astronaut families" — in search of economic, educational, and security advantages. This strategy dissolves the traditional link between cultural identity and a single nation-state, and it shows citizenship operating as a flexible, family-based accumulation strategy rather than a fixed political identity.

In *Neoliberalism as Exception*, Ong developed this insight into a general theory of contemporary sovereignty. Neoliberalism, she argued, is best understood not as a coherent global order but as a mobile technology of governing that states deploy in exceptional zones: special economic zones in socialist China, alliances between conservative Islam and capital in Malaysia, Singapore's specialisation as a scientific hub. Each "exception" creates a space of partial sovereignty inside the borders of a single state, with different rules for different populations. The book also traced how these exceptions produce graduated forms of citizenship, in which some populations are included and others abandoned.

## Influence and Critique

Ong's concepts have been taken up far beyond anthropology, in migration studies, political geography, Asian American studies, and the critical study of globalisation. *Flexible Citizenship* is among the most cited works in the study of transnationalism, and her account of neoliberalism as exception offered an alternative to accounts that treat neoliberalism as a single, uniform logic imposed everywhere alike. Her later work on biotechnology and biosecurity extended the same sensibility to the life sciences, asking how states govern populations through the management of biological risk.

Her framework has also been criticised for concentrating on mobile elites with large reserves of economic and cultural capital. Critics argue that the strategies of flexible citizenship she describes are available only to a privileged few, and that generalising from them risks overlooking the experience of the majority of migrants, who move under constraint and with little choice. Ong's defenders reply that her point was precisely to show how inequality is produced through differential access to mobility — and that her work on factory women and on refugees shows the other side of the same global order, in which flexibility is imposed rather than chosen.
