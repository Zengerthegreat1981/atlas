# Clifford Geertz: Interpretive Anthropology and Thick Description

Clifford Geertz (1926–2006) was an American anthropologist and the most widely read advocate of interpretive anthropology. He argued that the study of culture is not an experimental science in search of laws but an interpretive one in search of meaning. His essays, grounded in research in Indonesia and Morocco, influenced history, literary studies, political science, sociology, and the study of religion.

## Life and career

Born in San Francisco, Geertz served in the United States Navy during the Second World War, studied philosophy at Antioch College, and received a doctorate in 1956 from Harvard's Department of Social Relations, where the programme associated with Talcott Parsons and Clyde Kluckhohn shaped his early work. He conducted research in Java in the early 1950s as part of a team, with his first wife, the anthropologist Hildred Geertz, and in Bali in 1957–58. In the 1960s he began research in Morocco, centred on the town of Sefrou. After positions including one at the University of California, Berkeley, he taught at the University of Chicago from 1960. In 1970 he became the first professor of social science at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, where he remained.

## Early studies

*The Religion of Java* (1960) described overlapping religious orientations, which he termed *abangan*, *santri*, and *priyayi*; later scholars questioned how these categories divide Javanese Islam. *Agricultural Involution* (1963) argued that colonial arrangements and wet-rice cultivation led to increasing intricacy without transformation, a thesis much disputed by historians. *Peddlers and Princes* (1963) compared economic enterprise in a Javanese and a Balinese town. *Islam Observed* (1968) compared religious development in Morocco and Indonesia.

## Thick description

*The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973) opens with "Thick Description: Toward an Interpretive Theory of Culture". Borrowing the phrase from the philosopher Gilbert Ryle, Geertz contrasted a bare account of a contraction of an eyelid with an account that distinguishes a twitch, a wink, a parody of a wink, and a rehearsal of one. Ethnography requires sorting such layers of significance. Following Max Weber, he described human beings as suspended in webs of significance they have spun, and took culture to be those webs. Meaning is public, carried by symbols and conduct, not locked inside private minds.

"Religion as a Cultural System" defines religion through symbols that establish moods and motivations by formulating a general order of existence. Such symbols serve as models of reality and models for it, joining a people's ethos to their worldview. "Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight" reads the fight as a dramatisation of status concerns, and presents culture as an ensemble of texts.

## State, knowledge, and writing

*Negara: The Theatre State in Nineteenth-Century Bali* (1980) argues that royal ceremony was not simply a mask for administration or force: spectacle was a central aim of the political order. Historians have questioned the weight this gives to display over warfare, economy, and coercion. *Local Knowledge* (1983) extended interpretation to common sense, art, social thought, and comparative law. *Works and Lives* (1988) examined the writing of Claude Lévi-Strauss, E. E. Evans-Pritchard, Bronisław Malinowski, and Ruth Benedict, asking how texts persuade readers that their authors have "been there". Later books, including *After the Fact* (1995) and *Available Light* (2000), reflected on fieldwork, disciplinary change, and philosophy.

## Criticism and influence

Critics asked how one interpretation can be judged better than another. Talal Asad argued that Geertz's account of religion presumes a particular modern idea of belief and neglects the forms of power and discipline that authorise religious meanings. Others held that reading culture as text understates conflict, political economy, and history, or gives the ethnographer's voice too much control; the brevity of attention to the Indonesian killings of 1965–66 in some writings is often noted. Contributors to *Writing Culture* (1986) pushed questions of authority further than he did, and Geertz was sceptical of parts of that movement. His insistence that explanation include what actions mean to those who perform and witness them remains a durable reference point.
