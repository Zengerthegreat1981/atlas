# Hans Vaihinger

Hans Vaihinger was born on 25 September 1852 in Nehren in the Württemberg region and died on 18 December 1933 in Halle. He was a German philosopher of Neo-Kantianism, who founded the journal *Kant-Studien* in 1896 and the Kant Society in 1904, and published the *Annalen der Philosophie* in 1919. He lost most of his sight in the later years of his life. He is known for his major work **"The Philosophy of As If" (*Die Philosophie des Als Ob*)**, whose first draft he wrote in the late 1870s and did not publish until 1911.

## Intellectual formation

Vaihinger was formed between Kant, Schopenhauer, and naturalism. He was influenced by Friedrich Albert Lange's *History of Materialism* (1866), which held that metaphysical concepts are "conceptual poetry" rather than knowledge. He began with *Hartmann, Dühring und Lange* (1876), then wrote a two-volume *Commentary on Kant's Critique of Pure Reason* (1881 and 1892), one of the most important Kantian commentaries. He wrote *Nietzsche als Philosoph* (*Nietzsche as Philosopher*, 1902), reading Nietzsche as a thinker for whom knowledge and "truth" are useful instruments of life rather than mirrors of reality, which he took to be close to his own fictionalism.

## The central idea: conscious fictions

Vaihinger argued that many of the concepts we rely on, such as the rigid atom, the line with no width, the economic man, the soul, freedom, justice, and some boundaries in religion, are **assumptions that the thinker knows do not correspond to reality, but which he treats "as if" they were true** because they facilitate thinking and action. The German word *Fiktion* is translated as "fictions," "tales," and "conscious fictions." He distinguishes between the hypothesis, which seeks verification and is put forward as possibly true, the fiction, which is known to be false and retained for its usefulness, and the dogma, which is taken to be true. His philosophy holds that thought is a vital organ within the struggle of the organism for survival, and therefore it is measured by its usefulness. For this reason he called his position "idealistic positivism."

## A parallel in Arab-Islamic philosophy

In medieval Islamic philosophy there is a conceptually close principle: al-Farabi, Ibn Sina, and Ibn Rushd held that religion addresses the masses through *takhyil*, the representation of rational truths through sensible images and symbols, and addresses philosophers through demonstration. From this the rule of interpretation in Ibn Rushd's *Decisive Treatise* (*Fasl al-Maqal*) arose. This position differs from Vaihinger's in two respects: they regard the images as a truthful representation of the truth at the level of popular understanding, whereas Vaihinger regards his concepts as useful rather than true; and they mean the politics of the masses and truth, not the survival of a living organism. The comparison brings the two ideas closer without claiming influence. Muhsin Mahdi studied the place of *takhyil* in *Alfarabi and the Foundation of Islamic Political Philosophy* (University of Chicago Press, 2001).

## Influence in psychology and philosophy

- **Alfred Adler**: acknowledged taking the idea of guiding fiction from Vaihinger in *The Neurotic Constitution* (1912). The patient lives according to an imaginary goal that organizes his behavior, which he called the "guiding fiction." This appears in *The Individual Psychology of Alfred Adler* (Ansbacher and Ansbacher, Basic Books, 1956).
- **George Kelly**: cited Vaihinger in *The Psychology of Personal Constructs* (1955) as a source for the idea of "constructive alternatives" and for the fixed-role therapy, which asks the patient to act "as if" he were another person.
- **Contemporary fictionalist philosophy**: Kendall Walton in *Mimesis as Make-Believe* (Harvard University Press, 1990) and fictionalism in metaphysics (Arthur Fine discussed the term in an article published in *Midwest Studies in Philosophy*, 1993) build on an idea close to Vaihinger's.
- **Jeremy Bentham**: Ogden published Bentham's *Theory of Fictions* in 1932, highlighting an earlier formulation of some of Vaihinger's ideas.
- It is not right to say that Vaihinger influenced William James in *Pragmatism* (1907), since his book appeared after that, and the kinship between them is a parallel kinship. Likewise, the attribution of his influence on Freud, Skinner, and Buber is not supported by documents.

## Limits and criticism

It is held against Vaihinger that the criterion of usefulness is vague: what is useful is not always distinguishable from what is true, and the distinction between fiction and hypothesis shifts over time. The logical positivists held that theoretical concepts can be defined functionally without needing to say they are false. His book remained well known in the 1920s after Ogden's translation, then declined, and was then rediscovered in fictionalist philosophy and clinical psychology.
