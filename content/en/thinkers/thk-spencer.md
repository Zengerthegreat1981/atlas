# Herbert Spencer

Herbert Spencer (1820-1903) was an English philosopher and sociologist who applied the idea of evolution to society. He is associated with the phrase "survival of the fittest" and with an organic analogy for society, and he was among the most widely read thinkers of the Victorian era.

## Life and Context

Spencer was born in Derby in 1820 and was largely self-educated. He worked as a civil engineer on railways, then as a journalist and sub-editor at *The Economist*. He lived on income from his writings and subscriptions for his works, and he suffered from ill health. His long project was the *System of Synthetic Philosophy*, begun in 1860 and completed in 1896. He died in Brighton in 1903.

## Main Ideas

- **Social evolution:** societies evolve from simple to complex forms, from homogeneity to heterogeneity, with increasing differentiation and integration.
- **Organic analogy:** society resembles a biological organism whose parts have functions that sustain the whole, an approach later associated with functionalism.
- **Militant and industrial societies:** he distinguished societies organized around compulsory cooperation and warfare from those based on voluntary cooperation and trade.
- **Survival of the fittest:** he introduced this phrase in *Principles of Biology* (1864) after reading Darwin.
- **Liberalism:** he opposed state intervention and held a strongly individualist political philosophy, as in *Man versus the State* (1884).

## Major Works

- *The Man versus the State* (1884)

## Method

Spencer relied on comparative data about different societies, assembled in the *Descriptive Sociology* series, and on analogies with biology. He sought general laws of evolution applying across physical, biological, and social domains.

## Influence and Criticism

Spencer was enormously influential in his lifetime, including on Durkheim, who engaged critically with his account of social differentiation. His reputation declined in the twentieth century. Critics condemned his use of evolutionary ideas to justify laissez-faire politics and inequality (often labeled "social Darwinism," though he held Lamarckian views as well), questioned the organic analogy, and pointed to the weakness of his empirical basis. Talcott Parsons opened *The Structure of Social Action* (1937) by quoting the question "Who now reads Spencer?" to mark his eclipse.

## Applications and Cross-References

His evolutionary framework influenced early anthropology and later neo-evolutionary theory. Compare Comte's stages, Durkheim's division of labor, and Marx's historical materialism.

## The System, Equal Freedom, and the Limits of the Organic Analogy

From 1848 to 1853 Spencer was sub-editor of *The Economist*. A modest inheritance, together with payments from readers who subscribed to the *System of Synthetic Philosophy*, let him work outside the universities. The System applied one evolutionary scheme to biology, psychology, sociology, and ethics. He pressed the organic analogy only so far: unlike an organism, a society has no single sensorium, and consciousness remains in the individual members. The political principle he defended, already in *Social Statics* (1851), was that each person may act freely so long as that action does not infringe the equal freedom of others. *The Principles of Sociology* compares domestic, ceremonial, political, ecclesiastical, professional, and industrial institutions across societies. The evidence was largely secondhand historical and ethnographic report, much of it arranged for comparison in the *Descriptive Sociology* volumes he sponsored. *The Study of Sociology* examines the biases that obstruct inquiry into one's own society. He was widely read in Britain, the United States, and parts of Asia. Durkheim, while using themes of differentiation, rejected the claim that contract and self-interest are enough to hold a complex society together. Parsons attributed the question "who now reads Spencer" to the historian Crane Brinton. Critics also attacked his resistance to public provision for welfare. Historians continue to dispute how far the label "social Darwinism" fits a writer who also held Lamarckian views of inheritance.
