# Justin L. Barrett

An American cognitive psychologist (born 1971), a leading figure in the Cognitive Science of Religion, alongside Pascal Boyer and Scott Atran. He is known for the Hyperactive Agency Detection Device (HADD) hypothesis, which he developed, building on Stewart Guthrie's work on anthropomorphism, as a cognitive-evolutionary explanation for the widespread belief in invisible agents (gods, spirits).

## Definition

The Cognitive Science of Religion is a relatively recent approach, methodologically crystallised in the 1990s. It explains the spread and persistence of religious beliefs across cultures by reference to innate human cognitive structures, rather than relying solely on social, historical or purely theological explanations. Barrett is regarded as a leading name in this field, and as an early proponent of applying controlled experimental psychology (with children and adults) to questions about how divine beings are represented cognitively.

## Historical Context

Barrett was trained in experimental psychology, with a cognitive and developmental focus (PhD, Cornell University), and was influenced by the work of the French anthropologist Pascal Boyer. In the 1990s Boyer (*The Naturalness of Religious Ideas*, 1994) proposed that religious concepts succeed in spreading because they are "minimally counterintuitive": they violate innate expectations in a single striking detail (a god who hears everything, a spirit that passes through walls), while retaining the other ordinary innate assumptions. This makes them easy to remember and to transmit orally across generations.

## Main Ideas and Method

Barrett proposes that the human mind was equipped by evolution with a rapid mechanism for attributing an ambiguous movement or sound to a purposeful agent (a predatory animal, for example). This is because the cost of a false positive (imagining an agent that does not exist) is small compared with the cost of a false negative (missing a real predator). This mechanism is "hyperactive" by nature, producing many false alarms, which makes humans cognitively predisposed to explain obscure phenomena (thunder, illness, luck) by invisible agents. This is proposed as a cognitive basis for the spread of religious belief. In a 1996 study with Frank Keil, Barrett found that adults who explicitly endorsed "theologically correct" ideas about God (omniscience, simultaneous attention to many events) nevertheless used anthropomorphic assumptions when processing stories about God in real time. He later argued, with other researchers, that young children readily attribute special knowledge to invisible agents, a pattern he took as evidence of a natural basis for religious concepts.

## Main Works and Representative Studies

In his book *Why Would Anyone Believe in God?* (2004), Barrett reverses the usual question. Instead of asking "Why do people believe in God despite the absence of evidence?", he asks the cognitive question: "What makes the belief in invisible agents natural for a human cognitive architecture?" This methodological shift is characteristic of the cognitive science of religion approach. He also published a study with Nyhof in 2001 on the spread of counterintuitive concepts through serial narration, which reported that concepts with a small number of counterintuitive features tended to be recalled and transmitted better than concepts that were wholly ordinary or very bizarre.

## Impact and Criticism

Barrett helped develop the cognitive science of religion as a recognised research field combining psychology, anthropology and philosophy, and his HADD hypothesis influenced wide debates on the evolutionary origin of religious belief. Critics have argued that experimental results drawn largely from Western, often Protestant, samples may not generalise to other traditions, and that the HADD hypothesis remains debated. Barrett is also a committed Christian who has written on the compatibility of cognitive science of religion with theism; this personal stance is noted neutrally and does not by itself bear on the validity of his experimental work.

Barrett later held research positions at the University of Oxford (the Centre for Anthropology and Mind) and at Fuller Theological Seminary, where he was associated with the Thrive Center for Human Development, and he extended his interests to children's religious cognition. His later books include *Born Believers* (2012), written for a general audience.
