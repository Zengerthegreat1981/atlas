# Carl Rogers

American psychologist and psychotherapist (1902-1987), founding father of "Person-Centered Therapy" and one of the founders of the **humanistic psychology** movement (the "third force" in psychology) together with Abraham Maslow, and one of the most influential psychotherapists of the twentieth century.

## Biography

He worked as a professor at Ohio State University, the University of Chicago, and the University of Wisconsin, and spent his last decades in La Jolla, California.

## What He Contributed

- **Non-directive therapy**: in his first major book, *Counseling and Psychotherapy* (1942), he put forward the idea of **non-directive therapy**, rejecting the model of the expert therapist who interprets and directs, in favor of a therapist who accompanies the client in their discovery of themselves.
- **The actualizing tendency**: in *Client-Centered Therapy* (1951) he crystallized his mature theory: that every human being has an **innate tendency toward self-actualization** (Actualizing Tendency), and that psychological growth falters when the person receives "conditional positive regard," that is, love and acceptance conditional on performance or obedience, so that a split arises between the person's real self and their self "as it ought to be."
- **The core therapist conditions**: within the six necessary and sufficient conditions he set out in 1957, the three conditions provided by the therapist are: **congruence** or genuineness (Congruence/Authenticity), **unconditional positive regard**, and **accurate empathic understanding**. These conditions, in his view, are sufficient in themselves to bring about constructive personality change without any interpretation or analysis on the therapist's part, a thesis that constituted an explicit break with both classical psychoanalysis and behaviorism.
- **Extension of the model to education and groups**: he later extended his model to education ("student-centered learning") and group relations ("encounter groups"), and in his book *On Becoming a Person* (1961), one of the best-selling popular psychology books, he presented his vision of the "Fully Functioning Person" as the goal of healthy psychological growth.

## His Place in the Movement

He represents the humanistic pole of greatest worldwide influence, adjacent to existential therapy, and he intersected with existential philosophy in the centrality of lived experience and authenticity, despite his high anthropological optimism compared with the existentialists' pessimism about anxiety and death.

## Impact

His principles (empathy, congruence, and unconditional positive regard) became a basic component in the training of therapists from different schools, not only his direct followers, and influenced educational counseling, social work, and the literature on the **therapeutic alliance**, which contemporary research shows to be among the strongest predictors of psychotherapy success regardless of the theoretical school followed.

## Limits

His radical optimism about human nature and his assumption of an innate tendency toward the good made him a target of criticism by existentialists (such as Rollo May), who stressed the demonic, tragic, and evil dimension in human nature. His model is also criticized for assuming a universal innate tendency toward positive growth without decisive empirical support, and because its total reliance on the therapeutic relationship makes it less effective against severe disorders (such as psychosis or severe borderline personality disorders) that require more structured interventions.

## His Upbringing and Professional Formation

Carl Rogers was born on 8 January 1902 in Oak Park, Illinois, to a conservative Protestant family. He studied agriculture and then history at the University of Wisconsin and moved to Union Theological Seminary in New York before leaving theology for psychology at Teachers College, Columbia University, where he received his doctorate in 1931. He then worked for about twelve years in Rochester with delinquent and deprived children at the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Children, and learned from the field that the interpretations a therapist offers often fall short of the client's experience. He moved to Ohio State University in 1940, then established the counseling center at the University of Chicago in 1945, and returned to Wisconsin in 1957; after 1964 he settled in California, where he took part in founding the Center for Studies of the Person (1968). He died in La Jolla on 4 February 1987.

## Debates and Research

Rogers was among the first therapists to record therapy sessions and analyze them scientifically, and with Rosalind Dymond he produced the study *Psychotherapy and Personality Change* (1954), which used the Q-sort technique to measure change in self-image. In 1956 a debate took place between him and B. F. Skinner on the control of human behavior, published in the journal *Science*, in which Rogers defended subjective freedom against behavioral engineering.

### The Rogers–Buber Dialogue (1957)

On 18 April 1957, Rogers participated in a public dialogue with philosopher Martin Buber at the University of Michigan, moderated by Maurice Friedman. The encounter highlighted a foundational disagreement regarding mutuality in psychotherapy:
- **Buber's position**: Buber argued that while genuine empathy and helping occur in therapy, true I–Thou mutuality is structurally impossible in the clinical setting. The relationship is fundamentally asymmetrical because one person comes seeking help and reveals their inner life, while the therapist remains responsible for the process and does not reciprocally reveal themselves. Buber maintained that the therapist practices "inclusion" (experiencing the event from both poles), but the patient cannot experience the therapist's pole.
- **Rogers's position**: Rogers countered that in the deepest moments of therapeutic connection, when defenses drop, a fully mutual, person-to-person meeting occurs in which both participants experience genuine equality and shared presence beyond their institutional roles.

The Wisconsin project with hospitalized schizophrenic patients, in which Eugene Gendlin, Charles Truax, and Donald Kiesler participated, yielded mixed and inconclusive results, qualifying the claim that the three core conditions are on their own uniformly sufficient across severe psychotic conditions.

## Arab Reception

Rogers entered the Arab world mainly through **psychological guidance and counseling**, so his theory is taught in counseling programs at Arab universities as "indirect counseling" or "client-centered counseling," and Hamed Abdel Salam Zahran presents it in his book *Psychological Guidance and Counseling* (Alam al-Kutub, 1977) as one of the main approaches alongside directive and eclectic counseling. The concepts of acceptance, listening, and congruence appear in school counseling training and in community mental health, although original research on the suitability of the model to Arab culture remains limited.

## Additional Notes on the Model's Limits

Researchers in cross-cultural psychology such as Derald Wing Sue have pointed out that the Rogerian model assumes an independent individual who speaks about their feelings and makes their own decisions, an assumption that does not always suit cultures that put the group and the family first. In addition, the principle of non-directiveness may be understood in some settings as passivity on the part of the therapist. His students, among them Gendlin and Greenberg, tried to address these limits by introducing procedural tasks and bodily focus.
