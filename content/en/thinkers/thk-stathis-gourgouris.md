# Stathis Gourgouris

Stathis Gourgouris (born 1958) is a Greek-American scholar, poet, essayist, translator, and sound artist, and Professor of Classics, English, and Comparative Literature and Society at Columbia University. He is best known for *Dream Nation: Enlightenment, Colonization, and the Institution of Modern Greece* (1996), a study of how modern Greece was imagined into existence, and for a body of work that brings together literature, psychoanalysis, political theory, and the question of secular criticism. Few contemporary classicists have written so consistently across the boundary between poetry and philosophy, and between the ancient world and the politics of the present.

## Life and Career

Gourgouris was born on 18 December 1958 in Los Angeles, California, and grew up in Athens, Greece. He studied at the University of California, Los Angeles, where he took his BA in 1981, his MA in 1984, and his PhD in comparative literature in 1990, with a dissertation on the national imaginary and the institution of modern Greece. He taught comparative literature at Princeton University from 1992 to 2000, then at Columbia University from 2002 to 2005, and at UCLA from 2005 to 2008, before rejoining Columbia in 2008, where he has served as director of the Institute for Comparative Literature and Society and as director of the Program in Hellenic Studies. He was president of the Modern Greek Studies Association from 2006 to 2012, received Columbia's Lenfest Distinguished Faculty Award in 2015, and was awarded a Guggenheim Fellowship in 2023. He is also a published poet in Greek, with four volumes of poetry, and a translator and sound artist.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

*Dream Nation* is Gourgouris's founding work. Drawing on Cornelius Castoriadis's theory of the social imaginary, he argued that modern Greece was not simply the revival of an ancient nation but the product of a double fantasy: the European Enlightenment's idealisation of ancient Greece, and the Greek national movement's internalisation of that ideal. The "dream nation" was thus a colonial formation, in which Greeks learned to see themselves through the eyes of philhellenic Europe. The argument reframed the study of nationalism, showing that national identity is not only invented but imagined through the desires of others, and it became a central text in Modern Greek studies.

In *Does Literature Think?* (2003) Gourgouris argued that literature is not an object for theory to explain but a mode of thinking in its own right — a way of posing questions about truth, politics, and the human that philosophy cannot pose in the same way. Against the mythical and the mystical, he defended an "antimythical" literature, one that refuses consoling stories about origins and essences. *Lessons in Secular Criticism* (2013) extended this argument to religion and politics, defending secular criticism — in the tradition of Edward Said — as a practice of questioning all orthodoxies, including secular ones, and of refusing the authority of any single discourse, whether religious or scientific.

*The Perils of the One* (2019) is his most ambitious philosophical work. It examines the figure of the One — the unity, the whole, the single principle — across Greek philosophy, psychoanalysis, and political theory, and it argues that the desire for oneness, whether in the form of national unity, religious monotheism, or political totality, is the source of the most dangerous political fantasies. Against the One, Gourgouris defends multiplicity, contingency, and the democratic imagination. His most recent book, *Nothing Sacred* (2024), continues this inquiry into the question of religion in a secular age, asking what remains of the sacred when nothing is taken as given.

## Influence and Critique

Gourgouris's influence has been strongest in Modern Greek studies, comparative literature, and political theory, where *Dream Nation* is now a standard reference, and where his defence of secular criticism has shaped debates about the role of the humanities in public life. His work as a poet and translator — his poetry has been translated into English, German, French, Italian, and other languages — has made him a distinctive figure, a scholar who practises the literary art he theorises.

Critics have noted that his writing is demanding, moving between ancient Greek philosophy, psychoanalysis, and contemporary politics in ways that can be difficult to follow, and that his argument about the "dream nation" has been challenged by historians who emphasise the material and political forces behind Greek independence rather than the imaginary. Others have questioned whether his critique of the One risks dissolving all forms of collective identity, including those — such as democratic solidarity — that politics needs. Gourgouris has answered that his target is not solidarity but the fantasy of a unity without remainder, and that democracy, properly understood, is the politics of the many, not of the One.
