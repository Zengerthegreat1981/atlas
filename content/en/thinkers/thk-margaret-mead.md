# Margaret Mead

Margaret Mead (1901–1978) was the most famous anthropologist of the twentieth century. Her first book, *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928), made her a public figure at twenty-six and made cultural relativism a household idea. Over a career of fifty years she wrote more than twenty books, advised governments, appeared on television, and became the public face of anthropology in the United States. She was also one of the most contested figures in the discipline: her Samoan research was challenged after her death in one of anthropology's fiercest controversies, and her later career as a public intellectual drew criticism from colleagues who thought she had traded scholarship for celebrity.

## Life and Career

Mead was born on 16 December 1901 in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, and grew up in a household of academics and reformers. She studied at Barnard College, then moved to Columbia University, where she became a student of Franz Boas and Ruth Benedict and took her doctorate in 1929. Boas sent her to Samoa in 1925, when she was twenty-three, to study adolescent girls — a test of whether the turmoil of adolescence was a biological universal or a product of culture.

She conducted fieldwork in Samoa (1925–1926), in New Guinea among the Manus (1928–1929), and in the early 1930s among the Arapesh, Mundugumor, and Tchambuli of New Guinea, first with her second husband, Reo Fortune, and then with her third husband, Gregory Bateson, whom she met in the field. She spent most of her professional life at the American Museum of Natural History in New York, where she began as an assistant curator in 1926 and later became curator of ethnology, and she also taught at Columbia and Fordham. She died in New York on 15 November 1978, at the age of seventy-six.

## Key Works

*Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928) argued that Samoan adolescence was free of the storm and stress familiar in America, because Samoan culture did not burden young people with the same conflicts over sexuality and authority. The book's conclusion — that culture, not biology, shapes the experience of growing up — became the classic statement of the culture and personality school.

*Growing Up in New Guinea* (1930) studied childhood among the Manus, and *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies* (1935) compared the Arapesh, Mundugumor, and Tchambuli, arguing that temperament — what Americans called masculine and feminine — varied dramatically between societies, and that gender roles were culturally constructed rather than biologically fixed. *Male and Female: A Study of the Sexes in a Changing World* (1949) extended the argument to contemporary America. Later works, including *Culture and Commitment* (1970), addressed the generation gap and the pace of cultural change, and *Blackberry Winter* (1972), her memoir, became a best-seller.

## Main Ideas: Culture Over Biology

Mead's central contribution was to make the case for culture against biology, vividly and in public. Where psychologists and biologists attributed behaviour to instinct or heredity, Mead showed — or claimed to show — societies in which the supposedly natural took a different form: adolescents without turmoil, women who were aggressive and men who were gentle, children raised without the anxieties of American middle-class life. Her comparative method was deliberately simple: find a society that does things differently, and the universality of the American pattern collapses.

This argument had a double edge. Scientifically, it pushed anthropology toward the study of childhood socialisation and personality formation, and it gave the culture and personality school its most persuasive evidence. Politically, it gave American readers a tool for criticising their own society: if Samoan adolescents were happy, perhaps American adolescence need not be miserable; if Tchambuli women were dominant, perhaps American gender roles were not natural law. Mead became, in effect, anthropology's ambassador to the American public, using ethnography as a mirror in which her readers could see their own customs as choices rather than necessities.

## Influence and Critique

Mead served as president of the American Anthropological Association (1960) and of the American Association for the Advancement of Science (1975). Her influence was enormous and double-sided. She brought anthropology to a mass audience, shaped American debates about child-rearing, sexuality, and gender, and served as an adviser to governments and international bodies on questions ranging from nutrition to the generation gap. Her insistence that gender roles are culturally variable anticipated feminist anthropology by decades, and her work on childhood socialisation remains a reference point.

The most serious challenge came after her death. In *Margaret Mead and Samoa: The Making and Unmaking of an Anthropological Myth* (1983), the New Zealand anthropologist Derek Freeman argued that her Samoan findings were wrong — that Samoan adolescence was not free of conflict, that Samoan sexuality was restrained rather than free, and that Mead had been misled by her young informants, who had joked with her. The controversy divided the discipline for years. Most anthropologists now take a middle position: Freeman overstated his case and misrepresented some of Mead's claims, but Mead's Samoan fieldwork was brief, her Samoan was limited, and her conclusions were stated more confidently than her evidence warranted.

Other criticisms were quieter but persistent: that her ethnographies smoothed over internal conflict, that her later popular writing recycled earlier findings without new research, and that her public persona sometimes blurred the line between anthropologist and pundit. Yet even her critics acknowledge the scale of her achievement. She made the comparative study of culture matter to people who had never heard of anthropology, and she did so by asking a question that still drives the field: how much of what we take for human nature is, in fact, culture?
