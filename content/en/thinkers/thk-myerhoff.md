# Barbara Myerhoff: Ritual, Aging, and Self-Creation

Barbara Myerhoff (1935–1985) was the anthropologist of margin, liminality, and transformation. Her deceptively simple research question—**How do elderly Jewish immigrants use ritual to maintain identity and create meaning in the face of displacement and mortality?**—became the foundation for groundbreaking work on aging, death, ritual, and the relationship between ethnography and lived experience. Myerhoff demonstrated that ritual is not primarily a mechanism for social reproduction or expressing cosmology but **a practice through which people create and continually recreate themselves**—their identities, their communities, their sense of meaning. She pioneered **performative ethnography**, insisting that ethnography itself is a performative act, and that anthropologists must attend to how their own presence and interpretation shape what they study. Her tragically early death (from cancer at age 50) lent her work additional poignancy, as her final writing grappled directly with mortality, finitude, and what it means to create meaning in the face of death.

## Biographical and Intellectual Context

Barbara Myerhoff was born in Los Angeles to a Jewish family and grew up during the Depression and World War II. She studied at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) and later received her PhD in anthropology from the University of California, Los Angeles in 1965. Her intellectual formation was shaped by the University of Chicago's symbolic anthropology tradition (particularly Geertz and Turner, whom she engaged with directly), combined with a deep commitment to feminist anthropology and reflexive ethnography emerging in the 1970s.

She conducted her first major fieldwork among the **Huichol** people of Mexico (1966–1968), documenting their peyote rituals and cosmology. This fieldwork produced important ethnographic work, but it was her turn toward urban American ethnography that would prove most transformative. Beginning in the 1970s, she conducted research with elderly Jewish immigrants and Holocaust survivors in Los Angeles, particularly those gathered at a local senior center (which she called the "Israel Levin Senior Adult Center" or "ILSAC" in her writing). This seemingly unglamorous research location—not an exotic "other" culture but elderly immigrant Jews living in urban California—positioned her work as radical precisely through its focus on people, communities, and questions that mainstream anthropology had largely ignored.

Myerhoff held a position at the University of Southern California (1975–1985), where she developed the anthropology of aging and the phenomenology of embodiment as essential anthropological concerns. She was diagnosed with cancer in 1984 and died in 1985, leaving behind a body of work that addressed mortality not as external topic but as lived experience shaping all her writing and ethnography.

## Core Theoretical Framework

### Ritual as Self-Creation and Identity Work

Myerhoff's foundational insight reframes ritual fundamentally. Ritual is not primarily a mechanism for social reproduction (functionalism), nor primarily for expressing and maintaining social structure (structuralism), nor even primarily for generating communitas and managing liminality (Turner). Rather, ritual is **a practice through which people actively construct, maintain, and transform their identities and sense of self**.

This emphasis on self-creation is particularly evident in her work with elderly Jewish immigrants. These people—many separated from their families, survivors of displacement, living on the margins of American society—used ritual practices (gathering together, telling stories, preparing food, performing Jewish rituals) not to maintain some fixed "Jewish identity" but to *actively make themselves Jewish* in the present, again and again. The ritual work was itself the identity. Stop ritualizing, and the identity risks dissipating.

This reframing elevates ritual from the merely ceremonial or religious to the fundamental. All humans, Myerhoff argues, engage in ritual work—the ongoing performances and practices through which we maintain and create ourselves.

### Definitional Ceremony and the Politics of Self-Representation

Myerhoff developed the concept of the **definitional ceremony**—a ritual through which a community publicly declares its identity, stakes its claim to recognition and legitimacy. Her classic example is a Passover Seder performed by the elderly Jewish immigrants she studied. The Seder is simultaneously a religious ritual (following Jewish law), a family celebration, and—in the context of marginalized elderly immigrants—a powerful political statement: "We are Jewish, we continue, we matter, you must recognize us."

The definitional ceremony is necessarily public: its efficacy depends on being witnessed. One performs one's identity for an audience; the audience's recognition validates and stabilizes the identity. This has profound political implications: marginalized communities engage in ritual to assert their right to exist, to demand recognition from a society that often denies them.

### Embodiment, Aging, and Mortality

Myerhoff insisted that anthropology attend to the **body**—not as a cultural symbol or sign but as a lived, experiencing reality. Her work with elderly people forced attention to aging bodies, deteriorating health, and mortality. She rejected the tendency to treat aging and death as shameful or marginal topics, instead elevating them as central to human experience.

Ritual, she argued, is fundamentally **embodied**. We experience ritual through our bodies—the movements we make, the food we eat, the physical proximity to others. Through ritual, people work with and on their aging bodies to maintain meaning and connection despite physical decline.

Her work on Huichol peyote ritual also demonstrated the centrality of embodied experience. Consuming peyote, walking pilgrimage routes, dancing—these bodily experiences are not mere expressions of cosmological belief but are themselves the sources of religious meaning and transformation.

### Performance and Presence: The Ethnographer in the Field

Myerhoff developed a sophisticated reflexivity about ethnography itself. She insisted that the ethnographer is not a neutral observer but **a presence that changes what is observed**. Moreover, ethnography itself is a form of **performance**—the ethnographer performs the role of researcher, the ethnographic text performs a version of reality, and the ethnographic encounter is fundamentally dramatic.

This led her to experiment with ethnographic forms. Some of her work reads more like performance script than traditional ethnography. She collaborated with filmmaker Jay Ruby to create a film about the Israel Levin Center, bringing performance and visual documentation into ethnographic representation.

### Liminality and Aging as Permanent Liminality

Drawing on Turner but extending him, Myerhoff argued that elderly people inhabit a state of **permanent liminality**. They are no longer productive workers (by capitalist definitions) yet not ritually dead; they have completed normative life transitions (marriage, children, career) yet remain alive. They occupy a marginal status in societies structured around youth and productivity.

This liminality is not temporary (as in Turner's ritual passage) but enduring. Rituals and community gathering provide ways of managing this permanent marginality—creating meaning and value in a status that mainstream society devalues.

### History, Displacement, and Ritual Continuity

Myerhoff attended explicitly to how history and displacement shape ritual. Her elderly Jewish informants were refugees and immigrants—people whose lives had been radically disrupted by war, displacement, assimilation pressures. The rituals they practiced were not timeless traditions but **responses to displacement and loss**. Ritual becomes a way of maintaining connection to a past that is geographically and historically distant.

This historicizes ritual: we cannot understand ritual in isolation but must understand it as response to specific historical circumstances. The Seder performed by immigrants in Los Angeles is not identical to the Seder performed in pre-war Eastern Europe; it is a response to loss and a strategy of continuity in the face of rupture.

## Major Works

**Peyote Hunt** (1974) — Her ethnographic study of the Huichol pilgrimage to gather peyote cactus and the ritual uses of peyote in Huichol cosmology and religion. Demonstrates the centrality of embodied experience and movement to religious practice. Shows her early sophisticated attention to ritual performance and its transformative power.

**Number Our Days** (1978) — Her most famous work, an ethnographic study of an elderly Jewish senior center in Los Angeles. Documents the lives, rituals, relationships, and storytelling of elderly immigrants, many of whom are Holocaust survivors. Combines traditional ethnography with performance and autobiography. Won the National Book Award. Remains required reading in multiple disciplines.

**Remembered Lives: The Work of Ritual, Storytelling, and Growing Older** (1992, posthumously) — Collection of essays reflecting on aging, ritual, storytelling, and ethnography. Includes her essay "Life History as Integration of Multiple Pasts" and reflections on ethnography and mortality. Her most explicitly reflexive theoretical work.

**Telling Stories: A Lifetime of Collaboration: An Interview with Barbara Myerhoff** (1985, posthumously) — Interview conducted shortly before her death, where she reflects on her life, her ethnographic practice, and her mortality. Remarkable for its honesty and vulnerability.

**Unfinished Work: The Ethnography of Dying** (published in collections, written during her final illness) — Her final writing, reflecting on her experience of dying and what it means for ethnography. Pushes the reflexivity of ethnography to its limit.

## Ethnographic Focus: Elderly Jewish Immigrants and the Huichol

### The Israel Levin Senior Adult Center, Los Angeles

Myerhoff's research with elderly Jewish immigrants represents a radical reorientation of anthropological attention. Rather than studying an exotic "other," she studied a community of elderly immigrants living in urban America—many of them Holocaust survivors or Jews displaced by pogroms and immigration. The center was a gathering place where they engaged in secular and religious ritual, told stories, shared meals, and maintained connection.

**Ritual gathering**: The center's daily operations involved multiple forms of ritual—celebrating birthdays together, preparing Shabbat meals, reading Torah, conducting mock elections for center positions. These activities were simultaneously practical (providing social connection and services to isolated elderly) and deeply symbolic (maintaining Jewish identity, honoring the dead, asserting the dignity of marginalized people).

**Storytelling and memory**: Central to the center's culture was storytelling—people narrating their life histories, their experiences of immigration and displacement, their memories of family and community lost during the Holocaust. Myerhoff documented these stories as themselves forms of ritual work, through which people maintained connection to the past and asserted their existence.

**Food and preparation**: The preparation and sharing of traditional Jewish food was a significant ritual practice. The act of preparing gefilte fish, matzo brei, or chicken soup was not merely practical but was an embodied practice maintaining cultural continuity and creating moments of collective identity-making.

**Death and memorial**: The center conducted memorials for members who died, and Myerhoff attended carefully to how the community engaged with death and loss. Death was not hidden or shameful but acknowledged and ritualized, creating opportunities for reflection on life and community.

### The Huichol Peyote Pilgrimage

In her earlier work among the Huichol of Mexico, Myerhoff documented the annual peyote pilgrimage—a sacred journey to the Huichol's ancestral hunting grounds to gather peyote cactus for religious and medicinal use. The pilgrimage involved:

**Physical movement and embodied experience**: The journey itself was physically demanding, and the experience of traveling together, eating ritual foods, and arriving at the sacred site was central to its meaning. The body's experience—hunger, fatigue, arrival—was not peripheral to meaning but constitutive of it.

**Consumption and altered states**: The ritual use of peyote created altered states of consciousness through which people experienced visions and communications. Myerhoff insisted that understanding these experiences required taking seriously their subjective reality, not dismissing them as hallucination or primitive superstition.

**Cosmological engagement**: The pilgrimage was understood by participants as engagement with ancestors, sacred beings, and cosmic forces. Myerhoff documented participants' understandings while also attending to how the ritual structured and generated these experiences.

## Theoretical Influence and Debates

Myerhoff's work has been influential in multiple fields:

**In her favor**: She demonstrated that aging and death are crucial anthropological topics, not marginal ones. She showed that anthropology could and should study people in contemporary urban America, not only "exotic others." She pioneered performative ethnography and reflexivity, showing that attending to the ethnographer's presence enriches rather than compromises analysis.

**Major criticisms**:

**Romanticization of ritual**: Some critics argued that Myerhoff's emphasis on ritual as self-creation risks romanticizing the activities of marginalized people. Ritual may also be constrained by tradition, enforced by community pressure, limiting rather than liberating.

**The ethnographer's role**: While Myerhoff insisted on reflexivity about her own presence, critics questioned whether her emphasis on performance and mutual creation adequately addressed the power imbalances between ethnographer and subjects. Her presence may have influenced what people did and said in ways she didn't fully acknowledge.

**Universalizing from particular**: Myerhoff's work with elderly Jewish immigrants, while powerful, is specific. Some questioned whether her theoretical frameworks travel well to other contexts.

**Gender and embodiment**: While Myerhoff attended to embodiment, some feminist scholars argued that her analysis didn't sufficiently attend to how gender shapes embodied experience and aging.

## Methodological Contributions

Myerhoff revolutionized how anthropologists conduct ethnography:

**Performative ethnography**: She modeled ethnography as performance, experimenting with ethnographic form to bring readers into the presence of lived experience. Her work influenced subsequent generations to experiment with ethnographic writing.

**Reflexivity and presence**: She insisted that ethnographers be explicit about their own presence, role, and impact on their subjects. This became foundational for reflexive ethnography.

**Collaboration with media**: She worked with filmmakers and other collaborators to represent ethnographic knowledge through multiple media. This was pioneering for interdisciplinary anthropology.

**Auto-ethnography**: Her willingness to draw on personal experience and reflection in her ethnographic writing prefigured auto-ethnography as a legitimate method.

**Aging as ethnographic topic**: She established aging and life course as worthy anthropological subjects, opening a new field.

## Postcolonial Legacy and Critique

Myerhoff conducted research on the Huichol during a period of indigenous dispossession in Mexico. Her work represented indigenous people, raising questions about representation and authority.

**On representation**: While Myerhoff was relatively reflexive about her role, postcolonial scholars have questioned whether representation of indigenous or marginalized people (even sympathetic representation) is ethically sufficient. Does documentation preserve or risk commodifying the people one studies?

**On her Los Angeles work**: Her study of Jewish immigrants avoided some postcolonial problems by studying her own community (or nearby to it). However, scholars have questioned whose perspective she represented—the perspectives of the eldest, most vocal, most comfortable with the center?

Contemporary postcolonial scholars have built on Myerhoff's reflexivity while pushing further to address questions of power and representation.

## Contemporary Relevance

Myerhoff's work remains influential:

**Aging studies and gerontology**: Her work established aging as a serious anthropological and ethnographic topic. Contemporary gerontology and age studies draw on her frameworks.

**Death studies and mortality**: Her willingness to engage mortality directly influenced how anthropologists and scholars address death, dying, and the meaning-making surrounding mortality.

**Performance studies**: Her work on ritual as performance and ethnography as performance influenced performance theory and performance ethnography.

**Public anthropology and engaged ethnography**: Her work demonstrates how anthropology can be engaged with urgent contemporary issues (aging, displacement, identity) and can serve as public work.

**Holocaust studies and Jewish studies**: Scholars of the Holocaust and of Jewish identity engage her work as foundational.

**What has been superseded**: Her somewhat romantic view of ritual as freely creative may have underestimated constraint and coercion. Contemporary scholars attend more to how ritual can also enforce conformity.

**Ongoing debates**: How do we conduct ethnography with the dying and the dead? How do we represent those who cannot consent to representation? These remain live questions raised by and in response to Myerhoff's work.
