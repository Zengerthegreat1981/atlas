# Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Sina

A philosopher and physician, regarded as among the most influential philosophers of the Islamic world, in both East and West, and the author of the largest integrated philosophical-scientific system in the medieval Islamic heritage. He was known in Latin Europe as Avicenna, and his two encyclopedic works, *al-Shifa'* (The Cure) in philosophy and *al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (The Canon of Medicine), had a teaching influence that lasted for centuries in the Islamic East and in the universities of Europe until the seventeenth century.

## Biography

Ibn Sina was born in 370 AH / 980 CE in the village of Afshana near Bukhara, the capital of the Samanid state, to a father who was an official of the state and a mother from Afshana. He relates in his autobiography (which he later dictated to his pupil Abu Ubayd al-Juzjani) that he memorized the Quran and mastered literature at the age of ten, then learned jurisprudence, logic, geometry and medicine until he excelled in the latter before reaching eighteen, and that he gained access to the royal library of the Samanids in Bukhara and absorbed the sciences of his age. After the fall of the Samanid state, Ibn Sina moved among Gurganj, Rayy and Hamadan, where he served as vizier to a Buyid prince, and then Isfahan, where he settled under the patronage of Ala' al-Dawla al-Kakuyid in the last years of his life. He died in Hamadan in 428 AH / 1037 CE, according to the biographical sources after an illness of the intestines. The details of his travels and of his death rest largely on his autobiography and on al-Juzjani, a self-reported and partisan source.

## What he contributed

### *al-Shifa'*: the philosophical encyclopedia

His largest work, *al-Shifa'*, is an encyclopedia of logic, natural science, mathematics and metaphysics, in which Ibn Sina's famous metaphysics is built on the distinction between "existence" and "essence": every existent is possible in itself (its existence does not follow from its essence), and it emerges into actuality only through an external cause that makes it "necessary of existence through another"; this causal series ends of necessity in the "Necessary of Existence through itself", who is God, an argument later known as the "proof of the truthful" (*burhan al-siddiqin*). This distinction between existence and essence became one of Ibn Sina's most important contributions to the history of metaphysics, and passed through Thomas Aquinas into Latin scholastic philosophy.

### The theory of the soul and the intellect: the "Flying Man"

In the book on the soul in *al-Shifa'* Ibn Sina presented a thought experiment known as the "Flying Man" (a man created fully formed, suspended in the air with no sensory contact with anything): Ibn Sina holds that this man, despite the absence of all sensation, would remain aware of himself, which demonstrates, in his view, that the soul is a substance independent of the body that perceives itself directly without the mediation of the senses. This argument was later read as an early precedent for some of the problems of the Cartesian cogito.

### *The Canon of Medicine*

A medical encyclopedia in five books that gathered the medicine of Galen and Hippocrates and early Arabic medicine (such as al-Razi) into a single systematic framework, covering the principles of general medicine, simple drugs, diseases of the organs, general diseases and fevers, and compound drugs. It was translated into Latin in the twelfth century (Gerard of Cremona) and remained the basic reference in the teaching of medicine in the universities of Europe (Bologna, Montpellier, Louvain) until the seventeenth century, a remarkably long teaching life for a medical text.

### Eastern philosophy and logic

Late in his life Ibn Sina referred, in the introduction to *al-Mantiq al-Mashriqiyyin* (Eastern Logic), to a philosophical project that goes beyond traditional Aristotelian Peripateticism, though little of it has reached us; some scholars (such as Henry Corbin) interpreted this as a harbinger of the "illuminationist" philosophy later developed by al-Suhrawardi, while others (such as Dimitri Gutas) hold that this interpretation is exaggerated and that the "Eastern" philosophy of Ibn Sina is no more than a methodical reorganization of Peripateticism, not a break with it. Ibn Sina also composed symbolic recitals such as *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*, *Risalat al-Tayr* (The Treatise of the Bird) and *Salaman wa Absal*; scholars debate how far some of these mystical treatises are authentically his.

### His correspondence with al-Biruni

Early in his career Ibn Sina exchanged scientific letters with Abu Rayhan al-Biruni, who put to him ten critical questions about Aristotle's physics (the nature of the circular motion of the celestial spheres, the possibility of the void, the plurality of worlds). In his replies Ibn Sina defended the coherence of the Aristotelian system, while al-Biruni insisted that astronomical and mathematical observation does not settle these hypotheses by decisive demonstration, and Ibn Sina's pupil Abu Sahl al-Masihi took on the reply to the second round of al-Biruni's objections. This correspondence is among the earliest documents showing an internal critical discussion of Aristotle's physics in the Islamic world, preceding Ibn Rushd's commentaries by roughly a century and a half.

## Influence

Ibn Sina had a decisive influence on almost all later Islamic philosophy: al-Ghazali attacked his system in the *Incoherence of the Philosophers* but absorbed his logic and employed it in kalam; al-Suhrawardi built his illuminationist philosophy in critical dialogue with Ibn Sina's metaphysics; and Ibn Rushd in al-Andalus, while replying to al-Ghazali in the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*, criticized many of Ibn Sina's interpretations of Aristotle as a deviation from the First Teacher. In Latin Europe, *al-Shifa'* and the *Canon* together formed one of the pillars of scholastic philosophy and medicine, and Thomas Aquinas adopted his distinction between existence and essence in his own proof of the existence of God, although he differed with him on other fundamental points (such as emanation as against direct creation).

## Limits

The most important historical objection to Ibn Sina was made by al-Ghazali in the *Incoherence of the Philosophers*: he accused him (along with al-Farabi) of unbelief on three questions, namely the claim of the eternity of the world, the denial of God's knowledge of particulars (his knowing them in a universal rather than particular way), and the denial of bodily resurrection (interpreting the afterlife in a purely spiritual way). Although Ibn Rushd defended Ibn Sina in part in the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*, these three questions remained the central point of dispute between Peripatetic philosophy and the dominant Ash'ari kalam current. Contemporary scholars (such as Dimitri Gutas) also note that Ibn Sina's emanationist system, despite its internal coherence, faces a difficulty in explaining how multiplicity proceeds from the simple One without multiple intellectual intermediaries that may weaken the oneness of the First Principle in purely philosophical terms.

## His most important terms

The distinction Ibn Sina established between "existence" and "essence", and between "possible in itself, necessary through another", entered directly into both the Arabic and Latin philosophical lexicon (essentia/existentia, possibile/necessarium), and became a standard analytical tool used by all later kalam and philosophical schools, including his opponents among the Ash'ari theologians, who employed some of his logical tools even while rejecting his metaphysical conclusions.

## Major works

- *al-Shifa'* (The Cure)
- *al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (The Canon of Medicine)
- *al-Najat* (The Salvation, a summary of *al-Shifa'*)
- *al-Isharat wa al-Tanbihat* (Pointers and Reminders)
- *Risala fi al-Mantiq al-Mashriqiyyin* (Treatise on Eastern Logic)
- *Sirat Ibn Sina* (Ibn Sina's autobiography, dictated to al-Juzjani)
