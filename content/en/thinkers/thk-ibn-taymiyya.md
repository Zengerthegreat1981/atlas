# Ibn Taymiyya

A Hanbali jurist, traditionist, and theologian, born in Harran in 661 AH / 1263 CE, who moved with his family to Damascus as a child to escape the Mongol invasion. He is among the influential scholars of the Hanbali school in the history of Islamic thought, known for his sharp critical positions toward philosophy, Sufi philosophical currents, and kalam, and for his contributions to usul al-fiqh, Islamic governance (siyasa shar'iyya), and creed.

## Life

Ibn Taymiyya grew up in Damascus in a family of Hanbali scholars, his grandfather and father both being jurists of the school. He received an encyclopedic training in hadith, fiqh, exegesis, and creed, and began teaching and issuing legal opinions while still in his twenties. He took part in resistance to the Mongol invasion of Greater Syria and issued a fatwa that fighting the Mongols was obligatory despite their outward profession of Islam, on the grounds that they did not abide by the provisions of the Shariah. His anti-Mongol fatwas, together with the separate Mardin fatwa on the status of that city, were later invoked by modern Islamist writers in discussions of 'hakimiyya' (divine sovereignty).

He faced several trials and periods of imprisonment on account of his creedal positions, which conflicted with those of some theologians of his time, especially on the divine attributes, the visitation of graves, and tawassul. He was imprisoned several times, in Cairo (1306-07), Alexandria (1308-09) and finally the Citadel of Damascus (1326-28), and died in the Citadel of Damascus in 728 AH / 1328 CE, having been prohibited from writing in the last years of his life.

## Contributions

### Siyasa shar'iyya

In his book *Al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya fi Islah al-Ra'i wa-l-Ra'iyya* (Governance by Islamic Law: On Reforming the Ruler and the Ruled), Ibn Taymiyya developed a theory of the relationship between jurisprudence and rule that distinguishes 'siyasa shar'iyya', the domain in which the ruler manages the affairs of the subjects in pursuit of the public interest within the limits of the Shariah even where no specific text exists, from detailed fiqh rulings. This theory gave the ruler a broad space for legislative judgment in administrative, criminal, and fiscal matters, and later influenced the development of administrative law in the late Islamic sultanates (such as the Ottoman qanun, which operated alongside the Shariah).

### Critique of philosophy and logic

In his work *Al-Radd 'ala al-Mantiqiyyin* (Refutation of the Logicians, also known as *Naqd al-Mantiq*), Ibn Taymiyya launched a radical critique of Aristotelian logic itself, not merely of the conclusions reached by the philosophers, as al-Ghazali had done. He argued that the Aristotelian theory of the logical syllogism (definition and formal demonstration) adds no real certainty beyond what the mind grasps through innate disposition and induction, and that many of its universal premises are not certain. He also wrote *Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa-l-Naql* (The Averting of the Conflict between Reason and Revelation), a rebuttal of the thesis that reason takes precedence over revelation when they conflict. In it he tried to show that every apparent conflict between clear reason and sound transmitted revelation results from an error in understanding one of the two.

### Creed, kalam, and philosophical Sufism

Ibn Taymiyya reformulated the doctrine of the divine attributes on a method that rejects the interpretation (ta'wil) of the verses and hadiths on the attributes by the Ash'arite and Mu'tazilite theologians, calling for affirming them 'without modality, without comparison, without negation, and without distortion', a formula that came to be associated with the 'Salaf' approach to creed. He also offered a detailed critique of the doctrine of the 'unity of being' (wahdat al-wujud) in Ibn Arabi and his school, arguing that it leads to a kind of denial of the distinction between Creator and creature, and devoted several treatises to it within the *Majmu' al-Fatawa*.

### Usul al-fiqh and ijtihad

On matters of usul al-fiqh, Ibn Taymiyya called for widening the scope of ijtihad and rejected the blind imitation (taqlid) of the four Sunni schools. He examined the doctrines of public interest (maslaha) and analogy (qiyas) critically, while remaining methodologically close to the foundations of the Hanbali school in relying on the text and transmitted reports.

## Legacy

Ibn Taymiyya had a broad influence on later religious reform movements, most notably the Wahhabi movement founded by Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab in the eighteenth century, which adopted many of his creedal positions as its foundation. He also influenced modern Islamic reformist currents (such as contemporary Salafi groups) and some political Islamist currents that drew on his fatwa on the Mongols in support of jihad against rulers who did not abide by the Shariah, although scholars disagree about how accurately this later appeal reflects its original historical context. His student Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya remained the most prominent transmitter of his thought and expanded it in independent works.

## Criticism

Ibn Taymiyya faced strong opposition from scholars of his time. He was accused of likening God to His creation because of his literal affirmation of certain attributes such as 'istiwa' and 'nuzul'. He defended himself by arguing that affirming an attribute does not entail specifying its modality (takyif), but his opponents among the Ash'arites (such as Taqi al-Din al-Subki) held that his formulations, especially on the question of the eternity of the species of the world and the continuing creation by God, approached an anthropomorphism that he did not intend but that follows from the apparent sense of his words. This theological dispute has not been settled between the Hanbali and Ash'arite schools to this day.

Sufis of his age also objected to him and pursued him because of his attacks on the 'unity of being' and on certain practices of grave visitation and seeking blessing (tabarruk), arguing that his critique sometimes failed to distinguish carefully between Ibn Arabi's symbolic terminology and its literal sense. Contemporary scholars, including contributors to the volume *Ibn Taymiyya and His Times*, argue that depictions of Ibn Taymiyya in some modern readings, whether laudatory or disparaging, reduce the complexity of his actual position on Sufism. He did not reject Sufism outright; he distinguished between a moderate Sunni Sufism (such as that of al-Junayd) and a philosophical Sufism he considered deviant (such as that of Ibn Arabi and al-Hallaj). Other scholars warn against projecting contemporary political readings (such as interpreting his fatwa on the Mongols as the foundation of modern violence) onto its original context of defending Damascus against an actual military invasion.

## Key works

- **Majmu' al-Fatawa** (Collection of Fatwas): a later compilation of his fatwas and treatises in some 37 volumes.
- **Al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya fi Islah al-Ra'i wa-l-Ra'iyya**: on the theory of governance and political jurisprudence.
- **Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa-l-Naql**: on the relationship between reason and text.
- **Al-Radd 'ala al-Mantiqiyyin** (Naqd al-Mantiq): a critique of Aristotelian logic.
- **Minhaj al-Sunna al-Nabawiyya**: a refutation of Imami Shi'i thought (in response to al-Allama al-Hilli).
- **Al-Aqida al-Wasitiyya**: a concise treatise on Salafi creed.

## Creed and the Hanbali tradition

Specialists do not treat his creed as a simple reprint of earlier Hanbali teaching, nor as a school standing wholly apart from it. A standard philosophical survey places him as a theological traditionalist inside the Hanbali line of Sunni Islam: God is to be described as the Qur'an and the Prophet describe Him, negatively and positively, in line with the verse that nothing is like Him and that He is hearing and seeing. At the same time, studies of his theodicy find a writer who is hard to file under one contemporary heading. Hanbali insistence on revelation is joined there to arguments and terms drawn from Ibn Sina and, on some points, from Mu'tazili discussion, with the borrowed terms reset inside a Hanbali frame. Readers of that reconstruction are left asking whether the result is one coherent doctrine or a highly skilled joining of positions that do not finally settle into a single system. Later Hanbali authors continued to argue over where, on the spectrum of the school, a theologian who relied on him should be placed.
