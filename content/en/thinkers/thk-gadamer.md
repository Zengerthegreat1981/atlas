# Hans-Georg Gadamer

Hans-Georg Gadamer was a German philosopher born in Marburg on 11 February 1900 and died in Heidelberg on 13 March 2002, at the age of one hundred and two. He was a prominent student of Martin Heidegger, a leading figure of contemporary philosophical hermeneutics (interpretation theory), and the author of *Truth and Method* (*Wahrheit und Methode*, 1960), which transformed interpretation from a method for explaining texts into an ontology of human understanding.

## Biography

Gadamer grew up in Breslau, where his father was a professor of chemistry, studied at Marburg under Paul Natorp and Nicolai Hartmann, and received his doctorate in 1922 with a dissertation on pleasure in Plato's dialogues. In Freiburg in 1923 he met Heidegger and followed him to Marburg, and received his habilitation in 1929 with a thesis on "Plato's Dialectical Ethics" (*Platos dialektische Ethik*, 1931). He became a professor at Leipzig in 1939 and served as rector of the university between 1946 and 1947 under the Soviet occupation, then moved to Frankfurt in 1947 and to Heidelberg in 1949 as successor to Karl Jaspers, and retired in 1968 but continued teaching and lecturing inside Germany and abroad for decades. In the Nazi era he did not engage in politics and did not emigrate, but turned to classical studies, which is a subject of discussion among his historians.

## Truth and Method

Gadamer published *Truth and Method* when he was sixty years old. The book is divided into three parts: the experience of truth in art, then the experience of truth in the human sciences, then the ontological turn of interpretation guided by language. He starts from a critique of the idea that the human sciences attain truth by a method similar to that of the natural sciences, and holds that understanding is not a neutral objective grasp but an existential event. Among his major concepts are prejudices (*Vorurteil*), which should not be demolished all at once but examined, as a condition of understanding; tradition as what we owe a debt to; effective-historical consciousness (*wirkungsgeschichtliches Bewusstsein*); the fusion of horizons (*Horizontverschmelzung*) between the horizon of the reader and the horizon of the text; and application (*Anwendung*) as part of understanding, not something that comes after it. Among his famous phrases is that being that can be understood is language.

## Play, Art and Phronesis

In the first part he made play a model of the experience of art, for the game has its own existence that precedes the consciousness of the players and draws them in. He recovered concepts from the humanist tradition such as formation (*Bildung*), common sense (*sensus communis*), taste and judgment. He drew on Aristotle's ethics of phronesis, that is, practical wisdom, as a kind of knowledge that applies the universal to the particular, and made it a model of hermeneutic understanding. In *Philosophical Hermeneutics* (essays in English, 1976) and *Reason in the Age of Science* (*Vernunft im Zeitalter der Wissenschaft*, 1976) he extended these ideas to the fields of practical philosophy, politics and science, and in *The Relevance of the Beautiful* (*Die Aktualität des Schönen*, 1977) he discussed art, symbol and festival. In *The Enigma of Health* (*Über die Verborgenheit der Gesundheit*, 1993) he took up medicine and health, which opened an entry to interpretation in medicine and psychotherapy.

## Debates

He debated the Italian jurist Emilio Betti, who defended the objectivity of interpretation and a method that distinguishes between understanding and application, while Gadamer held that this ignores the historicity of the one who understands. In the period between 1967 and 1971 he engaged in a debate with Jürgen Habermas, who held that Gadamer's emphasis on the authority of tradition blinds one to ideological distortions, and that interpretation needs a critique of ideology, while Gadamer replied that critique itself moves within a tradition. In Paris in 1981 a tense encounter took place with Jacques Derrida over the possibility of dialogue and good will, which remained the subject of divergent interpretations.

## Influence

Gadamer influenced philosophy, theology, law, literary theory and the social sciences, and bridged dialogue with Paul Ricoeur, Habermas and Rorty. Arabic studies took up *Truth and Method* in several translations, among them the translation by Hasan Nazim and Ali Hakim Salih (2007), and Mohamed Chawki Zine has written on his hermeneutics. His ideas are used in the interpretation of religious texts, including the fusion of horizons in reading the sacred text, and in hermeneutic and narrative therapy. His collected works appeared in ten volumes from Mohr between 1985 and 1995.

## Limits and Critique

Habermas holds that Gadamer's exaltation of tradition weakens critical consciousness, and that language itself is a field of power and domination. Betti criticized his relativist tendency and his weakening of objectivity, and some held that he offers no criterion for distinguishing correct understanding from mistaken. Derrida held that the concept of good will in dialogue presupposes homogeneity among the interlocutors. Feminism and postcolonial critique criticized him because his referential tradition is essentially European. He departs from radical existentialism, which stresses rupture and individual rebellion, because he relies on continuity and dialogue. He is not a theorist of therapy, and his connection to therapy is indirect through his influence on narrative and hermeneutic therapy.
