# Abu Ishaq Ibrahim ibn Musa al-Shatibi

A Maliki jurist and usulist from al-Andalus, who lived in Granada in the eighth century AH (the fourteenth century CE); his year of birth is not known with certainty. He is a major developer of the theory of the objectives of the Sharia (maqasid al-shari'ah) in Islamic usul al-fiqh. He is known for his book *al-Muwafaqat fi Usul al-Shari'a*, in which he reformulated the relationship between the particular rulings of the Sharia and its universal objectives, with a coherent theoretical method developed in greater detail than in earlier usul works.

## Biography

Al-Shatibi is attributed to the town of Xàtiva in eastern al-Andalus, though his upbringing and scholarly life were spent mainly in Granada, capital of the Banu Nasr kingdom, the last of the Islamic principalities in al-Andalus. He was educated in Granada under a number of its leading scholars, among them Ibn al-Fakhkhar al-Biri (in Arabic grammar) and Abu Sa'id ibn Lubb (in jurisprudence and fatwa). He drew on a usulist tradition shaped by al-Juwayni, al-Ghazali, Ibn al-Hajib and al-Amidi.

Al-Shatibi worked as a teacher and jurisconsult in Granada. He engaged in several debates and confrontations with some jurists of his time, who accused him of innovation (bid'a) because of his critical positions on certain popular religious practices that he saw as innovations unsupported by any text. He devoted his book *al-I'tisam* to this subject. He died in Granada in 790 AH (1388 CE).

## What he gave

### The theory of the objectives of the Sharia: al-Muwafaqat fi Usul al-Shari'a

This book is a comprehensive classical theorization of the objectives of the Sharia, that is, the investigation of the purposes and wisdoms for whose realization the legal rulings were enacted, rather than their particular wording alone. Al-Shatibi built his theory on the earlier division of benefits (maslaha) into necessities (daruriyyat), needs (hajiyyat), and embellishments (tahsiniyyat), developed by al-Juwayni and al-Ghazali, but he expanded and grounded it theoretically in greater depth. The five necessities (preservation of religion, life, progeny, intellect, and property) are the overarching objectives around which all legislation revolves. The needs (removal of hardship) and the embellishments (noble character) serve and complete them.

Al-Shatibi also introduced the concept of "overall certainty" (al-qat'iyya al-kulliyya), arising from the inductive accumulation of the particulars of the Sharia. A multitude of individual textual particulars, each of which is speculative (zanni), together reach a general certainty about the overall objective, in a theory of induction somewhat close to logical induction.

### Critique of innovation: al-I'tisam

In this book, al-Shatibi developed a coherent theory defining innovation (bid'a) and the criteria for distinguishing it from legitimate unrestricted benefit (al-maslaha al-mursala). He rejected the introduction of new religious practices that have no basis in the text or in the general objectives of the Sharia, even if they appear good on the surface. He distinguished between true innovation (which has no basis at all) and additional innovation (which has a partial basis, but whose manner of practice is newly introduced). This detail later influenced the literature of religious reform.

### Usul al-fiqh and the theory of ijtihad

In several places in *al-Muwafaqat*, al-Shatibi discussed the conditions of the mujtahid and the categories of mujtahids, and the theory of "absolute ijtihad" as against partial ijtihad. He offered a precise analysis of the relationship between the particular textual rulings and the universal "spirit of legislation" that the jurist must keep in mind in every deduction, such that a particular text is not understood apart from its general purpose.

## Impact

The works of al-Shatibi had relatively limited circulation in the centuries immediately following his death. His theory of objectives (maqasid), however, witnessed a broad revival in modern and contemporary Islamic thought. Reformist thinkers such as Muhammad al-Tahir ibn Ashur adopted it in his book *Maqasid al-Shari'a al-Islamiyya*, which developed the theory and added new objectives (such as freedom of thought and the objective of the community). So did Allal al-Fasi in Morocco, and Yusuf al-Qaradawi and Taha Jabir al-Alwani in the contemporary "fiqh al-maqasid" project. The theory of objectives also became a basic reference in contemporary discussions of ijtihad on issues such as human rights, Islamic financial legislation, and the renewal of jurisprudence.

## Limitation

Despite his reference status today, contemporary researchers have noted that the use of the theory of "objectives" in modern Islamic discourse sometimes goes beyond what al-Shatibi himself laid down. Al-Shatibi developed his theory within a usulist framework committed to the particular text and to a specific inductive method. Some contemporary reformist readings, by contrast, use it as a tool to override explicit particular texts in the name of the "spirit of the Sharia", which extends his theory beyond what he intended. Scholars such as Ahmad al-Raysuni, in his reference study on the theory of objectives, have observed that al-Shatibi himself remained committed to the authority of the Maliki school in detailed legal application. He did not make "objectives" a complete substitute for the particular text, as some contemporary readings do.

On the other hand, al-Shatibi was criticized in his own time by some Granada jurists for his stance on innovation. Some considered him excessively strict in rejecting popular religious practices (such as certain popular devotional customs) that other jurists permitted as unrestricted public interest, whereas he regarded them as innovations with no basis.

## His method regarding text and objective

A key feature of al-Shatibi's method is that he did not make the overarching objectives a substitute for the particular texts. He established a complementary relationship between them. The particular text is the direct evidence for the ruling, but its correct understanding requires keeping its overall objective in mind, lest it be applied literally in a way that contradicts the spirit of the legislation. He therefore distinguished between what is "purely devotional" (ta'abbudi mahd), whose meaning is not necessarily understood, and what is "meaningful" (ma'qul al-ma'na), which can be connected to a clear benefit. He warned against the danger of extending analogy (qiyas) to purely devotional acts.

He also discussed the relationship between the Lawgiver's purpose and the purpose of the obligated person (*maqasid al-mukallaf*). He held that the legal obligation was enacted to free the obligated person from the dictates of his desires, so that he becomes a servant of God by choice, just as he is subjugated to Him by compulsion. Later scholars of comparative Islamic legal philosophy have revisited this idea as an early theory of the "teleology of legislation".

## Major works

- **al-Muwafaqat fi Usul al-Shari'a**: the general theory of the overall objectives of the Sharia.
- **al-I'tisam**: on the definition of innovation and its criteria.
- **al-Majalis**: a commentary on the book of sales (*Kitab al-Buyu'*) of Sahih al-Bukhari.
- **al-Ifadat wa-l-Inshadat**: on language and literature.
