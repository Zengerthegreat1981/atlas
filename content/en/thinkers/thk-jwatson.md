# John B. Watson

An American psychologist, founder of "Methodological Behaviorism" with his 1913 manifesto, and the father of academic behavioral psychology.

## What he contributed

In "Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" (1913) he proclaimed the "Behaviorist Manifesto": psychology is the "objective science" of observable behavior, and "mental processes" (thoughts, feelings) are not a subject for science. He established "behaviorism" as a rigorous methodological movement, which drew increasingly on Pavlov's conditioned reflex. He also conducted the **"Little Albert"** experiment (1920, with Rosalie Rayner): he conditioned an infant's fear of a white rat by pairing its appearance with a frightening sound, and then claimed that the fear **generalized** to other white and furry objects. For decades the experiment was presented as the proof that **classical conditioning** produces fears in humans.

- **"Give me twelve infants"**: his most famous statement, and the one most often truncated. He declared that if he were given twelve healthy infants and a world of his own specification to raise them in, he could make any one of them whatever he chose, whether doctor, lawyer, or thief, regardless of ancestry and talents. The sentence that follows immediately, and is hardly ever quoted, reads: **"I am going beyond my facts and I admit it, but so have the advocates of the contrary and they have been doing it for many thousands of years."** In other words, the statement was an **openly declared rhetorical challenge** and not a scientific claim, and reading it as a claim attributes to Watson what he disowned in the very same sentence.
- **Its application to child-rearing**: in *Psychological Care of Infant and Child* (1928) he advised parents to treat the child in an objective, cool manner: no kissing, no hugging, no letting the child sit on one's lap, and a handshake in the morning. The book became a widely read parenting guide in America and is one of the clearest examples of the harm done when an immature theory is turned into general advice.

## His place in the movement

His 1913 manifesto rested on two earlier foundations: the work of **Edward Thorndike** on animal learning (1898) and his law of effect, and the conditioned reflex of **Ivan Pavlov**, who became widely known in the American milieu after the 1927 translation of *Conditioned Reflexes*. From him **B. F. Skinner** took the program of a "science of behavior" and pushed it toward radical behaviorism in *The Behavior of Organisms* (1938).

Much is attributed to Watson that is not his: the **deconditioning of fear** in the child "Peter" was the work of **Mary Cover Jones**, who published it in 1924 in *Pedagogical Seminary* under the title "A Laboratory Study of Fear: The Case of Peter". She was Watson's student, not an assistant in the Albert experiment.

## The limitation
**The Little Albert experiment is not valid evidence**, and this is the most important thing to say about it:

- **A single subject**, with no control group and no standardized measurement; the fear was assessed by concrete observation by the two experimenters themselves.
- **It was never successfully replicated.** Others attempted it in the 1930s and did not obtain the same result.
- **The surviving film recordings do not show what the text describes**: the child's responses fluctuate, and the conditioning was repeated several times in between because the effect kept fading.
- **The conditioning was not removed**: Albert left the hospital without being treated for what had been done to him, which would not be accepted today by any ethics committee.

Since 2009, long after Watson's death, there has been wide controversy over **Albert's identity** and fate. It was suggested that he was a child who died at the age of six of a neurological illness, meaning that he may not have been "healthy and sound" as Watson described him; later, other research favored a second identity. The question is unsettled, but what is beyond dispute is that the most famous experiment in the history of behaviorism **cannot bear the weight placed on it**.

As for his biography, he was dismissed from Johns Hopkins in 1920 following his relationship with Rayner (whom he later married), and moved into commercial advertising, where he applied his principles to linking products with emotions, and he succeeded greatly at it. He burned his papers and correspondence before his death.

## His major works

"Psychology as the Behaviorist Views It" (1913), *Psychological Review*, the behaviorist manifesto · *Behavior: An Introduction to Comparative Psychology* (1914) · "Conditioned Emotional Reactions" (1920, with Rosalie Rayner), the Little Albert paper · *Behaviorism* (1925) · *Psychological Care of Infant and Child* (1928, with Rosalie Rayner Watson).
