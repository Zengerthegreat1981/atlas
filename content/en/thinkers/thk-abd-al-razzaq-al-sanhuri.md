# Abd al-Razzaq Ahmad al-Sanhuri

An Egyptian jurist, one of the leading drafters of civil law in the Arab world in the twentieth century, and a prominent pioneer of the project of **re-formulating Islamic jurisprudence in a modern codified form** that parallels the structure of European civil law without abandoning its sharia sources.

## Life and Context

Sanhuri was born in Alexandria. He studied law in Egypt, then was sent to France, where he obtained two doctorates from Lyon: one (1925) on contractual restrictions on freedom of labour in English case law, and a second (1926) with a thesis entitled "The Caliphate and its Development into an Eastern League of Nations" (*Le Califat*), in which he examined the classical Islamic theory of the imamate and its fate after the abolition of the Ottoman caliphate in 1924. He returned to Egypt and held senior academic and judicial posts. He served as dean of the Faculty of Law at Cairo University and later as president of the Egyptian Council of State, and supervised the drafting of the Egyptian Civil Code, issued in 1948 and in force from 1949. In 1954 he was assaulted at the Council of State and removed from public office; sources differ on the political context. The influence of his work later extended to the drafting of civil codes in Iraq, Syria, Libya and Kuwait, which made him a major figure in the shaping of modern Arab civil law. He died on 21 July 1971; accounts differ on whether in Alexandria or Cairo.

## Central Ideas

### The Codification Project: Islamic Jurisprudence as a Living Source

Sanhuri believed that Islamic jurisprudence could not regain practical effectiveness in the modern state unless it was re-formulated as **codified law** on the model of European civil codes, rather than remaining scattered in classical jurisprudential books, with their digressive method that is not organized according to modern legal categories. He put this conception into practice in the Egyptian Civil Code of 1948, which drew much of its provisions from French and comparative jurisprudence, but also incorporated principles and rules derived from Islamic jurisprudence wherever they were found to be compatible with modern legal needs (such as the theory of unforeseen circumstances, which is close to the Islamic jurisprudential concepts of necessity and excuse, and the rules on assignment of debts (*hawala*)).

### The Wasit: An Expository Encyclopedia of Civil Law

In his encyclopedic work *Al-Wasit fi Sharh al-Qanun al-Madani* (The Intermediate Commentary on the Civil Code), Sanhuri undertook an enormous comparative effort. He explained every provision of the Egyptian Civil Code by reference to its sources in French jurisprudence, Roman law and Islamic jurisprudence together. In many places he showed the origin of the Islamic jurisprudential text from which the modern legislator drew inspiration. This made the work a reference for everyone who subsequently studied the possibility of uniting Islamic jurisprudence with modern civil law within a single technical framework.

### The Caliphate Thesis: Critique and Reconsideration of the Theory of the Imamate

In his Lyon thesis on the caliphate, Sanhuri analysed the classical theory of the imamate (as formulated by al-Mawardi and others) critically in light of the collapse of the Ottoman caliphate. He proposed conceptions of modern Islamic forms of political cooperation (closer to an Islamic league of nations) to replace the traditional theory of a centralized caliphate, which was not practically applicable in a world of nation-states.

### Sources of Right: A General Theory from Islamic Jurisprudence

In his comparative work *Masadir al-Haqq fi al-Fiqh al-Islami* (Sources of Right in Islamic Jurisprudence), Sanhuri attempted to extract a general theory of obligations from within Islamic jurisprudence itself, parallel to what the "general theory of obligation" books do in French civil law. He surveyed the topics of contract, unilateral will, harmful acts, and unjust enrichment in Islamic jurisprudence across the different schools, in a project parallel to his direct practical work in drafting the civil code itself.

## Impact and Critique

Sanhuri was among the most influential jurists in shaping contemporary Arab civil codes. He drafted or supervised the drafting of civil codes in several Arab states, which remain in force today with varying amendments. His project nonetheless drew criticism from two opposite directions. One was a conservative Islamist current, which held that fitting jurisprudence into a Western civil code form strips it of its distinctive methodological character and subjects it to an alien legislative logic. The other was a secular liberal current, which held that invoking Islamic jurisprudential references in a modern civil code could open a gap for the religionisation of positive law. Sanhuri himself maintained a conciliatory position, holding that Islamic jurisprudence is a "source" of modern law, neither a complete replacement for it nor entirely isolated from it.
