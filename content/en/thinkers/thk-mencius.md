# Mencius (Mengzi)

A Confucian philosopher of the fourth century BCE, regarded as the most important of the "second sages" after Confucius, and his book *Mengzi* is one of the "Four Books" that Zhu Xi chose in the twelfth century CE as the basis of Confucian education. He developed the "theory of good nature" in the context of debate with the Mohists (who called for "impartial concern"), and with the doctrine of Yang Zhu, and then Xunzi, a later Confucian, countered him by saying that human nature is evil.

## His Life and Context

Mencius (Meng Ke) was traditionally said to have been born in 372 BCE in the small state of Zou near Lu, the homeland of Confucius, and the traditional account says that he lost his father young and that his mother moved her residence three times to improve the environment of his upbringing, and that she cut her weaving to teach him that study is not to be abandoned. These accounts were written down in the Han era and are not regarded as reliable documentation. He probably studied under the disciples of Zisi, the grandson of Confucius, and around 340 BCE traveled among the Warring States seeking a ruler who would implement his teachings. He visited King Hui of Liang and King Xuan of Qi, who hosted him as an honored adviser, though they ultimately declined to adopt his policies. He eventually returned to his homeland, devoting himself to teaching and recording the dialogues with his disciples, and died, by traditional dating, in 289 BCE.

It was an age of fierce wars and philosophical rivalries among the schools: the Mohists called for impartial benevolence and for rejecting music and rites, the Yangists for self-preservation, and the Legalists for ordering the state through punishment and reward. His book opens with a famous answer to King Hui, who asked what benefit he would bring to his kingdom, and Mencius answered that he should speak not of profit but of "ren" (humaneness) and "yi" (rightness).

## His Contribution

- **Good nature (性善 xìngshàn)**: a human being is born with an inclination toward the good, which can be corrupted by the environment but is not erased by it. The central argument: "the heart that cannot bear to see the suffering of others" (the heart of compassion and pity, 惻隱之心 cèyǐn zhī xīn), and his famous example: everyone who sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion immediately, without thinking of any interest, which is decisive evidence that the "four sprouts of goodness" (compassion, shame, deference, and the discrimination of right from wrong) are implanted in every human being.
- **The priority of the people (民本 mínběn)**: a ruler who does not serve the people loses the "Mandate of Heaven" (天命 tiānmìng). The saying "the people are most precious, then the altars of grain, then the ruler" (民為貴、社稷次之、君為輕) became a reference for Confucian political legitimacy.
- **The great person (大人 dàrén) and the expansive heart**: whoever cultivates "the heart that feels for others" can extend a moral influence that, in his words, flows together with heaven and earth, and moral growth is open, with no fixed ceiling.
- **Humane government (仁政 rénzhèng)**: the good ruler secures the people's livelihood, shares their joys, and cultivates morality in them, rather than ruling by coercion and punishment.

## His Place in the Tradition

Mencius stands at the heart of what is called the "debate on human nature" in early Confucianism, which was never settled. He was regarded, with Confucius, as the "orthodox doctrine" (which Zhu Xi later established) against Xunzi, but the debate remained effectively open over the following two thousand years. His position on evil: evil is not in "nature" but in the loss of the "original heart" through neglect and bad upbringing, which resembles the idea of "corruption" and not "original corruption."

## Moral Education and Reflection on "Qi"

Mencius conceives moral growth as the cultivation of four "sprouts" or "seeds" (duan): compassion, which blossoms into humaneness; shame at evil, which blossoms into rightness; deference, which blossoms into respect for ritual; and the discrimination of right from wrong, which blossoms into wisdom. These sprouts are not completed virtues but a disposition that needs care, and he gave famous examples of this: Ox Mountain, which was stripped of its trees by axes and grazing and appears bare, though that is not its nature; and the farmer of Song who pulled up his seedlings to hasten their growth and ruined them. He asks the learner to nurture the "flood-like qi" (haoran zhi qi) through the accumulation of good deeds and not by force. These images combine the insistence that the good is present with the insistence that education, environment and economic policy are necessary to protect it.

This is linked to his political position: the state must secure for the people a sufficient livelihood (such as the "well-field" system) before demanding virtue of them, and if the ruler turns into a tyrant he has lost the status of king, and his people have the right to depose him, for he described the killing of the last tyrant of the Shang dynasty as the killing of a "commoner," not a king. This view made his book undesirable to some emperors who objected to the permitting of deposition.

## Presence in Other Religions and Cultures

Mencian concepts, especially the concept of "nature" (xing) and the nature of the heart, passed through the Neo-Confucian school to Chinese Muslims, so that the intellectuals of the "Han Kitab" in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, among them Wang Daiyu and Liu Zhi, used Neo-Confucian vocabulary to explain Islamic doctrine in Chinese. Some contemporary scholars compare Mencius's belief in the innate disposition toward good with the concept of "fitra" in Islamic thought, while noting the theological difference: Mencius speaks of "Heaven" as a moral power behind the order of things, not as a creator who gives a revealed law.

## Limits

- Many of his sayings in the book *Mengzi* appear in the form of dialogue with kings and sages, which sometimes conceals the methodical structure of his thought.
- It has not been precisely determined when the recording of his sayings (the book attributed to him) began, or whether he wrote it himself.
- There is no independent information about his intellectual development (his childhood, his direct students, his travels).

## Discussions and Critique

Xunzi held that Mencius misreads reality, for the human being, in his view, is born with an inclination toward profit and envy, and becomes virtuous only through external ritual refinement; and before that Mencius criticized what Gaozi had said, that nature is neutral like the willow from which the craftsman makes cups. Some contemporary philosophers fault him that his plant analogies submerge the argument in biology and ignore the actual moral diversity among individuals. Others say that his evidence, such as the child and the well, shows the existence of early moral motives and not that nature is "good" in the full sense, since the experience itself does not rule out the existence of evil motives. In politics he is criticized because he set no institutional mechanism for holding the ruler accountable or deposing him, but relied on the ruler's virtue and on the "revolution" of the people after injustice has occurred.

## His Principal Works (Attributed)

- *Mengzi* (孟子, "The Book of Mencius," 7 chapters, one of the Four Books since the 12th century CE).
