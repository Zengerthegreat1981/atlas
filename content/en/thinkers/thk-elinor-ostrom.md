# Elinor Ostrom

**Elinor Ostrom** was an American political economist who was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009, becoming the first woman to win it. She received the prize for her pioneering work analysing how local communities successfully manage shared natural resources through self-governing institutions, outside the binary of a central state or the exclusive private market.

## Difficult Beginnings in a Male-Dominated Discipline

Ostrom studied political science at the University of California, Los Angeles (BA 1954, PhD 1965). By her own account she was discouraged from pursuing doctoral study in economics, and she therefore turned to political science, where she developed, together with her husband **Vincent Ostrom**, a hybrid approach combining the tools of institutional economic analysis with the study of local political governance. Together they founded the Workshop in Political Theory and Policy Analysis at Indiana University in 1973.

## The Groundwater Basins of California

Her practical interest in the problem of common resources began with her doctoral dissertation on how dozens of competing water companies in a groundwater basin in southern California dealt with the risk of over-extraction that threatened the basin. She observed that the water producers reached, through local negotiation and litigation rather than centrally imposed intervention, agreements that regulated water extraction and protected the basin from overdraft. This planted the seed of her later interest in the self-governance of common-pool resources.

## Critiquing the "Tragedy of the Commons" and Design Principles

Ostrom built her broader research project on a critique of Garrett Hardin's hypothesis in his article "The Tragedy of the Commons" (1968), which assumed that any common resource without individual ownership or government intervention is inevitably depleted. In her book *Governing the Commons* (1990), Ostrom assembled dozens of successful and failed field cases of managing common resources around the world. From them she derived a set of "design principles" shared by the successful cases: clearly defined boundaries of authorised users, rules of use devised by the users themselves to suit their local conditions, low-cost mutual monitoring, and graduated sanctions for violators that start light and become stricter with repetition.

## The Institutional Analysis and Development Framework

Ostrom and her research team developed a general methodological framework known as **Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD)** for studying any social situation in which a group of individuals interacts within defined rules, whether the subject is natural resources or other public services. It allows a systematic comparison between different institutional arrangements across diverse cultural contexts, rather than merely a narrative description of isolated cases. In outline, the framework treats an "action situation" (participants, positions, available actions, information, payoffs and the links between actions and outcomes) as shaped by three groups of external variables: the biophysical conditions, the attributes of the community, and the rules in use. Interactions in the action situation produce outcomes that are evaluated and can feed back on those variables. Ostrom distinguished several types of rules (boundary, position, choice, aggregation, information, payoff and scope rules). Later, with colleagues, she extended the approach into a framework for analysing social-ecological systems.

## The Nobel Prize and an Impact Beyond Economics

Ostrom shared the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009 with Oliver Williamson, "for her analysis of economic governance, especially the commons". The impact of her work extended beyond economics alone, to include policies on forest management and fisheries, and even the debates on managing shared digital and climate-related resources globally.

## Rigorous Fieldwork amid Early Academic Scepticism

In the early stages of her research career, Ostrom faced scepticism from fellow economists, who regarded her method of multiple field case studies as less "scientific" than the abstract mathematical modelling prevalent at the time. She insisted that understanding a complex phenomenon such as the collective governance of resources required first a broad, carefully documented empirical base before any theoretical generalisation. She applied a systematic standard in documenting each case, which gave her conclusions an empirical grounding that later won wide recognition.

## A Model for Breaking into a Male-Dominated Field

Ostrom's career is also remembered as a symbol of challenging the historical gender barriers in academic economics. Discouraged from entering doctoral economics in the 1950s, she studied political science instead. She eventually became, through an unconventional interdisciplinary path, the first woman ever to win the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences since the prize was established in 1969. This has made her professional story itself a recurring subject in discussions of diversity in the economics profession and of the historical barriers facing women researchers.

## Continuing Work Until Late in Life

Ostrom continued her research until the end of her life. In her final years she extended her analytical framework to larger global challenges, such as the governance of the shared atmosphere in the context of climate change. She argued that effective solutions to complex global environmental problems may draw on principles similar to those she observed in small local commons, while acknowledging the real additional difficulties of moving the scale of analysis from a small village to the entire planet.
