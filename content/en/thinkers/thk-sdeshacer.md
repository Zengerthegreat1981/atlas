# Steve de Shazer

An American psychotherapist (born in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, in 1940 and died in Vienna in 2005), founder, with Insoo Kim Berg, of **Solution-Focused Brief Therapy** (SFBT). He is regarded as one of the most important contributors to the second wave of family therapy (after Minuchin/Bateson).

## What He Contributed

### 1. The Shift from "Problem" to "Solution"

De Shazer developed a radical approach to therapy:
- **Traditional therapy**: begins by understanding the problem, then searches for a solution.
- **SFBT**: begins **with the solution**, and understands the problem only to the extent that helps reach the solution.

### 2. The Brief Family Therapy Center

De Shazer founded (with Berg and others) the **Brief Family Therapy Center** in Milwaukee (1978), which became a global reference in brief therapy.

### 3. The Founding Questions and Techniques

- **The Miracle Question**: "Suppose a miracle happened; what would you notice tomorrow?"
- **Scaling Questions**: "From 1 to 10, how much have you improved?"
- **Exception Questions**: "When were things better?"
- **Coping Questions**: "How do you cope with this?" (focusing on strength rather than helplessness).
- **Presupposition**: assuming that the client has the capacity to change.

### 4. The Theoretical Philosophy

SFBT established a pragmatic philosophy:
- **The solution is all that matters** (Solution is all that matters).
- **If it ain't broke, don't fix it**: "If it ain't broke, don't fix it."
- **Constructive Optimism**: assume the client will progress.

## His Place in the Current

- **Versus Minuchin (structural)**: de Shazer is not concerned with structure, only with what works.
- **Versus the MRI (Palo Alto) brief therapy**: de Shazer was influenced by the MRI approach, by Gregory Bateson and by Milton Erickson before developing his own SFBT.
- **Versus CBT**: SFBT focuses on the present and future, not on restructuring beliefs.
- **Versus Emotion-Focused Therapy (EFT)**: SFBT is less emotional and more goal-oriented.

## Major Works

- **"Keys to Solution in Brief Therapy" (1985)**: his founding work.
- **"Putting Difference to Work" (1991)**: the philosophy of difference.
- **"More Than Miracles" (posthumous, 2007)**: with Dolan and others.
- **"Clues: Investigating Solutions in Brief Therapy" (1988)**.
- **"Words Were Originally Magic" (1994)**.

## Later Research

- **Gingerich & Eisengart (2000)**: a review of outcome research concluding that the controlled studies give preliminary support to SFBT.
- **Franklin and colleagues (2012)**: an edited handbook of evidence-based practice in SFBT, including school and social work contexts.

## Applications

- **Individual and group therapy**: typically a small number of sessions.
- **Crises**: rapid intervention.
- **School contexts**: solving behavioral problems.
- **Work with minorities**: culturally respectful.
- **Family therapy**: short-term.

## Criticisms

- **Superficiality**: "searching for the solution" seems superficial in some cases (such as deep trauma).
- **Ignoring causes**: it is not concerned with root causes.
- **Limited application**: it is not for all disorders (acute psychosis, severe addiction).
- **Influence of the culture of the age**: it may accord with the contemporary philosophy of the "quick fix."

## Importance for the Atlas

SFBT is among the most important approaches that:
- restored **hope and empowerment** to therapy.
- reduced **stigma** by focusing on "what works" rather than "what is broken."
- demonstrated that **few sessions** may be sufficient for many cases.
- produced **techniques applicable** outside the clinic (in education, work, relationships).

## The Later Philosophical Foundations

After *Keys to Solution*, de Shazer moved in his work from describing the method to reflecting on its linguistic foundation. In *Words Were Originally Magic* (1994) he drew on the later Wittgenstein and on Derrida's critique of meaning, to hold that therapy is conversation and that what happens in it is a change in the language game between therapist and client, not the discovery of a truth hidden in the family. Hence his emphasis that talk about the solution brings about change in itself, and that the therapist does not need a theory of the causes of the problem. His practical rules came down to this: the client should do more of what works, stop what does not work and try something else, and not fix what they do not complain about.
