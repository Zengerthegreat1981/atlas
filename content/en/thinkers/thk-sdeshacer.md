# Steve de Shazer

Steve de Shazer (1940–2005) was an American psychotherapist, author, and primary co-founder—together with his wife Insoo Kim Berg—of **Solution-Focused Brief Therapy** (SFBT). Born in Milwaukee, Wisconsin, and dying in Vienna, Austria, de Shazer was a pivotal architect of the second wave of systemic and family therapies, radically steering clinical intervention away from analyzing pathology and etiology toward constructing practical solutions.

## The Brief Family Therapy Center and Collaborators

In 1978, de Shazer and Berg co-founded the **Brief Family Therapy Center** (BFTC) in Milwaukee, Wisconsin. The institute served as a clinical laboratory where therapists conducted sessions behind one-way mirrors with observing team consultation, analyzing video recordings frame by frame to observe which therapist interventions generated immediate, observable progress. 

The core BFTC development team included key colleagues such as Alex Molnar (who collaborated closely on the application of solution-focused strategies in educational and school settings), Eve Lipchik, Elam Nunnally, and Marilyn LaCourt. Contemporaneously, kindred thinkers such as William (Bill) O'Hanlon (a former student of Milton Erickson who co-developed "Solution-Oriented Therapy" with Michele Weiner-Davis) expanded the broader solution-oriented movement, demonstrating that therapeutic change does not require deep historical exploration or insight into pathology.

## Core Theoretical Philosophy

De Shazer introduced a fundamental paradigm shift in clinical practice:
- **Shift from Problem to Solution**: Traditional approaches assume that resolving a complaint requires identifying its historical cause and diagnosing systemic dysfunction. De Shazer insisted that the etiology of a problem is largely irrelevant to its solution, and that analyzing problems often entrenches "problem-talk."
- **Pragmatic Ground Rules**:
  - *If it ain't broke, don't fix it*: Intervene only where the client identifies a concern.
  - *Once you know what works, do more of it*: Amplify existing successful patterns.
  - *If it doesn't work, don't do it again; do something different*: Abandon unproductive strategies rather than pathologizing client resistance.
- **Linguistic and Constructivist Roots**: Influenced by Ludwig Wittgenstein's ordinary language philosophy and Jacques Derrida's deconstruction, de Shazer's later work—notably *Words Were Originally Magic* (1994)—posited that therapy is fundamentally a language game. Changing the conversational framing from problem-saturated descriptions to "solution-talk" actively creates new behavioural possibilities.

## Core Clinical Techniques

SFBT developed precise, structured conversational interventions designed to elicit client strengths and resources:

- **The Miracle Question**: Formulated to help clients visualize a detailed, concrete future unburdened by the presenting issue: *"Suppose you go to sleep tonight and a miracle happens, and the problem that brought you here is solved. Because you were sleeping, you did not know the miracle occurred. What will be the first small clues tomorrow morning that tell you life is different?"* This bypasses despair and generates actionable, behavioral goals.
- **Exception-Seeking Questions**: Grounded in the premise that no problem is constant. The therapist systematically searches for exceptions: *"Tell me about times when the urge was present, but you managed not to give in?"* or *"When were things even slightly better this week?"* These exceptions are mined to identify strategies the client already possesses.
- **Scaling Questions**: Subjective numeric scales (typically 0 to 10) used to measure intangible states such as hope, confidence, distress, and progress. Asking *"On a scale of 0 to 10, where 10 is where you want to be, where are you today?"* followed by *"What would moving just from a 4 to a 5 look like?"* breaks overwhelming problems into incremental, achievable steps.
- **Coping Questions**: Deployed when clients feel overwhelmed and unable to identify positive exceptions: *"How have you managed to keep going under such difficult circumstances?"* or *"What prevented things from becoming worse?"* Coping questions reframe chronic suffering as active endurance and resourcefulness.

## Place in the Field and Comparative Context

- **Versus Structural Family Therapy (Minuchin)**: Minuchin intervened to reorganize hierarchical boundaries and family subsystems; de Shazer regarded systemic structures as unnecessary theoretical constructs, attending solely to communicative patterns that foster change.
- **Versus MRI Brief Therapy (Weakland, Fisch, Watzlawick)**: De Shazer trained in and deeply respected the Mental Research Institute model, but shifted from MRI's focus on interrupting ironic problem-maintaining feedback loops to focusing directly on amplifying solution-building loops.
- **Versus Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT)**: While both are goal-directed and brief, SFBT does not seek to systematically dismantle cognitive distortions or prescribe rigid homework protocols, relying instead on client-generated solutions.

## Evidence Base and Applications

Initially met with skepticism by psychodynamic and structural theorists who questioned its brevity and avoidance of deep historical analysis, SFBT gradually established a substantial empirical evidence base. Reviews of the outcome research (such as Gingerich & Eisengart, 2000, and the handbook edited by Franklin et al., 2012) described promising but still limited evidence across several domains, including substance misuse treatment, school-based behavioral interventions, child welfare, family crises, and brief medical social work.
