# Elinor Ochs

An American linguistic anthropologist who carried out comparative ethnographic fieldwork in Madagascar, Western Samoa, and the United States. Together with Bambi Schieffelin, she is one of the founders of the field of language socialization.

## Language Socialization

Ochs, together with Bambi Schieffelin, developed an influential theoretical framework holding that children's learning of language and their learning of the rules of social and cultural interaction are intertwined processes rather than separate ones. The child is socialized *through* language as much as socialized *into* the use of language, so that every linguistic interaction with a child, even in the simplest everyday conversation, carries implicit cultural values about social roles, respect and responsibility.

## Culture and Linguistic Expression

In her comparative study of patterns of language socialization among Samoan and American children, Ochs showed profound cultural differences in how adults interact with young children who are beginning to speak. American parents tend to "simplify" their speech and adapt it to the child's linguistic level ("baby talk," or motherese). Samoan adults, by contrast, expect the child to adapt gradually to ordinary adult speech without a comparable simplification. This reflects deeper cultural differences concerning the concept of childhood and individual autonomy.

## Impact

Together with Schieffelin, Ochs established language socialization as an integrated subfield linking developmental linguistics with cultural anthropology. It showed that language acquisition is not a universal cognitive process detached from culture, but a process shaped profoundly by diverse local cultural values and practices.

## Research on Autism and Family Communication

Ochs later extended her interests to the study of American families with children diagnosed with autism. Using the same ethnographic method, she analyzed how these families reorganize their daily communication and family roles in order to adapt to neurologically different communication patterns. This extends her original interest in how family communication is shaped culturally and socially, regardless of whether a child's development is typical or atypical.

## Comparative Project on Everyday Family Life

Ochs also led a large, long-term ethnographic project studying everyday life inside the homes of middle-class American families in Los Angeles (the CELF project), documenting in close detail the actual daily rhythm of contemporary family life (meals, homework and time management). This extends her method of studying how cultural values are built through the finest linguistic and behavioral details of everyday life.

## Academic Standing

Ochs is a professor of anthropology at the University of California, Los Angeles, where she co-founded the Center on Everyday Lives of Families (CELF) and developed research combining linguistic anthropology, developmental psychology, and the study of autism. She also wrote widely on narrative, notably with Lisa Capps (*Living Narrative*, 2001).

## Early Academic Training

Ochs earned her doctorate in the 1970s, and her early training was shaped by the traditions of the ethnography of communication founded by Dell Hymes and John Gumperz. She then developed, with Bambi Schieffelin, an independent research path focused specifically on children's acquisition of language as a cultural and social process, rather than a purely cognitive one, as was the tendency of the dominant Chomskyan generative linguistics of the time.

## Influence on the Teaching of Language to Children with Disabilities

Ochs's research on autism has been used to inform discussion of how parents and teachers communicate with children with atypical communication patterns. Her ethnographic approach emphasizes understanding how each child participates in communication within a particular family context, rather than imposing a single "normal" model.

## Conclusion

Ochs's framework of language socialization remains an essential analytical tool for understanding how a child is shaped culturally and socially through the simplest everyday linguistic interactions with those around them. She extended the application of this framework from a small Samoan village to diverse contemporary issues, including disability and complex American family life.

## On Video-Based Ethnographic Method

Ochs and her colleagues used precise video recording techniques to document everyday family interaction moment by moment. With their research team, they developed new analytical methods for breaking down these recordings linguistically and socially in detail. This has influenced the development of video-based ethnographic research methods in the social sciences and humanities more generally.
