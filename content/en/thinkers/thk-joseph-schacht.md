# Joseph Schacht

A German orientalist (1902–1969), who taught at Freiburg, Königsberg, Cairo, Oxford, Leiden and, from 1959, Columbia University. He is a leading scholar who developed Ignaz Goldziher's critical approach to early Islamic hadith and jurisprudence, and a major figure in the Western critical study of the emergence of Islamic law.

## Definition

Schacht represents the second link in the chain of German critical orientalism on the origins of jurisprudence and hadith, after Ignaz Goldziher, who opened the question at the end of the nineteenth century in *Muhammedanische Studien* (1889–1890). Schacht applied rigorous philological and historical tools to the texts of early Islamic jurisprudence, and arrived at a radical thesis about the dating of the emergence of legal hadith, which provoked a wide controversy in twentieth-century Islamic studies.

## Historical Context

Schacht studied classical and Oriental languages in Germany and taught at the Egyptian University in Cairo (Fuad I University) in the 1930s, before later moving to Britain (Liverpool and Oxford), Leiden and Columbia. His major thesis was a continuation of Goldziher's critical project, but it went further in questioning the dating of both the text (matn) and the chain of transmission (isnad) of hadith.

## Main Ideas and Method

In his central work *The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence* (1950), Schacht developed methodological tools for analysing hadith chains of transmission, proposing what became known as the "backward projection" theory of isnads. According to this theory, complete chains of transmission that reach the Prophet directly grow gradually over time: the later a hadith appears in the sources, the more complete and connected its chain of transmission appears. For Schacht, this means that many legal hadith actually arose in the second and third centuries AH, not in the time of the Prophet. They reflected juristic disputes between emerging schools (the Hanafis, Malikis and, later, the Shafi'is), and were subsequently attributed to the Prophet or the Companions to lend them authority. Schacht also argued that Islamic law as a systematic body took shape mainly in the second century AH, and that the Prophet's own legal role in it was smaller than later tradition holds.

## Principal Works and Representatives

Alongside *The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence* (1950), Schacht wrote his teaching text *An Introduction to Islamic Law* (1964), a widely used survey in Western universities. His thesis was opposed in detail by the India-born scholar Muhammad Mustafa al-A'zami in his book *On Schacht's Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence* (1985). Al-A'zami argued from early written material and from the practice of early transmitters that isnads existed from an early date, and that Schacht's inferences from gaps in the sources were unwarranted.

## The Continuing Debate

Schacht's thesis, like Goldziher's before him, provoked a broad debate that continues to this day among Western orientalists. They are divided between those who accepted it as a general framework with modifications (such as the Dutch scholar G. H. A. Juynboll, who developed Schacht's "common link" idea into a method of isnad analysis; Harald Motzki, by contrast, argued that isnads can be used to date early material), and those who rejected it entirely. Among those who rejected it was al-A'zami, who considered Schacht's method to be based on a prior assumption of distrust in the internal Islamic system of authentication ('ilm al-jarh wa-l-ta'dil).

## Impact and Criticism

Schacht's method strongly influenced Western Islamic studies throughout the second half of the twentieth century. It became a widely used reference, taught and discussed in Islamic studies programmes around the world, whether accepted in part or subjected to detailed criticism. Although his thesis is no longer accepted today in its original sharp form, even within Western orientalism itself (after the work of scholars such as Juynboll and Harald Motzki), his fundamental methodological question remains present in academic discussion of the history of early Islamic jurisprudence: how do we date the emergence of religious texts that traditions transmitted orally before they were written down?

Schacht also edited early Arabic legal texts and was a co-founder, with Robert Brunschvig, of the journal *Studia Islamica* (1953). His command of Arabic allowed him to work directly from the legal sources.
