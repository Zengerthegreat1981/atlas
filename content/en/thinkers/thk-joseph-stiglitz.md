# Joseph Stiglitz

**Joseph Stiglitz** is an American economist at Columbia University, who was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2001, shared with George Akerlof and Michael Spence, for their research on markets with asymmetric information. He is also one of the most prominent critics of unregulated financial globalization and of the policies of the International Monetary Fund, and he served as chief economist of the World Bank.

## Academic and institutional path

Stiglitz studied at Amherst College and obtained his doctorate from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (1967). He held posts at Yale, Stanford, Oxford and Princeton before joining Columbia University in 2001. He combined an academic career with work in policy institutions. From 1995 to 1997 he chaired the Council of Economic Advisers under President Bill Clinton. He then served as chief economist and senior vice president of the World Bank between 1997 and 2000, a period in which he observed the management of the Asian financial crisis of 1997 at close quarters.

## The theory of asymmetric information

Stiglitz helped establish the field of the economics of asymmetric information. In a joint study with Michael Rothschild in 1976 on insurance markets, he showed how unequal information between insurer and insured can prevent a stable (pooling) market equilibrium from forming, and that an equilibrium may fail to exist at all. With Andrew Weiss (1981) he showed that credit markets can ration credit even in equilibrium. With Rothschild he developed the concept of **screening**, which describes how the less informed party designs contracts that induce the better-informed party to reveal its private information voluntarily. This complements the concept of **signalling** developed by Spence from the opposite side. Taken together, these studies showed that free markets, contrary to the assumptions of the classical neoclassical model, do not necessarily reach efficient outcomes when information is distributed unevenly among participants. This conclusion has broad implications for justifying government regulation of insurance, credit and labour markets.

## Critique of the Washington Consensus

After his direct experience at the World Bank during the Asian crisis, Stiglitz published his influential book *Globalization and Its Discontents* (2002), which sharply criticized what he called the **Washington Consensus**: the standard policy package imposed by the International Monetary Fund on developing and transition economies. It included rapid capital-account liberalization, hasty privatization and strict fiscal austerity, without sufficient regard for local institutional contexts or the proper sequencing of reforms. He argued that this approach worsened crises such as the Asian crisis rather than containing them, and he called for a more gradual approach that builds regulatory institutions before the full liberalization of financial markets.

## Inequality as a central theme

Stiglitz devoted much of his later output to analysing rising inequality in advanced economies, notably in his book *The Price of Inequality* (2012). There he argued that excessive disparities in income and wealth are not an inevitable outcome of free markets but the product of institutional and political rules that can be changed, from tax laws to competition policy and the regulation of the financial sector. He also argued that excessive inequality harms economic growth itself, by weakening aggregate demand and undermining social mobility.

## Nobel Prize and public recognition

Stiglitz was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2001, shared equally with Akerlof and Spence, for "their analyses of markets with asymmetric information". He has remained a prominent public voice in debates on globalization, fiscal policy and economic change, and has participated in many international advisory commissions, among them the United Nations Commission of Experts on reforms of the international monetary and financial system, which he chaired in 2009 after the financial crisis.

## Later positions and criticism of austerity

After the global financial crisis of 2008, Stiglitz continued his critique of the fiscal austerity policies adopted by many European governments in response to the sovereign debt crisis. He argued that they repeated the same mistake he had criticized in the management of the Asian crisis: imposing sharp fiscal contraction in times of recession, which deepens the downturn by weakening aggregate demand rather than curing it. In his view, the priority should have been productive public investment and reform of the banking sector first. In 2008, at the request of French President Nicolas Sarkozy, he chaired the Commission on the Measurement of Economic Performance and Social Progress (the Stiglitz–Sen–Fitoussi Commission) to reconsider measures of economic performance and social progress. The commission called for moving beyond exclusive reliance on gross domestic product as a measure of welfare, and for incorporating indicators of environmental sustainability, income distribution and quality of life into the assessment of economic performance.

## Intellectual style and general influence

Stiglitz combines mathematical rigour in his specialized academic research with a direct popular style in his articles and books aimed at the general public. This has made him one of the most visible economists in the media and in international forums since the turn of the millennium. He has also written extensively on sovereign debt, natural-resource management and reform of the international financial system.
