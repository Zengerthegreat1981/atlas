# Nikolai Kondratiev

**Nikolai Dmitrievich Kondratiev** was a Soviet economist best known for his theory of long economic cycles, known as **Kondratiev waves**, which describe the succession of economic cycles each lasting roughly fifty years. He paid with his life for the conflict between his ideas and official Stalinist doctrine: he was arrested in 1930 and executed in 1938, at the height of the Great Purge.

## From revolution to the management of economic policy

Kondratiev took part in the Russian revolutionary movement from his youth as a member of the Socialist Revolutionary party (not a Bolshevik). After the February Revolution of 1917 he served as deputy minister of food supply in the Provisional Government, but he continued afterward to work in technical roles within the new Soviet state. He founded and directed the Conjuncture Institute in Moscow (1920–1928) and was involved in economic and agricultural planning during the period of the New Economic Policy (NEP) in the 1920s, which allowed a limited margin of the market alongside state control of the commanding heights.

## Long waves in economic life

In 1925 Kondratiev published his best-known study, on the "major economic cycles" (published in English in 1935 as "The Long Waves in Economic Life"). Drawing on long statistical time series for prices, interest rates, wages and production in Britain, France, the United States and other countries spanning more than a century, he concluded that there exist long-term economic cycles, each lasting between forty and sixty years, consisting of a long expansion phase followed by a long contraction phase. This went beyond the short-term trade cycles on which classical and marginal economics focused exclusively. Kondratiev attributed these long waves to deep structural factors, among them the accumulation of investment in major capital infrastructure and waves of radical technological innovation.

## From Kondratiev to Schumpeter

Kondratiev's thesis drew particular attention from Joseph Schumpeter, who later incorporated it into his book *Business Cycles* (1939) as one of three overlapping levels of economic cycles (alongside the short Kitchin cycle and the medium Juglar cycle). Schumpeter linked it specifically to waves of "creative destruction" produced by the clustering of major radical technological innovations, from railways to electricity to automobiles, and named them "Kondratiev cycles" in his honour. This name remains prevalent in the evolutionary economics literature to this day.

## Collision with Stalinist orthodoxy

Kondratiev's theses conflicted with the Stalinist line. Official doctrine treated capitalism as heading toward a final crisis, and treated the idea of recurring long cycles as a political deviation. He was also critical of forced collectivisation. He was arrested in 1930, charged with leading a nonexistent "Labour Peasant Party" (also called the Working Peasants' Party), and sentenced to eight years. Accounts date the sentence to 1931 or 1932. He was shot on 17 September 1938 during the Great Purge. Rehabilitation came in stages: a 1963 decision concerned the 1938 verdict, and the 1932 case was cleared in 1987.

## Reception and later influence

Kondratiev's cycles became a recurring reference in evolutionary economics and comparative economic history. Some economists in the Schumpeterian tradition still use long waves to discuss clusters of innovation. Many others doubt that the statistics support regular cycles of fixed length. His arrest and execution are also part of the record of the suppression of independent economists in the Stalin period.

## Statistical method

Kondratiev's work was distinguished by a statistical care unusual for his time. He did not merely present general observations about economic periodicity; he built his analysis on a systematic mathematical treatment of long time series covering multiple indicators, such as prices, wages, interest rates, and coal and pig-iron production, over more than a century in several European and American economies. He tried to separate the long-term trend from short cycles, using smoothing methods that were relatively advanced by the standards of his era. Many later economists have questioned whether the statistical evidence supports regular cycles of fixed length.
