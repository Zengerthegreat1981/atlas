# Mark S. Granovetter

An American sociologist (born 1943), a professor at Stanford University, and the author of one of the most cited articles in the history of sociology: "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973).

## What He Contributed

- **The strength of weak ties**: In his pivotal article "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) he presented a counterintuitive discovery based on an empirical study of how individuals obtain new jobs. "Weak" ties (passing acquaintances, distant former colleagues, not close friends) are in fact more useful than "strong" ties (close friends, family members) for obtaining new information (such as job opportunities). Strong ties tend to be closed within similar, overlapping social circles that repeat the same information among themselves, whereas weak ties reach distant and varied social circles carrying entirely new information not available within the individual's immediate circle.
- **Economic embeddedness**: He later developed, in "Economic Action and Social Structure" (1985), the concept of "embeddedness." He argued that economic action, contrary to classical economics' assumption of an isolated "rational actor," is always embedded in actual networks of social relations that shape the trust, information and opportunity available to the economic actor.

## Historical Context

Mark Granovetter was born in 1943 and received his doctorate from Harvard University in 1970 under Harrison White, one of the pioneers of mathematical social network analysis in American sociology. This gave him a rigorous quantitative methodological foundation, which he later applied to an empirical question that seems simple but is pivotal: how do people actually find their new jobs? His key research rested on a survey he conducted on a sample of professional, technical and managerial workers in Newton, Massachusetts, together with interviews, asking them in detail about the source of the information behind their current job. He discovered a surprising pattern that overturned prevailing assumptions about the power of family and close-friendship ties.

## Main Ideas and Method

In his pivotal article "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973), published in the *American Journal of Sociology* and later one of the most cited articles in the history of all the social sciences, Granovetter presented a counterintuitive discovery. "Weak" ties (passing acquaintances, distant former colleagues, not close friends or family members) are in fact more useful than "strong" ties (close friends, family members) for obtaining new information such as job opportunities. He explained this phenomenon with a precise structural logic. Strong ties connect the individual to people who often share the same social circle (your close friends often know your same friends), so they tend to have the same information already available to him. Weak ties, by virtue of their relative social distance, reach distant and entirely different social circles carrying entirely new information not available within the individual's immediate circle. These weak ties thus act as "bridges" connecting distant social groups that would not be connected to one another without this passing link. In his other influential article, "Economic Action and Social Structure: The Problem of Embeddedness" (1985), he later developed the concept of "embeddedness," a term earlier associated with Karl Polanyi. He argued that economic action, contrary to the neoclassical economic assumption of an isolated "rational actor" free of any social context (the "undersocialized" model), is always embedded in concrete, actual networks of social relations that shape the trust, information and opportunity available to that economic actor. In doing so he also criticized the opposite, "oversocialized" model, which reduces economic action entirely to prior abstract social or moral rules, calling for a middle position that sees economic action as embedded in a specific network of relations without reducing it entirely to them.

## Key Works

Besides the two pivotal articles mentioned, his works include the book *Getting a Job: A Study of Contacts and Careers* (1974), in which he set out his original field study of job-search mechanisms in greater empirical depth.

## His Place in the Current

Granovetter's work is a landmark of network analysis in sociology, with influence reaching economics and computer science. The concepts of "weak ties" and "embeddedness" later provided a direct theoretical basis for understanding the structure of contemporary digital networks and social media, and for the theoretical link between sociology and economics through what is known today as the "New Economic Sociology," in which Granovetter is a leading figure.
