# Thomas Hobbes

An English philosopher (1588–1679), a founder of modern materialist and mechanical political philosophy and author of "Leviathan", the classic statement of social contract theory. He was born in Westport near Malmesbury and died at Hardwick Hall, and lived a long life that witnessed the English Civil War; he wrote on politics, physics, logic, psychology and religion, and provoked sharp intellectual quarrels in his time.

## Life and context

Hobbes was educated at Oxford (Magdalen Hall), then worked as a tutor and secretary in the Cavendish household and accompanied the sons of the family on trips to the Continent, and translated Thucydides into English (1629). On his third trip he met Galileo (1636) and Mersenne in Paris, and was influenced by the new science based on motion. He wrote "The Elements of Law" (1640), circulated in manuscript, then fled to France as the conflict between the king and Parliament intensified, and lived in Paris from 1640 to 1651, where he taught mathematics to the exiled Prince of Wales (c. 1646) and published "De Cive" (printed privately 1642, published 1647) and then "Leviathan" (1651), which brought on him the anger of the royalists and the Church alike. He returned to England and was subjected to attacks accusing him of atheism, and ended his life as an author and poet and as translator of Homer into English (1675–1676).

## What he contributed

- **A materialist mechanical psychology**: Hobbes was among the first to try to build a theory of man on the principles of motion alone. Sensation for him is not the reception of an image but a pressure transmitted from the external body to the organs, which rebounds outward and appears as if it were a thing out there. Imagination is "decaying sense", and memory is imagination itself when we attend to its age. In this way he reduced the whole mental life to small internal motions with no spiritual remainder, the clearest materialist program of the seventeenth century.
- **Desire and aversion as the origin of value**: all the passions for him are forms of an "initial voluntary motion" (endeavour): an inclination toward or away from. Hence his most famous inference: good and evil are not qualities in things but names for what we desire and what we shun; so there is no "highest good" that people agree on by nature.
- **The will as the last appetite in deliberation**: deliberation for him is an alternating succession of appetites and aversions, and the will is not a free faculty standing above them but simply the last appetite attached to the action. Hence his explicit determinist position, and his reconciliation of freedom and necessity: the free person is one who is not hindered by an external obstacle from doing what he wants, not one whose will is without cause.
- **The state of nature and the social contract**: in the absence of a common power that awes everyone, people are in a "war of all against all", not because of any evil in them but for three structural reasons: competition for resources, mutual distrust, and glory, that is, sensitivity to reputation. Human life is then "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". The way out is a mutual renunciation of the right of nature in favor of a single sovereignty, the "Leviathan", which derives its legitimacy from authorization and not from divine right.
- **Laughter as "sudden glory"**: his analysis of laughter as a sudden feeling of superiority on perceiving a weakness in another or in our own past self was an influential early theory of laughter, and remained a reference point for later superiority and incongruity theories.

## Intellectual controversy

Hobbes entered violent quarrels. He replied to Descartes in the "Third Objections" to the Meditations (1641), conducted a protracted dispute with Bishop Bramhall over freedom and necessity, debated Robert Boyle over the air pump and the vacuum (1661), and failed in his attempts to square the circle, so that mathematicians such as John Wallis mocked him. In "Behemoth" he presented the history of the Civil War with an eye that sees the sedition as a result of the corruption of the principles taught by preachers and universities. In 1666 his book was referred to a parliamentary committee to investigate atheism, and Leviathan was burned in Oxford in 1683 after his death.

## His place in the tradition

Hobbes is among the founders of modern philosophy, and the opposite of Descartes on the decisive point: whereas Descartes kept a thinking substance distinct from the body, Hobbes rejected immaterial substances wholesale. He is the origin from which all later social contract theories departed, whether in agreement or reply: Locke replied to him with natural rights prior to the state, Rousseau with a peaceful state of nature. He is also a distant ancestor of conceptions of man as a system of drives that can be described scientifically.

## Limits and critique

Hobbes was criticized because his conception of human nature is pessimistic and reflects only one kind of person, because the state of nature is a logical hypothesis and not a historical fact, and because his solution by absolute sovereignty justifies tyranny and sets no limit on the ruler's authority except the preservation of the subjects' lives. Locke objected (Second Treatise, §93) that it is foolish to guard against polecats and foxes while thinking it safe to be devoured by lions. Scholars criticized the mechanical explanation of the mind as unable to explain intentionality and meaning. Finally, his relation to religion has remained ambiguous: he is accused of atheism because of his materialism, and he affirms in Leviathan an unconventional Christian theology, so readings contend over his position.

## Arabic Translations

Hobbes's masterwork *Leviathan* was translated into Arabic in a complete scholarly edition by Diana Habib Harb and Bushra Sa'b, edited and introduced by Ridwan al-Sayyid as *al-Livyathan: al-Usul al-Tabi'iyya wa al-Siyasiyya li-Sultat al-Dawla* (Abu Dhabi: Kalima, 2011). While *Leviathan* has received detailed commentary and translation into Arabic, *De Cive* (*On the Citizen*) has not received an authoritative standalone unabridged translation, circulating instead through partial extracts, university curricula, and analytical monographs on modern social contract theory (such as works by Imam Abd al-Fattah Imam).

## Major works

- *The Elements of Law* (1640, published in 1650 in two books: *Human Nature* and *De Corpore Politico*).
- *De Cive* (1642): On the Citizen.
- *Leviathan* (1651).
- *De Corpore* (1655): On the Body.
- *Behemoth* (written about 1668, published 1679).
