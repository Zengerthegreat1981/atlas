# Friedrich August von Hayek

**Friedrich August von Hayek** was an economist and political philosopher, born in Austria, whose work shaped liberal political economy in the twentieth century. He was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1974, jointly with Gunnar Myrdal, for work on monetary theory and economic fluctuations and for analysis of the interdependence of economic, social and institutional phenomena.

## From Vienna to London

Hayek was born in Vienna in 1899, where he studied law and economics under Friedrich von Wieser, and joined the intellectual circle around Ludwig von Mises. In 1931 he moved to the London School of Economics at the invitation of Lionel Robbins, where he debated monetary theory and the business cycle with John Maynard Keynes. In 1950 he joined the University of Chicago's Committee on Social Thought (not its economics department). He then held chairs at the University of Freiburg (1962) and the University of Salzburg (1969).

## The Austrian Theory of the Business Cycle

In *Prices and Production* (1931), based on his LSE lectures, Hayek set out a theory of the business cycle that links unsustainable investment booms and later crises to credit expansion. On this account, banks lower the market rate of interest below the rate that would equate saving and investment. Relative prices between stages of production are then distorted, and the boom ends in a costly correction.

## The Debate with Keynes

Hayek conducted an extended intellectual debate with Keynes over the causes of the Great Depression and the ways out of it. He strongly opposed Keynes's prescription of government fiscal stimulus, warning that expansionary government spending entrenches structural distortions rather than remedying them. Subsequent history, however, recorded a relative predominance of Keynesian ideas in actual policy during the following decades, before Hayek's influence experienced a revival in the 1970s.

## *The Road to Serfdom* and a Warning to the General Public

In 1944 Hayek published his best-known book for a general audience, *The Road to Serfdom*. He warned that central economic planning, even within well-intentioned democratic structures, tends by its internal logic to slide gradually toward the concentration of political power and the undermining of individual freedoms. The book reached a wide readership outside academic circles, and it provoked sharp political controversy when it appeared in Britain, which was emerging from the war.

## The Knowledge Problem and the Spontaneous Order

In his essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945), Hayek argued that the central economic problem is coordinating knowledge dispersed among many individuals, knowledge that cannot be gathered in full by a central planning board. Competitive prices, on this view, transmit local signals so that no one person need know the whole picture. He later developed the argument into a broader account of "spontaneous order" in the emergence of social institutions.

## The Socialist Calculation Debate

These ideas formed a central pillar of the **socialist calculation debate** against economists such as Oskar Lange, who argued that a central planner could simulate the market. Hayek held that the problem lay not in computational capacity but in the impossibility of gathering local, dispersed and largely tacit information into a form that could be transmitted to a single centre.

## *The Constitution of Liberty* and Legal Theory

In *The Constitution of Liberty* (1960) and the series *Law, Legislation and Liberty* (1973–1979), Hayek extended his analysis to a theory of the emergence of customary law as a cumulative, undesigned process. He favoured general abstract rules over detailed legislation aimed at particular outcomes. He warned that democracy not constrained by a constitution could slide toward the tyranny of the majority, unless individual property rights were protected by firm constitutional safeguards.

## Founding the Mont Pelerin Society

In 1947 Hayek convened the **Mont Pelerin Society**, a forum of liberal economists and philosophers concerned with the decline of liberal institutions and the growth of discretionary state power. The society became an important network for that revival of liberal political economy.

## Final Years and an Evolving Legacy

Hayek retired from Salzburg in 1977 and spent his final years in Freiburg, where he died. He witnessed a broad political and academic revival of his ideas in the 1980s, as several Western governments adopted economic liberalisation policies partly inspired by his writings. He then saw the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991, which many regarded as a belated historical confirmation of his early warnings about the impossibility of sustaining comprehensive central planning. He died in 1992, leaving a legacy sharply divided in its reception. For his supporters he is a foundational reference for contemporary economic liberalism. For his opponents he is a subject of sharp political criticism, who see his warnings about planning as an exaggeration used to justify dismantling the welfare state.
