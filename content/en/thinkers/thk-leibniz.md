# Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

A German philosopher, mathematician, jurist and diplomat (1646–1716), often called the last **universal genius** (Universalgenie) of the European tradition. He discovered the infinitesimal calculus independently of **Newton**, and founded the theory of monads and the theory of **pre-established harmony** (harmonia praestabilita).

## What he contributed

Leibniz presented an integrated philosophical system in opposition to both **Cartesianism** and **Spinozism**. His most important concepts:

- **Monads** (Monades): simple, indivisible substances, each reflecting the universe from its own angle and not interacting causally with any other. In the **Monadologie** (1714, §7): "Monads have no windows through which anything can enter or leave."
- **Pre-established harmony** (harmonia praestabilita): God decreed in advance that monads proceed in perfect harmony like two identical clocks, so that the problem of mind-body interaction that **Descartes's** dualism could not solve is resolved, without dissolving the many into one substance as in **Spinoza**.
- **The principle of sufficient reason** (principium rationis sufficientis): nothing happens without a reason that explains why it is so and not otherwise. He established it in the **Monadologie** (1714, §32) together with the principle of non-contradiction (§31), and in his correspondence with **Samuel Clarke** made it a tool of attack on Newtonian absolute space and time.
- **The best of all possible worlds** (le meilleur des mondes possibles): the thesis of the **Essais de Théodicée** (1710), a reply to the entries of **Pierre Bayle** in the *Historical and Critical Dictionary* on the problem of evil. **Voltaire's** attack on it in **Candide** (published in 1759, after Leibniz's death) through the mouth of Pangloss is what fixed its satirical image.
- **The identity of indiscernibles** (principium identitatis indiscernibilium): no two substances agree in all their properties and differ only in number; he put it forward against Clarke as evidence that space is not an absolute container.

## Philosophy and science

- **The infinitesimal calculus**: he published it in the *Acta Eruditorum* in 1684 in the article *Nova Methodus pro Maximis et Minimis*, and followed it with the integral calculus in 1686. His symbols dx and ∫ are the ones that prevailed over Newton's notation.
- **The calculating machine** (Machina Arithmetica): he designed it between 1672 and 1673 and displayed its model to the Royal Society in London in 1673 — a design intended to carry out all four arithmetical operations.
- **The symbolic language project**: the **characteristica universalis** (a symbolic alphabet of ideas) and the **calculus ratiocinator** (rules that turn reasoning into calculation) — it remained incomplete and became a reference point of modern symbolic logic.

## Leibniz's legacy

- **Kant**: confronted Leibnizianism in the *Critique of Pure Reason* in his critique of the paralogisms and the cosmological antinomies, and rejected turning the simple substance into an object of knowledge.
- **The logical rediscovery**: Louis Couturat and **Bertrand Russell** — each in an independent book about him — presented him again after his death as a logical philosopher, not an obscure metaphysician.
- **Contemporary analytic philosophy**: **David Lewis** in *On the Plurality of Worlds* built his modal realism on turning Leibnizian "possible worlds" from ideas in the mind of God into existing worlds.

## Major works

- **Discours de Métaphysique** (written 1686): a discourse on metaphysics.
- **Nouveaux Essais sur l'Entendement Humain** (written 1704, and published posthumously in 1765): a chapter-by-chapter discussion of **John Locke's** *Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, and a defense of innate ideas.
- **Essais de Théodicée** (1710): the only major book he published himself in his lifetime.
- **Monadologie** (1714): ninety paragraphs summarizing his system; the title itself was not his own but was given by the publisher after Leibniz's death in 1720.
- **The Leibniz–Clarke correspondence** (1715–1716): five letters from each side with Samuel Clarke acting for Newton on absolute space, the void and the presence of God; Clarke published them after Leibniz's death in 1717.

## Limits

- **The priority dispute with Newton**: the most famous quarrel of his life. Fatio de Duillier accused him of plagiarism in 1699, and the Royal Society — whose president was Newton — formed a committee in 1712 whose report, the *Commercium Epistolicum*, was published in 1713 and ruled in favor of Newton, and Leibniz died in 1716 with the accusation standing. The verdict today is a dual independent discovery.
- **The place of freedom in the system**: if the concept of each substance contains in advance everything that is predicated of it (*Discours de Métaphysique*, 1686), his opponents regard "freedom" in his system as a name for a deferred necessity — an objection that the *Théodicée* (1710) tried to answer by distinguishing between absolute necessity and moral inclination.
- **An unpublished archive**: most of what he wrote remained in manuscript, in correspondence and drafts, and its critical edition (Akademie-Ausgabe) is not yet complete.
