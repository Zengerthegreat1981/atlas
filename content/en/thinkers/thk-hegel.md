# Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel

A German philosopher (1770–1831), a culminating figure of German Idealism after Kant, Fichte and Schelling, and author of the broadest philosophical system of the modern age. He is not an existential thinker, but it is hard to understand why Kierkegaard wrote as he did without him: Hegel is the systematic philosophy against which much of early existentialism defined itself.

## Biography and formation

Hegel was born in Stuttgart on 27 August 1770 to a Protestant family of officials, and entered the Tübingen theological seminary (1788–1793), where fellowship brought him together with the poet Hölderlin and the philosopher Schelling. He then worked as a private tutor in Bern and Frankfurt, then joined the University of Jena in 1801, where he worked with Schelling, and during the Napoleonic war completed the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807). He then worked as editor of a newspaper in Bamberg, and as headmaster of a secondary school in Nuremberg (1808–1816), where he wrote the *Science of Logic*, taught at Heidelberg (1816–1818), and moved in 1818 to the University of Berlin, where he became the most famous professor in Germany, and where he died of cholera on 14 November 1831.

## The System and the Individual

Hegel's systematic philosophy claims to absorb the individual, history and consciousness as a whole within a single comprehensive dialectical movement that ends in the realization of "Absolute Spirit" (Absoluter Geist) for itself through history. The individual in Hegel is realized only as a moment within this wider whole, not as an isolated self that chooses, is anxious and despairs apart from any system. Among his best-known contributions are the "master and slave" dialectic in the *Phenomenology*, where consciousness reaches itself only through mutual recognition by another consciousness; the concept of "sublation" (Aufhebung), which combines cancelling, preserving and elevating in a single movement; and the conception of history as progress in the consciousness of freedom.

## Influence on Kierkegaard

Much of Kierkegaard's work is built as a deliberate attack on this systematic claim: the "individual" against the "public" and the "system", and the rejection of dialectical "mediation" (Vermittlung) as dissolving the sharp existential choice into a comfortable rational synthesis; even Kierkegaard's style of writing under pseudonyms (Pseudonymity) is a structural rejection of the Hegelian philosopher's claim to possess a neutral position above all individual choices. Kierkegaard was not answering a detail of Hegel's philosophy but the ambition of the system as a whole.

## Logic, history and right

In the *Science of Logic* (1812–1816) Hegel wanted to construct the very categories of thought through an internal movement passing from pure being to the absolute idea, without relying on external givens. In the *Encyclopedia of the Philosophical Sciences* (1817) he presented the whole system in three parts: logic, philosophy of nature, and philosophy of spirit. In the *Elements of the Philosophy of Right* (1821) he set out a theory of freedom realized in three moments: abstract right, subjective morality, and ethical life (Sittlichkeit) with its family, civil society and state. After his death his pupils published his lectures on the philosophy of history, aesthetics, religion and the history of philosophy.

## Reception and schools

His school split after his death into Right Hegelians, who defended the agreement between philosophy and established religion, and Left Hegelians, such as David Strauss, Ludwig Feuerbach and Karl Marx, who turned the dialectic into a critique of religion and politics. In the twentieth century Alexandre Kojève re-read him in his Paris lectures in the 1930s, influencing Sartre, Merleau-Ponty and Lacan, and Habermas and Axel Honneth, and after them Robert Pippin and Charles Taylor, rebuilt the theory of recognition and freedom on his foundations. In psychology the debate about alienation and the self was linked with him.

## Limits and criticism

Hegel is often read as the adversary against whom existentialism defined itself; treating him as a direct source of existentialist ideas is a retrospective reading. Other criticisms were also directed at him: Schopenhauer accused him of deliberate obscurity, Trendelenburg criticized him for relying on unproven premises in logic, Marx attacked him for inverting the relation between the idea and material reality, and Karl Popper in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945) faulted him for paving the way for totalitarian tendencies, an accusation that Hegel scholars have discussed, holding that his philosophy of right defends individual freedom in the modern state. The myth of "thesis, antithesis and synthesis" about his dialectic is widespread, but it does not occur in him; it was popularized by later commentators (Mueller traces it to Chalybäus and others).

## Main works

- Phenomenology of Spirit (Phänomenologie des Geistes, 1807).
- Science of Logic (Wissenschaft der Logik, 1812–1816).
- Encyclopedia of the Philosophical Sciences (Enzyklopädie der philosophischen Wissenschaften, 1817).
- Elements of the Philosophy of Right (Grundlinien der Philosophie des Rechts, 1821).
