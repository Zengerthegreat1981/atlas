# Aristotle

A Greek philosopher and scientist (384–322 BCE), a student of Plato and the teacher of Alexander the Great, who developed logic as a systematic discipline and wrote on metaphysics, ethics, politics, biology, physics, rhetoric and poetics. His works shaped Greek, Arabic, Latin and modern thought for many centuries, and in the Arabic tradition he was called "the First Teacher."

## Life and Context

Aristotle was born in Stagira, in northern Greece, to Nicomachus, a physician at the Macedonian court. At about seventeen he went to Athens and joined Plato's Academy, where he remained for roughly twenty years until Plato's death in 347 BCE. He then left Athens, spent time at Assos and Lesbos, where he carried out much of his biological research, and around 343 BCE was invited by Philip II of Macedon to tutor his son Alexander. He returned to Athens in about 335 BCE and founded his own school, the Lyceum, whose members were called Peripatetics. After Alexander's death in 323 BCE, anti-Macedonian feeling in Athens led him to withdraw to Chalcis in Euboea, where he died in 322 BCE.

## Main Ideas

**Logic.** In the works later gathered as the *Organon*, he developed the syllogism and the first formal study of valid inference, and discussed categories, definition and demonstration.

**Metaphysics.** Against Plato's separate Forms, he held that the primary beings are individual substances, each composed of matter and form. He analysed explanation through four causes (material, formal, efficient and final) and distinguished potentiality from actuality. At the top of his cosmos stands an unmoved mover, a pure activity of thought that moves things as an object of desire.

**Natural philosophy.** He understood nature as a realm of things with internal principles of change, and gave extensive empirical descriptions of animals, which are often regarded as the beginning of biology. Many of his physical doctrines, such as natural place and the claim that heavier bodies fall faster, were later superseded.

**Ethics.** In the *Nicomachean Ethics* the highest human good is *eudaimonia*, usually translated as flourishing, which consists in activity of the soul in accordance with virtue. Moral virtue is a settled disposition to choose a mean relative to us, guided by practical wisdom (*phronesis*), and is acquired through habituation.

**Politics.** Human beings are by nature political animals, and the state exists for the good life and not mere survival. He compared constitutions, classifying them by whether the rulers govern for the common good, and favoured a mixed regime with a strong middle class.

**Poetics and rhetoric.** He analysed tragedy, including plot, catharsis and imitation, and wrote an early systematic treatise on persuasion.

## Major Works and Sources of Our Knowledge

The treatises we possess, among them the *Categories*, *Prior Analytics*, *Physics*, *De Anima*, *Metaphysics*, *Nicomachean Ethics*, *Politics*, *Rhetoric* and *Poetics*, were apparently lecture notes or working texts, not polished books. Their present arrangement owes much to later editors, traditionally including Andronicus of Rhodes in the first century BCE. The polished dialogues he is said to have published in his lifetime survive only in fragments. The title *Metaphysics* is not Aristotle's own, and the authenticity or composition of a few texts is disputed.

## Method

Aristotle begins from the appearances (*phainomena*) and from the reputable opinions (*endoxa*) of the many and of the wise, sets out the difficulties (*aporiai*), and tries to resolve them in a way that preserves as much of these views as possible. He insists that each field has its own degree of precision: one should not demand mathematical exactness in ethics. In the natural sciences he combined observation, collection of data and the search for causal explanations, though his ideal of demonstrative science, set out in the *Posterior Analytics*, was more deductive than much of his actual practice.

## Influence and Reception

After antiquity his works were studied by commentators such as Alexander of Aphrodisias and Simplicius. They were translated into Syriac and Arabic and examined by al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd (Averroes), the last of whom wrote extensive commentaries. When Latin translations spread in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Thomas Aquinas and other scholastics built their philosophical theology on Aristotelian concepts. Renaissance and early modern thinkers, including Galileo and Descartes, attacked his physics, but his logic remained a standard until the nineteenth century. In the twentieth century virtue ethics was revived with strong Aristotelian influences, notably in the work of Elizabeth Anscombe, Philippa Foot and Alasdair MacIntyre.

## Criticisms and Debates

Critics have pointed out his acceptance of natural slavery and his view of women as deficient in deliberative authority, both stated in the *Politics*, and these positions are widely rejected today. His physics and astronomy were overturned by modern science. Within the tradition, scholars debate the relation of his thought to Plato's (whether it was a break or a development), the unity of the *Metaphysics*, the status of the active intellect in *De Anima*, and the relation between the practical life and the contemplative life in the *Ethics*, where contemplation seems to be the highest activity.
