# Alfred Marshall

**Alfred Marshall** was a British economist who is regarded as the effective founder of neoclassical economics in its conciliatory British form, and the author of *Principles of Economics* (1890), which remained the standard textbook for teaching economics in English-speaking universities for more than forty years.

## From Mathematics to Applied Economics

Marshall, born in Bermondsey, London, in 1842, studied mathematics at St John's College, Cambridge (graduating as Second Wrangler in 1865) before turning, via moral philosophy, to economics. He drew on his precise mathematical training to formulate simple graphical analytical tools (the supply and demand curves) without burdening the main text with complex mathematical equations, which he referred to separate technical appendices at the end of his book. This was a deliberate methodological choice, aimed at reaching a broader audience of businessmen and policymakers, not only mathematical economists.

## Partial Equilibrium and the Supply and Demand Curves

Marshall popularised one of the best-known images in the history of economics (building on earlier work by Cournot, Jevons and others): the supply and demand curves intersecting on a single simple graph, which together determine the equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity in a single market, under the assumption that "all else remains equal" (*Ceteris Paribus*) in the rest of the economy. This methodological simplification allowed direct, practical analysis that was easy to teach, in contrast to Walras's broader and more abstract ambition of describing all markets together.

## The Concept of Price Elasticity

Marshall devised an original analytical tool, **price elasticity of demand**, which measures the sensitivity of the quantity demanded of a good to a relative change in its price. With it he distinguished between necessities with low elasticity and luxuries with high elasticity, providing a precise quantitative instrument for analysing the effects of taxes and customs tariffs on different goods.

## Distinguishing the Short Run from the Long Run

One of Marshall's most important methodological contributions is his distinction between the **short run**, in which some factors of production remain fixed, and the **long run**, in which all factors become fully adjustable, including the entry of new firms into an industry and their exit from it. This temporal framework made it possible to explain complex economic phenomena, such as the continuation of production despite temporary losses, and the exit of unprofitable firms from the market over the longer term.

## Consumer Surplus as a Measure of Welfare

Marshall developed, from earlier work by Jules Dupuit, the concept of **consumer surplus**: the difference between the maximum price that a consumer would realistically be prepared to pay for a good and the price he actually paid for it. This serves as an approximate quantitative measure of the net benefit that consumers gain from exchange. It later became fundamental in the cost-benefit analysis of a range of government policies.

## Founding an Independent Academic Discipline

Marshall worked hard to separate the teaching of economics from the broader moral sciences at the University of Cambridge. In 1903 he succeeded in establishing the "Economics Tripos" as an independent degree programme, thereby consolidating the image of economics as a distinct academic discipline with its own technical analytical tools, rather than a sub-branch of moral philosophy as had been the case previously.

## Students Who Shaped the History of Economics

Marshall supervised or directly influenced a whole generation of prominent British economists. Chief among them was Arthur Cecil Pigou, who developed welfare economics on the basis of Marshall's tools. Another was John Maynard Keynes, who studied under Marshall at Cambridge before later building a radical critique of core neoclassical propositions concerning the automatic full employment of markets.

## A Deliberately Conciliatory Approach Between the Classical and Marginal Schools

Unlike Jevons, who demanded an explicit break with classical economics, Marshall deliberately presented the new marginal analytical tools (marginal utility on the demand side) as a complementary extension of the heritage of Ricardo and Smith (production costs on the supply side), rather than as a replacement for it. He depicted the determination of price as the outcome of the intersection of the "two blades of a pair of scissors", supply and demand together, rather than as the victory of one side. This conciliatory tendency reflected his general preference for reconciling competing schools rather than provoking sharp theoretical breaks.

## A Joint Intellectual Partnership with Mary Paley Marshall

Marshall married Mary Paley in 1877; she had studied at Cambridge and lectured in economics at Newnham College. The two co-authored his first book, *The Economics of Industry* (1879), before Marshall devoted himself to writing *Principles of Economics* under his name alone. Historians of economic thought have documented Mary Paley Marshall's contribution to the editing, drafting and illustrative examples of many of his early works. Her name has remained less prominent in the history of the discipline than her contribution warrants.
