# Rudolf von Jhering

**Rudolf von Jhering** (1818–1892) was a German jurist and one of the great commentators on Roman law in the nineteenth century, and a significant figure in the move from conceptual formalist jurisprudence (*Begriffsjurisprudenz*) to a jurisprudence concerned with social purpose and interest. He taught at several German universities (Basel, Rostock, Kiel, Giessen and Vienna) before settling as a professor at Göttingen from 1872, and his intellectual trajectory represents a notable change of direction: he began from the heart of the historical and conceptual school founded by Savigny and his students, then turned against its method in the second half of his life.

## From Conceptual Jurisprudence to Purposive Jurisprudence

In his large work *Geist des römischen Rechts* (The Spirit of Roman Law, published in parts, 1852–1865), Jhering began as a scholar in the conventional sense, analyzing the system of Roman law with precise logical analysis. By the 1870s, however, he had sharply criticized what he called "jurisprudence of concepts" (*Begriffsjurisprudenz*): the idea that legal solutions can be deduced purely logically from abstract concepts, without reference to the social and economic ends that law serves. He proposed instead, in his book *Der Zweck im Recht* (The Purpose in Law, 1877–1883), the central thesis that **there is no legal rule without a purpose**: law is an instrument for achieving social interests, and purpose is the creator of all law. In this way he became an important forerunner of what was later known as **jurisprudence of interests** (*Interessenjurisprudenz*), which followers in the Tübingen school (Philipp Heck and others) developed at the beginning of the twentieth century. It holds that the task of the jurist and judge is to uncover the conflicting interests behind the legal text and to weigh them, rather than merely to deduce logically from rigid concepts.

## The Struggle for Law: Law as Conflict, Not Grant

Jhering's best-known work for the general reader is his famous lecture *The Struggle for Law* (*Der Kampf ums Recht*, delivered in Vienna in 1872), which has been translated into dozens of languages and has become a classic of popular legal philosophy. In it he advances a thesis opposed to the calm conception that prevailed in German historical jurisprudence (especially Savigny), which held that law grows organically and without conflict from the "spirit of the people" (*Volksgeist*). Jhering argues the reverse: **the right is not given, but must be won through struggle**; the life of law, at the level of both individuals and nations, is a permanent conflict, and whoever does not defend his right when it is violated contributes to undermining the entire legal order, because every individual who stands as a guardian of his own right stands as a guardian of the law. This thesis has a clear moral and political resonance: asserting one's right is a duty toward both oneself and society, not merely a personal choice.

## Influence and Extension

Jhering exerted a significant influence on the development of American jurisprudence through two main channels: the affinity between his purposive, practical view of law and that of **Oliver Wendell Holmes**, who knew and engaged with Jhering's work, and the direct influence of Jhering's jurisprudence of interests on **Roscoe Pound**'s formulation of what he called "sociological jurisprudence": Pound explicitly borrowed the idea of balancing individual, public and social interests as the essence of judicial and legislative work. His influence also extended in Germany through the school of jurisprudence of interests, which systematically applied his method to the interpretation of the German Civil Code (BGB) after it came into force in 1900.

## Criticism

Jhering was criticized from two opposing directions. On one side, he was accused of leaving his theory of "purpose" obscure about how social interests are discovered or how they are weighed when they conflict, a debate that continued with his successors in the jurisprudence of interests. On the other side, some historians of jurisprudence held that his break with his earlier method was not as complete as he himself portrayed it, and that his earlier work on the spirit of Roman law retained many of the conceptual analytical tools he had later criticized.

## His Place Among the Schools

Jhering occupies a difficult, intermediate position that resists classification: he is not an American legal realist in the later sense, nor an analytical positivist in the sense of Austin or Kelsen, but is usually classified in the current of **purposive or interest jurisprudence** (*Interessenjurisprudenz / Zweckjurisprudenz*), an intermediate current between traditional continental formalism and the sociological jurisprudence that would flourish in the twentieth century. It is true that the term "social roots of law" had not yet crystallized into the form known from Ehrlich or Pound, but Jhering remains the essential transitional point between traditional German jurisprudence of the nineteenth century and sociological jurisprudence of the twentieth century.
