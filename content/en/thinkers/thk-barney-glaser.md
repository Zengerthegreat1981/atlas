# Barney Glaser

Barney Glaser (1930-2022) was an American sociologist and the co-founder, with Anselm Strauss, of grounded theory, a systematic method for generating theory from qualitative data. He remained its most committed defender of a classic, inductive version of the method.

## Life and Context

Glaser studied at Columbia University, where he was trained in the traditions of Paul Lazarsfeld's quantitative methods and Robert Merton's theory of the middle range. He then joined the University of California, San Francisco, where he worked with Anselm Strauss on studies of hospital settings. The pairing of Columbia's rigor with Strauss's Chicago-school fieldwork tradition shaped the method they produced together.

## Main Ideas

Glaser and Strauss argued that sociology had overvalued the testing of theories from grand theorists and undervalued the discovery of new theory from empirical research. Grounded theory proposes that concepts and hypotheses should emerge from systematic analysis of data. Central procedures include the constant comparative method, in which incidents are continually compared, theoretical sampling, in which data collection is directed by emerging concepts, memo writing, and the identification of a core category. Researchers continue until theoretical saturation, when new data no longer alter the categories.

## Method

Glaser's classic grounded theory stresses emergence: the researcher should not impose preconceived categories, and should delay extensive literature review until the theory has begun to form. Coding moves from open, substantive coding toward theoretical coding that relates categories to one another.

## Case Studies

The research on dying in California hospitals shows grounded theory being built. In *Awareness of Dying* (1965), Glaser and Strauss compared what staff, patients, and relatives knew about an approaching death across different wards and hospitals. From these comparisons came the concept of awareness contexts, including closed awareness, suspected awareness, mutual pretence, and open awareness, together with an account of how one context gives way to another and what each means for interaction. *Time for Dying* (1968) turned to dying trajectories: the expected course and pace of a patient's decline as staff perceive it. A sudden or unexpectedly prolonged death could disrupt ward routines and emotional composure, because work had been organised around a different expectation. The concepts were designed to be more general than any one ward while remaining tied to observed incidents. Deliberately choosing settings that differed, for instance in how quickly patients died and how aware they were, illustrates theoretical sampling and constant comparison.

## Contemporary Empirical Examples

Later health research shows the approach, in various versions, applied to new questions. Kathy Charmaz's *Good Days, Bad Days* (1991), based on interviews with chronically ill people, examined how illness disrupts the sense of time and self; it reflects her own development of grounded theory rather than Glaser's classic form. Celia Orona (1990) analysed interviews with relatives caring for people with Alzheimer's disease and traced how gradual loss of the person's identity was experienced over time, along with relatives' efforts to maintain it. Both are studies of the late twentieth century, and they should not be taken to represent current practice as a whole. When reading any study described as grounded theory, it is useful to check whether data collection followed emerging concepts, whether comparison was continual, and whether the result is an integrated explanation or only a list of themes.

## Related Concepts

Related concepts include grounded theory, theoretical sampling, theoretical saturation, coding, middle-range theory, induction, abduction, qualitative research, and the sociology of health and illness. Analytic induction and thematic analysis are useful contrasts: the former tests and revises an explanation against successive cases, while the latter identifies patterns of meaning without necessarily building an integrated theory. Glaser's training also links the method to Robert Merton's middle-range theory and to Paul Lazarsfeld's concern with systematic analysis. Symbolic interactionism, associated with Strauss's Chicago background, is a further connection, although Glaser did not regard grounded theory as tied to any single theoretical perspective. Memo writing, the core category, and constant comparison are its working concepts.

## Influence and Criticism

Grounded theory became one of the most widely used qualitative approaches in sociology, nursing, education and management research. After Strauss and Juliet Corbin published *Basics of Qualitative Research* (1990), Glaser objected that their coding procedures forced data into preset frameworks, and the divergence between the Glaserian and Straussian schools persisted. Other critics, including constructivist grounded theorists such as Kathy Charmaz, have questioned the assumption that theory simply emerges from data without the researcher's own interpretive influence.
