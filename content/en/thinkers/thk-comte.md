# Auguste Comte

Auguste Comte (1798-1857) was a French philosopher usually credited with coining the term "sociology" and with founding positivism. He proposed that society could be studied scientifically and that knowledge develops through identifiable stages.

## Life and Context

Comte was born in Montpellier, France, in 1798. He studied at the École Polytechnique in Paris and from 1817 worked as secretary and collaborator of Henri de Saint-Simon, with whom he later broke. His career was shaped by the aftermath of the French Revolution and the search for social order amid industrialization. His later years included an intense attachment to Clotilde de Vaux, which influenced his turn toward the "religion of humanity." He died in Paris in 1857.

He entered the École Polytechnique in 1814. The collaboration with Saint-Simon lasted until 1824 and ended over intellectual independence and authorship. He then earned a precarious living by teaching and examining mathematics. His attachment to Clotilde de Vaux began in 1844; she died in 1846. He founded the Positivist Society in 1848.

## Main Ideas

- **Law of three stages:** human thought and society pass through a theological, a metaphysical, and a positive (scientific) stage. In the theological stage events are explained by supernatural wills; in the metaphysical stage, by abstract essences; in the positive stage, by laws connecting observable phenomena.
- **Hierarchy of the sciences:** mathematics, astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology, and finally sociology, each building on the one before. The series runs from greater generality and less complexity toward less generality and greater complexity, and later sciences are not reducible to earlier ones.
- **Altruism:** Comte coined the word and made living for others central to his later moral teaching.
- **Positivism:** knowledge should rest on observation and the discovery of laws among phenomena.
- **Social statics and social dynamics:** he divided sociology into the study of order (the conditions of social stability) and of progress (social development).
- **Religion of Humanity:** late in life he proposed a secular religion with rituals and a priesthood of intellectuals, including a calendar and rites in which Humanity replaced God as the object of devotion.

## Method

Comte promoted observation, comparison, and historical analysis as the methods of sociology. He argued that the historical method was particularly suited to social science, and that sociology should seek laws of social order and change. He also listed experiment among the methods of science, while holding that sociology depended especially on historical comparison. Isolated facts were of little use without theory to guide observation, so his positivism was not a bare empiricism. He rejected introspective psychology and was sceptical of applying probability to social questions.

## Influence and Criticism

Comte's program shaped Émile Durkheim, and John Stuart Mill engaged with it both admiringly and critically. Herbert Spencer, though independent of him, is often discussed alongside him, and positivist movements arose in Brazil and elsewhere. Harriet Martineau's condensed English version of the *Course* (1853) widened his audience. Mill accepted parts of the scientific program and rejected the later political and religious system. Durkheim treated Comte as a predecessor while criticizing the sweeping generality of his account of humanity's development. Brazil's flag carries the motto "Order and Progress," drawn from his formula. Critics also note that, in his ideal polity, inquiry was subordinated to moral reorganization. Critics have pointed to the speculative nature of the three-stage law, his authoritarian and hierarchical politics, and the quasi-religious turn of his later writing. Later positivism in sociology differs substantially from his own.

## Applications and Cross-References

The stage model was applied to world history and informed later evolutionary and modernization thinking. Compare Spencer's evolutionism, Durkheim's rules of sociological method, and Marx's historical materialism.
