# Auguste Comte

Auguste Comte (1798-1857) was a French philosopher usually credited with coining the term "sociology" and with founding positivism. He proposed that society could be studied scientifically and that knowledge develops through identifiable stages.

## Life and Context

Comte was born in Montpellier, France, in 1798. He studied at the École Polytechnique in Paris and from 1817 worked as secretary and collaborator of Henri de Saint-Simon, with whom he later broke. His career was shaped by the aftermath of the French Revolution and the search for social order amid industrialization. His later years included an intense attachment to Clotilde de Vaux, which influenced his turn toward the "religion of humanity." He died in Paris in 1857.

## Main Ideas

- **Law of three stages:** human thought and society pass through a theological, a metaphysical, and a positive (scientific) stage.
- **Hierarchy of the sciences:** mathematics, astronomy, physics, chemistry, biology, and finally sociology, each building on the one before.
- **Positivism:** knowledge should rest on observation and the discovery of laws among phenomena.
- **Social statics and social dynamics:** he divided sociology into the study of order (the conditions of social stability) and of progress (social development).
- **Religion of Humanity:** late in life he proposed a secular religion with rituals and a priesthood of intellectuals.

## Major Works

- *Course of Positive Philosophy* (*Cours de philosophie positive*, six volumes, 1830-1842)
- *Discourse on the Positive Spirit* (1844)
- *System of Positive Polity* (*Système de politique positive*, four volumes, 1851-1854)
- *Catechism of Positive Religion* (1852)

## Method

Comte promoted observation, comparison, and historical analysis as the methods of sociology. He argued that the historical method was particularly suited to social science, and that sociology should seek laws of social order and change.

## Influence and Criticism

Comte's program shaped Émile Durkheim, and John Stuart Mill engaged with it both admiringly and critically. Herbert Spencer, though independent of him, is often discussed alongside him, and positivist movements arose in Brazil and elsewhere. Critics have pointed to the speculative nature of the three-stage law, his authoritarian and hierarchical politics, and the quasi-religious turn of his later writing. Later positivism in sociology differs substantially from his own.

## Applications and Cross-References

The stage model was applied to world history and informed later evolutionary and modernization thinking. Compare Spencer's evolutionism, Durkheim's rules of sociological method, and Marx's historical materialism.
