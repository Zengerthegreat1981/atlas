# Paul Rabinow: Reflexive Anthropology, Ethics, and the Study of Human Practices

Paul Rabinow (b. 1944) pioneered **reflexive anthropology**, an approach that makes explicit the relationship between the anthropologist and those being studied, examining how fieldwork relationships shape knowledge production. His *Reflections on Fieldwork in Morocco* (1977), an innovative ethnographic account that foregrounds the ethnographer's emotional experiences and ethical dilemmas, transformed how anthropologists understood fieldwork and representation. Beyond reflexivity, Rabinow has developed a distinctive approach to studying human practices and ethics, particularly in science, medicine, and technology, arguing that anthropology should examine how people constitute themselves as ethical subjects and how they engage with systems of knowledge and power. His emphasis on the "contemporary" as a distinct anthropological domain—the present lived experiences of human beings navigating complex systems—offers an alternative to historical or purely comparative anthropology.

## Biographical Context and Intellectual Formation

Paul Rabinow was born in Chicago and studied at Duke University and the University of Chicago, where he received his doctorate in anthropology. He was heavily influenced by Michel Foucault, whose work on genealogy, ethics, and the constitution of subjects shaped Rabinow's approach. He has held positions at the University of California, Berkeley, where he directed the Center for Middle Eastern Studies and became a leading figure in anthropological engagement with ethics, science, and the contemporary.

Rabinow's intellectual formation combined anthropological fieldwork with philosophical engagement with ethics, epistemology, and the question of human freedom. He studied in French intellectual traditions, which influenced his engagement with Foucault, structuralism, and poststructuralism. His interdisciplinary positioning enabled him to bring philosophical and theoretical sophistication to anthropological practice while maintaining commitment to ethnographic engagement.

## Methodological Innovations: Reflexive Anthropology and Ethical Anthropology

Rabinow's central methodological innovations centered on **reflexivity** and **ethics**. His reflexivity was distinctive because it was not merely acknowledgment of the anthropologist's subjectivity but careful examination of the relationship between researcher and researched—how fieldwork relationships are formed, how they shape what is known, and how they raise ethical dilemmas.

His major innovations included: (1) **foregrounding emotional experience and ethical dilemmas in fieldwork**—treating these not as problems to be overcome but as central data about the research process itself; (2) **examining how the anthropologist is constituted through fieldwork**—understanding fieldwork as transforming the researcher as well as producing knowledge; (3) **studying human practices and ethical self-constitution**—examining not just what people believe or do but how they constitute themselves as ethical subjects; (4) **attending to relationships between knowledge and power**—following Foucault, examining how systems of knowledge shape human experience and how people resist or negotiate these systems.

Rabinow's approach differed from some reflexive anthropology by insisting that reflexivity must be grounded in ethical concern—commitment to understanding how fieldwork relationships involve ethical responsibilities and how anthropology should serve human flourishing rather than merely produce knowledge.

## Major Works and Theoretical Development

Rabinow's publications established reflexive and ethical anthropology as central concerns:

- **Reflections on Fieldwork in Morocco** (1977) — His groundbreaking reflexive ethnographic account, foregrounding his emotional experiences, ethical dilemmas, and the formation of fieldwork relationships. Rather than presenting polished ethnographic findings, Rabinow presented the messy, uncertain reality of ethnographic work.

- **Symbolic Domination: Cultural Form and Historical Change in Morocco** (1975) — Ethnographic study of Moroccan society examining symbolic systems and historical transformation.

- **French Modern: Norms and Forms of the Social Environment** (1989) — Study of French rationalism and modernization examining how people encounter rational systems and constituted as modern subjects.

- **Essays on the Anthropology of Reason** (1996) — Collection establishing his approach to studying human practices, reasoning, and ethical self-constitution, particularly in scientific and medical contexts.

- **Making PCR: A Story of Biotechnology** (1996, with Dan Rabinow) — Ethnographic study of polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technology, examining how scientific practices are constituted and how technology transforms human capability and ethics.

- **Marking Time: The Epic Quest to Invent the Perfect Calendar** (2007) — Study of calendar history examining how humans attempt to impose rational order on time and the ethical implications of these efforts.

These works established reflexive and ethical anthropology as essential approaches to anthropological practice.

## Fieldwork Practice: Morocco and Contemporary Scientific Practice

Rabinow's fieldwork in Morocco (1969–1971) exemplified his reflexive approach. Rather than claiming to have achieved objective understanding through immersion and observation, Rabinow's account foregrounded the formation of fieldwork relationships, the emotions these generated, and the ethical dilemmas they raised. He discussed his frustrations, his attractions to and repulsions from research subjects, his failure to fully understand Moroccan culture, the ambivalence of fieldwork relationships. This honesty transformed how anthropologists understood fieldwork—revealing it not as a transparent means of accessing cultural knowledge but as an emotionally charged, ethically complex social relationship.

His later work on science and technology involved fieldwork in laboratories, hospitals, and scientific institutions, studying how scientific practices are constituted, how scientists understand their work ethically, and how scientific knowledge shapes human experience. This work examined not culture in traditional sense but contemporary practices through which people engage with knowledge systems and constitute themselves as ethical subjects.

## Theoretical Impact: Reflexivity, Ethics, and the Contemporary

Rabinow's epistemological contribution positioned reflexivity and ethics as central to anthropological knowledge production. Rather than treating reflexivity as a methodological problem or a form of self-indulgence, he demonstrated that examining the relationship between researcher and researched was essential to understanding how knowledge is produced and what responsibilities knowledge production entails.

His emphasis on ethics—on understanding how people constitute themselves as ethical subjects and on anthropology's responsibility to support human flourishing—offered a distinctive vision of anthropological purpose. Rather than producing objective knowledge about culture, anthropology should examine how people engage with ethical questions and how anthropology can contribute to human freedom and dignity.

His focus on the "contemporary"—on the present, on how people live now, on contemporary practices and dilemmas—distinguished his work from anthropology focused on the past or on exotic difference. The contemporary, he argued, is itself a distinctive anthropological domain deserving serious attention.

## Critics and Responses to Reflexive and Ethical Anthropology

Rabinow's reflexive approach has influenced anthropological practice, but also generated debate. Some anthropologists worry that foregrounding the ethnographer's experience risks centering the researcher rather than the researched, a form of narcissism. Others argue that Rabinow's emphasis on ethical individual responsibility might obscure structural power relations and institutional constraints.

His engagement with Foucault and French theory has been critiqued for introducing philosophical abstraction into ethnography. Some anthropologists argue that his work is more philosophical essay than ethnography, and that it risks losing the grounded, particular knowledge that traditional ethnography pursues.

However, Rabinow's influence has been substantial. Contemporary anthropologists increasingly engage with questions of reflexivity, ethics, and researcher responsibility that Rabinow's work raised. His work on science, technology, and the contemporary has opened new anthropological domains.

## Postcolonial Positioning and Ethical Responsibility

Rabinow's work on ethics and responsibility arose partly from engagement with postcolonial critique. His reflexive anthropology acknowledged that fieldwork occurs within power relationships and that the anthropologist bears ethical responsibilities to research communities. His emphasis on understanding how people constitute themselves ethically suggested that anthropology should engage with others' ethical frameworks respectfully rather than treating them merely as data.

His work on science and technology examines how Western scientific knowledge systems circulate globally and how people engage with these systems—questions relevant to postcolonial analysis of knowledge and power. His emphasis on ethical anthropology that supports human flourishing offers a framework for research that serves rather than exploits.

## Contemporary Relevance and Legacy

Rabinow's reflexive and ethical approach has become foundational to contemporary anthropology. Few anthropologists would deny that fieldwork relationships are ethically complex and that researchers bear responsibilities to research communities. His work on science, technology, and the contemporary has opened new anthropological domains, particularly in science and technology studies (STS) and the anthropology of biomedicine.

Contemporary anthropologists engaging with artificial intelligence, biotechnology, medical practices, and digital systems build on frameworks Rabinow established. His insistence that anthropology should examine contemporary human practices and ethical dilemmas, rather than focusing only on the past or the exotic, has proven durably influential.

Questions remain about how reflexive and ethical anthropology can operate at scale in globally networked research, how it can address structural power inequalities beyond individual relationship ethics, and how it engages with non-Western ethical frameworks. Rabinow's later work continues to explore these questions.
