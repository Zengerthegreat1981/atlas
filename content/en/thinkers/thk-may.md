# Rollo May

Rollo Reese May (1909–1994) was an American psychologist and psychotherapist, the bridge who carried the European existential tradition into English and wrote the first serious American treatment of existential anxiety. Without him that tradition would have remained confined to German and French for American therapists.

## Life and Context

May was born in Ada, Ohio, and died in Tiburon, California. He studied English literature at Oberlin College, then spent three years teaching in Greece, and in the summer of 1932 attended Alfred Adler's seminars in Vienna. Back in the United States he studied at Union Theological Seminary in New York, where Paul Tillich became his closest friend and teacher, and in 1949 earned a doctorate in clinical psychology at Columbia University; his dissertation became his first book on anxiety. Around the time he completed his doctorate he contracted tuberculosis and spent about eighteen months in a sanatorium, an experience he regarded as the start of his reflections on the fear of death. He was later associated with the William Alanson White Institute and with Saybrook Graduate School in California.

## Main Ideas

**Normal and neurotic anxiety.** The key distinction is between normal anxiety, inherent in existence because the human being knows they will die and are free and responsible, and neurotic anxiety, produced by fleeing from normal anxiety and from responsibility. Anxiety is therefore not always a symptom to be removed; it can be a condition of growth and creativity. The aim of therapy is not that anxiety vanish but that it be borne and transformed. A distinction simple in appearance, and the basis of much later practice.

**Non-being.** For May anxiety is not primarily death anxiety but a confrontation with non-being in general, including the loss of meaning, values and identity. In *The Meaning of Anxiety* (1950) he drew on Kierkegaard, Freud, Sullivan and Goldstein.

**The daimonic.** In *Love and Will* (1969) he treats love and will as paired capacities in trouble in modern culture, and introduces the "daimonic", a natural psychological energy that can destroy or create contingent on whether it is integrated into the personality.

**Creativity, power, myth.** *Power and Innocence* (1972), *The Courage to Create* (1975), *Freedom and Destiny* (1981) and *The Cry for Myth* (1991) deal with themes largely neglected by later existential clinicians.

## The Volume That Introduced Existential Analysis

With Ernest Angel and Henri Ellenberger, May co-edited *Existence: A New Dimension in Psychiatry and Psychology* (1958), which introduced European existential analysis (Binswanger and others) to an American readership. He wrote the opening chapters on the origins and significance of the existential movement for therapy.

## What Distinguishes Him from Yalom

Broader philosophically and less systematic. Yalom orders existential concerns into four (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness) and builds a method on them; May's work reads closer to a philosophical essay than a clinical manual.

## Reception and Criticism

His books, especially *Love and Will*, reached a wide public beyond the profession. Within humanistic psychology he disagreed with Carl Rogers about the nature of evil and with Maslow about the optimism of spontaneous growth. Meta-analytic work (Vos, Craig & Cooper, 2015) found existential therapies had a moderate effect compared with no treatment and comparable outcomes to other therapies, with a limited number of studies. May himself did no empirical research, and critics find the daimonic vague and hard to operationalise or measure.
