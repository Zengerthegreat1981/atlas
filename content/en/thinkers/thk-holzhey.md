# Alice Holzhey-Kunz

Alice Holzhey-Kunz (born 1943) is a Swiss philosopher, psychoanalyst, and Daseinsanalyst. Trained in Zurich, she is a prominent contemporary voice in existential analysis (*Daseinsanalyse*). Her work represents a systematic renewal of Daseinsanalysis that synthesizes Martin Heidegger's fundamental ontology with Sigmund Freud's clinical discoveries, offering a distinct hermeneutic paradigm for understanding psychopathology.

## Background and Formation

Daseinsanalysis originated in the mid-twentieth century through the pioneering work of Swiss psychiatrists Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss, who applied the ontological insights of Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927) to clinical psychiatry. Boss subsequently collaborated closely with Heidegger during the Zollikon Seminars (1959–1969). 

Holzhey-Kunz studied philosophy and history in Zurich, completing her doctorate, and then completed clinical training in Daseinsanalysis in Zurich. She has been associated with the Daseinsanalytic Seminar (*Daseinsanalytisches Seminar*) in Zurich and with the Society for Hermeneutic Anthropology and Daseinsanalysis (*Gesellschaft für hermeneutische Anthropologie und Daseinsanalyse*).

## Theoretical Contributions: Suffering from Existence

While classical Daseinsanalysis sought to replace psychoanalytic concepts of the unconscious with phenomenological descriptions of openness to the world, Holzhey-Kunz argued that Binswanger and Boss had prematurely abandoned Freud's clinical acuity. She proposed a profound reinterpretation: psychological suffering (*seelisches Leiden*) is fundamentally a "suffering from existence" (*Leiden am Dasein*).

In her view, psychopathological symptoms are not merely dysfunctions, biological anomalies, or defensive flights from existential truth. Instead, neurotic and melancholic patients frequently exhibit an acute hypersensitivity (*Hellhörigkeit*) to the universal, anxiety-provoking structures of the human condition—mortality, groundlessness, vulnerability, guilt, and finitude. Where ordinary individuals maintain psychological stability through everyday absorption and tacit ontic denial, the symptomatic person is confronted with disruptive ontological truths that they lack the psychological resources to endure or integrate.

## Clinical Hermeneutics and Emotional Truth

Holzhey-Kunz's framework shifts the clinical posture in existential psychotherapy. Rather than treating symptoms solely as deficits to be corrected, the therapist attends to the symptom as a distorted disclosure of existential truth (*emotionale Wahrheit*). In books such as *Das Subjekt in der Kur* (2002), she argued how psychoanalytic concepts like transference, resistance, and defenses can be understood as existential strategies for managing the burden of being a finite self (*Dasein*). Clinical intervention aims not at restoring a superficial illusion of invulnerability, but at helping the patient acknowledge and bear their existential situation with courage.
