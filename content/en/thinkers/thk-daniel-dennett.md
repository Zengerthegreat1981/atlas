# Daniel Dennett

A leading naturalist and materialist philosopher, author of *Consciousness Explained* and of the theory of the intentional stance.

## What He Gave

- **The Intentional Stance**: his central thesis is that attributing beliefs and desires to a system is not a description of an inner substance but a **predictive strategy** we adopt because it works. Toward any system we can take three stances: the physical (predicting from laws and structure), the design (predicting from what the system was designed to do), and the intentional (predicting by assuming it is rational and believes and desires). To say that a thermostat "wants" to reach a certain temperature is neither an error nor a literal truth: it is a stance measured by its predictive usefulness. In this way Dennett stripped intentionality of its metaphysical obscurity without denying it.
- **The Multiple Drafts Model**: his critique of what he called the **"Cartesian theater"**, the illusion that there is a center in the brain where contents are displayed and thereby become conscious. His alternative: at every moment the brain undergoes parallel editing and revision of multiple drafts, and there is no decisive moment at which a content becomes "officially conscious". Hence questions such as "when exactly did it become conscious?" are questions without a factual answer, not because the answer is hard but because the question presupposes a theater that does not exist.
- **Demystifying the "hard problem"**: his best-known opponent in this debate is David Chalmers. Dennett holds that qualia and the philosophical zombie are intuitive tricks that do not hold up: once you explain all the discriminative, reportative, and behavioral capacities, nothing else remains that needs explanation, and the feeling that something remains is itself a phenomenon that needs explanation, not a given to be respected.
- **"Cranes" in evolution**: in *Darwin's Dangerous Idea* he described natural selection as a **"universal acid"** that dissolves every top-down teleological explanation, from biology to ethics and culture. He distinguished between "cranes" (mechanisms that explain complexity by accumulation) and "skyhooks" (explanations that invoke an intelligence prior to the process), and the latter are what he rejects.
- **Free will worth wanting**: an explicit compatibilist. Freedom is not an exception to causation but a capacity that actually evolved to represent alternatives, anticipate consequences, and modify behavior in response to reasons. This degree of freedom is the **"varieties worth wanting"**, entirely sufficient to ground moral responsibility.

## His Place in the Current

Dennett is a hard-line functionalist in the philosophy of mind, standing on the opposite axis from Chalmers on the question of consciousness, and alongside the Churchlands (Paul and Patricia) in naturalism though differing with them: he does not say that mental states do not exist (eliminativism), but that they are **real patterns** picked out by the intentional stance. Outside philosophy he was known as one of the "Four Horsemen" of the New Atheism, which is the most publicly visible, though not the central, aspect of his philosophical work.

## Biography and Formation

Daniel Clement Dennett was born in Boston on 28 March 1942, and studied at Harvard University, where he received his bachelor's degree in 1963, then received his doctorate from the University of Oxford in 1965 under the supervision of Gilbert Ryle; his dissertation was the basis of his first book, *Content and Consciousness* (1969). He taught at the University of California, Irvine, then in 1971 moved to Tufts University near Boston, where he led the Center for Cognitive Studies for many years and remained until his retirement. He died in Portland, Maine, on 19 April 2024.

## Additional Ideas

Dennett developed a method he called "heterophenomenology", in which the researcher treats people's reports of their experiences as data that need interpretation, not as infallible truths about experience, and treats them as an anthropologist treats the account of a culture's people about their world. In his essay "Real Patterns" (1991) he defended the claim that the patterns picked out by the intentional stance are real because they allow information compression and prediction not available in raw physical description, without the need for a metaphysical substance. In *Elbow Room: The Varieties of Free Will Worth Wanting* (1984) he argued that many fears of determinism rest on simplified images of causation and choice. In his essay "Quining Qualia" (1988) he attacked the idea of qualia as intrinsic, ineffable, and incomparable properties, and in *Kinds of Minds* (1996) he broadened the view to animal and machine minds. *Breaking the Spell* (2006) treated religion as a natural phenomenon open to scientific study.

## Influence and Reception

Dennett had a prominent presence in cognitive science, artificial intelligence, and evolutionary biology, and took part in collective works such as *The Mind's I* (1981) with Douglas Hofstadter. He entered wide controversies with Stephen Jay Gould over adaptationism, with John Searle over consciousness, and with Chalmers over the hard problem. His books addressed to the general public, among them *Intuition Pumps* (2013), made him a prominent contemporary presenter of philosophy to the general reader.

## Limits and Criticism

Searle and other critics accused him of making his book *Consciousness Explained* explain the disappearance of consciousness, not consciousness itself, since it replaces subjective experience with a description of behavior and capacities. Chalmers and Ned Block charged that he ignores the explanatory gap between functional description and experience. Gould held that Dennett exaggerates the explanation of traits by selective adaptation, and wrote a sharp critique of *Darwin's Dangerous Idea*. His theory of the intentional stance has also been accused of being instrumentalist, making beliefs dependent on the interpreter's interest, although Dennett in "Real Patterns" tried to give it a moderate realism. Some philosophers hold that his argument on free will is a compromise that changes the meaning of freedom instead of solving its problem.
