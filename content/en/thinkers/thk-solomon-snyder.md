# Solomon H. Snyder

An American psychiatrist and molecular neuroscientist (born 1938), one of the founders of modern molecular neuroscience. He is known for discovering, together with Candace Pert, **opioid receptors** in the brain in 1973, a finding that deepened medicine's understanding of how opioid painkillers act and how dependence on them develops.

## Training and Career

Snyder studied medicine at Georgetown University and graduated in 1962. He then worked at the US National Institutes of Health in the laboratory of Julius Axelrod, who taught him how to use radioactive materials to trace neurotransmitters and brain drugs, a method he later developed further. He trained in psychiatry at Johns Hopkins and joined the faculty of the Johns Hopkins School of Medicine in 1966 as an assistant professor of pharmacology and experimental therapeutics. In 1980 he founded the Department of Neuroscience at Johns Hopkins and chaired it until 2006; the department was later renamed in his honor (the "Solomon H. Snyder Department of Neuroscience"). He also wrote several books for the general public, including *Madness and the Brain* (1974), *Drugs and the Brain* (1986) and *Brainstorming: The Science and Politics of Opiate Research* (1989).

## Contributions

- **Discovery of opioid receptors (1973)**: together with Candace Pert, then a graduate student in his laboratory, he developed the "reversible ligand binding" technique to identify neurotransmitter receptors in brain tissue. They used it to locate opioid receptors for the first time and published the result in *Science* in 1973. The work coincided with independent findings by Terenius in Uppsala and Simon in New York. The discovery earned him the Albert Lasker Award in 1978 and paved the way for the discovery of endorphins and enkephalins (the endogenous opioids produced by the body), since it showed the existence of a receptor that requires a natural substance to bind to it. Hughes and Kosterlitz then identified the enkephalins in 1975.
- **Dopamine receptors and antipsychotics**: with Creese and Burt in 1976, he showed that the ability of schizophrenia medications to bind to dopamine receptors in the brain corresponds to their clinical effectiveness. In doing so he helped turn the dopamine hypothesis from a pharmacological observation into a measurable relationship. Philip Seeman and colleagues reported closely related findings in the same period.
- **Identifying other neurotransmitter receptors**: he and his team developed similar methods for identifying receptors of multiple neurotransmitters and drugs. This laid the methodological foundation for modern molecular neuropharmacology and for understanding how psychiatric drugs (antipsychotics, antidepressants) act.
- **Unconventional transmitters**: around 1990 his laboratory advanced the study of nitric oxide as a messenger in the brain; with Bredt he isolated the enzyme that synthesizes it (nitric oxide synthase) in 1990. His team then studied the roles of D-serine and hydrogen sulfide gas as neuromessengers.
- **Institution building**: he contributed centrally to establishing neuroscience as an independent academic discipline in the United States through the founding of the Johns Hopkins department, which became one of the leading neuroscience programs in the world, and he trained a large number of researchers who became leaders in the field.

## Impact

The discovery of opioid receptors changed medicine's understanding of how opioids (natural and synthetic) act in the body. It paved the way for a deeper understanding of the biology of pain and addiction. It is one of the most cited works in the history of neuropharmacology. The radioligand binding method he helped develop is still taught and used in drug development, as it allows the binding of thousands of compounds to receptors to be screened.

## Limits and Criticism

His core contribution lies in the molecular-biological discovery of neural receptors more than in direct therapeutic or clinical aspects. This reflects the limits of his specialty as a neuropharmacologist rather than a clinician. The discovery of opioid receptors also provoked debate over credit. The 1978 Lasker Award was given to Snyder together with Hans Kosterlitz and John Hughes, without Candace Pert. Pert publicly criticized this exclusion, and it is one of the episodes cited in discussions of how recognition is distributed among graduate students. This entry is educational and not clinical advice. Among the limits of the dopamine hypothesis that he supported is that drug binding to a receptor does not explain the cause of the disease. A drug may act on a pathway without being the origin of the malfunction, and antipsychotics do not treat the negative and cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia well.
