# Solomon H. Snyder

An American psychiatrist and molecular neuroscientist (born 1938), a central figure in receptor neuropharmacology. He is known for identifying, with Candace Pert, **opioid binding sites** in mammalian brain in 1973, a finding that changed how opioid painkillers and opioid dependence were discussed in biological terms.

## Training and Career

Snyder received his M.D. from Georgetown University in 1962. After a medical residency he worked from 1963 to 1965 at the US National Institutes of Health in the laboratory of Julius Axelrod, who taught him how to use radioactive materials to trace neurotransmitters and drugs, a method he later extended to receptors. He trained in psychiatry at Johns Hopkins from 1965 and joined the faculty in 1966 as an assistant professor of pharmacology. In 1980 he founded the Department of Neuroscience at Johns Hopkins and directed it until 2006, when it was renamed the Solomon H. Snyder Department of Neuroscience. He later became University Distinguished Service Professor of neuroscience, pharmacology and psychiatry. He also wrote books for a general audience, including *Madness and the Brain* (1974), *Drugs and the Brain* (1986) and *Brainstorming: The Science and Politics of Opiate Research* (1989).

## Contributions

- **Opioid binding sites (1973)**: with Candace Pert, then a graduate student, he used stereospecific radioligand binding to detect opioid sites in nervous tissue, published in *Science* in 1973. Independent reports came in the same period from Lars Terenius in Uppsala and Eric Simon in New York. The 1978 Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research went to Snyder, John Hughes and Hans Kosterlitz. Hughes and Kosterlitz identified the enkephalins in 1975; the receptor work had made a search for an endogenous ligand a defined problem, though it did not by itself identify that ligand.
- **Dopamine receptors and antipsychotics**: with Creese and Burt in 1976, he showed that the ability of schizophrenia medications to bind to dopamine receptors in the brain corresponds to their clinical effectiveness. In doing so he helped turn the dopamine hypothesis from a pharmacological observation into a measurable relationship. Philip Seeman and colleagues reported closely related findings in the same period.
- **Identifying other neurotransmitter receptors**: he and his team developed similar methods for identifying receptors of multiple neurotransmitters and drugs. This laid the methodological foundation for modern molecular neuropharmacology and for understanding how psychiatric drugs (antipsychotics, antidepressants) act.
- **Unconventional messengers**: with David Bredt he isolated the enzyme that synthesizes nitric oxide, reported in 1990 under the name then used, nitric oxide synthetase (now nitric oxide synthase). His laboratory later worked on D-serine and on gases such as carbon monoxide and hydrogen sulfide as candidate messengers.
- **Institution building**: the Johns Hopkins department he directed from 1980 became a major training ground in molecular neuroscience. He also received the U.S. National Medal of Science in 2005.

## Impact

The opioid-receptor papers changed how the actions of morphine-like drugs were described, by locating a specific binding site rather than inferring one only from behavior. Radioligand binding, which Snyder helped to adapt to brain tissue, became a standard screen in drug development. The clinical problems of pain and addiction are not solved by the existence of the receptor, but later work on endogenous opioids, tolerance and overdose sits downstream of that identification.

## Limits and Criticism

His core contribution lies in the molecular-biological discovery of neural receptors more than in direct therapeutic or clinical aspects. This reflects the limits of his specialty as a neuropharmacologist rather than a clinician. The discovery of opioid receptors also provoked debate over credit. The 1978 Lasker Award was given to Snyder together with Hans Kosterlitz and John Hughes, without Candace Pert. Pert publicly criticized this exclusion, and it is one of the episodes cited in discussions of how recognition is distributed among graduate students. This entry is educational and not clinical advice. Among the limits of the dopamine hypothesis that he supported is that drug binding to a receptor does not explain the cause of the disease. A drug may act on a pathway without being the origin of the malfunction, and antipsychotics do not treat the negative and cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia well.
