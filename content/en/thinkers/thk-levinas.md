# Emmanuel Levinas

Emmanuel Levinas (1906–1995) reverses the order of the whole existential tradition: freedom does not come first — responsibility for the Other does. Where Sartre begins from the self and its liberty, Levinas begins from the face of another person, whose very presence lays a claim on me before I have chosen anything. His thought has been drawn on in the ethics of therapy and care: on this view the therapist's responsibility to the person in front of them is not a contract or a choice, but the condition of the relationship itself.

## Life and Career

Levinas was born on 12 January 1906 in Kaunas (Kovno), Lithuania, into a Russian-speaking Jewish family, and grew up with Russian, Hebrew, and Yiddish. He studied philosophy at Strasbourg from 1923, then spent 1928–1929 in Freiburg, where he attended Edmund Husserl and witnessed Martin Heidegger taking over Husserl's chair. His doctoral thesis, *The Theory of Intuition in Husserl's Phenomenology* (1930), and his translation — with Gabrielle Peiffer — of Husserl's *Cartesian Meditations* (1931) were the route by which German phenomenology entered French philosophy.

In 1940 he was captured serving as an officer in the French army and remained a prisoner of war in Germany until 1945; during those years his Lithuanian family was murdered. From this background he wrote *Existence and Existents* (1947) and *Time and the Other* (1948), and collected his essays on his two teachers in *Discovering Existence with Husserl and Heidegger* (1949). After the war he directed the École Normale Israélite Orientale in Paris from 1946, and from 1947 studied the Talmud with the elusive teacher Mordechai Chouchani. He taught at Poitiers, then Nanterre, and from 1973 at the Sorbonne. He died in Paris on 25 December 1995.

## The Face of the Other

Levinas's major work, *Totality and Infinity* (1961), is his doctoral thesis, and it makes the **face of the Other** its central idea. The face is not a sight or an image but a call: the Other, in their weakness and exposure, demands something of me that I cannot refuse, and this demand comes before any choice or contract. Ethics, in his famous formula, is first philosophy — it precedes ontology rather than following from it. The Other cannot be absorbed into my categories or my projects; they remain infinitely other, and it is that very resistance to being totalised that founds my humanity.

In *Otherwise than Being, or Beyond Essence* (1974) he pushes the idea further still: the self is a "hostage" to the Other, and responsibility cannot be traced back to a consciousness that masters it. Subjectivity itself is formed by being claimed — I become a self by answering for another.

## Against Heidegger and Sartre

The phenomenology Levinas brought to France came from Husserl, but the ontology he opposed was Heidegger's. The opposition was not only theoretical: after 1933, Heidegger's Nazi commitment became for Levinas an unforgivable matter, and he had already written *Reflections on the Philosophy of Hitlerism* (1934), arguing that such a philosophy binds itself to being and blood rather than to ethics. Against Sartre, the contrast is equally sharp: where Sartre sees in the Other a threat that turns me into an object, Levinas sees in the Other the source of my humanity.

Jacques Derrida's essay "Violence and Metaphysics" (1964, reprinted in *Writing and Difference*, 1967) remains the most famous objection: Levinas, Derrida argued, cannot speak of the absolute Other except in the language of the Greek ontology he wants to escape. The exchange shaped both thinkers, and Derrida's later *Adieu to Emmanuel Levinas* (1997) is markedly gentler. Paul Ricoeur also engaged Levinas at length in *Oneself as Another* (1990).

## Influence and Critique

After *Totality and Infinity*, the idea of the face travelled far beyond phenomenology — into theology, the ethics of care, and political theory. His influence in the therapeutic literature itself remains limited, but it is growing in writing on professional ethics and the ethics of care, where his account of responsibility speaks directly to the clinician's position. Adriaan Peperzak's *To the Other* (1993) is a well-known systematic introduction in English.

Levinas is not an existentialist in the school sense, and he built no normative ethics and no rules for decision: anyone asking him for a practical rule finds only a description of the structure of being claimed. He also kept the "ethical" and the "political" apart in a way that has not convinced his critics, and a 1982 radio interview in which he was asked who counts as the "Other" in the Israeli–Palestinian context remains the most cited challenge to the political application of his philosophy. His Jewish and Talmudic writings — including *Four Talmudic Readings* (1968) and *Difficult Freedom* (1963) — run alongside his philosophy, and he insisted on keeping the two discourses distinct, even as the influence of Franz Rosenzweig's *The Star of Redemption* is visible in his idea that totality breaks before what exceeds it.
