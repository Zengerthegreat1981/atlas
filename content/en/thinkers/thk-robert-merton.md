# Robert K. Merton

Robert King Merton (1910-2003) was an American sociologist at Columbia University who shaped modern sociology through his advocacy of "middle-range" theory, his analysis of deviance and anomie, and his founding contributions to the sociology of science. He also coined many terms now in everyday use, including "self-fulfilling prophecy" and "role model".

## Life and Context

Merton was born Meyer R. Schkolnick in Philadelphia in 1910 to immigrant parents, and later adopted the name Robert K. Merton. He studied at Temple University and Harvard, where he took his doctorate in 1936 and was influenced by Pitirim Sorokin and Talcott Parsons. He spent most of his career at Columbia, working closely with Paul Lazarsfeld on empirical research. He received the National Medal of Science in 1994.

## Main Ideas

- Middle-range theory: sociology should build testable theories of limited scope, between detailed empirical description and grand systems.
- Anomie and strain: in "Social Structure and Anomie" (1938) he argued that deviance results when cultural goals and legitimate means are out of alignment, and identified modes of adaptation: conformity, innovation, ritualism, retreatism and rebellion.
- Manifest and latent functions: the intended and recognized consequences of social patterns differ from unintended, unrecognized ones.
- Self-fulfilling prophecy: a false definition of a situation can evoke behaviour that makes it true.
- Reference groups, role-sets and unintended consequences of purposive action.
- Sociology of science: the norms of science, and the "Matthew effect," where recognition accrues to those already famous.

## Case Studies and Examples

In *Mass Persuasion* (1946) Merton studied Kate Smith's 1943 radio war-bond marathon, using interviews and content analysis; the "focused interview" he developed with Lazarsfeld and Patricia Kendall is a forerunner of the focus group. To illustrate the self-fulfilling prophecy (named in his 1948 essay in the *Antioch Review*) he used the run on a solvent bank: a false belief that the bank is failing leads depositors to withdraw funds and so causes it to fail. His doctoral work on seventeenth-century England linked Puritan values to the growth of experimental science. In the sociology of science he showed that credit for joint work tends to go to already eminent scientists, which he called the "Matthew effect" (*Science*, 1968).

## Method

He combined theoretical codification with empirical research, and he argued for a close reciprocal relation between theory and data.

## Influence and Criticism

Strain theory became a foundation of criminology and later influenced Robert Agnew's general strain theory. Critics argued that Merton's anomie scheme assumes universal acceptance of success goals, and that functional analysis can imply that existing arrangements are necessary. His account of the norms of science has been challenged by later constructivist studies.
