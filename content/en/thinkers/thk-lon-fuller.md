# Lon L. Fuller

An American jurist (1902–1978), professor at Harvard Law School from 1940, and the foremost critic of modern legal positivism in the mid-twentieth century. He was not himself a positivist; he is placed beside the positivists in this atlas because his debate with Hart historically marks the limits of modern positivism. He wrote from the standpoint of **procedural natural law**, a position that does not ground law in a divine or metaphysical source of natural law (unlike the Thomist tradition), but in the idea that law has an **internal procedural structure** that must be respected for a system to count as a legal system at all.

## The Central Thesis: The "Internal Morality of Law"

In his book *The Morality of Law* (1964), Fuller argues that there are **eight procedural conditions** that any system deserving the name "law" must satisfy. See **Internal Morality of Law**. A system that fails to meet them grossly (such as the Nazi system) fails to be law in the full sense, not merely a law that is unjust.

## The Debate with Hart

Fuller engaged in a famous debate with **Hart**, published in the *Harvard Law Review* in 1958, which continued in their later books. See **The Hart–Fuller Debate**. The question was whether the positivist separation of law and morality is methodologically sound, or whether it conceals a structural moral condition inherent in the very idea of law.

## The Example of King Rex: Teaching the Eight Conditions

Fuller uses a famous teaching example in his book: an imaginary king named Rex who tries to make laws for his kingdom but fails again and again. Sometimes he issues secret decisions that he never announces; sometimes he enacts contradictory laws; sometimes he keeps changing them retroactively; and sometimes he applies them in a way that contradicts their announced text. From these successive failures Fuller derives his eight conditions: generality, promulgation, non-retroactivity, clarity, non-contradiction, possibility of compliance, stability over time, and congruence between the announced rule and official action. In Fuller's view, the gross absence of any of these conditions does not produce merely "bad law"; it fails to produce a legal system at all.

## Morality of Duty Versus Morality of Aspiration

In other essays, Fuller also distinguishes between the **morality of duty** (the minimum required for organized human coexistence at all) and the **morality of aspiration** (the pursuit of a higher human excellence). He holds that the eight conditions of the internal morality of law belong primarily to the morality of duty. They are a functional minimum, not a loftier moral ideal. This is what distinguishes his position from the more ambitious traditional natural law theories in their moral content.

## Impact and Reception

Although Hart (in his 1965 review of *The Morality of Law*) and other critics, including Joseph Raz, objected to calling Fuller's thesis a "morality" in the full sense, since an effective authoritarian system can satisfy the eight procedural conditions and remain substantively unjust, his work deeply influenced the development of the contemporary procedural concept of the **rule of law**. That concept often relies on his eight conditions as a technical standard for assessing the quality of legal systems regardless of their political content.

## The Nazi Informer Case: The Starting Point of the Debate with Hart

The famous debate between Fuller and Hart began with a concrete case that postwar West German courts handled. A woman reported her husband to the Nazi authorities for remarks in which he criticized Hitler. He was sentenced under a Nazi law then in force, and the later courts then sought to punish the woman herself. Hart asked: should one say that the Nazi reporting law was not law at all (so that the woman is condemned for violating a moral rule rather than a legal one), or that it was bad law that should not be applied retroactively (in which case explicit exceptional legislation would be needed)? Hart preferred the second solution, in order to preserve the clarity of the conceptual separation between law and morality. Fuller held that the Nazi system's failure to meet the minimum of his eight conditions (such as the secrecy and retroactivity of many of its measures) justifies saying that it was not a legal system in the full sense from the outset.

## Fuller's Legacy in Contract and Institutional Theory

Beyond general philosophy of law, Fuller made influential contributions to contract theory. His co-authored essay with William Perdue, "The Reliance Interest in Contract Damages" (*Yale Law Journal* 46, 1936-37), is a key example. He also wrote on the forms and limits of adjudication, treating adjudication, mediation and other processes as distinct procedural mechanisms, each with its own internal logic. This is a natural extension of his central interest in the procedural structure of law apart from its substantive content.
