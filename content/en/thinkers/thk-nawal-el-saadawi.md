# Nawal El Saadawi

Egyptian physician, psychiatrist, novelist and feminist writer (1931–2021). Her work treats the body, sexuality, religion and patriarchal authority together, and it ties the condition of women to class, law and the state. She practiced medicine and public health as well as writing, and she was dismissed, imprisoned and, for a time, abroad because of her positions.

## Biography

She graduated from the Faculty of Medicine at Cairo University (Qasr al-Ayni) in 1955 and worked as a physician in the Ministry of Health. She took a master's degree in public health at Columbia University in 1966 and that year became director-general of the ministry's department of health education. She was dismissed in 1972 after *Woman and Sex* (*al-Mar'a wa-l-Jins*). In 1982 she founded the Arab Women's Solidarity Association, which the Egyptian authorities closed in 1991.

In September 1981, during a wave of arrests of opponents of President Anwar Sadat, she was held without a formal charge in Qanatir women's prison and released in November 1981, after Sadat's assassination on 6 October. She wrote about the imprisonment in *Memoirs from the Women's Prison* (Arabic, Dar al-Mustaqbal al-Arabi, 1984). After reported threats from militant Islamist groups, she spent much of the 1990s teaching in the United States, including at Duke University, and later returned to Egypt.

## Her Contributions

- **Female genital cutting and control of the body**: In *Woman and Sex* (1972), the book that cost her the ministry post, she wrote from medical work in rural Egypt. She treated female genital cutting as a social mechanism for controlling women's sexuality, tied to family honour, and not as a practice that could be explained by scripture or hygiene alone.
- **A layered account of oppression**: *The Hidden Face of Eve* appeared in Arabic in 1977 as *al-Wajh al-Ari lil-Mar'a al-Arabiyya*. It connects women's subordination to economic dependence, personal-status law, male interpretation of religious texts and control of the body, and it sets those factors beside class and colonial power. The English book of 1980, translated and edited by Sherif Hetata, is an adaptation of the Arabic, shaped in part for readers outside the Arab world. The two texts differ.
- **Fiction as social argument**: The novel *Woman at Point Zero* (*Imra'a inda Nuqtat al-Sifr*, 1975) draws on her meeting with a woman prisoner, Firdaus, who was awaiting execution for killing a man who had exploited her. The novel has been widely read in feminist literary studies. It is a novel built from an interview, not a case history.
- **The interconnection of colonialism, patriarchy and religion**: She held that the oppression of Arab women was the product of a threefold intersection: the colonial legacy, local patriarchal structures, and the use of religious discourse to control the female body. This position brought her close to Third-Worldist and postcolonial feminist currents, despite her disagreements with some Western ones.
- **Questioning ready-made identities**: She rejected the sharp separation between "East" and "West" in questions of liberation, and insisted that the repression of women is a structural phenomenon that transcends cultures, although its forms differ.

## Impact

She is a major voice in late twentieth-century Arab feminist writing. She did not found Arab feminism: a women's press and public women activists preceded her. Her books have been widely assigned in women's studies, and she was a prominent campaigner against female genital cutting. In Egypt and the wider Arab world the same books were praised as a demand for liberation and attacked as hostility to religion and social custom. She continued to write against political authority and against religious coercion until her death in Cairo in 2021.

## Major Works

- *Woman and Sex* (1972).
- *Woman at Point Zero* (1975).
- *The Hidden Face of Eve* (1977).
- *Memoirs from the Women's Prison* (1984).
