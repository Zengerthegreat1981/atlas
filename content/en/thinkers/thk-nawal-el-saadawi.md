# Nawal El Saadawi

Egyptian physician, writer and feminist thinker (1931–2021), one of the most prominent critical Arab voices of the twentieth century on questions of women, the body, religion and patriarchal authority. She was among the most thorough analysts of the social, economic and religious roots of patriarchal domination over the body, sexual life and social existence of Arab women. She combined the practice of psychiatry and public health with literary and political writing, and was imprisoned and exiled because of her positions.

## Biography

She graduated from the Faculty of Medicine at Cairo University in 1955 and worked as a physician and then as director of health education. She rose to the post of **Director of Public Health** in the Egyptian Ministry of Health, before being dismissed from it because of her bold writing on sexuality and women. She obtained a master's degree in public health from Columbia University in 1966. In 1982 she founded the Arab Women's Solidarity Association, an independent feminist organization in Egypt, which was later closed by government decision in 1991.

In September 1981, in a broad wave of arrests targeting opponents of President Anwar Sadat, she was imprisoned for about three months in the Qanatir women's prison without a formal charge, and was released after Sadat's assassination in October 1981. She later wrote about that experience in *Memoirs from the Women's Prison* (1984). After threats from militant Islamist groups, following the inclusion of her name on "death lists", she lived in voluntary exile in the United States during the 1990s before returning to Egypt.

## Her Contributions

- **Criticism of female genital cutting and of the body as an instrument of control**: In *Woman and Sex* (1972), whose publication led to her dismissal from her government post, she drew on her direct experience as a rural gynecologist to offer a radical analysis of female genital cutting. She treated it not merely as a religious or health custom but as a structural social mechanism for controlling the female body and regulating female sexuality, in the service of a patriarchal order that ties the honour of family and tribe to women's virginity and obedience.
- **A multi-layered structure of oppression**: She expanded this analysis in *The Hidden Face of Eve* (1977), which documented the practice of female genital cutting from her personal and medical experience. The book extended the oppression of Arab women to its economic dimension (financial dependence), its legal dimension (personal status laws), its religious dimension (male interpretations of the texts) and its sexual dimension (control of the body). It linked this oppression to the structures of colonialism and class capitalism together, rather than to religion alone or to a local culture isolated from its economic and political context.
- **Literature as a tool of social critique**: Her novel *Woman at Point Zero* (1975), based partly on her meetings with women prisoners during her medical work, tells the story of Firdaus, a woman executed after killing the man who exploited her. The novel became a reference text in world feminist literature.
- **The interconnection of colonialism, patriarchy and religion**: She held that the oppression of Arab women was the product of a threefold intersection: the colonial legacy, local patriarchal structures, and the use of religious discourse to control the female body. This position brought her close to Third-Worldist and postcolonial feminist currents, despite her disagreements with some Western ones.
- **Questioning ready-made identities**: She rejected the sharp separation between "East" and "West" in questions of liberation, and insisted that the repression of women is a structural phenomenon that transcends cultures, although its forms differ.

## Impact

She is regarded as one of the most important founding voices of contemporary Arab feminism. Her works became a basic reference in Arab feminism and in cross-cultural women's studies, and deeply influenced movements against female genital cutting worldwide. Her writings provoked wide debate in Egypt and the Arab world, between those who regarded her as a pioneer of liberation and those who accused her of hostility to the religious and social heritage. Her works have been translated into many languages, and until her death in 2021 she remained a sharp critical voice against both political and religious regimes.
