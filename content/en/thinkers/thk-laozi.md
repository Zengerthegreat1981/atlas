# Laozi (Lao Dan)

A traditional figure to whom the founding of philosophical Daoism and the authorship of the "Dao De Jing" (Tao Te Ching, "The Book of the Way and Virtue") are attributed. The dominant tradition portrays him as a contemporary of Confucius (6th century BCE) and older than he, but many contemporary academics regard him as a semi-legendary figure: there is no decisive historical evidence of his existence, and the book attributed to him is more probably composed in the 4th century BCE by philosophers whose names are unknown.

## What He Gave

- **The Dao (道 Dao)**: the "cosmic principle" that connects everything, which cannot be spoken or grasped, only practiced. "The Dao that can be spoken is not the eternal Dao" (DDJ 1).
- **Wu wei (無為 wúwéi)**: "non-forced action" or "letting nature be": the best administration is the one that leaves the natural order to take its course. "I do not interfere, and the people reform themselves" (DDJ 57).
- **Ziran (自然 zìrán)**: "what is of itself", the opposite of what is imposed from outside. A human being in his deepest state is "natural", not "artificial".
- **The correlativity of opposites**: easy and difficult, long and short, before and after: each is defined by its opposite, and if the opposite fades, the meaning fades. "When people know beauty as beauty, ugliness arises" (DDJ 2).
- **The weak and the flexible are stronger than the strong and rigid**: "The seas and rivers rule a hundred valleys because they are good at being lower than them" (DDJ 66). "A strong army does not win" (DDJ 76).

## The Traditional Account

The historian Sima Qian reports in the *Records of the Grand Historian* that Lao Dan was an archivist in the court of Zhou, and was from the southern land of Chu. He saw the decline of Zhou and headed west, and at the Hangu Pass the gatekeeper Yin Xi asked him to write down what he had; he wrote a book of about five thousand characters and then went on, and his fate was not known. Sima Qian also mentions two other possibilities about Laozi's identity, and this hesitation shows that the matter was disputed in his own time. Afterward, the Daoist religion raised him to the rank of "Laojun", a deity, and later texts portrayed him as a teacher of the barbarians in the west, so that he became part of a controversy between Daoism and Buddhism.

## The Text and Commentaries

The circulating book is divided into a part on the "Dao" (chapters 1–37) and a part on the "De", that is, virtue (38–81), the arrangement of the received text transmitted with Wang Bi's third-century commentary, whereas the Mawangdui manuscripts put virtue before the Dao, and the text there is therefore called "De Dao Jing". Alongside Wang Bi's commentary, the commentary of "Heshang Gong" became famous and became a Daoist reference. The chapters of the text are short, and many are rhymed or poem-like, which suggests that it was composed to be memorized and recited, and that it was compiled from circulating sayings. Translations differ widely because the ancient Chinese sentence admits more than one reading, and because some expressions may have been circulating sayings before they were written down.

## His Place in the Current

The text of the *Dao De Jing* is often read as a counterpoint to the Confucian emphasis on ritual propriety, moral cultivation and active government: it distrusts conventional virtue and contrivance and values simplicity, restraint and yielding. Daoist and Confucian currents nevertheless coexisted and interacted in Chinese thought for centuries. In the 3rd century CE, the "Learning of the Mysterious" (Xuanxue), including Wang Bi and Guo Xiang, reread Laozi and Zhuangzi in a philosophical reinterpretation.

## Limits

- Laozi's historical existence is a matter of radical disagreement. Some academics (such as A. C. Graham) hold that "Laozi" is a legendary figure and that the DDJ was written by "Laozian philosophers" whose names are unknown. Others, following Sima Qian, accept a historical Lao Dan, though the relation of this figure to the text remains uncertain.
- The traditional encounter between Laozi and Confucius, as related in the *Zhuangzi* and in Sima Qian's *Records of the Historian*, is mythical rather than historical in character.
- Archaeological discoveries, including the Guodian slips (around 300 BCE) and the Mawangdui manuscripts (168 BCE), have shown versions older than the text, but they do not settle the author's identity.

## His Most Important Works (Attributed)

- *Dao De Jing* (道德經, "The Book of the Way and Virtue", 81 short chapters, about 5,000 Chinese characters).
