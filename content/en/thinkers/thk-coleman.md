# James S. Coleman

James Samuel Coleman (1926-1995) was an American sociologist who combined large-scale empirical research on education with a programme for a rational-choice foundation for social theory. He is widely associated with the concept of social capital.

## Life and Context

Coleman was born in Bedford, Indiana. He trained as a chemical engineer before turning to sociology, and received his doctorate at Columbia University in 1955, where he was influenced by Paul Lazarsfeld and Robert K. Merton. He took that engineering degree at Purdue University in 1949 and worked briefly in industry before graduate study. He taught at the University of Chicago, then Johns Hopkins University, and from 1973 the University of Chicago again. In 1959 he founded the Department of Social Relations at Johns Hopkins. He served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1992.

## Main Ideas

- **Rational-choice sociology.** In *Foundations of Social Theory* (1990), he argued that social phenomena should be explained as the outcome of purposive actors in structured situations. Those actors control resources and have interests in resources that others control.
- **Micro-macro link.** His schematic of macro conditions shaping individual action, and individual actions aggregating to macro outcomes, is often called "Coleman's boat" or "bathtub".
- **Social capital.** In "Social Capital in the Creation of Human Capital" (1988), he described social capital as features of social relations, such as trust, norms and information channels, that make cooperation possible. Closed networks, in which parents know one another's children, were for him a way of sustaining norms.
- **Adolescent society.** *The Adolescent Society* (1961) examined how peer culture in American high schools shapes values and effort.
- **Educational equality.** The report *Equality of Educational Opportunity* (1966), known as the Coleman Report, concluded that family background and peer composition were strongly related to achievement, while school resources mattered less than expected. It was commissioned under the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and surveyed students and schools across the United States.

## Method

Coleman used survey research, mathematical models and formal theory, and he favoured policy-relevant social research. He also used network data, and he argued that sociology should help in the design of social institutions.

## Case Studies

*The Adolescent Society* drew on surveys in ten high schools in northern Illinois, in communities differing in size and social composition. Coleman asked pupils what brought popularity and recognition and compared their answers with academic effort. Athletic success among boys, and social popularity among girls, often carried more peer prestige than scholarship. He argued that where peers reward achievement little, able pupils may direct their energies elsewhere, so schools should consider the social system among students and not only individual motivation. His 1988 article on social capital used several short cases. Wholesale diamond merchants in New York could hand one another bags of stones for private inspection without formal insurance, because close family, religious, and community ties made cheating very costly. A mother who moved from suburban Detroit to Jerusalem felt able to let young children go about unaccompanied, reflecting norms that other adults would look after them. These examples illustrate how a network can lower the risk of trust. They also show why such capital belongs to relationships rather than to one person, and may exclude outsiders or constrain members.

## Contemporary Empirical Examples

Raj Chetty and colleagues (2022) analysed data on friendships among a very large number of Facebook users in the United States and distinguished several kinds of social capital. Economic connectedness, the extent of friendship between people of lower and higher socioeconomic status, was strongly associated with upward income mobility across areas, whereas measures of network cohesion and civic engagement showed weaker relationships with it. The result suggests that who is connected matters, not simply how dense relationships are. It is based largely on associations across places and on users of one platform, so it does not by itself establish that creating a particular friendship will raise a person's income. It also uses a definition of social capital that differs in emphasis from Coleman's account of closed networks and enforceable norms.

## Related Concepts

Related concepts include rational choice, social capital, human capital, trust, norms, closure, micro-macro linkage, methodological individualism, and educational inequality. Comparisons with Pierre Bourdieu, Robert Putnam, George Homans on exchange, and Mancur Olson's analysis of collective action clarify differences in the treatment of resources, power, and cooperation. Bourdieu stresses unequal access and class reproduction; Putnam applies social capital to communities and political institutions; Olson explains why shared interests do not automatically produce joint action. Coleman's boat also connects with the study of social mechanisms and the problem of aggregation, which asks how many individual actions combine into outcomes that no actor intended.

## Influence and Criticism

His social-capital concept was taken up by Robert Putnam and many others, and his report reshaped debate on school policy. Critics have argued that the term social capital is vague and risks circular reasoning, that rational-choice assumptions overlook culture and emotion, and that his later work on school desegregation and on private schools was politically contested. In the 1970s his research linking urban school desegregation with white enrollment decline drew sharp dispute. Methodologists also questioned his comparisons of public and private schools, because students are not randomly assigned to sectors. His micro-macro scheme became a reference point for analytical sociology.
