# Michael White

Michael White (1948–2008) was an Australian family therapist who, with the New Zealander David Epston, co-founded Narrative Therapy, and in 1983 established the Dulwich Centre in Adelaide, South Australia. He is known for externalizing conversations, that is, "externalizing the problem" from the person's identity, for questions about "unique outcomes" and re-authoring, and for combining therapeutic practice with Michel Foucault's ideas on power and knowledge.

## Biography and Context

White was born in Adelaide and worked early in his career in social work and psychiatric care, then trained in family therapy. He was Australian by birth, while Epston is the New Zealander. He died in San Diego, California, in April 2008 while giving a training program, of a heart attack. He directed the Dulwich Centre and issued what are called Dulwich Centre Publications from it, and his wife Cheryl White was his partner in running the centre and publishing his work.

White started from the controversy that shook systemic family therapy in the 1980s: while family therapy treated the family as a cybernetic system, White saw in the style of the neutral expert who interprets the system a degree of power no less than the power of diagnosis. At this moment he became acquainted with Bateson's work on frame and knowledge, with Bruner's work on narrative, and with Foucault, and his vision took shape.

## Main Ideas

Externalizing the problem: in his article "The externalizing of the problem and the re-authoring of lives and relationships", published in the Dulwich Centre Newsletter in 1988/1989, he presented what came to be called externalizing conversation: the therapist and the client talk about the problem as an entity separate from the person, so that instead of saying the child is "aggressive", one says that "anger" sometimes takes hold of him. The stance thus changes from condemning the person to confronting the problem, and a space opens for choosing a position toward it.

The dominant story and unique outcomes: White holds that people live according to stories about themselves shaped by society and prevailing discourses, and that the problem is often tied to a problem-saturated story. He looks for "unique outcomes", that is, moments in the client's life that were not subject to the problem, such as a time when the client resisted or chose something different, and builds from them an alternative story; this is re-authoring.

The outsider witness and definitional ceremonies: he took from the anthropologist Barbara Myerhoff the idea of definitional ceremonies, and used an audience of "outsider witnesses" who listen to the client's story and then say what it stirred in them, in order to strengthen the new identity. Among his concepts are "re-membering conversations", which reconsider the "membership" of the significant people in the client's life.

The absent but implicit: in his later works, including a chapter published in 2000 in *Reflections on Narrative Practice*, he stressed that pain points to a violated value, so the therapist asks what the suffering indicates about attachment to something precious, in order to build a life from it. He described this concept as absent but implicit, because every description of an experience invokes what stands opposite it and has not been said.

Thick and thin description: a metaphor borrowed from Geertz and Gilbert Ryle, used to gauge the depth of the narrative formed about identity.

## Major Works

With Epston he published *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* (Norton, 1990), the foundational book, and then *Re-authoring Lives: Interviews and Essays* (Dulwich Centre Publications, 1995), a collection of interviews and essays. In 2007 *Maps of Narrative Practice* appeared from Norton, a systematic presentation of his tools.

## His Place in the Current

Narrative therapy is associated with a critical social constructionism that places power at the center of analysis, and it distinguishes itself from the work of Anderson and Goolishian in collaborative therapy and from the Milan team. White's influence extended to community work with Indigenous groups and victims of violence.

## Reception and Criticism

Narrative therapy has had a worldwide influence on family therapy, counseling and social work. Critics took it to task for scant empirical evidence, for concepts that are hard to measure, and for the fact that excessive externalizing may reduce a person's responsibility for his behavior. Some specialists in cognitive-behavioral therapy raised the question of the absence of demonstrated efficacy for severe disorders. A social criticism is that the focus on story may neglect material conditions.
