# Carl Schmitt

A German constitutional jurist and political theorist, born in 1888 in Plettenberg in the Catholic region of Westphalia. He studied law at the universities of Berlin, Munich and Strasbourg, and died in 1985 in his native town. He was interned by the Allies after 1945, never returned to a university chair, and spent his last decades in relative isolation, though he remained influential. He is one of the most controversial legal and political thinkers of the twentieth century: for the originality of his theoretical arguments on the one hand, and for joining the Nazi Party in 1933 and giving intellectual justification to authoritarian measures on the other.

## The opening thesis: "Sovereign is he who decides on the exception"

Schmitt opens his book **Political Theology** (1922) with a sentence that has become one of the most famous lines in political legal theory: **"Sovereign is he who decides on the exception"** (Souverän ist, wer über den Ausnahmezustand entscheidet).

## The critique of legal positivism (especially Kelsen)

Schmitt attacked legal positivism, led by **Kelsen**, on the grounds that it imagines the possibility of a purely legal, self-sufficient system that governs every case through predetermined rules. It overlooks that **every legal order presupposes, at a founding or critical moment, a sovereign decision prior to the rule itself**. This decision is not governed by any rule, because it is the source of all rules. Schmitt and Kelsen engaged in a direct debate on this question in 1931, over who should guard the German constitution: the president or the constitutional court.

## The later impact: Agamben

**Giorgio Agamben** took up Schmitt's concept of the state of exception in his project (*Homo Sacer*), but from the opposite critical standpoint. Whereas Schmitt sees in the state of exception a moment of **the manifestation of true sovereignty** (positively), Agamben sees in its transformation into a **permanent rule of contemporary politics** a structural danger that empties political life of its legal protection.

## The friend–enemy distinction: the essence of "the political"

In another influential work, *The Concept of the Political* (Der Begriff des Politischen, 1932), Schmitt presents his second major thesis. The **political domain**, unlike the moral domain (good/evil), the aesthetic domain (beautiful/ugly) or the economic domain (profitable/unprofitable), is defined essentially by a single distinction that cannot be reduced to any other: **the distinction between friend and enemy** (Freund-Feind-Unterscheidung). For Schmitt the enemy is not a moral or commercial opponent, but the existential "other" against whom the political community is threatened with annihilation. In decisive moments this justifies the suspension of ordinary rules and recourse to pure political decision. This distinction is structurally complementary to the thesis of the state of exception: both affirm that the essence of politics and sovereignty lies not in the abstract rule but in the **decision** that precedes and founds it.

## Critical reception and the contested legacy

Schmitt's reading has remained a site of sharp disagreement between two intellectual camps. Some liberals (Kelsen was among his sharpest critics) see in him an authoritarian thinker who philosophically justifies the suspension of the rule of law whenever the ruler wishes, making his project in essence a theoretical justification of tyranny. By contrast, some critical leftist thinkers (such as Chantal Mouffe) found in his diagnosis of the limits of procedural liberalism a valuable analytical tool. They saw liberalism's inability to absorb real political conflict, and its reduction of politics to neutral technical negotiation, as a real weakness, despite their categorical rejection of his personal political course. This tension between the gravity of the person and the depth of the thesis remains a constant feature of every serious academic reading of Schmitt to this day.

## Schmitt and National Socialism

Schmitt joined the Nazi Party in May 1933 and held prominent academic and legal posts in the early years of the regime. He wrote texts that justified Nazi measures, for example his 1934 essay "Der Führer schützt das Recht", which defended the killings of the "Night of the Long Knives". In December 1936 he was attacked in the SS journal *Das Schwarze Korps* and lost his party offices, though he kept his Berlin chair. This history does not cancel the academic impact of his arguments on legal and political theory, with which Kelsen, Agamben and Chantal Mouffe, among others, engaged.

## The dispute with Kelsen over the guardian of the constitution

In *Der Hüter der Verfassung* (1931) Schmitt argued that the Reich President, as a neutral power above party politics, rather than a constitutional court, was the proper guardian of the constitution, and that adjudicating constitutional conflicts is a political rather than a purely judicial task. Kelsen replied in "Wer soll der Hüter der Verfassung sein?" (*Die Justiz*, 1930-31) that political conflicts over the constitution are precisely what a constitutional court exists to resolve, and that giving the role to the President would undermine the democratic constitution.

## Political theology as a thesis on the secularization of concepts

In *Political Theology* Schmitt offers a parallel thesis, no less famous than his opening sentence: that **"all the central concepts of the modern theory of the state are secularized theological concepts"**. The concept of the absolute "sovereign" structurally parallels the concept of God intervening miraculously in the order of the world, the miracle being analogous to the state of exception that suspends law from outside it. This historical-conceptual reading made Schmitt a basic reference for every subsequent debate on the relationship of modern political sovereignty to its Christian theological heritage, regardless of one's position on his personal political project.

## From Weimar to the Third Reich: the institutional context of the thesis

Schmitt wrote his early theses on the state of exception and sovereignty in the context of the repeated constitutional crises of the Weimar Republic (1919–1933). Article 48 of the Weimar Constitution, which empowers the president with broad emergency powers, was repeatedly used to suspend ordinary constitutional guarantees. Schmitt was among the most prominent legal theorists supporting the expansion of these presidential powers, which he saw as the only guarantor of state stability in the face of sharp polarization between the extreme left and the extreme right. Interpreters have linked this position to his later acceptance in 1933 of the Nazi one-party system, though it was not the only factor.
