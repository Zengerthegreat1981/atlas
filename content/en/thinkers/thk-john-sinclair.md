# John Sinclair

A British linguist, a pioneer of computational corpus linguistics, and the leader of the COBUILD project, which produced the first major English dictionary compiled from the analysis of a large text corpus rather than primarily from the intuition of the lexicographer.

## The COBUILD Project

At the University of Birmingham, Sinclair led the "Bank of English" project, a large computerized corpus of real English texts, and used it primarily to build the Collins COBUILD dictionary (1987), the first major dictionary to base its entries and examples on evidence of actual usage.

## The Idiom Principle

Sinclair proposed that language is not built only from individual words combined according to free rules (the "open-choice principle"), but depends to a large extent on semi-fixed "ready-made phrases" (prefabricated chunks) that speakers use as complete units. He called this the "idiom principle" (in *Corpus, Concordance, Collocation*, 1991), and illustrated it empirically through analysis of the recurrence of patterns in large corpora.

## Collocation and the Method of Textual Concordances

Sinclair made central use of a basic tool for dealing with large corpora, the "concordance," which displays all the contexts in which a particular word appears throughout the entire corpus side by side. This makes it possible to detect "collocation" (the tendency of certain words to appear together with a frequency exceeding statistical chance, such as the strong collocation of "strong" with "tea" rather than "powerful" with "tea") with a statistical precision that was not available before the existence of large digital texts that could be automatically indexed.

## Relationship to Firth and the London School

Sinclair belonged to the British tradition of J. R. Firth (the "London School") and drew on Firth's central principle that "you shall know a word by the company it keeps." Sinclair transferred this intuitive principle in Firth's work into a precise, quantitative computational methodology open to empirical verification across millions of actual words, instead of relying on limited examples that the linguist recalls from memory and intuition alone.

## Impact

Sinclair's work established the field of corpus linguistics as a quantitative empirical method, as an alternative to relying on the speaker's intuition alone. It has profoundly influenced computational lexicography and the teaching of English as a foreign language: dictionaries and English-as-a-foreign-language courses (such as the Collins COBUILD series itself) are now built on evidence from large real corpora rather than on invented illustrative sentences. His approach has also extended into contemporary computational natural language processing, which relies fundamentally on analyzing large textual data.

## The Idiom Principle and Its Effect on Language Teaching

The idiom principle formulated by Sinclair produced a practical shift in foreign language teaching methods. Instead of teaching abstract grammatical rules followed by separate vocabulary, many modern curricula (influenced in part by corpus research such as his) have moved toward teaching ready-made "lexical chunks" as units to be learned and used whole, consistent with his argument that this is a predominant pattern in the natural language use of native speakers themselves.

## The Principle of "Trust the Text"

In the later part of his career, Sinclair formulated a general methodological principle which he called "Trust the Text": that linguistic generalizations must arise from the patterns actually observed in real usage data, not from prior theoretical hypotheses to which the text is then required to conform. This strongly empirical stance contrasted with approaches that relied mainly on introspective judgments.

## Institutional Legacy

Sinclair held the chair of Modern English Language at the University of Birmingham and founded the COBUILD research group there, whose corpus work continued after his death in 2007.
