# Martin Buber

Martin Buber (1878–1965) was a Jewish Austrian-Israeli philosopher and the founder of the philosophy of dialogue. His distinction between the "I–Thou" relation and the "I–It" relation, set out in *I and Thou* (*Ich und Du*, 1923), made him one of the closest religious voices to existentialism without ever fully belonging to it. He was not a therapist, yet his account of meeting shaped the idea of the therapeutic encounter for many existential therapists.

## Life

Buber was born in Vienna on 8 February 1878. After his parents separated, he was raised in Lemberg (now Lviv) in the house of his grandfather Solomon Buber, a great scholar of the Midrash and publisher of its texts, so the boy grew up between the Hasidic traditions of Eastern Europe and German culture. He studied philosophy and art history in Vienna, Leipzig, Berlin, and Zurich, and became involved early in the Zionist movement, editing the journal *Die Welt* in 1901 and founding *Der Jude*, which he published from 1916 to 1924. He soon moved away from the political leadership of Zionism and argued for a "cultural Zionism" that saw Palestine as a centre of spiritual renewal rather than simply a state-building project.

In the 1920s he began, with Franz Rosenzweig, a German translation of the Hebrew Bible that aimed to echo the rhythm and repetition of the original; after Rosenzweig's death in 1929, Buber carried the project on alone until its completion in 1961. He taught religion at the University of Frankfurt from 1923 until the Nazis dismissed him in 1933. He emigrated to Palestine in 1938 and worked as professor of social philosophy at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem until his retirement in 1951. He died in Jerusalem on 13 June 1965.

## What he gave

- ***I and Thou* (*Ich und Du*, 1923):** the founding book. Buber argues that the primary words are not single words but pairs of words, "I–Thou" and "I–It".
  - "I–Thou": a direct, whole, present relation that cannot be used or analysed.
  - "I–It": an objective, analytical relation in which the other is treated as a thing.
  - The "Eternal Thou": God is present in every genuine "I–Thou" relation.
- **Dialogue:** in *Zwiesprache* (1930) and the essays collected in English as *Between Man and Man* (1947), he developed the philosophy of dialogue further.
- **The Prophets:** *Torat ha-Nevi'im* (1942; English *The Prophetic Faith*, 1949) is a study of the Hebrew prophets.
- **Hasidism:** in *Die Geschichten des Rabbi Nachman* (1906), *Die Legende des Baalschem* (1908), and *The Way of Man According to the Hasidic Teaching* (1948), he collected and retold Hasidic tales as evidence that piety means sanctifying everyday life, not escaping from it.

Buber's starting point is that a human being does not exist first and then enter into relation; relation comes before the self. The "I" that says "I" in a dialogical relation is a different "I" from the one that says it while using things. There is no final separation between the two worlds: every "Thou" becomes an "It" once the moment of meeting passes, and that is not a sin but a condition of practical life. The danger is that the whole world becomes a world of "It", and the human being loses their humanity.

He applied this method to several fields: education, which he saw as resting on a meeting between teacher and pupil rather than on the transfer of content; art, which begins in the artist's dialogue with the material; and politics, which he criticised whenever it turned the community into an instrument.

## His relation to existentialism

Buber's relation to philosophical existentialism is indirect. He is usually read as parallel to it rather than as a member of it, and his closeness to Gabriel Marcel (Christian existentialism) is greater than his closeness to Sartre or Camus. The classification remains unsettled: he is sometimes placed among the existentialists and sometimes treated as independent of them. What is not in doubt is the influence of his distinction between "I–Thou" and "I–It" on the concept of the therapeutic encounter among existential therapists, even though Buber himself was never a therapist.

## Political stance

Buber was known for his binationalist position on the conflict in Palestine. In 1942 he took part in founding the Ihud association with Judah Magnes, president of the Hebrew University, which called for a state shared by Arabs and Jews instead of partition. He opposed the partition decision of 1947, and after 1948 he criticised the treatment of Palestinian refugees. He held that a Zionism which ignored the existence of the Arabs betrayed its own spiritual core. This position remained a minority one in the Zionist movement, but it made his name a point of interest for Arab writers exploring the possibilities of coexistence, and a target of attack from Israeli nationalists.

## Limits and criticism

Emmanuel Levinas criticised Buber for making the relation mutual and symmetrical, while Levinas holds that the Other precedes me and stands above me without exchange. In his dialogue with Carl Rogers at the University of Michigan in 1957, Buber insisted that the therapist–patient relation is unequal by virtue of the roles and never reaches full mutuality, while Rogers saw in it a meeting between two persons equal in dignity. Other critics argue that his poetic language leaves his concepts vague, that his reading of Hasidism is selective and neglects its ritual and legal side, and that the link between the Eternal Thou and God makes his work a religious meditation rather than a philosophical theory that can be tested.

## Influence

Buber influenced the philosophy of dialogue in Emmanuel Levinas and humanistic psychology through his famous 1957 dialogue with Carl Rogers at the University of Michigan on the nature of the therapeutic relationship.
