# William Stokoe

## Definition

William Stokoe (1919–2000) was an American linguist widely credited with establishing sign-language linguistics as a research field. In *Sign Language Structure* (1960) he argued, on structural grounds, that American Sign Language (ASL) is a natural language with an internal organization of its own, not a set of improvised gestures or a sign-by-sign code for English.

## Historical Context

Stokoe taught English at Gallaudet College, then the principal college for deaf students in the United States. He arrived in 1955 with a doctorate in English literature, not in linguistics. Stokoe observed that deaf students communicated among themselves using a complex and organized system that had not been academically recognized as a genuine language. At the time, psychology and special education generally regarded sign language as a primitive compensatory gestural system, or merely a manual code translating the structure of spoken language sign by sign, rather than a linguistic system with its own internal rules.

## Main Ideas and Method

### Discovery of the Sub-Meaningful Phonological Structure

In *Sign Language Structure* (1960), Stokoe argued that a single sign in ASL is not an indivisible unit. It is composed of three minimal, independent parameters that combine together: handshape (the way the fingers are configured), location (the place of the sign relative to the body), and movement (the path of the hand through space). Stokoe coined the term "cherology" (from the Greek *kheir*, meaning hand), paralleling the term "phonology," to describe this sub-meaningful structural level of analysis specific to sign languages. Changing one of these parameters while the others remain constant can produce a sign with an entirely different meaning, just as replacing a single phoneme in a spoken word changes its meaning entirely. This structure functionally parallels the sub-meaningful phonemes of spoken language, even though the sensory medium differs radically between a visual-manual language and an audio-vocal one.

### From Academic Rejection to Recognition

Stokoe's work initially met wide academic rejection, and even ridicule from some of his colleagues at Gallaudet itself. The prevailing view then regarded sign language as a primitive gestural system rather than a full language, and institutions for the deaf preferred teaching "oralism" (lip-reading and speech) over the use of sign language. Wider linguistic acceptance took years. Ursula Bellugi and Edward Klima, in *The Signs of Language* (1979), and later work with colleagues on the brain, treated ASL as a language with structural complexity and, in deaf signers, a strong left-hemisphere involvement of a kind familiar from spoken language. Stokoe's analysis became a reference point for that field.

## Key Works and Figures

His core reference work is *Sign Language Structure: An Outline of the Visual Communication Systems of the American Deaf* (1960). It was followed by *A Dictionary of American Sign Language on Linguistic Principles* (1965), co-authored with Dorothy Casterline and Carl Croneberg. That dictionary was the first sign language dictionary organized according to the structural parameters of signs rather than by their English equivalent translation. Among those who continued his line of work were Ursula Bellugi and Edward Klima, in their book *The Signs of Language* (1979), and, later, Wendy Sandler, who developed phonological models of sign language structure.

## Impact and Critique

Stokoe's analysis opened a line of research that treats human linguistic capacity as realizable in a visual-manual medium. This had a profound social and political impact beyond academia. It contributed to the movement for recognizing Deaf culture as a linguistic and cultural minority with its own full natural language, rather than a group of "disabled" people in need of corrective therapy alone. His original notation system was later criticized as insufficient (for example, it omitted hand orientation and non-manual features), and more detailed notation and phonological models were subsequently developed. The composite, sub-meaningful structure of the sign remains a standard assumption, even where his notation has been replaced.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Stokoe has no direct connection to the Arab-Islamic heritage. However, his method of structural analysis of American Sign Language paved the way for later structural linguistic studies of other sign languages around the world, including those used in Arab societies. Researchers have documented some of these languages in recent decades, drawing on the methodological framework that Stokoe first established in the study of American Sign Language.
