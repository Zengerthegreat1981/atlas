# William Stokoe

## Definition

William Stokoe (1919–2000) was an American linguist and the founder of the field of sign language linguistics. He was the first to argue systematically, on structural grounds, that American Sign Language (ASL) is a complete natural language with an independent phonological structure. It is not merely a set of improvised visual gestures or a manual translation of English, as was commonly assumed in his era.

## Historical Context

Stokoe worked as a professor of English at Gallaudet College, the world's only liberal arts college for deaf students. He arrived there in 1955 with a doctorate in English literature, not in linguistics. Stokoe observed that deaf students communicated among themselves using a complex and organized system that had not been academically recognized as a genuine language. At the time, psychology and special education generally regarded sign language as a primitive compensatory gestural system, or merely a manual code translating the structure of spoken language sign by sign, rather than a linguistic system with its own internal rules.

## Main Ideas and Method

### Discovery of the Sub-Meaningful Phonological Structure

In his book *Sign Language Structure* (1960), Stokoe demonstrated that a single sign in ASL is not an indivisible unit. It is composed of three minimal, independent parameters that combine together: handshape (the way the fingers are configured), location (the place of the sign relative to the body), and movement (the path of the hand through space). Stokoe coined the term "cherology" (from the Greek *kheir*, meaning hand), paralleling the term "phonology," to describe this sub-meaningful structural level of analysis specific to sign languages. Changing one of these parameters while the others remain constant can produce a sign with an entirely different meaning, just as replacing a single phoneme in a spoken word changes its meaning entirely. This structure functionally parallels the sub-meaningful phonemes of spoken language, even though the sensory medium differs radically between a visual-manual language and an audio-vocal one.

### From Academic Rejection to Recognition

Stokoe's work initially met wide academic rejection, and even ridicule from some of his colleagues at Gallaudet itself. The prevailing view then regarded sign language as a primitive gestural system rather than a full language, and institutions for the deaf preferred teaching "oralism" (lip-reading and speech) over the use of sign language. It took decades for his findings to be confirmed and accepted linguistically. Later experimental research by Ursula Bellugi and Edward Klima in the 1970s was decisive. It showed, among other things, that sign languages have the structural complexity and left-hemisphere neural organization characteristic of spoken languages. Stokoe's work thereby became the acknowledged cornerstone of a full academic field.

## Key Works and Figures

His core reference work is *Sign Language Structure: An Outline of the Visual Communication Systems of the American Deaf* (1960). It was followed by *A Dictionary of American Sign Language on Linguistic Principles* (1965), co-authored with Dorothy Casterline and Carl Croneberg. That dictionary was the first sign language dictionary organized according to the structural parameters of signs rather than by their English equivalent translation. Among those who continued his line of work were Ursula Bellugi and Edward Klima, in their book *The Signs of Language* (1979), and, later, Wendy Sandler, who developed phonological models of sign language structure.

## Impact and Critique

Stokoe founded the field of sign language linguistics and provided decisive evidence that human linguistic capacity can be realized in a visual-manual medium with the same structural organization it has in the audio-vocal medium. This had a profound social and political impact beyond academia. It contributed to the movement for recognizing Deaf culture as a linguistic and cultural minority with its own full natural language, rather than a group of "disabled" people in need of corrective therapy alone. His original notation system was later criticized as insufficient (for example, it omitted hand orientation and non-manual features), and more detailed notation and phonological models were subsequently developed. The essence of his basic discovery, the composite sub-meaningful structure of the sign, has remained an undisputed foundation of the field.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Stokoe has no direct connection to the Arab-Islamic heritage. However, his method of structural analysis of American Sign Language paved the way for later structural linguistic studies of other sign languages around the world, including those used in Arab societies. Researchers have documented some of these languages in recent decades, drawing on the methodological framework that Stokoe first established in the study of American Sign Language.
