# George Homans

George Caspar Homans (1910-1989) was an American sociologist at Harvard University who developed exchange theory and argued that social behavior should be explained through general psychological propositions about reward and cost.

## Life and Context

Homans was born in Boston into an old New England family and studied at Harvard, graduating in 1932. He was influenced by the physiologist L. J. Henderson and the Pareto Circle at Harvard, and by Elton Mayo's industrial research. After naval service in the Second World War he returned to Harvard, where he spent the rest of his career. He served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1964.

## Main Ideas

In *The Human Group*, Homans analyzed small groups through the interrelated elements of activities, interactions and sentiments, with a distinction between the external system that meets group needs and the internal system that develops from interaction. In his 1964 presidential address, "Bringing Men Back In," he criticized sociology for neglecting individual behavior. In *Social Behavior: Its Elementary Forms*, he proposed that social behavior is an exchange of activity, tangible or intangible, rewarding or costly, between at least two persons. He formulated a set of propositions, including the success, stimulus, value, deprivation-satiation and aggression-approval propositions, drawn from behavioral psychology, and a rationality proposition. He also discussed distributive justice, the idea that people expect rewards proportional to their investments.

## Major Works

- *English Villagers of the Thirteenth Century* (1941)
- *The Human Group* (1950)
- *Social Behavior: Its Elementary Forms* (1961)
- *Sentiments and Activities* (1962)
- *Coming to My Senses* (autobiography, 1984)

## Case Studies

*The Human Group* reinterpreted several field studies, including the Bank Wiring Observation Room of the Hawthorne research, William Foote Whyte's Street Corner Society (the Norton Street gang) and Raymond Firth's study of Tikopia. *English Villagers of the Thirteenth Century* applied his approach to a medieval agrarian community.

## Method

Homans favored deductive explanation, in which general propositions about individuals explain group-level regularities, and he used examples from small-group field studies and historical cases.

## Influence and Criticism

His exchange theory influenced Peter Blau, Richard Emerson and later network exchange research. Critics, including Peter Blau, argued that his psychological reductionism cannot account for emergent features of social structure, norms and institutions, and that his propositions are too general or trivial to explain complex social phenomena.
