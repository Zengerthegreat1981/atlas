# Friedrich Schelling

A German philosopher (1775–1854), one of the pillars of German Idealism together with Fichte and Hegel, author of the philosophy of nature, the philosophy of identity, and then the "positive philosophy" of his late period. Schelling represents the voice that held that existence cannot be deduced from thought, and that at the origin of human freedom and of being lies a dark and irrational ground that cannot be reduced to logical concepts. He is among the philosophers of his age whose positions changed most across the stages of his life.

## His Life

He was born in Leonberg in the province of Württemberg on 27 January 1775, and entered the Tübingen theological seminary (Tübinger Stift) at the age of fifteen, where he befriended Hegel and Hölderlin and lived with them in an atmosphere of enthusiasm for the French Revolution and the philosophy of Kant. He published his first works under the influence of Fichte, and was appointed professor at Jena in 1798 at the age of twenty-three, where he came into contact with Goethe and the circle of the Romantics, and after leaving it married Caroline Schlegel. He then taught in Würzburg and Munich, then in Erlangen, then at the University of Munich from 1827 until 1841, when King Friedrich Wilhelm IV summoned him to Berlin to confront the influence of Hegelianism. He died in Bad Ragaz in Switzerland on 20 August 1854.

## Stages of His Thought

Schelling began with the philosophy of nature ("Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature" 1797 and "On the World Soul" 1798), in which he saw nature as a living activity directed toward consciousness, so that it is not regarded as a dead machine but as an unconscious subject growing toward spirit. Then in the "System of Transcendental Idealism" (1800) he formulated that art is the highest realization of the identity between subject and object, and called for the centrality of aesthetic intuition. After 1801 he put forward the "philosophy of identity," which makes the Absolute an identity in which there is no difference between subject and object, the philosophy to which Hegel replied in the preface to the "Phenomenology of Spirit" (1807) with his famous phrase about the night in which all cows are black.

## What He Offered

In his major treatise on "Human Freedom" (1809), Schelling moved philosophy from logic to living being, saying that in God Himself there is a "ground" (Grund) that is not God but is the condition of His existence, and from it springs the darkness and blind impulse that aspires to appear. In the human being this appears in the possibility of separation between the particular will and the universal will, and this possibility is the origin of evil. For him evil is not merely a deficiency or an absence of good as the Platonic and Augustinian tradition wished, but a positive realization of a freedom capable of overturning the order. Thus he presented a conception of freedom as the capacity for good and evil together, not a calm rational choice, and from this the existentialists were influenced by him.

In the drafts of "The Ages of the World" (1811–1815) he tried to sketch the history of God and the world as a life taking shape in time, and in his late lectures "Philosophy of Mythology" and "Philosophy of Revelation" (1841–1854) he distinguished between "negative philosophy," which deduces by concept what can be, and "positive philosophy," which begins from the actual real existence that cannot be deduced, and understands history, myths and revelation as real events, not conceptual truths. He held that pure reason cannot explain why there is something rather than nothing.

## His Place in the Current

Schelling is an important philosophical bridge between German Idealism and later existential thought. Kierkegaard, Engels and Bakunin attended his famous lectures in Berlin (1841–1842) and wrote about them with differing views; Kierkegaard at first saw them as promising and then was disappointed, and Engels mocked them. Heidegger devoted in-depth lectures to his treatise on freedom (1936), Paul Tillich was influenced by him in his theology, Jaspers wrote about him, and Jürgen Habermas and Slavoj Žižek have revived interest in his thought in recent decades. In English literature his influence appears in Coleridge.

## Limits and Critique

His complex metaphysical language and mystical ontology make him belong to the difficult classical German philosophy, and his positions changed so much that he was considered unsystematic. Hegel criticized him on the ground that the "Absolute" for him remains a mere starting point without development, and the opponents of idealism among the materialists criticized him on the ground that his last philosophy is a flight into theology. Some scholars held that his ambition for a "positive" philosophy that goes beyond reason did not turn into a completed system. Moreover, much of his late work (such as the lectures on mythology and revelation and the drafts of *The Ages of the World*) was published only after his death, in the collected edition prepared by his son Karl Friedrich August Schelling.

## Most Important Works

- Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature (Ideen zu einer Philosophie der Natur, 1797).
- System of Transcendental Idealism (System des transzendentalen Idealismus, 1800).
- Philosophical Investigations into the Essence of Human Freedom (Philosophische Untersuchungen über das Wesen der menschlichen Freiheit, 1809).
- The Ages of the World (Die Weltalter, drafts 1811–1815).
- Philosophy of Mythology and Revelation (lectures 1841–1854).
