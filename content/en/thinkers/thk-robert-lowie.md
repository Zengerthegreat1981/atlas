# Robert H. Lowie

Robert Harry Lowie (1883–1957) was an Austrian-born American anthropologist and a leading figure of the second generation of the Boasian school. A student of Franz Boas at Columbia University, he carried out his principal fieldwork among the Crow Indians of the northern Plains and spent most of his career at the University of California, Berkeley, alongside Alfred L. Kroeber. His book *Primitive Society* (1920) delivered the most systematic critique of classical evolutionism yet written, and his careful comparative method helped establish historical particularism as the standard approach of American anthropology.

## Life and Career

Lowie was born on 12 June 1883 in Vienna, and his family emigrated to New York in 1893, when he was ten. He studied at Columbia University under Franz Boas, receiving his doctorate in 1908, and worked as a researcher at the American Museum of Natural History. In 1921 he moved to the University of California, Berkeley, where he spent the rest of his career beside Kroeber, and together they made Berkeley one of the two great centres of American anthropology. He served as president of the American Anthropological Association (1935) and edited the journal *American Anthropologist* (1924–1933). He died in 1957.

Lowie began his fieldwork among the Crow of Montana in 1907 and returned to them repeatedly over the following decades, building one of the most detailed ethnographic records of any Plains society. He kept throughout his life a close relationship with the German and Austrian culture of his childhood, and drew on German-language anthropological and sociological work in his English writings, acting as an intermediary between European and American academic traditions.

## Key Works

Lowie's central work is *Primitive Society* (1920). Drawing on wide comparative ethnographic evidence, above all from the Crow, he attacked the unilinear evolutionism of Lewis Henry Morgan and his successors, in particular the assumption of a fixed universal sequence of stages in kinship, marriage, and property. The actual diversity of kinship, property, and political systems among so-called primitive peoples, he showed, supported no single linear evolutionary sequence, but rather multiple independent historical paths.

*Primitive Religion* (1924) approached religious phenomena with the same descriptive, comparative method, avoiding evolutionary generalisations. *The Crow Indians* (1935) presented a comprehensive ethnography of Crow social life, including his detailed study of the military societies — voluntary associations based on personal achievement in war and hunting rather than on birth, which offered an alternative path to status outside the kinship system. His historical and historical survey, *The History of Ethnological Theory* (1937), traced the development of anthropological theory since the nineteenth century with a critical eye and remains a useful history of the discipline.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Lowie's main contribution was methodological: a rigorous historical particularism. He insisted that generalisations must be built from detailed ethnographic evidence gathered from many sources before any theoretical conclusion is drawn, and he refused to leap from single cases to universal laws, as the classical evolutionists had done. This caution became the hallmark of the second Boasian generation and made its work empirically more reliable than that of its predecessors, even at the cost of some of the earlier boldness of theory.

Substantively, his critique of evolutionism helped consolidate the principle that each culture has its own history and must be understood on its own terms. His study of the Crow military societies also revealed a complexity in the structure of power in stateless societies that went beyond the simplified image of the "tribe" as a homogeneous block governed by kinship rules alone: it showed spaces of competition and individual mobility inside these societies themselves. His wide reading in European sources, including the German-Austrian tradition, enriched American anthropology.

## Influence and Critique

Together with Kroeber, Lowie consolidated the Boasian tradition in its second generation and helped establish anthropology as a recognised university discipline in the United States. His critique of evolutionism made historical particularism the standard method of American anthropology for a generation, and his Crow ethnography remains a reference point for Plains studies.

Later scholars criticised his rejection of evolutionism as too sweeping, arguing that it sometimes refused any cross-cultural pattern even where the ethnographic evidence supported one — a criticism that later opened the way for the return of cultural evolutionism in the work of Leslie White and Julian Steward. His caution about generalisation has also been seen as a limitation: the Boasian generation's care with evidence sometimes came at the expense of ambitious theory. Yet the standard Lowie set — that theory must answer to evidence, case by case — remains one of the enduring legacies of American anthropology.
