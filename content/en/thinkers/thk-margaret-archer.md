# Margaret S. Archer

A British sociologist (1943–2023), the first woman to preside over the International Sociological Association, and the developer of "Morphogenetic Theory," which rests on Roy Bhaskar's philosophy of critical realism.

## The Central Idea

In *Realist Social Theory: The Morphogenetic Approach* (1995), Archer offered a solution to the structure/agency dualism, which she saw as left unresolved in Giddens's structuration theory (which merged structure and agency into a single "duality" to the point that the analytical distinction between them became impossible). In its place she proposed "Analytical Dualism." Social structure temporally precedes any particular individual action (it is the product of earlier actions), but it does not determine that action. Rather, it constrains or enables human agency, which in turn, through a "morphogenetic" cycle (form-generating) or a "morphostatic" one (form-preserving), reproduces or transforms the structure over time. The condition of this framework is a strict analytical separation between two temporally successive moments: the prior structural condition, and the subsequent agential interaction that reproduces or changes it.

## Historical Context

Margaret Archer was born in Britain in 1943 and studied at the London School of Economics before becoming a professor at the University of Warwick, where she spent most of her academic career. Her work came in the context of a heated British and European sociological debate in the 1980s and 1990s about how to overcome the traditional dichotomy between structural approaches (which privilege social structure over individual action) and individualist approaches (which privilege individual action over structure). It was specifically in direct critical dialogue with Anthony Giddens's reconciling attempt in "structuration theory," which Archer considered, despite its theoretical elegance, to have failed to offer a workable solution applicable to actual empirical research.

## Additional Ideas

In *Structure, Agency and the Internal Conversation* (2003), Archer later developed a theory of "reflexivity," which explains how individuals actually mediate between structural constraints and available opportunities through an ongoing process of "internal conversation" with themselves, in which they weigh their options and decide their courses of action. She classified four main modes of this reflexivity. Communicative Reflexives need to discuss their thoughts with others before acting. Autonomous Reflexives decide alone and translate their thoughts directly into action. Meta-Reflexives engage in continuous critical reflection on themselves and their society. Fractured Reflexives fail to translate their inner reflection into effective, coherent action, their deliberation tending to intensify distress instead of guiding action.

## Impact

This framework offered an influential and methodologically explicit approach to the structure/agency problem, and it influenced the sociology of education and studies of reflexive identity. Archer presided over the International Sociological Association (1986-1990), the first woman to hold the post, and later served as president of the Pontifical Academy of Social Sciences (2014-2019). Her earlier works include *Social Origins of Educational Systems* (1979) and *Culture and Agency* (1988). In building "analytical dualism," Archer drew on the philosophy of "critical realism" founded by the philosopher Roy Bhaskar. It assumes the existence of real social structures with actual causal powers, and not merely discursive or purely linguistic structures as some post-structuralist currents claimed, while at the same time rejecting the strict structural determinism that denies any real margin for independent human agency. The morphogenetic approach grew out of her comparative research on state education systems, which analyzed how group interaction produced centralized or decentralized structures that then conditioned later interaction and change. She contrasted her approach with theories she regarded as overly deterministic, such as Bourdieu's account of habitus and cultural reproduction, and with Giddens's structuration theory, and these contrasts have been much discussed.
