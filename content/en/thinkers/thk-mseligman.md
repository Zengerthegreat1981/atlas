# Martin Seligman

Martin E. P. Seligman (born 1942) is an American psychologist, a professor at the University of Pennsylvania, and one of the best-known psychologists of recent decades. He began by studying learned helplessness in the laboratory, then became the most prominent face of the positive psychology movement, which he proclaimed when he assumed the presidency of the American Psychological Association in 1998. He is credited with the theory of the optimistic explanatory style and with a series of widely read books, and his career is also surrounded by ethical and scientific controversies, which this entry presents.

## Biography and Context

Seligman was born in Albany, New York, studied at Princeton University, and received his doctorate from the University of Pennsylvania in 1967. He remained at that university, where he directs the Positive Psychology Center. He came to psychology at a time when behaviorism dominated, and his early findings challenged some of its assumptions.

## Learned Helplessness

The first experiments were carried out in 1967 with Overmier and Maier (Overmier & Seligman in the Journal of Comparative and Physiological Psychology; Seligman & Maier in the Journal of Experimental Psychology), in which dogs were exposed to inescapable electric shocks and then placed in a situation in which they could escape; many of them made no attempt. Seligman concluded that the organism learns that outcomes are unrelated to its actions, and so becomes passive. He then offered this discovery as a model of human depression. In 1978, with Abramson and Teasdale, he reformulated the theory, making the decisive factor the explanatory style, that is, the way a person explains negative events: whether internal or external, stable or temporary, global or specific. His book *Helplessness* was published in 1975.

In 2016 Maier and Seligman published in Psychological Review a review titled "Learned Helplessness at Fifty," in which they argued, on the basis of neuroscience, that passivity is the default response to uncontrollable shock, that what the organism learns is control, and that this process takes place in the medial prefrontal cortex. It is thus a reading that reverses the original explanation.

## Optimism and Positive Psychology

In *Learned Optimism* (1991) he argued that optimism is an explanatory style that can be trained, and he offered exercises drawn from cognitive therapy that he called the ABCDE model: adversity, belief, consequence, disputation and energization. It was followed by *The Optimistic Child* (1995).

In his 1998 presidential address he called for psychology to expand from the treatment of illness to the study of what makes life worth living. With Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi he wrote an introductory article in a special issue of American Psychologist in 2000 titled "Positive Psychology: An Introduction." *Authentic Happiness* (2002) followed, and then, with Christopher Peterson, *Character Strengths and Virtues* (2004), a handbook that classifies twenty-four character strengths under six virtues: wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance and transcendence, and from which the VIA questionnaire for measuring strengths was derived.

In *Flourish* (2011) he replaced the authentic happiness theory with a multidimensional theory of well-being, arguing that the ultimate goal of positive psychology is human flourishing rather than merely positive mood. He organized flourishing into five core pillars abbreviated as the **PERMA** model:
- **Positive emotions (P)**: feeling good, experiencing pleasure, gratitude, hope, and contentment.
- **Engagement (E)**: being completely absorbed in activities, experiencing "flow" where time stops and capacities match challenges.
- **Relationships (R)**: authentic, positive social connections, mutual support, and intimacy.
- **Meaning (M)**: belonging to and serving something considered larger than the self (such as family, science, community, or spirituality).
- **Accomplishment (A)**: the pursuit of success, mastery, and achievement for its own sake.

### Post-Traumatic Growth and Grit

Seligman also expanded positive psychology into domains of resilience and persistent effort:
- **Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG)**: drawing on the research of Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, Seligman emphasized that severe adversity and trauma do not inevitably lead solely to PTSD or a mere return to baseline functioning. For many individuals, struggling with trauma can catalyze deep positive psychological change, including an intensified appreciation for life, deepened relationships, and new spiritual or existential clarity. This principle was incorporated into the Penn Resiliency Program and military resilience training.
- **Grit**: within the Positive Psychology Center at the University of Pennsylvania, Seligman mentored and worked alongside Angela Duckworth, whose landmark research defined "grit" as passion and perseverance for long-term goals. Duckworth and colleagues reported that grit predicted success in demanding domains (such as West Point military training and spelling bee contests), sometimes beyond what standardized measures of cognitive ability predicted; the construct's predictive value has since been debated.

In 2018 he published *The Hope Circuit*, a memoir and review of his career.

## His Place in the Field

He is linked to the legacy of Maslow and Rogers in their concern with growth and potential, but he presented it in the language of empirical study and measurement rather than philosophical discourse, which is what distinguishes positive psychology from the humanistic movement. His collaboration with Csikszentmihalyi, the originator of the concept of flow, is what led their two names to be mentioned together at the founding.

## Reception and Criticism

Positive psychology achieved a broad institutional presence, but it drew criticism from several directions. Becker and Marecek argued in an article in the journal Theory & Psychology in 2008 that it presents an individualistic picture of happiness and overlooks the influence of social and political conditions on well-being. Barbara Ehrenreich criticized it in *Bright-Sided* (2009) because, in her view, it places the burden on the individual and covers up structural injustice, a criticism that recurs in critical studies of happiness as a duty. On the methodological side, it emerged that a number of the results of positive interventions have small effects and that some studies were not easily reproduced.

His role in the Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program, which the US Army adopted in 2009 on the basis of the Penn Resiliency Program, is also criticized, and Eidelson, Pilisuk and Soldz wrote in American Psychologist in 2011 about its dark sides, among them the lack of sufficient prior evaluation. As for the controversy over torture after 2001, the 2015 Hoffman report for the American Psychological Association reported that Seligman gave a lecture on learned helplessness at a meeting connected to the Survival, Evasion, Resistance and Escape (SERE) program in 2002; Seligman affirms that he did not know his ideas were being used in interrogation, and the matter has differing readings.
