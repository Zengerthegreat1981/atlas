# Harvey Sacks

An American sociologist (1935–1975) who founded **Conversation Analysis**, a research programme that treats ordinary everyday talk not as chaotic behaviour needing to be cleaned up before study, but as an activity **organized down to its finest details**, an organization that can be described with high descriptive precision. His radical thesis: social order is not something inferred from aggregate statistics but something **accomplished and reproduced in every conversational exchange**, and it can be captured from a recording of a two-minute telephone call.

## What He Contributed

- **Moving the subject matter of sociology to the audio recording**: he insisted on working with **recorded natural materials** (calls to suicide-prevention lines, ordinary calls) instead of interviews, questionnaires and recall from memory. The justification is methodological: the recording allows endless re-listening and public verification of the analysis, which the researcher's written observation does not allow.
- **The turn-taking system**: in the founding paper "A Simplest Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation" (1974, with Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson), he showed that alternation in speaking is managed by precise local rules: turn-constructional units, possible transition places, and rules for allocating the next turn. The observed result: far less silence and overlap than any random model would predict, that is, a coordination machine operating without the speakers' awareness of it.
- **Adjacency pairs**: a question creates a **normative obligation** to answer, a greeting to a greeting, an invitation to an acceptance or refusal. The strength of the idea is that it explains absence: a non-answer is not a non-event but a **noticeable event for which one is held accountable** and which generates an explanation ("he didn't hear me", "he's ignoring me").
- **Preference organization**: responses are not structurally equivalent. Acceptance comes quickly and simply; refusal comes late, preceded by hesitation, preface and justification. That is, "politeness" is not a psychological trait of the speaker but a **structural form observed in the sequencing of talk**.
- **Repair**: conversation has a built-in system for dealing with problems of hearing, understanding and speaking, with a clear order of preference: self-repair by the speaker is preferred over the other's repair of the speaker, an observation that explains why correcting others seems a socially heavy act.
- **Membership categorization devices**: how speakers choose one particular description from among descriptions that are all correct ("woman", "doctor", "my neighbour", "the car driver"), and how the choice invokes rights, duties and expectations. In his famous analysis of a child's sentence ("The baby cried. The mommy picked it up.") he showed how we immediately read the mommy as **the baby's own mother** with no statement to that effect in the text, which is a social inference and not a linguistic one.
- **"Doing 'Being Ordinary'"**: ordinariness is not a state one falls into but an **accomplishment one works at** with skill and diligence. It is among the most incisive writing in the sociology of everyday life.

## His Place in the Current

- **Extension of ethnomethodology and break from it**: a younger associate of Harold Garfinkel, he took from him the basic question (how do members practically produce their social order?) but replaced breaching experiments with a descriptive, sequential method applied to recorded materials, so that conversation analysis became a discipline in its own right with its own journals and standards.
- **From Goffman and beyond**: he studied with Erving Goffman at Berkeley and inherited from him the seriousness of taking face-to-face interaction as an independent subject, but he rejected Goffman's theatrical and interpretive vocabulary ("impression management") in favour of a verifiable description of the sequence itself.
- **Phenomenological root through Schutz**: Alfred Schutz's typifications and stock of knowledge at hand are the theoretical background to the question of "how people categorize one another in the moment"; but Sacks brought the question down from philosophical description to empirical data.
- **Break with generative linguistics**: whereas mainstream linguistics separated the competence of the ideal speaker from actual performance and treated ordinary speech as "defective", Sacks made **actual performance itself** the site of order. His later influence is wide in pragmatic analysis and interactional linguistics.
- **Applied impact**: conversation analysis became a standard tool in the study of medical communication, judicial and police interrogation, crisis lines, the classroom, and later the design of computer dialogue systems.

## Limitation

- **An edited, not authored, corpus**: he died in a car accident in 1975 at the age of forty, and no book of his was published in his lifetime. *Lectures on Conversation*, published posthumously in 1992, was edited by Gail Jefferson from recordings of his lectures, and the problem of textual attribution here is real: what Sacks said while exploring aloud is read today as though it were a settled thesis.
- **The charge of "methodological triviality"**: the recurrent criticism is that high descriptive precision yields small-scale results, a splendid description of the machinery of talk that says nothing about class, power or the state.
- **Deliberate omission of structure and context**: classical analysis refuses to bring class, gender and race into the explanation unless **the speakers themselves make them relevant in the talk itself**. This is a consistent methodological condition, but it is sharply contested: feminist and critical critique (and the critical discourse analysis of Fairclough and van Dijk) holds that domination works especially when it goes unspoken.
- **The limit of generalizing from the sample**: the system was founded on white, middle-class American telephone calls of the 1960s and 1970s. The claim that turn-taking and repair mechanisms are universal needs verification in other languages and cultures, and later comparative work has shown real variation in the amounts of silence and in the patterns of overlap.
- **The burden of transcription**: the method requires careful transcription in the Jefferson system that consumes hours for every minute of speech, which keeps samples small and limits scalability.

## His Most Important Works

- "A Simplest Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation" (with Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson, *Language*, 1974).
- "On the Analyzability of Stories by Children" (1972).
- "On Doing 'Being Ordinary'" (lecture, published posthumously in 1984 in *Structures of Social Action*).
- "The Preference for Self-Correction in the Organization of Repair in Conversation" (with Schegloff and Jefferson, *Language*, 1977, published after his death).
- *Lectures on Conversation* (two volumes, edited by Gail Jefferson, published posthumously in 1992, seventeen years after his death).
