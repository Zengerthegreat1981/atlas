# Walter W. Powell

An American sociologist, professor at Stanford University, and co-founder (with Paul DiMaggio) of the "new institutionalism" in the sociology of organizations, focusing on how the "organizational field" is formed and on the logic of isomorphism within it.

## Contributions

- **Institutional isomorphism**: with Paul DiMaggio he wrote "The Iron Cage Revisited" (1983), explaining organizational similarity by pressures for legitimacy (see below).
- **The concept of the organizational field**: He helped develop the concept of the "organizational field," the set of organizations that together constitute a recognized area of institutional life (producers in a given industry, key funders, regulatory agencies, competing organizations producing similar services or products), as the most suitable unit of analysis for understanding how organizational norms and practices spread and converge over time.

## Historical Context

Walter Powell received his doctorate from Yale University and later held posts including at the University of Arizona before settling at Stanford University. He developed his pivotal collaboration with Paul DiMaggio, his colleague at Yale, within a broader intellectual context in the sociology of organizations in the late 1970s and 1980s, when many researchers began to challenge the assumption of the traditional economic theory of organization that the structure of organizations is shaped mainly in response to purely competitive economic-efficiency pressures, proposing instead that social and institutional factors (not purely technical-economic ones) play a decisive role in explaining why organizations resemble one another to such a great extent even when operating in entirely different environments and conditions.

## Key Ideas and Method

In "The Iron Cage Revisited" (1983), Powell and DiMaggio borrowed Max Weber's famous metaphor of the "iron cage" of mounting bureaucratic rationalization in modern society, but offering an alternative and complementary institutional-social explanation of why this "cage" persists and intensifies to this day: not only bureaucratic technical efficiency, as Weber originally assumed, but also organizations' pressing need for social legitimacy and mutual similarity within the "organizational field" to which they belong, that is, the set of organizations that together constitute a recognized area of institutional life (producers in a given industry, key funders, governmental regulatory agencies, competing organizations producing similar services or products). Powell and DiMaggio identified three main mechanisms driving "institutional isomorphism" among different organizations within a single organizational field: "coercive" isomorphism, resulting from direct legal or governmental regulatory pressure that imposes uniform standards; "mimetic" isomorphism, to which organizations resort under conditions of environmental uncertainty by imitating other organizations that appear successful or legitimate, without certain knowledge that what they imitate is actually effective, seeking psychological and social security more than technical certainty; and "normative" isomorphism, resulting from shared professionalization (graduates of the same business schools, uniform professional standards across professional associations), which spreads similar norms and practices across different organizations that employ staff from the same professional background.

## Major Works

Besides "The Iron Cage Revisited" (1983), Powell and DiMaggio jointly edited an influential collection titled *The New Institutionalism in Organizational Analysis* (1991), which gathered the leading founding research of this current. Powell also contributed influential later research on collaboration among organizations: "Neither Market nor Hierarchy: Network Forms of Organization" (1990) treated networks as a distinct governance form, and with Kenneth Koput and Laurel Smith-Doerr he studied "networks of learning" in biotechnology ("Interorganizational Collaboration and the Locus of Innovation", *Administrative Science Quarterly*, 1996). He also edited work on the nonprofit sector.

## Place in the Current

Together with DiMaggio, Powell helped found a complete reference framework in contemporary organizational sociology that explains widespread organizational similarity by institutional-social factors (the search for legitimacy, imitation under uncertainty, shared professional norms) and not by competitive economic efficiency alone, as traditional economic theories of organization assumed, and their article is among the most cited in organizational sociology.
