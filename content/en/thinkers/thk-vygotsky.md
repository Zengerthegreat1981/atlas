# Lev Vygotsky

A Soviet Jewish psychologist, founder of **Sociocultural Theory** in developmental psychology, and one of the most important theorists of the relation between language and consciousness. He lived a short life (1896–1934) and left a research legacy that was not translated into English until the 1960s, which brought about a **revolution in the understanding of cognitive development**. Vygotsky is regarded as Piaget's principal "intellectual counterpart", and as the turning point from structural individualism to the sociocultural approach in the twentieth century.

## What He Contributed

### The Central Hypothesis: Development Through Culture

Vygotsky held that **cognitive development does not occur within the individual in isolation**, but in a **sociocultural context**, through **mediating tools** (mediation), above all language. The short formulation: **"Every function in the child's cultural development appears twice: first on the social level, and later on the individual level; first between people (interpsychological), and then inside the child (intrapsychological)."**

This means that the child does not acquire language merely as a tool of communication, but **language reshapes his very thinking** — as if the child borrows the thinking of others before internalizing it.

### The Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)

The most important concept Vygotsky introduced, in his work of the early 1930s. The ZPD is commonly summarized as (a paraphrase of the formulation in *Mind in Society*):

> **The distance between the child's actual level of performance (what he can do alone) and the level of potential performance with the help of a more knowledgeable person (what he can do with guidance).**

What falls within this zone is the real **site of learning**. The teacher or adult does not teach what the child can do alone (no point), nor what is entirely impossible for him (no benefit), but what he can do **with brief help** that can be gradually withdrawn (fading).

### Scaffolding

The term was coined after Vygotsky's death, by Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), to describe the process by which a more knowledgeable person provides help within the zone of proximal development. Scaffolding is temporary and is removed when the child can act independently. The concept later influenced educational practice.

### The More Knowledgeable Other (MKO)

Not necessarily the "mother" or the "teacher" — it may be an older child, a book, or a computer program. What matters is that it possesses knowledge or a skill the child does not possess at that moment, and that it offers it in an assimilable form.

### Inner Speech

In his late works (1934), Vygotsky distinguished three types of speech:

1. **Social speech (External Speech)**: communication with others.
2. **Speech aloud to oneself (Private Speech)**: the child talks to himself aloud while solving a problem — a natural **transitional stage**.
3. **Inner speech**: the transformation of audible speech into silent, abbreviated, condensed thinking; the sound disappears and the meaning remains.

This path explains **why children talk aloud while playing or solving puzzles** — it is not random talk but a tool of self-regulation in the process of internalization.

## His Place in the Current

Vygotsky occupies a **pivotal position** in developmental psychology:

- **Versus Piaget**: Piaget saw cognitive development as individual and structural (the child discovers knowledge by manipulating objects), while Vygotsky saw development as fundamentally social. Nevertheless, it is noted that both converge on the idea of "construction" (constructivism), and their disagreement is internal to the **constructivist current**.
- **Versus Pavlov and official Soviet psychology**: Vygotsky was in a fragile position under the Stalinist regime — his works were accepted in his lifetime and then banned after his death in 1934, and were not recovered until the late 1950s.
- **His influence on Alexei Leontiev** and **Alexander Luria**: the three formed the "troika" at the Institute of Experimental Psychology in Moscow.

## Limitations
- **The political course of his thought**: he was subjected to a Stalinist ban after his death in 1934 (he was accused of "idealism" and "distortion of Marxism"), and the publication of his complete works was not permitted until the end of the 1950s. This means that many of the references accepted today are a **post-historical reconstruction** by his students.
- **The educational applications** of the ZPD concept built on it later — such as the scaffolding model of Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976) — sometimes conflate **the original concept in Vygotsky** with **later extensions** that may not accurately reflect what he intended.
- There is no unified, authoritative Arabic translation of his terms (Zone of Proximal Development has been rendered in Arabic by several variant names).

## Major Works

- **"Psychology of Art"** (*Психология искусства*, 1925) — his early work on the psychology of literary creativity (written in 1925, first published in Russian in 1965).
- **"Thought and Language"** (*Мышление и речь*, 1934) — his greatest work, published after his death, and translated into English, also posthumously, in 1962.
- **"Mind in Society"** — a collection of lectures published posthumously in English in 1978, edited by Michael Cole, Vera John-Steiner and Sylvia Scribner.
- **"Fundamentals of Defectology"** (*Основы дефектологии*) — his writings of the late 1920s and early 1930s on disability (hearing impairment, visual impairment, intellectual disability) — he was among the first to advocate educating children with disabilities within a unified school system.

## The Rule of the "Instrumental Element" (Mediation)

Vygotsky developed, from Marxist ideas about tools and labor, the concept of **"mediation"**: the relation between the human being and the environment is not direct but **mediated by psychological tools**, above all **language**. The child does not interact with the world directly, but through the symbols and tools that his culture provides. These tools **reshape** the higher psychological functions (abstract thinking, planning, control of emotions, conscious memory) from their primitive biological form into their cultural form.

The fundamental difference between Piaget and Vygotsky here: Piaget focused on **objects** (the child explores water and cups), while Vygotsky focused on **symbolic mediation** (the child explores the world through his mother's language and the concepts of his culture).
