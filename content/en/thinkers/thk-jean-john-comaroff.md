# Jean & John Comaroff

Jean Comaroff and John L. Comaroff are South African-born anthropologists, a married couple, who have worked together for decades. Their long-term fieldwork among the Tshidi Barolong, a Tswana-speaking people in South Africa and Botswana, underlies a body of joint work on colonialism, religion, law, and capitalism that makes them among the most prominent figures in the historical anthropology of southern Africa.

## Biography and Academic Career

Both earned doctorates at the London School of Economics. They carried out fieldwork among the Tswana in the borderlands of South Africa and Botswana, taught for many years at the University of Chicago, and later moved to Harvard University. Because they publish largely as co-authors, they are treated here in a single profile.

## Of Revelation and Revolution

In *Of Revelation and Revolution: Christianity, Colonialism, and Consciousness in South Africa* (vol. 1, 1991; vol. 2, 1997), the Comaroffs examined with long-term historical and ethnographic attention how nineteenth-century Protestant missionaries from British societies, above all the London Missionary Society, became entangled with economic and political colonialism among the Tswana. They showed that "conversion" was not simply a doctrinal imposition from above but a contested process of negotiation, resistance, and mutual adaptation, producing hybrid forms of religious and political consciousness that were neither purely Western nor purely "traditional."

They argued that the missionary project worked less through explicit belief than through everyday life: dress, house form, agriculture, and ideas of time and work. This is the basis of their account of the "colonization of consciousness" and of the ways in which hegemony operates through small details of daily practice rather than through coercion alone.

## Historical Anthropology

The Comaroffs combined archival work (missionary and colonial records) with prolonged ethnography. Their essays in *Ethnography and the Historical Imagination* (1992) became a model for historical anthropology, challenging the separation between history and social anthropology and the assumption that modernity moves along a single line from the "traditional" to the "modern."

## Occult Economies and Millennial Capitalism

In essays on "occult economies" and "millennial capitalism" (late 1990s and early 2000s), they analysed the proliferation of beliefs in witchcraft and zombies in South Africa and elsewhere as a cultural response to the contradictions of global neoliberal capitalism, such as sudden unexplained wealth and structural unemployment, rather than as lingering "superstition."

## Law and Disorder in the Postcolony

In *Law and Disorder in the Postcolony* (2006), an edited volume, they examined how a widespread preoccupation with law, crime, and the rule of law accompanies rising inequality and insecurity in postcolonial states, and how the legitimacy of the state is tested in this context. In *Ethnicity, Inc.* (2009) they turned to the commodification of cultural identity, and in *Theory from the South* (2012) they argued that the global South is a source of theory about the contemporary world, not only a site of data.

## Impact and Criticism

The Comaroffs are among the most influential anthropologists of their generation in the study of colonialism and its aftermath in Africa, and their work has influenced colonial history, the study of religion, and the anthropology of capitalism. Critics have questioned the breadth and sometimes abstract register of their generalizations about neoliberalism and about the postcolony, and some Africanist scholars have asked whether claims drawn largely from southern Africa extend to the rest of the continent.
