# Friedrich Nietzsche

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) is one of the two founding fathers of existential thought, alongside Søren Kierkegaard — and his exact opposite in the answer he gives. Where Kierkegaard turned toward faith, Nietzsche diagnosed the collapse of inherited meaning and demanded that human beings create values of their own. His aphoristic, unsystematic writing has made him one of the most quoted and most contested philosophers of the modern age.

## Life and Career

Nietzsche was born on 15 October 1844 in Röcken, in Prussian Saxony, the son of a Lutheran pastor who died when Friedrich was four. He studied classical philology at Bonn and Leipzig, and in 1869, at twenty-four and before completing his doctorate, he was appointed professor of classical philology at the University of Basel. There he published his first book, *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872), an unorthodox study of Greek culture that scandalised his philological colleagues and already announced his real subject: the tension between Apollonian order and Dionysian ecstasy in human life.

Ill health forced his resignation from Basel in 1879. For the next decade he lived a wandering, solitary life in Switzerland, Italy and France, writing the books on which his reputation rests between 1878 and 1888. In January 1889 he collapsed in Turin and never recovered his mind. He spent his remaining eleven years in the care of his mother and then his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, who managed his archive and did much to shape, and distort, his posthumous reputation. He died in Weimar on 25 August 1900.

## Key Works

Nietzsche's mature work falls into a clear sequence. *Human, All Too Human* (1878) broke with his early Wagnerian enthusiasms and adopted the aphoristic style that became his signature. *The Gay Science* (1882) introduced the death of God. *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1883–1885) is his most literary book, a prophetic poem-philosophy spoken by the Persian sage Zarathustra. *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886) and *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887) are his most systematic works of moral criticism. *Twilight of the Idols* (written 1888, published 1889) and *The Antichrist* (written 1888, published 1895) were among his final polemics. The posthumous *The Will to Power* was assembled by his sister from notebooks, not composed by him in that form.

## The Death of God and Nihilism

"The death of God" is not an announcement but a cultural diagnosis: the guarantees that once supplied meaning from outside — God, metaphysical truth, moral absolutes — have lost their credibility in modern Europe. The consequence, unless an alternative is created, is nihilism: the sense that nothing matters, that values have no ground. Nietzsche regarded nihilism as both a danger and an opportunity — the honest recognition of a world without given meaning, which clears the ground for the creation of new values.

## Will to Power, Eternal Recurrence, and the Übermensch

The will to power is Nietzsche's central psychological and metaphysical idea, and it is widely misunderstood. It is not the drive to dominate other people but the drive to grow, to overcome one's present condition, to become more than one is. Every living thing, on this view, seeks not mere self-preservation but expansion and self-overcoming.

The eternal recurrence, introduced in *The Gay Science*, is a thought experiment rather than a cosmological doctrine: suppose you had to live this life, exactly as it is, again and again for eternity — would you curse the demon who told you, or bless it? The question tests whether one can affirm one's life unconditionally. From it comes *amor fati*, love of fate: acceptance of what was and what is, not as resignation but as an active affirmation of life. In *Zarathustra*, Nietzsche presents the Übermensch, the "overman", as the figure who creates values after the death of God — not a biologically superior type, as later misreadings claimed, but a human possibility: one who gives form to one's own life rather than inheriting it.

## Genealogy of Morals and Perspectivism

In *On the Genealogy of Morals*, Nietzsche traces the history of moral values and argues that they arose from relations of power and conflict. What he calls "slave morality", built on ressentiment — the resentment of the powerless — inverted the older "master morality" that prized strength, pride and nobility, and revalued humility, pity and obedience as virtues. The method was revolutionary: values are not timeless truths but products of a history that can be investigated. His perspectivism holds that there is no knowledge without a point of view, and that a multiplicity of perspectives enriches understanding rather than abolishing truth. To "become what you are" is therefore not to discover a ready-made self but to make one.

## Influence and Critique

Nietzsche's influence runs through twentieth-century philosophy, literature, psychology and political thought: existentialism, psychoanalysis, post-structuralism and modernist art all bear his mark. In the clinic his influence can be traced in the reluctance to direct a client toward a ready-made meaning and in work on unexamined values; he stands in contrast to Frankl within the existential tradition, since Frankl insists that meaning is discovered rather than created.

His legacy is complicated by the political misuse of his work. His sister's editing, her nationalist sympathies, and the Nazi appropriation of slogans like the Übermensch and the will to power attached his name to a politics he explicitly attacked — he denounced German nationalism and antisemitism in print. That history of distortion is displayed openly and is not used to dismiss his thought. Critics also note that his aphoristic, fragmentary style invites contradictory readings, and that his attacks on Christian and democratic morality can feed an elitism that despises the weak — a use Nietzsche himself rejected.
