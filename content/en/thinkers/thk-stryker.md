# Sheldon Stryker

An American sociologist (1924–2016), professor at Indiana University, and founder of "Identity Theory" as a more structural and quantitatively measurable version of symbolic interactionism, in response to what he saw as the interpretive looseness of Herbert Blumer's version.

## What He Contributed

- **Structural critique of Blumer**: Stryker criticized Blumer's exclusive focus on the momentary fluidity of meaning formed in direct interaction, arguing that society in fact imposes relatively stable structures (social roles, statuses, networks of relations) that limit this absolute fluidity and make systematic sociological prediction, testable empirically, possible, rather than merely a later interpretive description of each case individually.
- **Identity theory**: in *Symbolic Interactionism: A Social Structural Version* (1980) he developed a framework that assumes the individual possesses multiple "identities" tied to his or her different social roles (father, employee, friend, club member), organized in a "salience hierarchy" that determines which of these identities is most likely to be activated and to affect behaviour in a given situation. He formulated a central principle: the greater the "commitment" to a particular identity, that is, the number and importance of the social relations built specifically on enacting that identity, the higher its salience in the hierarchy and the greater the likelihood of its activation.

## His Place in the Current

With this development, Stryker offered a framework that can be measured quantitatively and tested empirically and systematically within the symbolic interactionist tradition, bringing it closer to the methodology of structural social psychology, which is statistically verifiable, than to the interpretive tradition that Blumer defended. His work directly paved the way for Peter Burke's later development of identity verification theory.

## Relation to the Iowa School and Manford Kuhn

Stryker's orientation toward measurable approaches had a precursor in the sociologist Manford Kuhn, founder of what is known as the "Iowa School" of symbolic interactionism, who developed early quantitative measurement tools (such as the "Who Am I?" Twenty Statements Test) to study the concept of the self in a statistically replicable way, in contrast to Blumer's unmeasured interpretive methodology in the "Chicago School". Stryker drew on this methodological legacy of the Iowa School but added to it a deeper structural dimension that explicitly links the structure of objective social roles (networks, commitments) with the formation of the hierarchy of personal identities, going beyond Kuhn's narrower focus on measuring the self-concept alone, apart from a broader structural analysis.

## Identity Verification in Peter Burke

Peter Burke, a long-time associate of Stryker at Indiana University, developed "Identity Verification Theory" as a direct extension of Stryker's identity theory, adding a precise psychological-social mechanism: the individual constantly compares the "meaning" of his or her identity as subjectively conceived with the actual reactions of others toward him or her in interaction, and any contradiction between the two (the identity's not being socially "verified") produces psychological distress that drives the individual to modify his or her behaviour or interpretation of the situation in order to restore consistency, in a circular feedback loop model that can be tested empirically and rigorously.

## Academic Biography

Sheldon Stryker (1924-2016) took his doctorate at Cornell University and spent most of his career as a professor of sociology at Indiana University. His early formation was in classical symbolic interactionism; he later developed a structural version that borrowed measurement concerns from empirical social psychology. An early statement is his 1968 article "Identity Salience and Role Performance".

## Applications of Identity Theory

Identity theory has been applied to occupational identity and the balance of work and family, commitment to volunteer and civic roles, and the negotiation among multiple, sometimes conflicting identities. Its central predictions, that commitment raises the salience of an identity and that salience raises the likelihood of role-consistent behaviour, have been tested with survey and experimental data, and the theory is summarized in Burke and Stets (2009).
