# Émile Durkheim

Émile Durkheim (1858-1917) was a French sociologist and one of the founders of the discipline. He argued that social facts are real, external to individuals, and explainable by other social facts, and he studied solidarity, suicide, and religion.

## Life and Context

Durkheim was born in Épinal, in the Lorraine region of France, in 1858, into a Jewish family with a rabbinical tradition. He studied at the École Normale Supérieure, taught philosophy in secondary schools and then pedagogy and social science at the University of Bordeaux, and from 1902 was at the Sorbonne in Paris. In 1913 his chair was renamed to include sociology. He founded the journal *L'Année sociologique* in 1898 and gathered a school of collaborators including Marcel Mauss, Henri Hubert and Maurice Halbwachs. The French Third Republic's concerns about moral order and secular education shaped his work, and he publicly defended Dreyfus. His son André died in the First World War in 1915, and Durkheim died in 1917.

## Main Ideas

- **Social facts:** ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that are external to the individual and exert constraint.
- **Mechanical and organic solidarity:** in simple societies cohesion rests on similarity; in modern societies it rests on interdependence through the division of labor. He treated repressive law as a sign of the first and restitutive law as a sign of the second.
- **Anomie:** a condition of weak or unclear social regulation, linked to certain forms of suicide and to rapid social change, including economic disorder.
- **Moral individualism:** in "Individualism and the Intellectuals" (1898) he argued that respect for the person can itself be a shared moral ideal.
- **Four types of suicide:** egoistic, altruistic, anomic, and fatalistic, related to levels of social integration and regulation.
- **Religion and collective effervescence:** religion expresses and reinforces the collective life of society, distinguishing the sacred from the profane.
- **Collective representations:** shared categories of thought originate in social life. With Marcel Mauss, in *Primitive Classification* (1903), he related systems of classification to social organization. The shared beliefs and sentiments of a collective consciousness underpin his account of solidarity.

## Method

Durkheim urged treating social facts "as things" and explaining them sociologically rather than psychologically. He asked researchers to set aside preconceptions, to explain social facts by other social facts, and to distinguish causes from functions. Comparison, especially concomitant variation, stood in for experiment. In *Suicide* he compared official suicide rates across groups and countries, a landmark of quantitative, comparative social research. In *Elementary Forms* he analyzed ethnographic material on Australian Aboriginal totemism.

## Influence and Criticism

Durkheim shaped functionalism, structural anthropology, structuralism, and the sociology of religion, education, law and deviance. Critics have argued that his data on suicide were of uneven quality, that his account risks the ecological fallacy, that he understated conflict and power, and that his ethnographic sources were secondhand.

## Applications and Cross-References

Anomie was developed by Robert Merton in the theory of deviance, and Durkheimian ideas inform studies of civic ritual and social integration. Compare Marx on conflict and Weber on meaning and action. Anna Mueller and Seth Abrutyn (2016) studied an American community with repeated youth suicides and argued that close ties and demanding shared expectations of achievement could heighten fear of failure, spread knowledge of private troubles, and discourage help-seeking, so that strong integration is not automatically protective. Dimitris Xygalatas and colleagues (2013) found that participants in, and observers of, a painful Hindu ritual in Mauritius donated more than people observed after a less intense rite, which bears on the claim that collective ritual can strengthen moral attachment without establishing a full theory of religion.
