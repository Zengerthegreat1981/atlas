# Robert Michels

A German-Italian sociologist (1876–1936), best known for his critical study of the parties of the European left, from which he derived the "iron law of oligarchy", one of the most influential principles in the sociology of organizations and political parties.

## What He Contributed

- **The iron law of oligarchy (Das eherne Gesetz der Oligarchie)**: he formulated it in *Political Parties* (1911) on the basis of his study of the socialist parties of Europe, above all the German Social Democratic Party (SPD), parties that formally proclaimed internal democracy and equality, arguing that any large organization, regardless of its declared principles, inevitably tends toward the concentration of real power in the hands of a small leadership.
- **Organizational necessities**: he explained this tendency by structural organizational necessities and not by individual conspiracy: the technical specialization required for effective administration, control over the channels of information and internal communication, and the leadership's job stability as against the constant turnover of the ordinary rank-and-file membership. These are factors that give the leadership a cumulative superiority that the rank and file can hardly challenge democratically, whatever the declared intentions.

## His Place in the Current

- He is mentioned with Pareto and Mosca as the third of the "classical elite theory" figures, but his contribution is more institutionally specific (focusing on the party organization itself and not on society as a whole).
- His analysis deeply influenced the sociology of political parties, trade unions and contemporary non-governmental organizations, since his law is cited even today to explain the internal-democracy gap in organizations that proclaim themselves democratic.

## Limitation

- The "iron law" has sometimes been generalized in an excessively deterministic way that ignores counter-cases documenting the success of some organizations in maintaining a relatively effective internal democracy through institutional mechanisms designed specifically to counter this tendency.
- His later joining of the Italian Fascist Party (1928) raised questions about the relation between his early pessimism about the possibility of organizational democracy and his later political turn. Michels began as a socialist and syndicalist sympathizer, left Germany for Italy after being barred from an academic career there because of his politics, taught at Turin, Basel and, from 1928, Perugia, and drifted toward nationalism and then Fascism. Some commentators see continuity (his elitist pessimism fitted charismatic leadership), others stress that *Political Parties* was a diagnosis of democratic organization, not a programme, and that reading the later politics back into the 1911 book is not warranted by direct evidence; the question remains debated.

## His Most Important Works

- *Zur Soziologie des Parteiwesens in der modernen Demokratie* (1911; English translation by Eden and Cedar Paul, *Political Parties*, 1915).
