# Rudolf Steiner

Austrian philosopher and thinker (1861–1925), born in Kraljevec (present-day Croatia, then within the Austro-Hungarian Empire). He began his career as an editor of Goethe's scientific writings in Vienna and then Weimar, and as a philosopher in works including *The Philosophy of Freedom* (1894). In 1902 he joined the Theosophical Society and became General Secretary of its German section.

## Definition

Steiner is the direct founder of the Anthroposophical movement (Anthroposophy), a modern Western esoteric system that claims it is possible to attain a methodically disciplined spiritual knowledge of supersensible worlds through gradual inner training, rather than through revelation or passive visionary experience. Steiner broke away from the Theosophical Society in 1912–1913 after a dispute with its leadership at Adyar in India over the promotion of the young Jiddu Krishnamurti as an expected "World Teacher." Steiner rejected this as a departure from the Western-Christian roots of the movement, which he wished to preserve.

## Historical Context

Anthroposophy emerged in the context of the flourishing of modern Western esoteric currents in Europe at the beginning of the twentieth century. The Theosophical Society, founded by Helena Blavatsky, had established a broad interest in the Hermetic tradition, Rosicrucian symbolism and claims of an "esoteric wisdom" of the East. Steiner sought to offer an alternative that would re-anchor this interest in a Western-Christian European soil, drawing on his own reading of Goethe, Fichte and Hegel, and developing a method he called "spiritual science" (Geisteswissenschaft), which claimed to be trainable and verifiable, similar to the method of the natural sciences though its subject matter was supersensible.

## Main Ideas and Method

In his book *Theosophie* (1904) Steiner set out a systematic account of the threefold spiritual structure of the human being (body, soul and spirit), and of the worlds after death. In *An Outline of Occult Science* (1910) he elaborated a complete evolutionary cosmology of the earth and humanity through successive spiritual epochs. It reflects, in esoteric terminology, ideas of evolution and spiritual development current in German thought of his time. Steiner applied his system to several practical fields: education through the Waldorf schools, the first of which he founded in 1919 for workers at the Waldorf-Astoria cigarette factory in Stuttgart; agriculture through biodynamic agriculture, which grew out of lectures he gave in 1924; and medicine through what he developed together with the physician Ita Wegman under the name anthroposophic medicine.

## His Own Christian Doctrine

Unlike Blavatsky, who leaned towards a centring on Eastern traditions, Steiner made the "Christ Event" (Christ Event) the cosmic axis of humanity's entire spiritual development, regarding the crucifixion and resurrection as an actual turning point in the spiritual history of the earth rather than merely a doctrinal symbol. In doing so he combined concepts borrowed from Hinduism and Buddhism, such as karma and reincarnation, with a Western-Christian framework that reinterprets them within an individual evolutionary path towards spiritual freedom. This is what distinguished Anthroposophy from the original Theosophy as a path explicitly "Western" in its symbolic identity.

## Major Works and Representatives

His most important works include *The Philosophy of Freedom* (1894), *Theosophie* (1904), *How to Know Higher Worlds* (serialised 1904–1905), and *An Outline of Occult Science* (1910). He also wrote esoteric plays (the Mystery Dramas) and designed the Goetheanum building in Dornach, Switzerland (the first building burned in 1922 and was replaced by a second, completed in 1928), which remains the headquarters of the Anthroposophical Society.

## Impact and Criticism

Academic researchers study Steiner within the field of "studies of modern Western esotericism," in particular the German historian Helmut Zander, whose encyclopedic study *Anthroposophie in Deutschland* (2007) offers a historical-critical deconstruction of the actual sources of Steiner's ideas, as opposed to his claim of direct knowledge of spiritual worlds. Zander distinguishes between the documented historical biography and the internal Anthroposophical narrative of his life, which he also treated in his biography *Rudolf Steiner: Die Biographie* (2011). Steiner has also been criticized for some of his racial statements, linked to the evolutionary spiritual doctrines prevalent at the time, an issue that remains a matter of controversy within the Waldorf education movement to this day. Nevertheless, Steiner's institutional influence has been broad: over a thousand Waldorf schools exist around the world today, and biodynamic agriculture (certified through the Demeter label) and anthroposophic medicine continue as practical extensions of his system.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Steiner's writings contain no direct treatment of the Islamic tradition or of Arabic philosophy. His non-Western reference points were largely limited to Hinduism and Buddhism, drawn from Theosophical sources.
