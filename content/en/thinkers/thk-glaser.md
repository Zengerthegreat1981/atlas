# Barney Glaser

An American sociologist (1930–2022) who received his doctoral training at Columbia University before moving to the University of California, San Francisco, and a co-founder (with Anselm Strauss) of the "grounded theory" method in qualitative research. At UCSF he worked with Strauss on studies of hospital settings, and he remained the most committed defender of a classic, inductive version of the method.

## What He Contributed

- **Grounded theory**: With Anselm Strauss, in *The Discovery of Grounded Theory* (1967), he developed a systematic research method for generating sociological theory directly from the field data themselves, rather than confining research to testing hypotheses derived from grand pre-existing theories (such as the Parsonian structural functionalism then dominant). With his statistical background he helped formulate precise methodological procedures (coding, constant comparison, theoretical sampling) that give qualitative research a methodological rigor comparable to, though different in logic from, that of quantitative research.
- **His insistence on prior theoretical neutrality**: After he later split from Strauss methodologically, Glaser insisted strongly that the researcher must enter the field without prior theoretical hypotheses that direct his observation, leaving the theory to emerge from the data themselves with as little direction as possible.

## His Place in the Current

Thanks to this joint founding, grounded theory became one of the most widespread and widely used qualitative research methods across sociology, nursing, management and education worldwide, despite the later methodological split between its founders over the correct interpretation of the shared method itself.

## The Steps of the Method: Coding and Constant Comparison

Glaser and Strauss broke grounded theory down into sequential, applicable procedures. "Open Coding" dismantles raw data (interviews, field notes) into initial conceptual units. The "Constant Comparative Method" compares each new incident or statement with previously coded incidents in order to develop progressively more precise conceptual categories. In "Theoretical Sampling," the researcher decides whom to interview next or what data to collect based on what the emerging theory itself needs in order to develop, not on a statistical sampling plan fixed entirely in advance. This process ends when the conceptual categories reach "Theoretical Saturation": the point at which new data add no substantial further understanding or category. Memo writing records the developing ideas along the way, and the analyst identifies a core category around which the theory is integrated. In Glaser's classic version, researchers should not impose preconceived categories and should delay extensive literature review until the theory has begun to form; coding moves from open, substantive coding toward theoretical coding that relates categories to one another.

## Case Studies: The Research on Dying

The research on dying in California hospitals shows grounded theory being built. In *Awareness of Dying* (1965), Glaser and Strauss compared what staff, patients, and relatives knew about an approaching death across different wards and hospitals. From these comparisons came the concept of awareness contexts, including closed awareness, suspected awareness, mutual pretence, and open awareness, together with an account of how one context gives way to another and what each means for interaction. *Time for Dying* (1968) turned to dying trajectories: the expected course and pace of a patient's decline as staff perceive it. A sudden or unexpectedly prolonged death could disrupt ward routines and emotional composure, because work had been organised around a different expectation. The concepts were designed to be more general than any one ward while remaining tied to observed incidents. Deliberately choosing settings that differed, for instance in how quickly patients died and how aware they were, illustrates theoretical sampling and constant comparison.

## The Split with Anselm Strauss

A deep methodological dispute developed between Glaser and Strauss two decades after the publication of their joint work. It crystallized clearly when Strauss, with his collaborator Juliet Corbin, published a book (1990) presenting more structured and directive coding procedures ("Axial Coding") for linking conceptual categories to one another in a semi-procedural pattern fixed in advance. Glaser held that this imposes a prior structure on the data that conflicts with the original spirit of the method, which rests on the free "emergence" of theory from the data without prior direction. He published a sharp critical reply (*Basics of Grounded Theory Analysis*, 1992) accusing the Strauss-Corbin version of actually betraying the method's founding principles. It was a public dispute that lasted for decades and produced two distinct methodological schools for interpreting the same shared legacy.

## Kathy Charmaz's Constructivist Critique

The sociologist Kathy Charmaz, who trained with both Glaser and Strauss at the University of California, San Francisco, presented a third version of grounded theory that she called "Constructivist Grounded Theory." She criticized the implicit positivist assumption in Glaser's original version that social reality "awaits" objective, neutral discovery by a wholly neutral researcher. Charmaz held that the researcher does not neutrally discover a theory already present in the data but "constructs" an interpretation jointly with it, through interpretive interaction with the participants in the study and with the researcher's own cognitive and personal background. The resulting theory is therefore a participatory interpretive construction and not a purely objective discovery. This position is closer to the interpretive and phenomenological tradition than to the statistical positivism from which Glaser started by virtue of his training.

## The Spread of the Method beyond Sociology

The influence of grounded theory went beyond the bounds of purely academic sociology, and it became one of the most used qualitative research methods in many applied fields: nursing and health research (to understand patients' subjective experiences), business administration and organization (to study institutional work cultures), and education (to understand students' and teachers' experiences from the inside). This is thanks to its methodological flexibility and adaptability to very varied research contexts that are not necessarily united by a single shared sociological theoretical affiliation.

## Later Empirical Examples

Later health research shows the approach, in various versions, applied to new questions. Kathy Charmaz's *Good Days, Bad Days* (1991), based on interviews with chronically ill people, examined how illness disrupts the sense of time and self; it reflects her own development of grounded theory rather than Glaser's classic form. Celia Orona (1990) examined how a family member perceives the gradual loss of identity in a relative with Alzheimer's disease, including the experience of time and caregivers' efforts to maintain that identity; the article was later reprinted in Strauss and Corbin's *Grounded Theory in Practice* (1997). Both are studies of the late twentieth century and should not be taken to represent current practice as a whole. When reading any study described as grounded theory, it is useful to check whether data collection followed emerging concepts, whether comparison was continual, and whether the result is an integrated explanation or only a list of themes.

## Related Concepts

Related concepts include middle-range theory, induction, abduction, qualitative research, and the sociology of health and illness. Analytic induction and thematic analysis are useful contrasts: the former tests and revises an explanation against successive cases, while the latter identifies patterns of meaning without necessarily building an integrated theory. Symbolic interactionism, associated with Strauss's Chicago background, is a further connection, although Glaser did not regard grounded theory as tied to any single theoretical perspective.

## His Statistical Background and Its Effect on His Method

Glaser received his doctoral training at Columbia University, where he studied with Paul Lazarsfeld and Robert Merton, in a department strongly associated with quantitative methods. His training drew on Lazarsfeld's concern with systematic analysis and Merton's theory of the middle range, and it met Strauss's Chicago-school fieldwork tradition in the method they produced together. This is an unusual background for the founder of a qualitative method, but it explained his lasting insistence on the procedural methodological rigor of grounded theory (clear coding steps, criteria for verifying theoretical saturation) even though it is qualitative by nature. He sought to give qualitative research a scientific credibility comparable to that of the quantitative research in which he had originally trained, instead of settling for it as an intuitive, unstructured alternative to quantitative research.
