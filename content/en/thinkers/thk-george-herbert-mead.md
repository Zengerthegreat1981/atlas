# George Herbert Mead

George Herbert Mead (1863-1931) was an American philosopher and social psychologist whose account of how the self arises in social interaction became the foundation of symbolic interactionism.

## Life and Context

Mead was born in South Hadley, Massachusetts, and studied at Oberlin College and Harvard. He taught philosophy at the University of Michigan and from 1894 at the University of Chicago, where he was a colleague of John Dewey and part of the pragmatist circle. He published mainly articles in his lifetime. His best-known books were assembled after his death from lecture notes and manuscripts.

## Main Ideas

- **Social origin of the self.** The self is not given at birth but emerges through communication and by taking the attitude of others toward oneself.
- **Significant symbols.** Humans use gestures, above all language, that evoke in the speaker the same response as in the listener, enabling shared meaning.
- **Role-taking.** Children learn by taking the role of others, first in imitation and "play", and then in organised "games" that require awareness of several roles at once.
- **The generalized other.** The attitudes of the wider community become internalised and provide the standpoint from which individuals judge their own conduct.
- **"I" and "me".** The "me" is the self as seen through others' attitudes, while the "I" is the spontaneous, creative response of the individual.
- **Mind as a social process.** Thought is an internalised conversation of gestures.

## Major Works

- *The Philosophy of the Present* (1932)
- *Mind, Self and Society* (1934), edited by Charles W. Morris from student notes
- *Movements of Thought in the Nineteenth Century* (1936)
- *The Philosophy of the Act* (1938)

## Illustrations

Mead's account of play and the game draws on everyday child development: a child playing at being a parent or teacher takes one role at a time, while a child in an organized game such as baseball must anticipate the actions of all the other players at once. This is the basis of his account of the generalized other.

## Method

Mead's approach was pragmatist and behaviourist in a broad sense, with a focus on observable social acts and communication, though he treated inner experience as part of social conduct.

## Related Concepts

Mead's ideas connect with several concepts used across sociology and social psychology. The self and identity are the most direct, together with socialization, since play and the game describe how a child acquires the standpoint of others. The generalized other can be compared with Emile Durkheim's collective conscience, as both concern the presence of society within the individual, although Mead stresses ongoing communication. Charles Horton Cooley's looking-glass self is a close neighbour: it emphasises imagined judgements by others, while Mead gives a fuller account of language and role-taking. Role-taking and significant symbols are basic to symbolic interactionism and inform later work on the definition of the situation, a concept associated with W. I. Thomas, on Erving Goffman's presentation of self, and on labelling theory. Mead's pragmatism links the self to action and problem solving, in company with John Dewey. These concepts overlap without being interchangeable.

## Influence and Criticism

His student Herbert Blumer coined the term "symbolic interactionism" in 1937 and developed Mead's ideas into a research tradition. Mead also influenced Erving Goffman and Chicago sociology. Critics note that *Mind, Self and Society* is a posthumous compilation, so the exact wording is not Mead's own, and that his account gives little attention to power, structure and inequality. Some also ask how the creative "I" can be studied empirically.
