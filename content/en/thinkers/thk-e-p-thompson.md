# E. P. Thompson

A British Marxist historian by training rather than a sociologist (Edward Palmer Thompson, 1924–1993), included here for his influence on the sociology and history of class, and one of the founders of "History from Below." He was known for his sharp critique of Louis Althusser's structural Marxism and his defense of the role of agency and lived historical experience in the making of social class.

## What He Contributed

- **The Making of the English Working Class (1963)**: In this landmark work he showed how the English working class actually "made itself" through lived historical experience and conscious political and cultural struggle (1780–1832). It was not the automatic, inevitable product of purely objective structural economic changes, as simplified economistic Marxist readings assume when they reduce human history to a mechanical reflection of the economic base alone. Such readings neglect the role of cultural traditions and collective moral consciousness in shaping the actual historical action that people lived for themselves, rather than as abstract theory imagined it for them in advance. He defined class as a "historical relationship" formed in shared action and experience, not a fixed "structure" prior to human action.
- **Critique of Althusserian structural Marxism**: In *The Poverty of Theory* (1978) he attacked Louis Althusser's structuralist theory sharply. He accused it of turning Marxism into a closed abstract theoretical system that wholly excludes human agents and their lived, conscious experience in favor of abstract objective structures that determine everything in advance ("functions" without real agents). He regarded this as a fundamental betrayal of the spirit of the original historical humanist Marxism.

## The Making of Class as a Historical Relationship

In *The Making of the English Working Class*, which covers the period from the 1780s to the early 1830s, Thompson firmly rejected defining class as a rigid objective economic position measured merely by owning or not owning the means of production. He insisted instead that class forms historically when different people live a shared experience (similar economic exploitation, close work locations) and express this shared experience in a collective consciousness that crystallizes through culture, traditions, language and institutions (trade unions, reading societies, radical religious preaching). It thus turns from a mere category into a self-aware social force, conscious of its common interests and capable of collective political action.

## Career and Political Commitment

Thompson came out of the ranks of the British Communist Party, which he left in 1956 in protest at the Soviet suppression of the Hungarian uprising, and became one of the founders of the British "New Left" and a co-editor (with John Saville) of the journal *The New Reasoner*, which merged with *Universities and Left Review* in 1960 to form *New Left Review*. For years (from 1948) he worked as a tutor in adult education extra-mural programs in Yorkshire, northern England, a direct experience with working adult students that formed part of his inspiration for writing history "from below," recovering the voices of ordinary actors marginalized in the traditional official historical narratives that focus on elites, leaders and great events alone.

## The Concept of the Moral Economy

Among his most important additional contributions is the concept of the "Moral Economy of the Crowd," which he developed in his study of the bread riots in eighteenth-century England. He showed that these actions, which seemed to the superficial observer mere random hungry chaos, were in fact organized by a coherent popular moral logic. It rested on traditional norms about the "fair price" and a "minimum" right to subsistence, and it rose up against the logic of the new free market, which had begun to displace these traditional norms in favor of free speculative pricing. This offered an influential model for understanding traditional popular protest as conscious political and moral action and not merely a random venting of anger.

## His Place in the Current and Later Critique

Thompson represents the "humanist" pole of Marxism (Humanist Marxism), directly opposed to Althusserian structural Marxism. He stressed the role of agency and conscious historical experience in shaping class structure, not the reverse, and his debate with Althusser became one of the most important reference theoretical discussions within British Marxism in the late twentieth century. His work deeply influenced the development of the disciplines of "working-class history" and "history from below" worldwide, inspiring social historians in many fields (the history of slavery, women's history, colonial history) to recover the agency of historically marginalized groups. However, some orthodox Marxist critics held that his excessive focus on "experience" and "consciousness" risks sliding into an inadequate idealist explanation, out of commitment to the structural-economic analysis that remains essential in any coherent Marxist explanation of history. This disagreement remained open between Thompson's intellectual heirs and the heirs of the stricter structuralist tradition even after his death.
