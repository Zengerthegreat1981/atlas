# Arthur Schopenhauer

A German philosopher (1788–1860), author of "The World as Will and Representation," and father of modern philosophical pessimism, which paved the way for existentialism and analytical psychology. He made the "will" a hidden substance standing behind the phenomena of the world, and held that human life swings between pain and boredom, and that salvation lies in aesthetic contemplation, compassion, and asceticism. His philosophy was met with long silence in his lifetime and then with wide diffusion in the second half of the nineteenth century.

## His Life

He was born in Danzig on 22 February 1788 to a wealthy merchant, Heinrich Floris, who died in 1805, most likely by suicide, and to a literary mother, Johanna Schopenhauer, who kept a literary salon in Weimar and whose relationship with her son was strained. He studied at Göttingen and then Berlin, attended the lectures of Fichte and Schleiermacher and was disappointed in them, and received his doctorate from Jena in 1813 with the dissertation "On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason." He published the first volume of "The World as Will and Representation" at the end of 1818, at the age of thirty, and tried to lecture at the University of Berlin in 1820 at the same hour at which Hegel lectured, and only a few attended his class. He afterwards settled in Frankfurt and lived austerely with his dog, and in 1844 issued a second, expanded edition in two volumes, then "Parerga and Paralipomena" (1851), which brought him fame. He died in Frankfurt on 21 September 1860.

## What He Contributed

Schopenhauer begins from Kant: the world we know is "representation," that is, a phenomenon subject to the forms of time, space, and causality, and it does not give us the thing-in-itself. But he adds that we have a direct access to the thing-in-itself from within our body: we do not know our bodies from outside only, but we know their actions as will. From this he concluded that the thing-in-itself is the "will," a blind, irrational, perpetually hungry force, manifested in all of nature from gravity to instinct to human desire. In this he shattered the illusions of rationalism and of historical optimism, and held that reason is an instrument in the service of the will, not its master.

From this conception he drew his view of life: since the will cannot be satisfied, desire is pain, and when it is satisfied boredom follows, hence his saying that life swings between pain and boredom like a pendulum. He then charted the paths of liberation: first, aesthetic contemplation, which temporarily frees the self from the insistence of the will, the highest of which is music because it is a direct image of the will itself and not of the things of the world; second, compassion (Mitleid) as the basis of ethics, for he holds that the self and the other are at depth one, and that the other's pain is my pain; third, the denial of the will through asceticism and austerity. In this last he was influenced by the Upanishads, which he read in Anquetil-Duperron's Latin translation, and by Buddhism.

## His Influence in Arab and World Culture

Abdurrahman Badawi introduced the Arab reader to Schopenhauer in his book "Schopenhauer" (1942), one of the first things written in Arabic about the philosopher of will and pessimism. In the wider world he influenced Nietzsche, who wrote "Schopenhauer as Educator" and then turned to criticizing him; Wagner, who was influenced by him in his conception of music and redemption; Freud, who acknowledged in the will a forerunner of the concept of the unconscious and of drive (the blind, striving will, working beneath reason, anticipates the Freudian id and, more loosely, the existential theme of anxiety before a world without given meaning); and Wittgenstein, Tolstoy, Thomas Mann, Borges, and Beckett.

## His Place in the Tradition

He is regarded as the direct philosophical inspiration for Nietzsche and Freud, and as an interlocutor for later German philosophy, and as the deepest source of the themes of absurdity, suffering, and meaninglessness in literature and existential philosophy. Among the philosophers who followed Kant he is the exception who rejected the idealism of Fichte and Hegel, describing them as charlatans, offering instead a philosophy that starts from the body and pain.

## Limitation and Criticism

His ascetic tendency and his denial of the value of positive action in the world distinguish him from the existentialists committed to action and freedom (such as Sartre and Camus). Nietzsche criticized him because the denial of the will is a nihilism that rejects life instead of affirming it. Other critics hold that his move from "my will" to the "cosmic will" is an unjustified leap, and that his concept of the "thing-in-itself" contradicts Kant in making it known. Moreover, his saying that ethics is built on compassion does not explain commitment to duty, and his attitudes toward women and others reflect prejudices not supported by his philosophical work.

## His Most Important Works

- On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason (Über die vierfache Wurzel des Satzes vom zureichenden Grunde, 1813).
- The World as Will and Representation (Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung, 1818/1844).
- On the Freedom of the Human Will (Über die Freiheit des menschlichen Willens, 1839).
- Parerga and Paralipomena: Philosophical Fragments (Parerga und Paralipomena, 1851).
