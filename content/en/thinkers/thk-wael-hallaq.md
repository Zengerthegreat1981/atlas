# Wael B. Hallaq

A Palestinian-born historian of Islamic law (born 1955), formerly of McGill University and long associated with Columbia University, and a leading contemporary researcher on the history and theory of **Islamic legal theory (usul al-fiqh)**. He is the author of the widely cited reference *A History of Islamic Legal Theories* (1997).

## "Was the Gate of Ijtihad Closed?" (1984): Dismantling an Established Orientalist Thesis

In his pivotal article *Was the Gate of Ijtihad Closed?* (1984), Hallaq challenges a dominant orientalist thesis, advanced notably by Joseph Schacht and others in the twentieth century, that the **gate of ijtihad was officially closed** in Islamic jurisprudence around the beginning of the fourth century AH (tenth century CE), and that independent legal development then stopped in favor of pure imitation (taqlid). This thesis was sometimes used to explain the supposed "stagnation" of Islamic thought. Hallaq presents documented textual evidence showing the continuation of actual ijtihad practice among prominent jurists centuries after this supposed date. He concludes that the idea of "closing the gate" is a **historical myth rather than a documented fact**. See the **Gate of Ijtihad Debate**.

## Methodological Impact: A Model for Reviewing Orientalist Narratives

This work is an influential model of Hallaq's broader methodology: reviewing established orientalist assumptions about legal "decline" or "stagnation" by returning to the original texts themselves. He applied this approach more extensively in *A History of Islamic Legal Theories* (1997), which documented the continuous and complex development of usul al-fiqh over centuries. It traces the formation of schools of legal reasoning from al-Shafi'i (d. 204 AH) through al-Juwayni, al-Ghazali, and Ibn al-Hajib to later jurists. It shows that the later jurists' statements about the "closing of the gate of ijtihad" were often a rhetorical topos of humility rather than an accurate description of their actual practices.

## From the Critique of Orientalism to the Critique of Modernity: Hallaq's Broader Project

Hallaq's critique does not stop at refuting the closing-of-the-gate thesis. In later work, especially *Shari'a: Theory, Practice, Transformations* (2009), it extends to a more provocative argument: that the **modern nation-state**, with its centralized bureaucratic structure and single-source legislation, is **structurally incapable** of accommodating the Sharia in its original historical sense. The Sharia was a decentralized, multi-school legal system whose authority derived from the consensus of scholars independent of the state, rather than from governmental legislative delegation. He developed this thesis in *The Impossible State: Islam, Politics, and Modernity's Moral Predicament* (2013). It has prompted broad debate over whether "Islamizing" law within modern state frameworks, as in contemporary Arab codification experiments, is a feasible project at all or a structural contradiction in its essence.

## Standing Compared with Other Historians of Islamic Law

Hallaq is distinguished from earlier Western historians of Islamic law, such as Joseph Schacht, whose accounts stressed the late origin of usul theory and its gap with early judicial practice. He stresses the **continuity and internal logical development** of the discipline over its long history. He holds that usul theory is not a formal construction detached from actual judicial practice; rather, it was a living methodological tool that responded to the challenges of each era.

## Reception and Criticism

Hallaq's arguments have been widely welcomed in Western Islamic studies as a necessary corrective to entrenched orientalist biases. They have also drawn criticism from scholars who saw in the "impossible state" thesis a neglect of contemporary codification experiments. Examples include the Ottoman *Majallat al-Ahkam al-Adliyya* and the Arab personal status codes of the twentieth century, which, to varying degrees, succeeded in integrating the content of jurisprudence into modern legislative frameworks without the Sharia losing all of its historical juristic content.

## His Academic Career

Hallaq was born in Nazareth in 1955. He studied at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem and completed his doctorate at the University of Washington, then became a prominent critic of earlier Western scholarly approaches to Islamic law. He taught for many years at McGill University in Canada, where he was associated with the Institute of Islamic Studies, before moving to Columbia University, and he continues to write.
