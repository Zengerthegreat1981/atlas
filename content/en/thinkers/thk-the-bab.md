# The Báb (Siyyid ʿAlí-Muhammad Shírází)

A young Shirazi merchant (1819–1850) who declared in 1844 that he was "the Báb" (the Gate) to the Hidden Twelfth Imam of the Twelver Shi'a. He later escalated his claim to an independent prophetic station carrying a new law, thereby founding the Babi movement, which later became the historical nucleus from which the Baha'i Faith emerged. The Qajar authorities executed him in 1850 on charges of heresy, after a series of uprisings and bloody massacres that accompanied the suppression of his followers.

## Definition

The Báb is studied academically as an object of critical historical inquiry within the modern academic field of Baha'i and Babi studies. He is studied neither as an insider biography that believes his religious claims nor as a polemical refutation of them, but as a documented case of a messianic-mahdist movement arising from within an existing religious tradition.

## Historical Context

The Báb's claim arose from within the Shaykhi school, a Shi'i interpretive current that developed around Shaykh Ahmad al-Ahsa'i and his disciple Kazim Rashti, and that was marked by an intense expectation of the appearance of the Mahdi. Academic historians, among them Moojan Momen and Abbas Amanat, have documented how the Báb used this interpretive climate at the start of his mission before moving beyond it to an independent prophetic claim, announcing his book the *Bayan* as a new law that abrogates Islamic law for those who believe in him.

## Main Ideas and Method

Academic historians, especially Moojan Momen in his documentary survey of 1981, Abbas Amanat in *Resurrection and Renewal* (1989), and Denis MacEoin in his specialized studies, adopted a method that compares multiple accounts (internal Babi, contemporary Shi'i and Western diplomatic) to reconstruct a chronological sequence of events. The sequence runs from the first declaration in 1844, through the escalation of the claim and the widening of his followers, to the armed uprisings in Mazandaran, Nayriz and Zanjan, and then his public execution in Tabriz in 1850. These researchers distinguish between the successive layers of the claim: from the position of the intermediary "Báb" to the Hidden Imam; then to the claim of being the "Qa'im," the awaited Mahdi himself; and then to an independent prophetic claim carrying a new, abrogating book.

## His Execution and Immediate Impact

The Báb was publicly executed by firing squad in the square of Tabriz in 1850, by order of the Qajar authorities. The incident is documented in Babi narratives and in contemporary reports by European diplomats and travellers. This allowed later academic historians to compare these sources with one another in order to reconstruct the events, although the accounts differ on some details.

## Major Works and Figures

Among his most important writings are the Persian *Bayan* and the Arabic *Bayan*, which carry his new law and his symbolic organization of the calendar and rulings. The Bayan establishes a calendar of nineteen months of nineteen days and arranges its material in units of nineteen (each called a *vahid*), and it looks forward to the coming of "He whom God shall make manifest." Among his most prominent direct followers is Qurrat al-ʿAyn (Tahirih), a poet and preacher who became known for publicly removing her veil at the Badasht conference, announcing the separation of the Babi law from Islamic law.

## Impact and Critique

The Báb's biography constitutes a relatively well-documented case, with multilingual sources contemporary with the events, of a messianic-mahdist movement emerging from within an existing religious tradition and gradually separating from it. This makes it possible to test theories of religious sociology concerning new religious movements against a case that relies not on later legends but on documents close in time to the event. After the Báb's execution, his followers divided between Subh-i Azal, who claimed his direct succession, and Baha'u'llah, who later founded the Baha'i Faith and regarded himself as "He whom God shall make manifest," the figure the Báb had announced. The academic researchers documented this division in detail, which allows the transformation of a single religious movement into two distinct religions to be traced.

## The Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The Báb's claim arose entirely from within the Twelver Shi'i Iranian context and its theological heritage concerning occultation and waiting. In his writings the Báb used Qur'anic and hadith language and symbols intensively, even while he was formulating an alternative law. This makes his study a central case for understanding how new religious movements are born from within the Islamic Shi'i heritage itself, through reinterpretation of the concept of the Mahdi and of legal abrogation (naskh) in ways that went beyond both the traditional Sunni and Shi'i frameworks. His academic study thus makes a direct contribution to understanding the dynamics of religious schism within the modern Islamic world.

## The Badasht Conference and the Early Schism

The Badasht conference of 1848 was a turning point in the history of the movement. It brought together the leading followers of the Báb, including Qurrat al-ʿAyn and Baha'u'llah. At it, the separation of the Babi law from Islamic law was publicly declared, an irrevocable declaration that accelerated the confrontation between the movement and the Qajar state and the Shi'i religious establishment. It paved the way for the later armed uprisings that ended with the execution of the Báb himself.
