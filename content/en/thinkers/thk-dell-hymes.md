# Dell Hymes

American linguistic anthropologist, one of the founders of the **Ethnography of Communication**, and author of the concept of **communicative competence**, which transformed the course of sociolinguistics and educational linguistics. He received his PhD from Indiana University in 1955, taught at Harvard, the University of California at Berkeley, the University of Pennsylvania (where he was also dean of the Graduate School of Education) and the University of Virginia, and helped found the journal *Language in Society*.

## What He Contributed

### Ethnography of Speaking / Ethnography of Communication

In his foundational article "The Ethnography of Speaking" (1962, later developed intellectually together with John Gumperz), Hymes called for the study of speech as a **cultural event** with social structure and rules, rather than as a mere realization of an abstract linguistic system as conceived by Noam Chomsky's generative linguistics. He proposed an analytical framework summarized by the acronym **SPEAKING**: Setting and scene, Participants, Ends, Act sequence, Key, Instrumentalities, Norms, and Genres. The framework became a standard tool for studying speech events across cultures.

### Communicative Competence

In his best-known article "On Communicative Competence" (written 1966, published 1972), Hymes criticized Noam Chomsky's distinction between linguistic **competence** and **performance** as one that neglects the social dimension of language. In its place he proposed the concept of **communicative competence**: the speaker's knowledge of when, how and with whom it is permissible to say what, that is, the social and cultural rules that govern the **appropriateness** of speech rather than its grammatical correctness alone. This concept became a cornerstone of foreign language teaching (the communicative approach) and of sociolinguistics.

### Ethnopoetics

Drawing on his analysis of oral narrative texts among Native American peoples (especially the Chinookan), Hymes developed a method for re-transcribing oral literature as **poetry** organised in lines, verses, and stanzas whose patterning (often in sets of two, three, four, or five) he argued could be recovered from the text's grammatical and rhetorical features, rather than as prose as traditional ethnographers had recorded it, in his book *In Vain I Tried to Tell You* (1981).

## Impact

- **Sociolinguistics**: the concept of communicative competence is central to the work of John Gumperz, Deborah Tannen and others.
- **Language teaching**: it forms the basis of "Communicative Language Teaching," which has dominated worldwide since the 1970s.
- **Linguistic anthropology**: the SPEAKING framework is still taught as a standard for analyzing speech events.

## Limitations

- The SPEAKING framework has sometimes been criticized as a descriptive list rather than an explanatory theory capable of prediction.
- His method for reconstructing the "poetic lines" in ethnopoetics depended on the analyst's judgments about where lines and verses fall, which prompted debate about how objectively the patterning can be demonstrated.

## Context: American Linguistic Anthropology and the Chomskyan Challenge

Hymes was trained in the tradition of Boas, Sapir and Whorf, in which American Indigenous languages are studied as systems linked to culture. In the 1950s and 1960s, however, he faced the rise of generative linguistics, which shifted the center of attention to an ideal speaker in a homogeneous speech community. His objection was not to the formal description of rules, but to the exclusion of what makes speech socially possible: who speaks, to whom, on what occasion, and in what form. In his article "Toward Ethnographies of Communication" (*American Anthropologist*, 1964), he formulated a programme for the field that treats the speech community, rather than abstract language, as the unit of analysis. In the volume he edited with John Gumperz, *Directions in Sociolinguistics* (Holt, Rinehart and Winston, 1972), this programme was brought together with other research in the new field.

## Analytical Units: Situation, Event and Speech Act

Hymes distinguished between three overlapping levels: the speech situation (such as a celebration, a hunt or a trial), in which patterns of speaking and silence may occur; the speech event (such as a conversation, a sermon or a plea), whose use of speech is governed by rules; and the speech act (such as a greeting, a command or a promise). Through this gradation he linked what Austin and Searle called speech acts to the social organization of occasions, and showed that the functions of speech are not only referential but also poetic, emotive and phatic. He also stressed that communities differ in the value they place on speech itself: some honor silence in a setting where others require speech, which made the comparison of speech norms a central part of anthropology.

## Language, Education and Equity

In the 1970s and 1980s Hymes turned to the analysis of schooling. He argued that the mismatch between the norms of speech in a child's home and their norms in the classroom is a major cause of the failure of minority children, rather than their language being deficient. His book *Reinventing Anthropology* (Pantheon, 1972) gathered critical essays arguing that anthropology owes a debt to the people it studies, and calling for it to become a discipline more committed to justice. From this line of work branched ethnographic educational studies, inspired by Shirley Brice Heath and others. Hymes also served as president of several learned societies, including the American Folklore Society, the Linguistic Society of America and the American Anthropological Association.

## Continuing Debate

Debate continues over the suitability of the concept of the speech community for today's mobile and multilingual societies, where it is no longer clear where one community ends and another begins. Later performance-oriented approaches have emphasised that speakers help create the situation itself rather than simply applying prior rules to it. His lasting contribution, however, is that he made the question of the social appropriateness of speech a scientific question.
