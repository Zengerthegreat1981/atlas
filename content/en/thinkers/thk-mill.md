# John Stuart Mill

An English philosopher and economist, a leading theorist of classical **utilitarianism** in the 19th century. He was raised by his father **James Mill** and by **Bentham**, and began Greek at the age of three and Latin at eight. He fell into a **spiritual crisis** (1826) that reshaped his later philosophy.

## His Life and Formation

Mill was born in London on 20 May 1806 to a Scottish father, the historian and economist James Mill, who subjected him from childhood to a strict education: Greek at three, and logic and political economy in his boyhood years. At seventeen he joined the East India Company, in the office for the management of correspondence, and remained an employee there until the Company was dissolved in 1858. In 1820 he lived in France as the guest of Sir Samuel Bentham, brother of Jeremy Bentham, and became acquainted with French culture. After his psychological breakdown in 1826 he read the poetry of Wordsworth and encountered Coleridge, Carlyle and Saint-Simon, which softened the rigor of his original utilitarianism. In 1830 he met Harriet Taylor, who became his wife in 1851 after the death of her first husband, and who is regarded as an intellectual partner in *On Liberty*. He was elected a Member of the British Parliament between 1865 and 1868 and called for granting women the vote. He died in Avignon on 8 May 1873.

## What He Contributed

### *On Liberty* (1859)

An influential defense of individual liberty in modern political philosophy. Mill formulates the famous **harm principle**: "The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others." Mill articulates three broad domains of liberty:
- **Liberty of thought and discussion**: freedom of opinion, conscience, and public debate.
- **Liberty of tastes and pursuits**: freedom to plan our own lives and pursue our own inclinations without societal interference, so long as our actions do not harm others.
- **Liberty of combination (association)**: freedom to unite with other individuals for any purpose not involving harm to others.

This text remains a fundamental reference for liberal political philosophy, constitutional protections of free speech, and theories of personal autonomy.

### The 1826 Crisis and Individuality in *On Liberty*

In his *Autobiography*, Mill recounts the severe mental and emotional crisis he suffered in the autumn of 1826. Having been conditioned by Benthamite associationist psychology and strict utilitarian analysis, he realized that rigorous analytic dissection had trained his intellect while leaving his emotional capacities and desires uncultivated. Reading William Wordsworth's poetry offered him relief and, as he saw it, showed that aesthetic sensibility and feeling are essential to well-being. This realization transformed his philosophical vision, directly shaping Chapter 3 of *On Liberty* ("Of Individuality, as One of the Elements of Well-Being"). Drawing on Wilhelm von Humboldt's ideal of self-cultivation (*Bildung*), Mill argued that liberty is valuable not only as negative non-interference, but as the essential prerequisite for individual development, diversity of character, and "experiments of living" against the stultifying tyranny of social conformity.

### *Utilitarianism* (1863)

A widely read defense of utilitarian ethics. Mill introduces crucial modifications to Bentham's quantitative hedonism:
- **Qualitative distinctions between pleasures**: Mill famously declares, "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." Pleasures of the intellect, feelings, imagination, and moral sentiments are qualitatively superior to mere bodily sensations.
- **The criterion of competent judges**: When deciding which of two pleasures is superior in quality, Mill appeals to the verdict of those who have experienced both and are competent to appreciate both.

### *The Subjection of Women* (1869)

A key text of liberal feminism, written with significant input from his wife Harriet Taylor Mill and stepdaughter Helen Taylor. Mill argued that the legal subordination of women was not only inherently unjust, but a relic of the past that impeded the moral and intellectual progress of humanity. He advocated full legal equality, equal access to education, suffrage, and unrestricted entry into professions.

Mill also revised editions of his *Principles of Political Economy* (1848) in light of the European revolutions of 1848, incorporating sympathetic appraisals of workers' cooperatives and profit-sharing schemes, prefiguring a form of liberal or market socialism.

### *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861)

A theory of democratic governance balancing popular participation with expert competence. Mill argued that while democracy fosters active citizenship, safeguards against majority tyranny and demagoguery are necessary. He endorsed proportional representation (supporting Thomas Hare's system) and controversially advocated plural voting, giving additional votes to citizens with higher educational or intellectual attainments to counterbalance unreflective majoritarianism.

## Reception in Arab Thought

Mill entered Arab thought in the age of the Nahda and after. Albert Hourani, in *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age*, notes that Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid, one of the most prominent liberal intellectuals of Egypt in the early 20th century, adopted the principle of utility and individual liberty under the influence of Mill and Spencer, and carried concepts of liberty and the state into the Egyptian debate. Arab writers also circulated the discussion of *On Liberty* when speaking of freedom of opinion, the press and women. Conversely, scholars point out that the Arab adoption of Mill's ideas was not a pure transfer, but was intertwined with the debate over despotism and the British occupation, a British imperial order in which Mill himself served institutionally, as an employee of the East India Company.

## Influence

- **R. M. Hare**, **J. J. C. Smart** and **Peter Singer** developed utilitarianism in the 20th century, while **Bernard Williams** criticized it and **Derek Parfit** amended its premises in the theory of persons.
- **Contemporary liberalism**: Mill's arguments remain a **reference point** for later liberal theorists (including Rawls and Dworkin).
- **Feminism**: *The Subjection of Women* is a basic **text**.
- **Economics**: *Principles of Political Economy* (1848) remained a standard textbook of classical economics until the late 19th century.

## Limits of His Thought and Its Criticism

Critics have charged that Mill's "harm principle" is not decisive, because the limits of "harm" are themselves disputed: what counts as harm to others, and does it include moral or ethical offense? Others objected that his distinction between "higher" and "lower" pleasures in fact departs from pure utilitarianism, which F. H. Bradley and G. E. Moore held against him. J. F. Stephen, in *Liberty, Equality, Fraternity* (1873), held that Mill overestimates the individual at the expense of social morality.

More serious in the balance of postcolonial studies is that *On Liberty* exempts from its principle societies he regarded as backward and still in their "nonage", holding that despotism is a legitimate mode of governing them provided its end is their improvement; this exemption was relied on by the colonial administration, and Uday Mehta examines in his book *Liberalism and Empire* how classical liberalism is tied to the justification of empire. This is what makes reading him in Arab contexts combine benefiting from the principle of liberty with reservations about its imperial side.

## His Major Works

- **A System of Logic** (1843).
- **Principles of Political Economy** (1848).
- **On Liberty** (1859).
- **Considerations on Representative Government** (1861).
- **Utilitarianism** (1863).
- **The Subjection of Women** (1869).
- **Three Essays on Religion**: published after his death (1874), edited by his stepdaughter Helen Taylor from manuscripts he wrote late in life.
- **An Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy** (1865).
