# Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi

An Arab philosopher of pure Arab Qahtani descent (from the Banu Kinda), traditionally known by the title "the Philosopher of the Arabs", because he is regarded as the first major philosopher writing in Arabic and one of the first to try to root Greek philosophy in the Arabic language and in a coherent Islamic theological framework. He lived in Basra and Kufa and then settled in Baghdad, where he was in contact with the Abbasid caliphs al-Ma'mun, al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq, and he supervised a circle of translators who rendered for him, and for others, a portion of the works of Aristotle, Plotinus, Euclid and Ptolemy.

## Biography

Al-Kindi was born in Kufa or Basra around 185 AH/801 CE, into a family of the nobles of Kinda; his father Ishaq ibn al-Sabbah was governor of Kufa in the time of al-Mahdi and al-Rashid. He moved to Baghdad in his youth to seek knowledge and was in contact with the Abbasid court under al-Ma'mun (who founded the House of Wisdom) and then al-Mu'tasim, whose son Ahmad he tutored, and so al-Kindi attributed some of his treatises to him. Al-Kindi reached the peak of his standing under al-Mu'tasim and al-Wathiq, but under al-Mutawakkil — after the caliph inclined toward Hanbali rigor and retreated from Mu'tazilism — he suffered a trial said to have included being beaten and having his library (the "Kindiyya") confiscated for a period, on the denunciation of his opponents among the Banu Musa ibn Shakir. He died in Baghdad; the date of his death is uncertain, with 252 AH/866 CE the most widely adopted and other sources giving a later date, up to about 873 CE.

## What He Contributed

### Rooting Philosophy in the Islamic Framework

In his best-known treatise, "On First Philosophy", al-Kindi begins with a methodical defense of the legitimacy of engaging in philosophy, and defines it as "the knowledge of things in their realities to the extent of human capacity", the noblest part of which is first philosophy, that is, knowledge of the First Truth, which is the cause of every truth. He tried to reconcile this conception with the Qur'anic doctrine of the world's temporal origination, and in doing so he departed from Aristotle on the eternity of the world and championed the idea of creation from nothing (ibda'), relying on rational proofs of the finitude of time and motion similar to those later used by the theologians.

### Supervising the Translation Movement

Al-Kindi was for the most part not a translator himself, but directed a team of translators (among them Abd al-Masih ibn Na'ima al-Himsi and Yahya ibn al-Bitriq), revised their translations and added commentaries and summaries to them. Among the most important texts associated with his circle is the text known as the "Theology of Aristotle" (which is in reality a summary of parts of Plotinus's *Enneads*), and the book "On the Pure Good" (the *Liber de causis* in Latin, an adaptation of Proclus's *Elements of Theology*), whose connection with his circle is uncertain, and which led early Arabic philosophy to read Aristotle through a Neoplatonic lens.

### Philosophical and Scientific Treatises

According to Ibn al-Nadim's *Fihrist* and Ibn Abi Usaybi'a's *Uyun al-Anba'*, al-Kindi left several hundred short treatises (the *Fihrist* lists a very large number of titles) on varied subjects: logic, the soul, the intellect, alchemy, medicine, optics, music and astronomy. Among the most prominent is the treatise "On the Intellect", in which he distinguished kinds of intellect (the intellect that is always in act, the potential intellect, the acquired intellect and the manifest intellect) in an early attempt to read the Aristotelian theory of intellect; the treatise "On the Quantity of Aristotle's Books"; and the treatise "On the Definitions of Things and Their Descriptions", in which he compiled a philosophical lexicon in Arabic of the translated Greek terms, a fundamental contribution to the formation of Arabic philosophical vocabulary.

### Optics, Number Theory and Astrology

In "On the Causes of Vision" al-Kindi discussed the question of how things are seen, influenced by the Euclidean theory of the visual ray, and this work later influenced Roger Bacon in Latin Europe. He also wrote on cryptography the treatise "On Deciphering Cryptographic Messages", which is counted among the earliest known texts on code-breaking by the statistical analysis of letter frequency, a contribution that preceded by centuries what became known later in the history of Western cryptography.

## Influence

Al-Kindi's treatises were transmitted into Latin at the Toledo school of translation in the twelfth century, and he was known in Latin Europe by the name Alkindus; thinkers such as Roger Bacon were influenced by his works in optics and psychology. In Islamic philosophy, al-Kindi paved the way for the Peripatetic philosophers who came after him, such as al-Farabi and Avicenna, although they went far beyond many of his positions (especially on the question of the eternity of the world in Avicenna). He is also credited with an influence on the development of Mu'tazili theology through his use of dialectical tools shared with it, although he was not a Mu'tazili in the sectarian sense.

### Music and Psychology

Al-Kindi also left treatises on music theory ("On the Composition of Melodies", "On String Sounds") in which he linked the science of melody to mathematics, preceding al-Farabi into a field not yet approached with a rigorous theoretical method in Arabic. In his treatise "On the Art of Averting Sorrows" he offers an early philosophical-psychological treatment, influenced by Greek Stoicism, of how a person may free himself from grief by returning perishable things to their transient nature and attaching true happiness to what does not perish, one of the earliest Arabic texts in this field.

## Limits

It is held against al-Kindi that his philosophy is closer to an encyclopedia of scattered treatises than to a philosophical system complete in its pillars, as in al-Farabi or later Avicenna; he left no comprehensive work comparable to Avicenna's *al-Shifa'*. Moreover, his attempt to reconcile the world's origination with the principles of Greek philosophy was regarded by some scholars as a conciliatory solution of incomplete logical consistency, since he did not deal in sufficient depth with the problem of the relation between an eternal first cause and an act of creation that is originated in time. Most of his direct philosophical legacy has been lost (unlike his scientific treatises) and only a limited part of it has reached us, which makes judgment on his system as a whole depend in part on later testimonies (such as Ibn al-Nadim and Ibn Abi Usaybi'a) rather than on the complete texts.

## His Most Important Terms

In his treatise "On the Definitions of Things and Their Descriptions" al-Kindi contributed precise Arabic definitions of dozens of Greek philosophical terms (such as substance, accident, form, matter, cause and effect), a foundational attempt that formed part of the Arabic philosophical lexicon used by those who came after him from al-Farabi to Avicenna, even when they differed with him on the details of doctrine.

## His Most Important Works

- "On First Philosophy" (Fi al-Falsafa al-Ula)
- "On the Intellect" (Risala fi al-'Aql)
- "On the Definitions of Things and Their Descriptions"
- "On the Quantity of Aristotle's Books"
- "On the Causes of Vision"
- "On Deciphering Cryptographic Messages"
