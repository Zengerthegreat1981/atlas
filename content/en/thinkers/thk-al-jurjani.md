# Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani

A grammarian and rhetorician of Persian origin who wrote in Arabic, from the city of Jurjan (Gurgan), south of the Caspian Sea. He is regarded as the author of the most influential systematic Arabic theory of the rhetorical inimitability (*i'jaz*) of the Qur'an and of the mechanisms of metaphor and simile, through two pivotal books: *Dala'il al-I'jaz* and *Asrar al-Balagha*. Little is known of his practical life, but his theoretical legacy transformed the entire course of the Arabic rhetorical tradition after him.

## Biography

Few details of al-Jurjani's life are available relative to his intellectual stature. Biographical sources (such as al-Sam'ani and Yaqut al-Hamawi) summarize his life by noting that he lived in Jurjan as a teacher of grammar, influenced by the Basran grammatical school as it was transmitted through Abu Ali al-Farisi and his pupil Ibn Jinni, although no direct chain of discipleship with either of them is documented in the sources. He became known by the nisba "al-Jurjani" after his birthplace, and died there. His exact birth date is not known (c. 400 AH is an estimate), and his death is generally given as 471 AH (some sources say 474 AH). Sources name his teacher in grammar as Abu al-Husayn Muhammad ibn al-Husayn al-Farisi, a nephew of Abu Ali al-Farisi. He lived during the flourishing of linguistic scholarship in the eastern Islamic world in the fifth century AH. Alongside his two rhetorical books, he wrote grammatical works such as *al-'Awamil al-Mi'a* (The Hundred Governors) and *al-Jumal* (The Sentences), which were traditionally used in teaching elementary grammar.

## What he contributed

### The theory of nazm (composition)

In *Dala'il al-I'jaz*, al-Jurjani put forward his central thesis: the eloquence of speech does not lie in individual words considered in themselves (as earlier scholars of embellishment believed, who restricted rhetoric to verbal ornaments), but in **nazm**, that is, in the way words are arranged according to the meanings of grammar (fronting and postponement, definiteness and indefiniteness, omission and mention, separation and connection) so as to match the intended meaning. A single word has no "eloquence" outside its syntactic context; eloquence is a property of the composition, not of the isolated word. From this, al-Jurjani argued for the aspect of the Qur'an's inimitability: not in the strangeness of its words, but in the perfection of its composition, to such a degree that it cannot be imitated.

### The theory of metaphor and imagination

In *Asrar al-Balagha*, al-Jurjani analyzed the mechanisms of **simile** (*tashbih*), **metaphor** (*isti'ara*), **metonymy** (*kinaya*) and figurative attribution (*majaz 'aqli*), distinguishing between figurative usage that "increases the meaning in claim and imagination" (such as metaphor, which makes the likened thing appear to be the very thing it is likened to), and figurative usage that is merely a transfer of designation. In this way he offered a functional explanation of why some poetic images are more eloquent than a direct formulation of the same meaning: the eloquence lies in **stimulating the mind** to perceive the point of resemblance or the relation through an implicit process of inference, not in merely decorating the wording.

### Context: the response to the school of verbal embellishment

Al-Jurjani wrote in opposition to critical tendencies, associated with writers on poetics and style such as Abu Hilal al-Askari, that gave priority to the wording (*lafz*) and confined much rhetorical inquiry to cataloguing the types of verbal "embellishments" (paronomasia, antithesis, rhymed prose), regarded as ornaments added to speech from outside its meaning. Al-Jurjani saw this method as a fundamental error: it separates form from meaning, as if the wording were a neutral vessel to be decorated from outside. His counter-thesis was that form (composition) is itself what carries and shapes the meaning, so that "what was said" cannot be separated from "how it was said" except through a temporary analytical separation for teaching purposes.

### The concept of "the meanings of grammar"

The foundation of al-Jurjani's theory of composition is what he calls "the meanings of grammar": each grammatical construction (fronting, indefiniteness, omission, the conditional particle, the form of emphasis) has a functional meaning that goes beyond its direct lexical sense. Thus, placing the object before the subject, or using an indefinite predicate instead of a definite one, changes the intended meaning precisely, even though the "words" remain the same. Correct composition is therefore the matching of these precise grammatical choices to the meaning the speaker wishes to convey, not merely the soundness of the rules of inflection.

## Legacy

The theory of composition and imagination in al-Jurjani is the foundation on which **al-Sakkaki** (d. 626 AH) later built his systematic division of the three sciences of rhetoric (meanings, figurative expression and embellishment) in *Miftah al-Ulum*. This division was abridged by **al-Khatib al-Qazwini** in *Talkhis al-Miftah*, and became the standard curriculum for teaching rhetoric in Islamic schools until today. Thus al-Jurjani's open analytical ideas were transformed into a relatively closed scholastic classification. This is a point of debate among modern researchers as to whether this codification impoverished his original thesis or organized and disseminated it. In recent studies, researchers such as Margaret Larkin and Kamal Abu Deeb have approached al-Jurjani's theories of composition and imagery in light of modern linguistic and literary theory.

The influence of al-Jurjani also continued through successive commentaries and summaries. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi abridged *Dala'il al-I'jaz* and *Asrar al-Balagha* together in a single book entitled *Nihayat al-Ijaz fi Dirayat al-I'jaz*, which helped spread al-Jurjani's ideas in a concentrated form before al-Sakkaki and al-Qazwini reformulated them within the well-known tripartite division. In both Western and modern Arab scholarship, interest in al-Jurjani returned strongly in the twentieth century with Arab critics and linguists (such as Amin al-Khuli, Shukri Ayyad and Muhammad Mandur), who read the theory of composition as an early Arab precedent for modern critical approaches concerned with the relationship between form and meaning, and sought to "rediscover" it outside the traditional scholastic reading established by later rhetorical manuals.

## Limitations

Al-Jurjani's exposition drew mainly on examples from classical Arabic poetry and the Qur'an, and he did not develop a general theory of style that goes beyond the bounds of classical standard Arabic. His analysis also remained intuitive and descriptive rather than formal; he did not provide a rigorous systematic formulation of the "meanings of grammar" on which composition rests. This left the door open to multiple and contested interpretations of his precise intent, even among his early Muslim commentators, before his ideas were condensed into the school of al-Sakkaki and al-Qazwini.

In addition, al-Jurjani wrote primarily as a defender of the inimitability of the Qur'an in a theological-polemical context (responding to those who claimed that it was possible to produce a stylistic counterpart to the Qur'an). This theological purpose runs through his text and is difficult to separate precisely from the purely linguistic thesis that modern readers extract from it. When he states that the composition of the Qur'an is inimitable, it remains a point of debate among researchers, to this day, whether this judgment is the result of a neutral rhetorical analysis or a theological premise assumed in advance.

### Comparison with modern Western rhetoric

Several comparative scholars, Arab and Western, have pointed out the closeness of some of al-Jurjani's statements to modern linguistic concepts that arose independently of him centuries later. His distinction between the single "word" (*lafz*) and the "composition" (*nazm*), which acquires its meaning from its position within the structure, is sometimes compared to Ferdinand de Saussure's distinction between the value (*valeur*) of an individual linguistic sign and the system that determines that value. Although the two intellectual frameworks differ fundamentally in their starting points and aims (al-Jurjani starts from explaining the inimitability of a particular text, the Qur'an, whereas Saussure builds a general theory of the structure of language as a system), this comparison, while useful pedagogically for highlighting the depth of al-Jurjani's theorizing, carries the risk of projecting modern concepts onto a medieval text apart from its original context, a reservation raised by many historians of Arabic linguistic thought.

## Major works

- **Dala'il al-I'jaz**: the theory of composition and the rhetorical inimitability of the Qur'an.
- **Asrar al-Balagha**: the theory of simile, metaphor, figurative language and imagination.
- **al-'Awamil al-Mi'a**: a concise treatise on grammatical governors (*'awamil*), traditionally used in elementary grammar instruction.
- **al-Jumal**: a short treatise on grammar.
- Shorter grammatical epistles and articles addressing specific questions of inflection and morphology, some of which are preserved within later collections.
