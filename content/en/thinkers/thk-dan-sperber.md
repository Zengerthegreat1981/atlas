# Dan Sperber

A French scholar in cognitive anthropology, philosophy, and pragmatic linguistics, born in 1942, and a researcher at the Institut Jean Nicod in Paris (CNRS) and at Central European University. He is best known for co-founding, with the British linguist Deirdre Wilson, "Relevance Theory," one of the most influential pragmatic frameworks after Gricean pragmatics itself.

## From Anthropology to Pragmatics

Sperber began his career as an anthropologist, with fieldwork among the Dorze of southern Ethiopia, and in *Rethinking Symbolism* (1975) he argued against treating symbolism as a code with fixed meanings, proposing instead that symbolic thought works by cognitive evocation. His anthropological work is also associated with a rejection of the idea of a distinct "primitive mentality" of the kind associated with Lucien Lévy-Bruhl: apparent differences between cultural belief systems do not reflect differences in basic human cognitive mechanisms. From this interest in how beliefs and representations are transmitted across societies, he gradually moved toward studying linguistic communication itself as a process of cognitive inference.

## Relevance Theory

In their landmark book *Relevance: Communication and Cognition* (1986, revised 1995), Sperber and Wilson developed a cognitive alternative to Grice's four conversational maxims. Instead of positing multiple cooperative principles that the speaker must obey, they proposed two principles of relevance: a cognitive principle (human cognition tends to maximize relevance) and a communicative principle (every act of ostensive communication carries a presumption of its own optimal relevance), i.e. that what is said is worth the processing effort it requires, balancing the new cognitive effect against the cost of the inferential effort needed to understand it. In this way, relevance theory replaced Grice's maxims with a general cognitive account of inferential comprehension.

## Epidemiology of Representations

Sperber later developed, in his book *Explaining Culture* (1996), the theory of the "epidemiology of representations," also known as "cultural attraction theory": an approach to explaining the spread of cultural beliefs and practices across societies by analogy with the spread of epidemics. It focuses on how every mental representation is partially reformulated each time it is transmitted between individuals (rather than copied literally), which explains both the stability of some cultural patterns and the transformation of others.

## Impact

Relevance theory has had a wide influence on contemporary pragmatics, and research has branched from it into metaphor, irony, and non-literal inference. Sperber's influence has also extended into cognitive psychology through the "argumentative theory of reasoning" that he later developed with Hugo Mercier, which holds that human reasoning evolved chiefly for producing and evaluating arguments in social exchange (Mercier and Sperber, 2011; *The Enigma of Reason*, 2017), rather than as a faculty for solitary truth-seeking.
