# Zellig Harris

American linguist, the most prominent representative of American distributional structuralism after Bloomfield, and developer of a rigorous method for analyzing grammar through "transformations." It is a framework on which Noam Chomsky was directly trained before the latter developed it into transformational generative grammar.

## Operator Grammar

In his later work, Harris developed an ambitious theory of "Operator Grammar." In it he attempted to derive the grammatical structure of an entire language from a small set of primitive "operators" and their dependency relations on their "arguments." This went beyond even the concept of transformation he had developed earlier. His aim was a highly economical theory in which sentence structure is built from a few primitive dependency relations, with minimal reliance on traditional grammatical categories. It is set out in *Mathematical Structures of Language* (1968) and *A Theory of Language and Information* (1991).

## Strict Distributional Analysis

Harris carried the methodology of American structuralism, established by Bloomfield, to the highest degree of procedural rigor. Every grammatical unit must be derived from its actually observed distribution in the corpus, without appeal to intuition about meaning. This is achieved through strictly mechanical substitution and segmentation procedures applied independently of semantics.

## Grammatical Transformations

In his influential article "Co-occurrence and Transformation in Linguistic Structure" (1957), Harris developed the concept of "transformation": a systematic relation between two sentences that share the same lexical components but differ in order or form, such as an active declarative sentence and its passive counterpart. Chomsky, Harris's direct student at the University of Pennsylvania, borrowed this concept and developed it radically in *Syntactic Structures* (1957). He turned it into a generative theory in which transformational rules operate on underlying structures (the term "deep structure" became prominent in his *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax*, 1965), thereby going beyond his mentor's strict distributional method.

## Academic Biography

Zellig Sabbatai Harris was born in 1909 in the Ukrainian city of Balta, to a Jewish family that emigrated to the United States in 1913, when he was a child. He grew up in Philadelphia and obtained his doctorate in Semitic languages from the University of Pennsylvania in 1934. He remained associated with the university throughout his academic career and founded its linguistics department in 1946, one of the first independent linguistics departments in the United States. Harris combined his linguistic work with left-wing political commitments and was active in socialist and Zionist circles; Chomsky later recalled his political as well as his intellectual influence.

## Computational Linguistics and Discourse Analysis

Alongside his grammatical work, Harris founded the field of "discourse analysis" with his pioneering article "Discourse Analysis" (1952). That article extended the tools of distributional analysis from the level of the single sentence to continuous text. He later developed, in a long-term project, an account of the "sublanguages" of science (see *The Form of Information in Science*, 1989). He showed that scientific and technical texts are subject to distributional constraints more regular than those of general language. This made his work an early reference for computational linguistics, notably for information extraction from medical and scientific text, developed by associates such as Naomi Sager.

## Harris's Relation to Chomsky and the Methodological Break

Harris supervised Chomsky's training at the University of Pennsylvania and transmitted to him the method of strict distributional analysis and the concept of transformation. Chomsky later broke radically from his mentor's commitment to pure empirical descriptivism, which refused any reliance on intuition about meaning or deep structure. He adopted instead a rationalist, innatist conception of language that posits a deep grammatical structure residing in the mind. Many historians of linguistics regard this shift as central to the "generative revolution" and as a break with the distributional structuralism that Harris embodied.

## Impact

Harris is a pivotal historical link between American structuralism and the generative revolution, although his distributional method remained closer to strict descriptivism than to the later formal generativism developed by his student. He is also regarded as an insufficiently recognized pioneer in the history of computational linguistics, since his concepts of sublanguage and discourse analysis paved the way for later applications in automatically extracting information from scientific and technical texts. His method established a standard of procedural precision that remained influential even after the decline of distributional structuralism, and his work on discourse analysis and sublanguage anticipated later concerns of corpus-based and computational approaches to language.
