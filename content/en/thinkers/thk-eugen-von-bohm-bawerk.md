# Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk

**Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk** was an Austrian economist, the direct pupil of Carl Menger and one of the leading founders of the mature Austrian tradition within the Marginal Revolution. He is known for his theory of capital and interest, grounded in individuals' time preference, and for his detailed critique of the Marxian theory of value.

## Academic and political career

Böhm-Bawerk combined an academic career, as professor at the University of Innsbruck (from 1881) and later at the University of Vienna (from 1904), with a senior career in the Austrian civil service. He served three times as Austrian Minister of Finance (1895, 1896–1897 and 1900–1904), applying conservative fiscal policies reflecting his commitment to balanced budgets and the gold standard. He resigned from his last term in 1904 amid disputes over rising military spending, which he regarded as a threat to the state's financial stability.

## The theory of capital and interest

In his extensive work *Capital and Interest* (published in parts between 1884 and 1889), Böhm-Bawerk presented an original theory of interest: interest is not an exploitation of workers, as Marx claimed, but reflects **time preference**, namely the general tendency of individuals to prefer present consumption over deferred consumption of an equal quantity of goods. Whoever lends capital to another temporarily forgoes present consumption and therefore deserves compensation (interest) for this deferral, entirely irrespective of any exploitation of the labour of others.

## The roundabout production process and the period of capital

Böhm-Bawerk developed another central idea: "roundabout" or indirect production processes (which use machinery and complex capital instead of simple direct labour) are more productive than short, direct processes, but they take longer to yield their fruits. This explains why capital deserves a return: it enables longer and more productive production processes, but it requires patience and the deferral of present consumption in exchange for a larger output in the future. In his positive theory, interest has three grounds: differences between present and future needs, a systematic tendency to underestimate future wants, and the technical superiority of present goods used in roundabout production.

## His detailed critique of the Marxian theory of value

Böhm-Bawerk wrote a detailed and influential critique of Karl Marx's theory of value in *Karl Marx and the Close of His System* (1896), focusing on what became known as the **transformation problem**: the apparent contradiction between the first volume of *Capital*, in which prices are determined by the quantity of labour embodied in commodities, and the third volume, in which actual prices are determined by a uniform rate of profit across sectors, which requires deviation from the original labour values. Böhm-Bawerk regarded this contradiction as evidence of the logical failure of the Marxian labour theory of value from within. The transformation problem was later given a mathematical treatment by Ladislaus von Bortkiewicz (1907), and the controversy continued throughout the twentieth century.

## Exceptionally clear prose

Unlike many of his fellow Austrian economists, who tended toward abstract philosophical formulations, Böhm-Bawerk was known for a clear and orderly prose style in presenting his logical arguments step by step. This made his writings relatively easy to follow, even in English translation, and contributed to the spread of his ideas beyond German-speaking circles.

## Pupils who carried the Austrian name into the twentieth century

Böhm-Bawerk taught a later generation of Austrian economists, most notably Joseph Schumpeter and Ludwig von Mises, who attended his Vienna seminar. They carried the Austrian tradition into the twentieth century, although in divergent directions later on, establishing the continuity of a distinct line of thought within the wider marginal economics. It remained relatively non-mathematical in character, in contrast to the rigorous mathematical formulations that dominated the Lausanne school and its followers.

## The exploitation theory: a direct response

In *Capital and Interest*, Böhm-Bawerk devoted whole chapters to a direct response to the "exploitation theory," which holds that the capitalist appropriates value produced by the worker without compensation. He argued that the wage the capitalist pays the worker is in fact an immediate advance on the value of the future product that the labour will produce after the long roundabout production process is completed. The capitalist bears the risk of waiting and uncertainty, and the interest he earns is a fair reward for this temporal advance, not an appropriation of unpaid labour as Marx portrayed it.

## Direct debate with contemporary Marxists

Böhm-Bawerk's critical book provoked immediate and sharp debate with contemporary Marxist economists, most notably Rudolf Hilferding, who wrote a detailed reply (1904) defending the systematic consistency of Marx's theory. The discussion of the transformation problem continued to feature in the literature of both Marxian and Austrian economics throughout the twentieth century, without reaching a complete consensus between the two sides to this day. Böhm-Bawerk's notion of an average period of production was later also a target of criticism in the Cambridge capital controversy, which followed Piero Sraffa's 1960 critique of marginalist capital theory.
