# Scott Atran

A French-American cognitive anthropologist, born in 1952, and an early contributor to the cognitive science of religion, alongside Pascal Boyer and Justin Barrett. His work combines field anthropology with evolutionary cognitive psychology. His most prominent contribution is his book *In Gods We Trust: The Evolutionary Landscape of Religion* (2002). He worked as a researcher at the French National Centre for Scientific Research (CNRS) and at the University of Michigan.

## Definition

Atran belongs to the field of the cognitive science of religion, which explains the spread and persistence of religious beliefs throughout human history by reference to general cognitive structures that evolved in the human brain, not by reference to the content or truth of the belief itself.

## Historical Context

The field emerged in the 1990s in response to earlier social-functional explanations of religion (such as Durkheim's explanation of religion as social cohesion), drawing on the development of evolutionary cognitive psychology and neuroscience. Atran's work continued his early research on folk classification (folk biology), that is, the way humans across different cultures classify living things, before he carried his methodological tools over into the study of religious belief.

## Main Ideas and Method

Atran starts from an observation he regards as an evolutionary paradox: religious beliefs impose heavy costs on their holders, in time, rituals, sacrifices and food taboos, and they posit supernatural beings that contradict everyday empirical intuition. So how did they spread and persist through human evolution despite this apparent cost? His answer, like that of his colleagues Boyer and Barrett, is that they are a by-product of general cognitive mechanisms, such as the agency detection system and the selective memory for minimally counterintuitive concepts, rather than a direct adaptation existing for religion itself. Alongside the individual cognitive explanation, Atran highlights the social dimension, drawing on work on costly signaling by evolutionary anthropologists such as William Irons and Richard Sosis. Costly religious commitment, such as fasting, demanding rituals and economic sacrifices, works as an honest signal that is difficult to counterfeit of an individual's commitment to the group. It thereby raises the cooperative trust among members and solves the "free-rider" problem in large groups.

## Career Path

Atran worked as a researcher at the French National Centre for Scientific Research in Paris, and as a visiting professor at several American universities, including Michigan and John Jay. He combined a French academic position with an American research trajectory, which allowed him to move between the French tradition of theoretical anthropology and the American tradition of experimental cognitive psychology.

## Main Works and Figures

His central work is *In Gods We Trust: The Evolutionary Landscape of Religion* (Oxford University Press, 2002). Later, together with Joseph Henrich, he published *The Evolution of Religion* (2010) in the journal *Biological Theory*, in which he developed the relationship between the cognitive and social explanations. In later works Atran turned to field studies of political violence and extremism, using the same method to understand costly group commitment, although this strand lies closer to political psychology than to the comparative study of religion as such.

## Impact and Criticism

Atran shares with Boyer and Barrett the general reductive thesis that religion is a by-product of general cognitive structures. He distinguishes himself, however, by insisting that the cognitive explanation alone is not enough to understand the survival and evolutionary social success of religion, and that it requires a complementary explanation at the level of the group rather than the individual alone. Some researchers in religious studies have criticized his approach for treating all religions by one evolutionary and functional standard, which overlooks the essential theological and historical differences between traditions.

## Relation to Arab-Islamic Heritage

Atran's analytical tools, especially the theory of costly signaling, are cited in the study of Islamic practices such as fasting in Ramadan and the Hajj, as costly collective commitments that strengthen the social cohesion and the trust of the Muslim community in its committed members. However, Atran himself did not devote a separate study to Islam in his main book. He includes it among the cross-religious comparative examples alongside Christianity, Judaism, Buddhism and Hinduism.

## The Folk Classification Method and Its Effect on His Religious Theory

In his early studies of folk classification, Atran relied on comparative fieldwork across many cultures, from the Amazon jungle to Western cities. He argued that humans share innate classificatory structures for living things regardless of their culture. Atran carried this same method into the study of religion. He argued that supernatural religious concepts exploit this same innate classificatory mechanism by violating its natural expectations in a limited and calculated way. This makes them more memorable and easier to transmit between individuals and generations than ordinary concepts that are fully expected, or entirely counterintuitive.
