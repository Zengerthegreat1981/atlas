# Nancy Scheper-Hughes

Nancy Scheper-Hughes (born 1944) is an American medical anthropologist and professor emerita at the University of California, Berkeley, where she directed the doctoral programme in Critical Studies in Medicine, Science, and the Body. She is one of the leading voices of critical medical anthropology, known above all for *Death Without Weeping: The Violence of Everyday Life in Brazil* (1992), her ethnography of infant death and maternal grief in a Brazilian shantytown, and for her later investigations of the global trade in human organs. Her work is inseparable from her argument that anthropologists should abandon the pretence of neutrality and practise a politically and morally engaged ethnography.

## Life and Career

Scheper-Hughes was born in 1944. Her first sustained encounter with Brazil came in the mid-1960s, when she worked there as a Peace Corps volunteer in the impoverished hillside community of Alto do Cruzeiro, in the north-east of the country. She returned as an ethnographer and followed the community over several decades. Her doctoral research had earlier taken her to rural Ireland, where she studied mental illness in a village in County Kerry, published as *Saints, Scholars, and Schizophrenics: Mental Illness in Rural Ireland* (1979). She taught at the University of California, Berkeley, for most of her career, training a generation of medical anthropologists.

From the mid-1990s she turned to the global traffic in human organs, carrying out multi-sited ethnographic research in many countries and co-founding Organs Watch, a research and documentation project on the trade in kidneys and other organs, with Lawrence Cohen. She took part in an international meeting on organ trafficking at Bellagio, Italy, in 1995, a mark of how far her work had moved beyond the boundaries of the discipline.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

*Death Without Weeping* is Scheper-Hughes's central achievement. In Alto do Cruzeiro, where infant mortality was extraordinarily high, she documented what she called mortal selective neglect: mothers, facing a world in which many babies die, unconsciously concentrated care on infants they perceived as strong enough to survive, while weaker infants were left to fade. She insisted that this "selective mothering" was not individual moral failure but a psychological and cultural adaptation to chronic structural poverty — a response to conditions in which grief itself had to be rationed. The book made the concept of structural violence concrete, showing how hunger, lack of clean water, and the absence of medical care are experienced in the body and in the most intimate relations of family life.

Her work on organ trafficking extended the same analysis to the global body. She described kidneys flowing from the poor of the global South to wealthy patients in the North, the Gulf, and Israel, brokered by networks of middlemen, surgeons, and hospitals, and she called this a form of invisible, sacrificial violence. Where bioethics treated organ sale as a matter of individual consent, she insisted on the structural coercion that makes the poor into sellers and the rich into buyers.

Methodologically, her most controversial contribution was the call for a "militant anthropology" — an ethnography that is openly partisan on behalf of the people it studies. In "The Primacy of the Ethical" (1995) she argued that the fieldworker's duty to witness and to intervene outweighs any obligation to neutrality. Together with Philippe Bourgois, she also developed the idea of a continuum of violence, in which the visible violence of war is continuous with the quieter violences of everyday life: neglect, domestic abuse, and the slow death of poverty.

## Influence and Critique

Scheper-Hughes's influence on medical anthropology has been profound. The concept of mortal selective neglect became a standard reference in studies of motherhood, poverty, and infant mortality across cultures, and her work helped to drive international pressure to criminalise organ trafficking and tighten its regulation. Her defence of engaged ethnography reshaped debate about the anthropologist's role as witness and moral actor rather than detached observer.

Her work has also drawn sustained criticism. Other researchers who worked in Brazil, among them Claudia Fonseca, questioned whether the pattern of maternal indifference she described could be generalised to other Brazilian contexts. Her investigative fieldwork on the organ trade, which relied on interviews and sources that were hard to verify independently because of the clandestine nature of the trade, was criticised as closer to investigative journalism than to conventional ethnography — a charge she answered by insisting that a hidden crime requires methods that go beyond the traditional ones. These debates remain unresolved, but they are themselves a measure of her importance: few anthropologists have forced the discipline to confront so directly the question of what ethnography owes to the people it describes.
