# Hannah Arendt
A German-American political theorist (1906–1975). She studied under **Martin Heidegger** in Marburg in 1924, and obtained her doctorate at Heidelberg in 1929 under **Karl Jaspers** with a dissertation on *the concept of love in Augustine*. She fled Germany in 1933 to Paris, was interned in the Gurs camp in 1940, and arrived in New York in 1941 stateless until she was naturalized in 1951 — the experience that became the material of her analysis of the stateless.
## The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951)
In three parts: antisemitism, imperialism and totalitarianism. It gathers Nazism and Stalinism under a single pattern of rule that is not understood through the categories of classical tyranny, but through terror, ideology and the atomization of society. In the chapter on the decline of the nation-state she formulated **"the right to have rights"**: the stateless person first loses the political position that makes any other right claimable — and on this Giorgio Agamben built his analysis of bare life.
## The Human Condition (1958)
She divides the `vita activa` into three activities: **labor** (the cycle of biological survival), **work** (the production of lasting things), and **action** (speech and initiative among human beings). Action alone is the locus of freedom, and its condition is **natality** (Natality) — the capacity of every newborn to begin what was not — in contrast to Heidegger's "being-toward-death." Her critical thesis is the rise of "the social," which swallows the public space and turns politics into the administration of an enlarged household economy, and what she calls **"world alienation"** (Weltentfremdung). From this **Jürgen Habermas** drew one of the roots of his concept of the public sphere in 1962, although he made rational communication, not singular action, the criterion of publicness.
## Eichmann in Jerusalem (1963) — The Most Famous Controversy Around Her
Her coverage of the trial of Adolf Eichmann in Jerusalem in 1961 was as a correspondent for *The New Yorker*, and she then collected the coverage in *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963). Her thesis is that Eichmann was not an ideological monster but a functionary incapable of thinking from the standpoint of another — and this is the meaning of "the banality of evil": a description of the perpetrator, not a belittling of the crime. The centre of the attack on it was her section on the **Jewish Councils** (Judenräte) and her statement that the cooperation of Jewish leaderships raised the number of dead. **Gershom Scholem**, in his correspondence with her in 1963, accused her of lacking "love of Israel" (Ahabath Israel) and of a mocking tone toward victims, **Norman Podhoretz** attacked her in *Commentary* (1963), and **Jacob Robinson** devoted a counter-book to her in 1965. Her essay "Reflections on Little Rock" (*Dissent*, 1959) had earlier brought on her the criticism of **Ralph Ellison**.
## Her Place in the Current
Arendt rejected the label "philosopher": in her conversation with Günter Gaus in 1964 she said that her profession is **political theory**. Her distance from Heidegger and Jaspers lies not in the phenomenological method but in the subject — plurality and the shared public space instead of the solitary self before its death.
## Most Important Works
- *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951).
- *Rahel Varnhagen: The Life of a Jewess* (1957).
- *The Human Condition* (1958).
- *Eichmann in Jerusalem* (1963) and *On Revolution* (1963).
- *Men in Dark Times* (1968) and *On Violence* (1970).
- *The Life of the Mind* — published incomplete after her death in 1978, edited by Mary McCarthy.
- *Lectures on Kant's Political Philosophy* — after her death in 1982, edited by Ronald Beiner.
## Limits
Arendt did not write a normative moral theory or a psychology, and she states in *The Human Condition* (1958) that her aim is "to think what we are doing", not to prescribe what we ought to do. Therefore "the banality of evil" is not a diagnosis of the structure of Eichmann's personality — and a major objection to the 1963 book, pressed for example by Bettina Stangneth in *Eichmann Before Jerusalem* (2011), is that the later-discovered Sassen interviews and other documents show Eichmann as a committed anti-Semitic ideologue rather than the thoughtless functionary she portrayed.
## Selected Quotations
"the lesson of the fearsome, word-and-thought-defying banality of evil" — *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, 1963, closing pages.
"I want to understand" — her conversation with Günter Gaus, 1964.
