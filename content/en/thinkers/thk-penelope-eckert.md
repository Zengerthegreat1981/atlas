# Penelope Eckert

American sociolinguist, professor emerita at Stanford University, and one of the most prominent names in contemporary **variationist sociolinguistics**. She reshaped the field by introducing the concept of the **community of practice** and by shifting the focus from a static statistical relation between linguistic variables and given social categories (such as class or gender) to the study of how speakers build effective **social meaning** through their speech style.

## Her Contributions

### *Jocks and Burnouts* (1989)

An extensive ethnographic-linguistic study at a high school in the suburbs of Detroit, in which Eckert revealed a central social opposition between two locally defined student categories: the "Jocks," who embody middle-class values and abide by the norms of the school institution, and the "Burnouts," who embody working-class culture and resist these norms in preparation for entering manual labour. She showed that the phonetic differences between the two groups (in pronouncing certain vowels as part of the Northern Cities Vowel Shift) are not directly tied to the social class of the family. Rather, they relate to the student's actual, lived membership in a local **community of practice** within the school.

### The Concept of "Community of Practice"

Eckert, together with Sally McConnell-Ginet, borrowed the concept of "community of practice" from social learning theory (Étienne Wenger and Jean Lave) and developed it into an analytical tool for sociolinguistics. A social group is defined here neither by abstract shared characteristics (such as gender or class) nor merely by physical proximity, but by its members' actual shared practice, from which distinctive linguistic and stylistic norms of the group arise. This framework became essential to the study of language and gender after their joint article "Think Practically and Look Locally" (1992).

### Linguistic Style as the Construction of Social Meaning (Third Wave Sociolinguistics)

In *Linguistic Variation as Social Practice* (2000) and *Meaning and Linguistic Variation* (2018), Eckert led what is known as the "third wave" of variationist sociolinguistics. Rather than treating linguistic variation as a passive reflection of a fixed social affiliation (as the first and second waves did), she treats it as an **active** resource that speakers use, consciously or unconsciously, to build their social identities, stances and style moment by moment.

## Impact

- Her concept of "community of practice" redefined the basic unit of analysis in sociolinguistics, moving it away from rigid demographic categories.
- The "third wave" she led influenced a whole generation of contemporary sociolinguistic studies of identity and style.
- She is among the key founding figures of the field of **language and gender**, together with McConnell-Ginet.

## Limitations

- The "community of practice" framework has sometimes been criticized for the difficulty of defining its boundaries empirically with precision: when a group begins and ends as a coherent unit of practice.
- The reliance of *Jocks and Burnouts* on a specific American context (a predominantly white high school in the 1980s) raises a question about how far the model can be generalized to different school and cultural settings.
