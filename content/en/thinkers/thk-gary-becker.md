# Gary Becker

**Gary Becker** was an American economist and a long-serving professor at the University of Chicago. He applied marginal analysis and rational choice to subjects that had usually been left to sociology and law, including racial discrimination, crime, marriage and addiction. He was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1992 "for having extended the domain of microeconomic analysis to a wide range of human behaviour and interaction, including nonmarket behaviour".

## The doctoral dissertation: racial discrimination as an economic question

In his doctoral dissertation at the University of Chicago, and then in his book *The Economics of Discrimination* (1957), Becker offered an innovative economic analysis of racial discrimination in the labour market. He argued that employers who discriminate against workers of a particular skin colour in fact bear a real economic cost of their own: they pay higher wages to the preferred workers or forgo more efficient workers. In his formulation employers have a "taste for discrimination", which they indulge at a cost. This makes discrimination an economically "costly" behaviour in a competitive market over the long run, rather than merely a social phenomenon with no economic cost to the person practising it.

## Economics studies crime and punishment

In his influential article "Crime and Punishment: An Economic Approach" (1968), Becker developed a model that treats the criminal as a rational actor who compares the expected return from committing a crime (the potential gains) with its expected cost (the probability of being caught multiplied by the severity of the punishment). He proposed that effective criminal policy should be designed through a precise economic calculation of this relationship, not on purely moral or deterrent grounds alone. This framework became the basis for a whole subfield known as the economics of crime.

## Family economics: marriage and divorce as a market

Becker extended his logic to family relationships in his articles on the theory of marriage (1973–1974) and in *A Treatise on the Family* (1981); *The Economic Approach to Human Behavior* (1976) collects his broader programme. He analysed decisions about marriage, divorce and the number of children as rational choices that maximize the joint utility of the two partners, through a specialized division of labour within the household (one partner specializing in market work and the other in household work, for example). This calculation resembles Ricardo's logic of international comparative advantage, but is applied this time to the marital relationship itself.

## Human capital theory

Becker developed the concept of **human capital** in *Human Capital* (1964), building on earlier work by Theodore Schultz and Jacob Mincer. He held that spending on education and training is a rational economic investment, in which the individual compares the present cost (tuition fees and the income lost during study) with the expected future return (the higher income expected later), just as an investor evaluates a conventional physical investment project. This framework became fundamental in the economics of education and in human development policy around the world.

## Continuity with Robbins's definition

Becker's project is a sustained application of Lionel Robbins's definition of economics as the science of choice, independent of the particular subject of that choice. As long as a decision involves scarce resources (time, money, effort) and multiple competing ends, Becker argued that the tools of marginal economic analysis are applicable to it in principle, whether the matter is buying a car or choosing the number of children in a family.

## "Economic imperialism": assessment and critique

Some critics of Becker, including prominent sociologists, labelled his project "economic imperialism", arguing that reducing complex human phenomena such as family love or moral commitment to a narrow utility calculation strips them of much of their actual meaning. Becker replied that this deliberate methodological reduction is not a claim that human life is in fact merely a cold calculation. It is an analytical tool that reveals predictable behavioural regularities that were not clear before this logic was rigorously applied to them.

## Sen's critique: ethics the model does not capture

Amartya Sen launched a deep philosophical critique of the model of the actor driven entirely by a purely self-interested utility calculation, the kind of model on which Becker built his project. In his essay "Rational Fools" (1977) he described this actor as a "rational fool", incapable of explaining moral commitment or collective solidarity that does not serve the individual's direct interest by any direct utility calculation. Sen did not reject the tools of economic analysis altogether. He called for extending them to accommodate human motives beyond self-interest alone. This critique has remained present in every later discussion of the limits of Becker's expansive project.

## Institutional influence in development and health economics

Applications of Becker's analytical framework extended into development economics and public health. The logic of human capital was used principally to justify government investment in education and nutrition in developing countries. The same cost-benefit logic was used to analyse individuals' decisions about smoking and addiction (the theory of "rational addiction" that Becker developed with Kevin Murphy in 1988). This extended the boundaries of economic application to behaviours that appear at first glance far removed from any conventional rational calculation, and affirmed the continuity of his research programme even in fields that are among the most difficult tests of the validity of the assumption of full rationality.
