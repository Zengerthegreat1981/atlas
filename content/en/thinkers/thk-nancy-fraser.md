# Nancy Fraser

An American critical philosopher and sociologist who, in her later phase, developed an analysis of the contemporary "crisis of care" as a structural contradiction in capitalism itself, not merely a passing shortcoming of social policy.

## Historical Context

Nancy Fraser is an American critical philosopher and sociologist, a professor at the New School for Social Research in New York. She built her intellectual career through successive stages. She began with a feminist critique of Jürgen Habermas's philosophy on the "public sphere". In the 1990s she then developed an influential theoretical framework on "redistribution" versus "recognition", in critical collaboration with Axel Honneth (published in *Redistribution or Recognition?*, 2003). In the second decade of the millennium she moved to her later phase, more focused on the structural analysis of capitalism, influenced by the 2008 global financial crisis and by the tradition of Karl Polanyi in *The Great Transformation* (1944), which analyzed the contradictions of the "double movement" between the expansion of the market and the counter-movements of social protection against it.

## Recognition, Redistribution and Representation

In the 1990s Fraser argued ("From Redistribution to Recognition?", *New Left Review*, 1995) that injustice has two analytically distinct faces: economic maldistribution and cultural misrecognition. Against Honneth, who treated recognition as the master category, she defended a "perspectival dualism" and a "status model" of recognition: misrecognition means institutionalized patterns of cultural value that deny people the standing of full partners in social interaction, and the criterion of justice is "participatory parity". In *Scales of Justice* (2008) she added a third dimension, representation (who counts as a subject of justice and how political claims are framed), producing a three-dimensional theory.

## Main Ideas and Method

In "Contradictions of Capital and Care" (2016, building on her 2013 essays), Fraser argued that capitalism depends structurally on the labor of "social reproduction" (Social Reproduction): the care of children and the elderly, domestic work, and daily emotional and health support. This labor produces and renews the labor force itself every day and generation after generation, and it is an indispensable structural condition for the continuation of any capitalist system of production, however marginal it appears in official economic accounting (unpaid domestic work, for example, is not counted in gross domestic product). But capitalism at the same time structurally impoverishes this labor and turns it into labor that is invisible and traditionally unpaid (usually falling on women in particular), or paid at a very low wage when it is commodified in the newly globalized care economy (migrant domestic workers from poor countries caring for the children of wealthy families in rich countries, leaving their own children to less adequate substitute care). According to her, this produces a genuine internal structural "contradiction" at the very heart of capitalism itself: the system badly needs social reproduction in order to continue and to produce a healthy, trained labor force, but its own accumulative logic (the constant drive to cut costs and increase hours of paid work) continually dismantles the social conditions (available time, resources, family and community bonds) that make this care work possible in the first place. This produces a recurring periodic "crisis of care" that worsens especially under contemporary neoliberalism, which intensified women's integration into paid work without a corresponding redistribution of the burden of unpaid care.

## Major Works

Besides "Contradictions of Capital and Care" (2016), her pivotal works include *Scales of Justice* (2008); *Fortunes of Feminism* (2013); *Redistribution or Recognition? A Political-Philosophical Exchange* (with Axel Honneth, 2003); her essay "A Triple Movement?" (2013), which adds to Polanyi's "double movement" of marketization and social protection a third force, emancipation, and applies the scheme to the contemporary crisis; and her later book *Cannibal Capitalism* (2022), which extended the analysis of structural contradictions to include, besides the crisis of care, a simultaneous ecological crisis and a political-democratic crisis.

## Influence and Criticism

This analysis offered an influential bridge between Marxist feminism and the contemporary sociology of work and care. It influenced political debates over social care policy, maternity leave and the domestic economy, offering a theoretical alternative to analyses of care that content themselves with policy description without linking it to the deep structure of capitalism itself. She has been criticized on the grounds that the structural "contradiction" concept she uses is sometimes so loose that it is hard to test empirically with precision, and that her proposed political solutions (a radical reorganization of work and care together) remain theoretically ambitious rather than a detailed political program that can be applied immediately.

Fraser continues her active intellectual work, combining precise theoretical philosophical commitment with direct practical political engagement in contemporary feminist and left movements, constantly stressing the need to link any feminist critique of care to a deeper structural critique of capitalism itself, rather than settling for partial social-policy demands separate from it.

At the New School Fraser has held the title of Henry A. and Louise Loeb Professor of Political and Social Science and Professor of Philosophy. Her intellectual career has also been marked by public critical exchanges with other feminist thinkers (notably Judith Butler, in the exchange around "Merely Cultural", published in *Social Text* in 1997 and in *New Left Review* in 1998) over the priority of economic redistribution versus cultural recognition in the contemporary feminist agenda, always insisting on the need to combine the two dimensions rather than sacrificing one for the other.
