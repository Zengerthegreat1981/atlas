# Edmund Bourne

American psychologist and author specializing in the treatment of anxiety disorders. He holds a Ph.D. and for many years directed the Anxiety Treatment Center in San Jose and Santa Rosa, California. He is known for *The Anxiety and Phobia Workbook*, a self-help book first published in 1990 and still in print in revised editions.

## Professional Career

At the Anxiety Treatment Center he worked with panic disorder, agoraphobia, generalized anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and post-traumatic stress disorder. Related books include *Healing Fear* (1998) and *Beyond Anxiety and Phobia* (2001), both from New Harbinger Publications, which also published the workbook. In *Global Shift: How a New Worldview Is Transforming Humanity* (2008) he turned to cultural change and consciousness. Later anxiety titles include *Natural Relief for Anxiety* (2004) and *Coping with Anxiety*. A public birth date and a documented account of his university training are not available here.

## The Scientific Context: How the Book Came About

The workbook does not present new methods; it gathers what had accumulated in the behavioral and cognitive treatment of anxiety. Joseph Wolpe introduced systematic desensitization in the 1950s, drawing on the muscle relaxation that Edmund Jacobson had developed in *Progressive Relaxation* (1938). Isaac Marks's research in *Fears, Phobias and Rituals* (1987) showed that gradual live exposure is the most effective treatment for agoraphobia and specific phobias. Aaron Beck and Gary Emery formulated a cognitive framework linking anxiety to an exaggerated estimate of danger, in *Anxiety Disorders and Phobias* (1985). David Barlow presented the model of panic and its treatment through exposure to internal sensations in *Anxiety and Its Disorders* (1988), and then came David Clark's cognitive model (1986). The workbook draws on these components and arranges them in a practical order suited to the non-specialist reader.

## *The Anxiety and Phobia Workbook*

Bourne first published the book in 1990, and it has since appeared in several revised editions. It presents practical, gradual therapeutic strategies drawn mainly from cognitive behavioral therapy, relaxation techniques, and graded exposure, aimed at non-specialist readers for managing anxiety symptoms on their own. It has been widely read and translated. It moves from understanding the types of anxiety and self-assessment, to relaxation and breathing, then graded exposure to feared situations, then modifying self-talk and catastrophic thoughts, then attention to nutrition, exercise, relationships, and values. Later editions have added material on mindfulness-based and acceptance-based approaches, reflecting developments in cognitive behavioral therapy itself.

## Practical Contribution

Unlike many self-help books, Bourne's work combines an explanation of the physiological and cognitive mechanisms of anxiety (activation of the autonomic nervous system, catastrophic thinking patterns) with detailed practical tools (monitoring logs, graded exposure exercises, breathing and mindfulness techniques, and self-talk modification), in a style that is easy to use outside direct therapy sessions. This approach helped spread the principles of cognitive behavioral therapy for anxiety disorders to a wide popular audience. Therapists sometimes recommend such workbooks as a supportive tool between sessions.

## Evidence on the Effectiveness of Self-Help

Research supports guided self-help as a class of intervention. It does not establish the effect of any one book. Gould and Clum's 1993 meta-analysis found that self-help treatments outperformed no treatment across several problems. A systematic review by Catrin Lewis, Jennifer Pearce, and Jonathan I. Bisson in the *British Journal of Psychiatry* (2012) found that self-help for anxiety disorders beat waiting-list control, especially for social phobia and panic disorder, while therapist-delivered treatment retained an advantage, and that guidance and multimedia or web-based formats improved results.

## Limits and Criticism

The book relies on self-help, so it does not suit severe cases, cases accompanied by severe depression, suicidal thoughts, or complex trauma, which require a therapist. The results of pure self-help vary: many readers stop completing the exercises, and results clearly improve when a guide is present. It is also criticized for being written from an American perspective, so it does not address cultural differences in expressing anxiety, such as the prevalence of physical complaints in some cultures, or the role of family and religion in coping. It also falls within a critical trend that holds that "workbooks" may reduce social and economic causes of anxiety to an individual problem. This entry is educational and is not clinical advice; a workbook is not a substitute for assessment by a qualified clinician.
