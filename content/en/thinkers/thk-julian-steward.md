# Julian Steward

## Definition

Julian Haynes Steward (1902–1972) was an American anthropologist who taught at the University of Michigan, the University of Utah, Columbia University, and the University of Illinois, and who worked at the Smithsonian's Bureau of American Ethnology. He is the founder of "cultural ecology" in American anthropology. His method linked social and cultural patterns to the natural environment in which they take shape, as an alternative to the unilinear evolutionism of the nineteenth century.

## Historical Context

Steward received his PhD at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1929, under Alfred Kroeber. He developed his method in the 1930s and 1940s, as American anthropology was moving beyond the historical particularism of Franz Boas, which was wary of broad comparative generalization. He drew on his fieldwork among Shoshonean and other peoples of the Great Basin in the western United States, where he observed how scarce desert resources shaped the dispersal of social groups into small, mobile family bands.

## Key Ideas and Method

Steward developed the concept of the "cultural core," the set of social, political, and religious features most closely tied to a society's subsistence activities and economic arrangements. These were contrasted with "secondary" features, which are more open to independent historical variation. On this basis he formulated a method he called "multilinear evolution." He rejected the unilinear schemes of Morgan and Tylor, as well as the single universal law favored by Leslie White, and proposed instead the comparative study of parallel developments in particular environmental and historical settings. He also proposed "levels of sociocultural integration" as a way of comparing societies of different scale.

## Key Works

- *Basin-Plateau Aboriginal Sociopolitical Groups* (1938), his Great Basin study, in which he analyzed how environment shaped band organization.
- *Handbook of South American Indians* (1946–1959), a seven-volume reference that he edited for the Bureau of American Ethnology and that is still consulted.
- *Theory of Culture Change: The Methodology of Multilinear Evolution* (1955), which sets out his theoretical framework.
- *The People of Puerto Rico* (1956), the report of a large collaborative field project, which he directed, involving several research teams studying different communities on one island. Among its participants were Sidney Mintz and Eric Wolf.

## Impact and Criticism

Steward influenced the development of ecological anthropology in the 1960s and 1970s, and his students and colleagues included Sidney Mintz, Eric Wolf, Robert Murphy, and Elman Service; Marshall Sahlins and others also engaged with his work. His thesis on the cultural core is taught as a basic analytical tool in introductory courses in ecological anthropology.

Critics argue that his focus on the economic-ecological core can neglect the role of ideology, power, and colonial history in shaping societies. Some of his own students, among them Mintz and Wolf, moved away from his ecological framework toward an anthropological political economy centered on colonial and global capitalist history.

Steward is often compared with Leslie White as one of the two main pillars of American neo-evolutionism: Steward favored historical multilinearity, while White held to a single universal law of cultural evolution.
