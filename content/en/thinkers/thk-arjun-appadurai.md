# Arjun Appadurai: Ethnography of Globalization and the Circulation of Culture

Arjun Appadurai (born 1949) is an Indian-American anthropologist and a leading figure in the anthropology of globalization. He is best known for analysing global cultural flows through five dimensions, or "scapes": ethnoscapes, mediascapes, technoscapes, financescapes and ideoscapes. The framework, set out in the essay "Disjuncture and Difference in the Global Cultural Economy" (1990) and developed in *Modernity at Large* (1996), helped redirect anthropological attention from bounded communities to circulation, imagination and the production of locality.

## Biographical Context and Intellectual Formation

Appadurai was born in Bombay (now Mumbai) and studied in India and the United States, receiving his PhD from the Committee on Social Thought at the University of Chicago in 1976. He taught at the University of Pennsylvania, the University of Chicago, Yale, The New School in New York (where he also served as provost) and New York University. With Carol Breckenridge he established the journal *Public Culture*. His work draws on historical anthropology of South Asia, cultural studies, social theory and postcolonial debates, and he has also engaged with organisations of the urban poor in Mumbai.

## The Five Scapes

Appadurai argued that the global cultural economy is marked by disjunctures that a simple centre-periphery model cannot capture. The scapes are fluid constructs that look different according to the position of the actors concerned:

- **Ethnoscapes**: the shifting landscape of people on the move, such as migrants, refugees, exiles, tourists and guest workers.
- **Technoscapes**: the global configuration of technology.
- **Financescapes**: the movement of capital and currencies.
- **Mediascapes**: the distribution of images and information and the narratives they supply.
- **Ideoscapes**: the circulation of political ideas and ideologies.

## Imagination, Locality and Method

Drawing partly on Benedict Anderson's account of imagined communities, Appadurai treated imagination as a social practice rather than mere fantasy. Electronic media and migration, he argued, widen the range of lives that people can envisage, so that imagination becomes an everyday resource for identity and collective action. Globalization therefore does not simply homogenize culture: people appropriate global forms for local purposes and produce uneven new combinations. For ethnography this implies following people, objects and images across places, and treating locality as something that has to be produced and maintained rather than a given setting.

## Major Works

- **Worship and Conflict under Colonial Rule: A South Indian Case** (1981): a historical anthropological study of the Parthasarathy temple in Triplicane, Madras, concerning ritual honours, disputes over authority and colonial administration.
- **The Social Life of Things: Commodities in Cultural Perspective** (1986, editor): essays on how things acquire value as they circulate; his introduction on the politics of value is widely cited.
- **Modernity at Large: Cultural Dimensions of Globalization** (1996): the fullest statement of the scapes and of the role of imagination.
- **Globalization** (2001, editor): a collection that includes his essay "Grassroots Globalization and the Research Imagination."
- **Fear of Small Numbers: An Essay on the Geography of Anger** (2006): an argument about how the uncertainties of globalization can fuel hostility and violence toward minorities.
- **The Future as Cultural Fact** (2013) and **Banking on Words** (2015): later work on aspiration, the future and derivative finance.

## Digital Ethnography

The scapes framework appeared before social media, but its attention to circulation and imagination can be applied to digital fieldwork. Online platforms bring mediascapes and technoscapes together with the movement of migrants and money, and the idea of producing locality prompts the question of how users create a sense of neighbourhood among people who are far apart. It also directs attention to disjunctures: images and messages may travel where people cannot, while access depends on cost, language, state control, and the rules of platforms.

Related research illustrates these possibilities without being a simple application of one theory. Victoria Bernal's *Nation as Network* (2014) examined how diaspora websites became arenas of Eritrean political debate. Methodologically, Christine Hine's *Virtual Ethnography* (2000) and the volume *Digital Ethnography* (2016) by Sarah Pink and colleagues discuss how to study connections across online and offline settings. A digital ethnography informed by Appadurai would follow particular images, people, and objects across these settings and ask who controls the infrastructure, which returns to the questions of power raised by his critics.

## Postcolonial Positioning and Non-Western Modernity

The phrase "modernity at large" presents modernity not as a simple Western export but as a plural and uneven field that different societies engage in their own ways. Appadurai's writing on India, including the decolonization of cricket and the urban life of Bombay, treats formerly colonized societies as active producers of global culture.

## Critics and Responses

The scapes framework has been criticized as abstract and as saying too little about power and inequality: some people and capital move easily while others face severe restrictions. Some postcolonial critics hold that it does not sufficiently centre the colonial histories that shaped contemporary globalization, and others that its emphasis on imagination and creativity can understate material domination. *Fear of Small Numbers* and later work deal more directly with violence and inequality.

## Legacy

The vocabulary of scapes is widely used in the study of globalization, migration, diaspora and transnational media, and has travelled into geography, cultural studies and development studies.
