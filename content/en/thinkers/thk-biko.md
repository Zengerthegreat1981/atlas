# Steve Biko

A South African political activist and the founder of the "Black Consciousness" movement. He was arrested in 1977 and died days later in police custody from head injuries, and his funeral and the subsequent exposure of the circumstances of his death were a turning point in the international isolation of the apartheid regime.

His basic thesis is psychological before it is political: that **"the most potent weapon in the hands of the oppressor is the mind of the oppressed"** — that is, apartheid does not rest on law and police alone but on the oppressed internalizing their inferiority, and liberation begins with the recovery of collective self-esteem before any political demand.

## Biography

Stephen Bantu Biko was born on 18 December 1946 in King William's Town (today Qonce) in the Eastern Cape, to a family of the Xhosa people; his father died while he was young, and he was raised by his mother, who worked in domestic service. He studied at Lovedale school and was then expelled from it because of his brother's link with political activity, so he continued at St Francis College in Mariannhill, Natal, and then entered the medical school of the University of Natal (the section reserved for Blacks) in Durban in 1966. There he became involved in student work within the National Union of South African Students (NUSAS), a union whose majority was white, but he concluded that the white liberal movement speaks in the name of Blacks and decides for them. So in 1968–1969 he took part in founding the South African Students' Organisation (SASO) and was elected its first president at the 1969 conference. After his expulsion from medical school in 1972 he worked in Black community programs, including the Zanempilo health clinic near King William's Town, and took part in founding the Black People's Convention (BPC) in 1972. In March 1973 the authorities imposed on him a banning order restricting his residence and movement and prohibiting the publication of his words.

## The thought of Black Consciousness

For Biko "Black" is not limited to color, but is a political and mental stance: it includes all who suffer political discrimination and unite in the struggle for liberation, that is, Africans, Coloureds and Indians in the South African framework. He defines Black Consciousness as a mental attitude and a way of life, whose essence is that the Black person recognizes his own worth and refuses to accept the inferiority imposed on him. He holds that the first step is "liberating the mind" from fear and dependence, because the legal structures of apartheid endure only through the victims' tacit acceptance of them. For this reason he criticized white liberals who object to segregation but keep their privileges and claim leadership, and demanded that Blacks lead their own struggle themselves, which became known as the slogan of self-reliance.

## Intellectual roots and context

Biko was influenced by the writers of Négritude and postcolonial thought: Aimé Césaire, Léopold Senghor and Frantz Fanon, who offers a psychological analysis of colonial alienation, and he also drew on American Black theology (James Cone) and the Black Power movement in the United States, on his Christian background, and on the collective African heritage. In his essays he presents a vision of African culture based on community, mutual giving and shared humanity, a vision that later scholars regard as part of the background to a political reading of Ubuntu, although Biko did not make this term the axis of his thesis. His idea of "a true humanity" rests on the notion that a future South African society must not repeat racial hierarchy but offer the world a new human value.

## Arrest and death

On 18 August 1977 the police stopped him at a roadblock near Grahamstown (Makhanda) and he was detained under the Terrorism Act, held in Port Elizabeth and subjected to torture and beating. He was transported naked and shackled in a vehicle about a thousand kilometers to Pretoria, where he died on 12 September 1977 at the age of thirty. Minister of Justice Jimmy Kruger said that his death "leaves him cold", and claimed that he had gone on a hunger strike, an account refuted by the facts of the inquest. The judicial inquest concluded that no one bore criminal responsibility, and then in 1999 the Truth and Reconciliation Commission rejected the amnesty applications submitted by the officers concerned, and Biko became a global symbol of resistance; the journalist Donald Woods immortalized his story in his book *Biko* (1978) and in the film *Cry Freedom* in 1987.

## What he contributed

- **"Black Consciousness"**: an intellectual movement that stressed the value of the Black self in the face of colonial oppression.
- **"Removing the psychological legacy of colonialism"**: an analysis of how colonialism affects the psyche of the African person.
- **"Ubuntu as resistance"**: a reading of Ubuntu as a tool for liberating the self from colonialism, a later reading among his scholars rather than a central term in his texts.

## His place in the current

Biko stands at the intersection of politics and psychology, which makes him a basic reference for Ubuntu psychology in its political dimensions. He is read with Fanon and Césaire within Black existentialism, since he shares with them the question of freedom and alienation under colonialism, but differs from them in being a field activist and a builder of social institutions (such as the health clinic and community programs) and not only a writing theorist.

## Major works

- *I Write What I Like* (published posthumously in 1978, edited by Aelred Stubbs) — a collection of his essays that he used to publish in the newsletter of the Black student union under the pseudonym "Frank Talk".

## Limits and critique

Biko's texts are difficult to separate from their circumstances: they were written under censorship and banning, and some are short journalistic essays rather than methodical studies, so their theoretical precision varies. Some criticized his focus on the psychological and cultural dimension as against the class-economic structure of colonialism and capitalism, and some Marxist critics held that consciousness alone is not enough. His broad definition of "Black" also provoked controversy over the position of Coloureds and Indians, and later studies pointed to the limited place of women in the leadership of the movement. While *I Write What I Like* established Biko as a central theorist of Black existentialism and anticolonial philosophy worldwide, his writings have occasionally been overshadowed in Western academic curricula by those of Fanon and Cabral.
