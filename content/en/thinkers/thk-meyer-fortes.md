# Meyer Fortes

Meyer Fortes (1906–1983) was a British social anthropologist and, with E. E. Evans-Pritchard, the founder of modern descent theory. His fieldwork among the Tallensi of northern Ghana produced the classic studies *The Dynamics of Clanship Among the Tallensi* (1945) and *The Web of Kinship Among the Tallensi* (1949), and his model of the segmentary lineage system dominated British anthropology for decades. Fortes was unusual among his contemporaries in combining the structural analysis of kinship with an interest in psychology, arguing that kinship is felt as well as structured — a line of thought that opened the way for the later anthropology of the person.

## Life and Career

Fortes was born on 25 April 1906 in Britstown, South Africa, to a Jewish family of Polish origin. He studied mathematics and the natural sciences at the University of Cape Town before moving to Britain, where he studied psychology at University College London in the late 1920s. Drawn to anthropology under the influence of both A. R. Radcliffe-Brown and Bronislaw Malinowski — though closer in theory to the former — he undertook doctoral research at the London School of Economics and began fieldwork among the Tallensi in 1934.

He spent long periods among the Tallensi, in the Gold Coast (now Ghana), in 1934–1937 and again in the 1940s, studying a society of farmers and herders organised in patrilineal clans without centralised political authority. His academic career culminated in the William Wyse Professorship of Social Anthropology at the University of Cambridge, which he held from 1950 to 1973. He died in 1983, after a scholarly career of nearly six decades.

## Key Works

Fortes's reputation rests above all on his two Tallensi monographs. *The Dynamics of Clanship Among the Tallensi* (1945) described how the patrilineal clan system worked in everyday practice, showing that the lineage system was not a set of fixed rules but a dynamic order that adapted to circumstances and individual interests. *The Web of Kinship Among the Tallensi* (1949) analysed the full complexity of the network of kinship relations in which Tallensi life was embedded.

Earlier, with Evans-Pritchard, he edited *African Political Systems* (1940), the volume that defined the segmentary lineage model and set the agenda for the political anthropology of stateless societies. His later theoretical work included the influential essay "Oedipus and Job in West African Religion" (1959), which brought psychoanalytic ideas to bear on Tallensi religion and the moral imagination, and *Kinship and the Social Order: The Legacy of Lewis Henry Morgan* (1969), his Lewis Henry Morgan Lectures, which restated his theory of descent. His essay on the psychological aspects of kinship, developed in the 1950s and 1960s, argued that kinship relations are experienced through feeling — that a person experiences kinship ties as real even when they are not biological.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Fortes developed a sophisticated model for the study of descent that joined three elements. First, a functional analysis of the lineage system: descent organises marriage, property, and political authority, but it also performs a deep psycho-social function, shaping personal identity, emotional attachments, and moral obligations. Second, the concept of "psychological kinship": kinship ties are not deduced from biology alone but from the feeling of kinship, so that adoption, fosterage, and spiritual kinship can create bonds that are psychologically real. Third, the segmentary lineage principle, developed with Evans-Pritchard: lineages are arranged in nested branches of equal standing that unite against larger groups, so that political order can exist without a centralised state.

This model became the standard framework for studying non-centralised societies in Africa, the Middle East, and Asia, and it dominated British anthropology through the 1960s. Fortes's insistence that kinship has a psychological dimension also distinguished him from more strictly structural contemporaries and kept open a conversation between anthropology and psychoanalysis that later scholars would develop further.

## Influence and Critique

Fortes's work remains a core reference in the teaching of descent and kinship theory, and his Tallensi ethnography is still read as a model of patient, detailed fieldwork. The segmentary lineage model shaped the study of stateless societies for a generation, and his psychological approach to kinship continues to stimulate work in psychological anthropology and in the study of gender and relatedness.

Criticism came from several directions. From the 1960s onward, critics argued that the segmentary model presented lineage systems as more rigid and timeless than ethnographic reality warranted, underplaying negotiation, change, and individual strategy. Feminist anthropologists noted that Fortes gave too little weight to women's roles in the descent system, assuming patrilineal descent as the fundamental form without sustained attention to women's agency. Postcolonial critics observed that his work, conducted under British colonial rule, treated the kinship systems he studied as "traditional" and stable, saying too little about how colonialism and economic change were already reshaping them. Fortes was among the most respected British anthropologists of his generation, and his defenders note that he sought to understand the Tallensi system from within; but the debate over the static quality of his model remains central to his legacy.
