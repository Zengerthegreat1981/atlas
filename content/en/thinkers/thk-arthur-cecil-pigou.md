# Arthur Cecil Pigou

**Arthur Cecil Pigou** was a British economist. He succeeded his teacher Alfred Marshall in the economics chair at the University of Cambridge, and gave early welfare economics its standard Cambridge form. The corrective tax on external damage later took his name.

## Succeeding Marshall at Cambridge

Pigou was a direct student of Alfred Marshall at Cambridge. He succeeded him in the economics chair there in 1908, at the age of thirty, which made him one of the youngest holders of this prestigious academic post. He held the chair until 1943. Pigou took Marshall's partial equilibrium tools and applied them in a new direction. Rather than limiting himself to describing how prices are determined in the market, he asked how one could evaluate whether these market outcomes are socially "good" at all.

## Welfare Economics

Pigou published *The Economics of Welfare* in 1920, revising *Wealth and Welfare* (1912). The book examines how the size and distribution of the national dividend relate to economic welfare. His criterion was broadly utilitarian and cardinal. Later welfare economics, associated with Pareto and with Hicks and Kaldor, dropped interpersonal comparisons of utility and used ordinal and compensation tests instead. He distinguished between the "private marginal product," which an individual or firm calculates when making an economic decision, and the "social marginal product," which covers all the effects on society as a whole, including effects that do not appear in direct market calculations.

## The Theory of Externalities

Pigou systematised the analysis of **externalities** (building on Marshall's external economies) to describe the gap between these two marginal products. When a factory pollutes the surrounding air without bearing the cost of that pollution itself, its private cost (which it calculates in its production decision) is lower than the actual social cost (which includes the harm of pollution to others). The factory therefore produces a larger quantity than the socially optimal level. This analysis precisely explained why the free competitive market fails to achieve social efficiency in such cases, despite its theoretical efficiency in the absence of such externalities.

## The Pigouvian Tax: A Practical and Influential Solution

Pigou proposed a simple remedy that has remained influential to this day: imposing a tax on polluting activity equal to the external harm it inflicts on others. In this way the producer is forced to absorb the full social cost of its activity in its own calculations, and adjusts its output automatically to the socially optimal level without needing direct administrative intervention that sets permitted quantities. This idea, known as the **Pigouvian tax**, became the theoretical basis for carbon taxes and other corrective levies used today in environmental policy; tradable-permit systems rest on a different, property-rights logic associated with Coase and Dales.

## The Ethical Dimension of Defining Welfare

In defining the concept of "economic welfare," Pigou was influenced by the Cambridge philosopher G. E. Moore. He sought to confine his analysis to the measurable monetary aspect of the broader human welfare, while explicitly acknowledging that this aspect does not exhaust all the moral and spiritual dimensions of the individual's well-being. This deliberate methodological modesty distinguished his approach from some later, more ambitious extensions of economics that aimed to explain every aspect of human behaviour.

## The Debate with Keynes: The End of an Era

In the 1930s Pigou debated John Maynard Keynes, a younger Cambridge colleague who had studied chiefly under Marshall, over the causes of persistent mass unemployment during the Great Depression. Pigou held a neoclassical position: that downward wage flexibility would be theoretically sufficient to restore the economy to full employment. Keynes argued that wage rigidity and pessimistic expectations could keep the economy stuck in an equilibrium without full employment for long periods. Pigou later argued (1943) that falling prices would raise the real value of money balances and so stimulate spending, an argument now called the "Pigou effect", which Keynesians regarded as of limited practical force. The debate is regarded as one of the key turning points that moved the field from full neoclassical dominance towards the Keynesian revolution.

## A Reserved Personality and an Austere Way of Life

Pigou was known for a relatively reclusive personality and an austere way of life. He preferred mountain climbing and rural solitude to an active academic social life, and he remained unmarried throughout his life, devoting himself entirely to research and teaching at Cambridge. Despite this personal isolation, he maintained a wide intellectual influence through his direct teaching of generations of Cambridge students, including Keynes, who would later become his most prominent intellectual critic.

## A Legacy Beyond the Keynesian Debate

Although the debate with Keynes dominated the later years of Pigou's academic career, his deeper legacy lies in his methodological foundation of the entire field of welfare economics, which has continued to exist and develop regardless of the outcome of that specific Keynesian debate. The logic of externalities and corrective taxation that Pigou formulated is still used today to analyse problems such as carbon emissions and congestion, making him one of the most practically influential economists on contemporary environmental policy.
