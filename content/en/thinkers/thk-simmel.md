# Georg Simmel

Georg Simmel (1858-1918) was a German sociologist and philosopher who helped establish sociology as a discipline by studying the forms of social interaction, from the stranger and the metropolis to money and fashion.

## Life and Context

Simmel was born in Berlin and studied history, philosophy and psychology at the University of Berlin. He taught there for many years as a Privatdozent and then an extraordinary professor, a position without full academic standing, and was only given a regular chair at Strasbourg in 1914. He lectured to wide audiences and wrote in newspapers and journals. He was a founding member of the German Society for Sociology in 1909 along with Weber and Toennies.

## Main Ideas

- **Forms of sociation.** Simmel held that sociology should study the recurring forms of interaction, such as conflict, exchange, domination, cooperation and secrecy, apart from their varied content. He called this "formal sociology".
- **Quantity and group size.** In his analysis of the dyad and the triad, he showed how adding a third person changes social relations.
- **The stranger.** In his essay on the stranger, he described a person who is both near and far, within the group yet not of it.
- **The metropolis.** In "The Metropolis and Mental Life" (1903), he argued that urban life, with its stimuli, anonymity and money economy, fosters intellectualism, reserve and individual freedom.
- **Money and modernity.** *The Philosophy of Money* (1900) discussed how a money economy shapes abstraction, calculation and personal freedom, along with new forms of alienation.
- **Conflict.** He treated conflict as a form of sociation that can unite as well as divide.

## Applications and Cross-References

Simmel's essays supply many compact examples of his method: the sociology of secrecy and the secret society, of fashion (imitation and distinction), of sociability as the "play form" of association, of adornment, and of the adventurer. His analysis of the triad (the mediator, and the "third who rejoices", *tertius gaudens*) and of conflict was taken up by later writers, notably Lewis Coser in *The Functions of Social Conflict* (1956). His stranger and metropolitan types fed Robert Park's concept of the marginal man and Chicago studies of the city, and his formal approach to interaction anticipated parts of Erving Goffman's work. Compare Max Weber's contrasting approach to rationalization and Emile Durkheim's emphasis on social facts.

## Method

Simmel wrote in an essayistic style, drawing on small observations to reveal general patterns, and sought to find the general in the particular.

## Influence and Criticism

Simmel strongly influenced the Chicago School, notably Robert Park, who had attended his lectures, as well as symbolic interactionism, network analysis and the sociology of culture. Critics have found his work unsystematic and fragmentary, and some have faulted the lack of a clear research method. His reputation has grown in the study of modernity and urban life.

## Posts, the Tragedy of Culture, and Further Forms

Simmel received his doctorate in philosophy at the University of Berlin in 1881, began teaching there as a Privatdozent in 1885, and was named an associate professor (Extraordinarius) in 1900. A regular chair continued to elude him in Berlin, a delay linked both to antisemitism and to the unconventional range of his writing. He died in Strasbourg in 1918. He treated society as sociation: a continuing process in which individuals affect one another, rather than a substance standing apart from interaction. In the analysis of the triad he also described divide and rule, in which the third party keeps the other two separated in order to dominate them. In "The Concept and Tragedy of Culture" (1911) he distinguished subjective culture, what a person can take up and make his own, from objective culture, the accumulated products of collective life, which grow faster than any individual can absorb. Essays in the 1908 *Soziologie* also take up the poor, defined by the relation of receiving assistance, and the sociology of the senses. He did not carry out systematic field research. Georg Lukács was among those shaped by his teaching. Durkheim doubted that isolating social forms gave sociology a coherent method, and later critics have found the work thin on institutions and organized power.
