# Edward Said

Edward Said (1935-2003) was a Palestinian-American literary critic and public intellectual whose book Orientalism (1978) founded postcolonial studies as an academic field. His analysis of how Western writing, scholarship and policy represented "the Orient" influenced sociology, anthropology, history and cultural studies.

## Life and Context

Said was born in Jerusalem in 1935 and grew up between Jerusalem and Cairo. He was educated in the United States, at Princeton and Harvard, and spent his career as a professor of English and comparative literature at Columbia University. He was also a prominent commentator on Palestine and the Middle East, and wrote on music. He died in New York in 2003 after a long illness.

## Main Ideas

- Orientalism: a body of discourse, tied to European imperial power, which produced the Orient as exotic, backward and unchanging, and so as an object of Western knowledge and control.
- Knowledge and power: drawing on Michel Foucault's notion of discourse and Antonio Gramsci's idea of hegemony, he argued that representation is never politically innocent.
- Contrapuntal reading: reading metropolitan texts together with the colonial histories they depend on, developed in Culture and Imperialism.
- The intellectual: a figure who should speak truth to power and refuse easy allegiance to authority.
- Exile and worldliness: a critical position built on displacement.

## Method

Said used a humanistic textual and discourse analysis, reading literary, scholarly and administrative texts together to show patterns of representation.

## Influence and Criticism

Orientalism reshaped debates on colonialism and representation across the social sciences. Critics such as Aijaz Ahmad (In Theory, 1992) challenged its treatment of the West as a unified entity and its mixing of Foucauldian and humanist premises; historians including Bernard Lewis disputed its accuracy about scholarship. Others noted the book's limited attention to the views of those who were represented.
