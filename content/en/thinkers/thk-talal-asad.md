# Talal Asad

Talal Asad (born 1932) is one of the most influential critical theorists in modern anthropology. Born in Medina to the Austrian-born convert to Islam Muhammad Asad and a Saudi mother, and educated at Oxford, he moved from classical ethnographic fieldwork among the Kababish Bedouin of Sudan to a sweeping critique of the discipline itself. His work exposed the colonial foundations of anthropology, dismantled the idea of "religion" as a universal category, and reshaped how scholars think about secularism, Islam, and the translation of cultures.

## Life and Career

Asad was born in Medina in 1932. His father, Muhammad Asad — born Leopold Weiss, an Austrian Jewish journalist who converted to Islam and became the author of *The Road to Mecca* and a translation of the Qur'an — was one of the most prominent European Muslim intellectuals of the twentieth century. Asad grew up between Pakistan, where the family settled after its founding, and Britain, and studied anthropology at the University of Oxford. His early fieldwork was among the Kababish nomads of Sudan, in the classical descriptive tradition of British social anthropology.

From the late 1960s onward, Asad's work turned increasingly critical. He taught at the University of Hull and later at the Graduate Center of the City University of New York, where he became a central figure in the postcolonial critique of anthropology and in the interdisciplinary study of religion and secularism. His students and readers — including Veena Das and T. N. Madan in their critiques of secularism in South Asia — carried his arguments into a wide range of fields, from the anthropology of Islam to the study of human rights and liberalism.

## Key Works

Asad's first landmark was the edited volume *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973), which gathered essays examining how anthropology grew up inside European colonialism and depended on it structurally — for research funding, for access to the "field" through colonial infrastructure, and for its very object of study. The book argued that the discipline's history could not be separated from the power relations that made it possible, even when anthropologists claimed scientific neutrality.

His later books extended the critique from the history of the discipline to its analytical concepts. In *Genealogies of Religion: Discipline and Reasons of Power in Christianity and Islam* (1993), he argued that "religion" is not a timeless human universal but a category produced by a specific European Christian and post-Reformation history. *Formations of the Secular: Christianity, Islam, Modernity* (2003) applied the same genealogical method to secularism, showing it to be a historically specific formation rather than a neutral space left behind when religion retreats. His essay "The Concept of Cultural Translation in British Social Anthropology" (1986), published in *Writing Culture*, examined how anthropologists assume they simply translate another culture's meanings into Western academic language, ignoring the unequal power relations built into that translation. He has also written critically on human rights discourse and, in *On Suicide Bombing* (2007), on the politics of violence and terror.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Asad's critique of the concept of religion is his most far-reaching idea. Against Clifford Geertz's famous definition of religion as a "system of symbols," Asad argued that such a definition wrongly assumes religion has a fixed essence across history and power. Religion, he showed, has been formed historically through systems of discipline and authority — the church, monasticism, the Inquisition — that cannot be separated from religious practice itself. Scholars should therefore study "discursive traditions": the historically specific ways in which a tradition defines what counts as correct practice, with its own internal standards. He applied this approach to the Islamic tradition in particular, insisting it be treated as a living discursive tradition with its own history and logic, not as a passive object for Western classification.

A second major contribution is his analysis of secularism. Asad argued that the secular is not the absence of religion but a positive formation with its own sensibilities, institutions, and modes of power — one that shapes what counts as religious, and that can reproduce patterns of colonial tutelage when imposed on societies, including Muslim ones, whose moral and legal traditions differ. Relatedly, he criticised the liberal claim that human rights constitute a neutral universal standard, showing that the discourse itself arises from a specific Western political and legal history.

## Influence and Critique

Asad is a key bridge between postcolonial criticism — above all Edward Said's critique of Orientalism — and academic anthropology, and his work deeply influenced the "crisis of representation" and the reflexive turn that questioned the neutrality of ethnographic description. His concepts of discursive tradition and of the genealogies of religion and secularism have become foundational in the anthropology of Islam, religious studies, and the critical study of secularism, extending from Johns Hopkins and Chicago to universities in the Arab world and Pakistan.

His influence has also invited debate. Some critics argue that his emphasis on authoritative tradition underplays the creativity and dissent of ordinary believers, and that his genealogical method, focused on European Christianity, risks reproducing the very Eurocentrism it criticises. Others have questioned whether his critique of secularism and human rights leaves room for a defence of human dignity that is not tied to a particular tradition. Asad himself has insisted that his aim is not to reject human dignity or secular institutions outright, but to show that the categories through which they are understood have histories — and that those histories matter for how they are applied.
