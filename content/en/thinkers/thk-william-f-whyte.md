# William F. Whyte

William Foote Whyte (1914-2000) was an American sociologist best known for Street Corner Society (1943), a classic participant-observation study of an Italian-American neighbourhood in Boston, and later for work on industrial relations, cooperatives and participatory action research.

## Life and Context

Whyte was born in Springfield, Massachusetts, in 1914 and studied at Swarthmore College. As a junior fellow at Harvard's Society of Fellows he spent about three and a half years in Boston's North End, which he called "Cornerville". He gained entry through a local man he called "Doc" and studied street gangs and local politics. He later earned his doctorate at the University of Chicago, taught there and then at Cornell University, where he was a long-time professor in the School of Industrial and Labor Relations.

## Main Ideas

- Social structure of the slum: against images of slum disorganization, Whyte showed that Cornerville was highly organized, with stable informal groups, leadership hierarchies and links between gangs, racketeers and politicians.
- Group structure and behaviour: an individual's status within a group shapes performance, for example in bowling.
- Organizational behaviour: in work on the restaurant industry and in industrial settings he studied how status, technology and interaction affect cooperation.
- Participatory action research: research should involve those studied in defining problems and applying results.
- Cooperatives: later study of the Mondragon cooperatives in Spain.

## Major Works

- Street Corner Society: The Social Structure of an Italian Slum (1943; expanded edition 1955, with a methodological appendix)
- Human Relations in the Restaurant Industry (1948)
- Money and Motivation (1955)
- Men at Work (1961)
- Learning from the Field (1984)
- Making Mondragon (1988, with Kathleen King Whyte)
- Participatory Action Research (edited, 1991)

## Method

Whyte relied on long-term participant observation, informal interviewing and building trust with key informants. His appendix on how the study was done became a model account of fieldwork practice.

## Influence and Criticism

Street Corner Society remains a standard text in urban sociology and ethnography. Critics such as Boelen (1992) re-examined Cornerville and questioned the study's representativeness and some interpretations; Whyte responded to these. Others raise concerns about the early fieldwork, including how informants were identified and the reliance on a single key informant.
