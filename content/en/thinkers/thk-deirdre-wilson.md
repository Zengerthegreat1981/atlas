# Deirdre Wilson

A British linguist, Emeritus Professor at University College London, who co-founded **Relevance Theory** with Dan Sperber, one of the most important contemporary cognitive pragmatic frameworks.

## Contribution to Relevance Theory

Together with Sperber, she developed a model that explains linguistic communication by a single cognitive principle: the search for relevance (the greatest cognitive effect for the least processing effort). This replaces Grice's system of social conversational rules. Their foundational book, *Relevance: Communication and Cognition*, was first published in 1986 (with a revised second edition in 1995). It proposed that linguistic comprehension is an inferential process (not a simple encoding-decoding operation), in which the addressee follows a path of least effort and stops at the first interpretation that satisfies the expectation of relevance. This replaces Paul Grice's maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation and Manner).

## Applications to Metaphor and Irony

Wilson and Sperber extended Relevance Theory to explain pragmatic phenomena that had traditionally been treated as special exceptions to "literal" communication (such as the deliberate violation of one of Grice's norms), including metaphor, irony and hyperbole. They proposed that all of these phenomena arise from the same inferential mechanism of searching for relevance, rather than from separate rules for each rhetorical device. For them, irony is a kind of "echoic use," in which the speaker expresses a dissociative attitude toward an attributed thought or utterance, and metaphor is a case of loose (or broad) use of words.

## Pragmatics, Cognition and Child Acquisition

Within Relevance Theory, Wilson and Sperber have argued that pragmatic comprehension is a form of mind-reading, a specialized inferential ability that interacts with general theory-of-mind capacities. This invites research on how children come to understand speaker intentions, including irony and indirect meaning.

## Impact

Together with Sperber, she established a cognitive pragmatic framework that is among the most influential in contemporary pragmatics. It competes with the traditional Gricean school and offers a psychologically grounded alternative that can be tested experimentally. She taught and supervised researchers in pragmatics at University College London, and the theory has been developed by others in areas such as discourse analysis, literary interpretation and translation.

## Critique of the Gricean System

Wilson and Sperber criticized Paul Grice's traditional system (built on the Cooperative Principle and its four maxims) as a descriptive list of observed behaviors rather than an explanatory theory of the actual cognitive mechanism of comprehension. In its place they proposed one more economical cognitive principle that explains the same pragmatic phenomena that Grice's four maxims explained together (such as the derivation of implicature), through a single mechanism of search for relevance, rather than through a set of multiple social rules that speakers are presumed to follow implicitly.

## The Cognitive and Communicative Principles of Relevance

The theory distinguishes between two principles: the "cognitive principle of relevance" (every human cognitive process tends spontaneously toward maximal relevance) and the "communicative principle of relevance" (every act of ostensive communication carries a guarantee that it is relevant enough to deserve the addressee's attention). This precise distinction makes it possible to explain how the addressee spontaneously concludes that the speaker intended a particular implicit meaning (and not some other logically possible meaning), without assuming a slow, conscious reasoning process each time; the inference is assumed to be fast and largely automatic.

## Continuing Research

Relevance Theory has been extended by Wilson, Sperber and many others to areas such as translation, lexical pragmatics and the study of pragmatic impairment, and a substantial research community has grown around it, including researchers at University College London.

## Interdisciplinary Collaboration

Wilson's collaboration with Dan Sperber combined two different specializations: Wilson is a linguist specializing in pragmatics and semantics, while Sperber is a cognitive anthropologist. This meeting of two disciplines produced a theoretical framework that goes beyond the boundaries of traditional linguistics into the broader field of cognitive science. It draws on tools and models from cognitive psychology (such as the idea of limited processing resources) to explain how the human mind manages the process of linguistic communication.
