# Imru' al-Qais

An Arab poet of the Jahiliyya, traditionally ranked at the head of the pre-Islamic poets. Later critics credited him with motifs that became common: stopping at an abandoned camp, describing the horse, the night and the storm. His *Mu'allaqa* is not, however, the pattern of the panegyric ode as Ibn Qutayba describes it. He belonged to the royal house of **Kinda**, the son of Hujr ibn al-Harith, one of the kings of Kinda. Literary accounts place him in Najd in the late fifth and early sixth centuries CE. The dates are traditional, not documentary.

## Biography and literary tradition

Traditional literary accounts say that his life changed after his father Hujr was killed by Banu Asad. He is said to have sworn not to drink wine, eat meat, anoint himself with oil, approach women or wash his head until he had taken revenge. The same accounts send him from tribe to tribe and then to the Byzantine court, and they have him die on the way back, near Ancyra (Ankara), of an illness that earned him the epithet *dhu al-quruh* ("the man of the ulcers"). This biography was written down long afterwards. It is a literary narrative, not a contemporary record.

## His Mu'allaqa

His *Mu'allaqa* opens with *qifa nabki min dhikra habibin wa-manzili*, conventionally "Halt, and let us weep for the memory of a beloved and an abode." The ode then moves through amorous adventures, including the episode commentators place at Darat Juljul, to the horse and the hunt, the night and a storm. It does not close with a journey and a patron's praise. That tripartite shape, nasib, journey and purpose, is the one Ibn Qutayba describes as typical of the ode, and other poems fit it better than this one. Early critics still said that Imru' al-Qays came first in images of the horse, the night and the clouds. Those claims of priority belong to the critical tradition. They are not a record of invention.

## Between life and legend

The literary books agree on one event: the killing of his father Hujr by Banu Asad. Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani, in *al-Aghani*, has the news reach him while he was drinking, and gives him the proverbial reply whose sense is "today wine, tomorrow the affair" (*al-yawm khamr wa-ghadan amr*). The same narrative has him seek help from the tribes and then from Justinian I, and die at Ancyra from a poisoned robe. Scholars treat the Byzantine episode as legend, shaped by Abbasid narrators around the poetry. The beloved whom commentators name Unayza, and the pool of Darat Juljul, are material of the ode. The poem does not separate a biographical fact from a scene.

## Imagery and simile

The strength of the *Mu'allaqa* lies in its imagery. In describing the horse, he says it is charging and retreating, advancing and turning back all at once, like a boulder of rock that the torrent has dislodged from a height, bringing opposing movements together in a single moment. In describing the night, he likens it to the waves of the sea, letting down its veils upon him with all kinds of cares to test him. Early critics counted these similes among his innovations, and poets after him continued to weave in his manner. These features are what led Ibn Sallam to place him in the first class of poets.

## Modern readings

Modern readings do not agree on the structure. Suzanne Stetkevych, in *The Mute Immortals Speak*, reads the abandoned camp, the erotic passages and the chivalric scenes as a rite of passage. Zwettler's oral-formulaic study treats repeated phrasing as a sign of oral composition. Montgomery is more skeptical of a single explanatory scheme. Neither result erases the poet, and neither decides which lines are his. Alan Jones edited the *Mu'allaqa* with the early commentaries. The studies agree on a narrower point: the historical individual is much less secure than the place of the ode in the tradition.

## Critical standing

A report transmitted in later literature, and widely judged weak, calls him the bearer of the poets' banner on the way to the Fire. It is not a sound hadith. It does show the place he held in later memory. On the traditional dating he died decades before the Prophetic mission, so the report is not about a man who refused Islam. Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi, in *Tabaqat Fuhul al-Shu'ara'*, puts him in the first class of pre-Islamic poets, with al-Nabigha al-Dhubyani, Zuhayr and al-A'sha.

## Legacy

The poetry of Imru' al-Qais, alongside the other *Mu'allaqat*, was among the primary sources cited by Sibawayh and the early grammarians to establish the rules of Arabic grammar and morphology, and exegetes drew on it to explain the rare words of the Qur'an. It thus became a reference text whose function exceeds its purely literary value, acquiring a linguistic and religious role.

## Caveats

His birth and death dates are approximate and rest on literary accounts, not on contemporary documents. As with pre-Islamic poetry in general, many lines attributed to him were transmitted orally for generations before being written down in the Abbasid period. The argument over that corpus, including D. S. Margoliouth in 1925 and Taha Husayn in 1926–1927, has not been closed. Husayn was an Egyptian critic drawing on Margoliouth, not an orientalist himself.
