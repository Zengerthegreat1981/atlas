# Imru' al-Qais

An Arab poet of the Jahiliyyah (pre-Islamic) era, traditionally regarded as **"the prince of pre-Islamic poets"** and the most famous of them all, and credited by the tradition with fixing the classical structure of the Arabic ode that poets followed for centuries after him. He belonged to the royal house of the tribe of **Kinda**, being the son of Hujr ibn al-Harith, one of the kings of Kinda. Literary accounts date him to the late fifth and early sixth centuries CE, in Najd.

## Biography and literary tradition

Traditional literary accounts say that Imru' al-Qais's life was transformed radically after his father Hujr was killed by the tribe of Banu Asad. He swore not to drink wine or wash from ritual impurity until he had avenged his father. He abandoned the life of pleasure for which he had been known in his youth and set out seeking support from the tribes, until he reached, according to the account, the court of the Byzantine Empire to seek help from the emperor. He died on his return journey near Ancyra (Ankara), and was given the epithet *dhu l-quruh* ("the one with sores") because of an illness that struck him during that journey. The details of this biography remain a late-recorded literary narrative, not a documented historical record in the modern sense.

## His Mu'allaqa

His *Mu'allaqa*, which opens with the famous line "Halt, let us weep at the memory of a beloved and a dwelling," is the founding model of the **classical pre-Islamic ode**: an opening lament over the abandoned encampment where the beloved once lived, followed by a description of amorous adventures and feats of chivalry, then an intensely detailed pictorial description of the horse, the night or nature, and sometimes a concluding maxim. This tripartite structure became a normative reference for later generations of poets. Early Arab critics said that Imru' al-Qais preceded the poets in things he invented, such as his pictorial description of horses, the night and clouds, in an innovative manner that no one had anticipated.

## Between life and legend

The literary books agree on a pivotal event in his life: the killing of his father Hujr by the Banu Asad. Abu al-Faraj al-Isfahani, in *al-Aghani*, relates that the news reached him while he was drinking, and he uttered his famous phrase, meaning that today is for wine and tomorrow is for the matter at hand. The accounts state that he sought help from the tribes and then from the emperor Justinian I, and that a poisoned robe was sent to him, by which he died in Ancyra. This carries a mythical significance more than a historical one; researchers hold that many of these reports were shaped by narrators in the Abbasid period to explain his poetry. As for his love for Unaiza and the story of Dara Juljul, which he recounts in the *Mu'allaqa*, they have become literary material in which truth cannot be separated from artifice.

## Imagery and simile

The strength of the *Mu'allaqa* lies in its imagery. In describing the horse, he says it is charging and retreating, advancing and turning back all at once, like a boulder of rock that the torrent has dislodged from a height, bringing opposing movements together in a single moment. In describing the night, he likens it to the waves of the sea, letting down its veils upon him with all kinds of cares to test him. Early critics counted these similes among his innovations, and poets after him continued to weave in his manner. These features are what led Ibn Sallam to place him in the first class of poets.

## Modern readings

Modern readings do not agree on how to understand the structure of the *Mu'allaqa*. Suzanne Stetkevych, in *The Mute Immortals Speak*, reads the opening lament and the love and chivalry sections together as a rite of passage by which the poet recovers life after loss. The oral-formulaic school, including Zwettler and Montgomery, holds that repeated formulations and commonly circulated meanings show that the poem is the product of an oral tradition rather than of individual invention. Alan Jones, in his edition and study, presented the text of the *Mu'allaqa* together with the commentaries of the early scholars. These studies draw attention to the fact that Imru' al-Qais is more a symbol of an artistic structure than a historically precise individual.

## Critical standing

According to a famous report in the literary books, the Prophet Muhammad is said to have described Imru' al-Qais as "the greatest of poets, and their leader into the Fire." The chain of transmission of this report is not sound, but it reflects the exceptional place he held in Arab literary consciousness despite not converting to Islam, since he died before the Prophetic mission. Classical Arab critics such as Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi, in *Tabaqat fuhul al-shu'ara'* (*The Classes of the Champion Poets*), placed him in the first class of pre-Islamic masters.

## Legacy

The poetry of Imru' al-Qais, alongside the other *Mu'allaqat*, was among the primary sources cited by Sibawayh and the early grammarians to establish the rules of Arabic grammar and morphology, and exegetes drew on it to explain the rare words of the Qur'an. It thus became a reference text whose function exceeds its purely literary value, acquiring a linguistic and religious role.

## Caveats

His birth and death dates are approximate and rest on traditional literary accounts rather than contemporary documents. As is the case with pre-Islamic poetry in general, the attribution of many verses to Imru' al-Qais is subject to scholarly doubt, because this heritage was transmitted orally for centuries before being written down in the Abbasid period. Critics and orientalists have debated this since the twentieth century (for example Taha Husayn, influenced by Margoliouth) without reaching a final resolution.
