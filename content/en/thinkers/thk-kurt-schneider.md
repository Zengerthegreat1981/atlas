# Kurt Schneider

A German psychiatrist (1887–1967), one of the leading figures of **phenomenological psychiatry** in the twentieth century. He is known for formulating the **first-rank symptoms** of schizophrenia, which profoundly influenced psychiatric diagnostic classifications until the late twentieth century.

## Training and Career

Schneider was born in Crailsheim in the Kingdom of Württemberg. He studied medicine in Berlin and Tübingen, received his medical doctorate in 1912, served in the First World War, and qualified as a university lecturer in psychiatry in Cologne in 1919. Early in his career he worked in Cologne, close to the philosopher Max Scheler and the school of Gustav Aschaffenburg, an environment that brought together phenomenological philosophy and psychiatry. He worked within the tradition of **Emil Kraepelin** and was strongly influenced by **Karl Jaspers**, who established the "phenomenological" method for describing the subjective experience of patients in his *General Psychopathology* (1913). Schneider applied this method systematically in investigating psychological symptoms, by recording the patient's own direct description of their experience instead of interpreting it theoretically. In 1931 he became head of the clinical department of the German Research Institute for Psychiatry in Munich, which Kraepelin had founded. He served as a military doctor during the Second World War. In 1946 he took up the chair of psychiatry and neurology at the University of Heidelberg, which he held until his retirement in 1955.

## Central Ideas

- **First-rank symptoms of schizophrenia**: in work published in 1939 and later in *Clinical Psychopathology*, Schneider identified eleven symptoms that he considered of strong (though not absolute) diagnostic significance for schizophrenia. Among them were **hearing voices that comment on the patient's actions or converse among themselves**, **thought broadcasting**, **thought withdrawal or insertion**, and **somatic passivity** (the patient's feeling that their body or actions are controlled from outside). The list also includes hearing thoughts spoken aloud, **delusional perception** (in which the patient gives a sudden abnormal meaning to an ordinary perception), and the feeling that emotions, drives and voluntary actions are imposed from outside.
- **Second-rank symptoms**: he also identified less diagnostically specific symptoms (such as visual hallucinations, perplexity, and mood changes associated with psychosis), which he classified as "second-rank." These were regarded as supportive of diagnosis rather than decisive.
- **Phenomenological method in psychological assessment**: Schneider insisted that the description of a psychological symptom must rest on an accurate description of the patient's subjective experience as the patient narrates it, not on the therapist's prior interpretation. This distinguished his school from the psychoanalytic approaches prevalent at the time. He regarded the first-rank symptoms as practical diagnostic signs rather than a theory explaining the disease, and he assumed that schizophrenia generally results from an organic cause not yet discovered.
- **Classification of "abnormal" personalities**: he also contributed to the development of an early classification of abnormal personality types (*Psychopathic Personalities*, 1923), which formed an early basis for later classifications of personality disorders. In it he defined the psychopathic personality as one whose holder suffers, or whose society suffers, because of its deviance. He enumerated types including the hyperthymic, depressive, labile, attention-seeking, explosive, emotionally cold and weak-willed.

## Research After 1950

Schneider summarized his work in *Clinical Psychopathology* (*Klinische Psychopathologie*; the first two editions, from 1946, were titled *Beiträge zur Psychiatrie*), which M. W. Hamilton translated into English in 1959. It became a guide for English-speaking physicians in psychiatric examination. In the book he drew the distinction between symptoms observed in any disorder and those regarded as key to diagnosis. He cautioned that the absence of first-rank symptoms does not rule out schizophrenia, in which case diagnosis rests on second-rank symptoms and expression. His influence continued through his students at Heidelberg, including Werner Janzarik, and the symptoms were adopted in the World Health Organization's studies of schizophrenia in the 1960s and 1970s.

## Impact and Criticism

First-rank symptoms profoundly influenced the official diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia, particularly the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) and the American Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-III and DSM-IV). For decades they were regarded as a prominent diagnostic indicator, and DSM-IV allowed a single such symptom (such as a voice keeping up a running commentary, or two or more voices conversing) to satisfy Criterion A for schizophrenia. Later studies, however, showed that these symptoms are not exclusive to schizophrenia, as they also appear in other psychotic disorders (such as schizoaffective disorder and even some psychotic mood disorders). Peralta and Cuesta reported in 1999 that their ability to distinguish schizophrenia from other psychoses is limited. Nordgaard, Arnfred, Handest and Parnas reassessed their diagnostic status in 2008, concluding that the studies neither reconfirm nor reject Schneider's claims and that DSM-IV and ICD-10 emphasized these symptoms more than the evidence supports; they also suggested that the symptoms may reflect a disturbance of the basic sense of self. DSM-5 (2013) accordingly dropped the special weight DSM-IV had given to them, although they remain useful as clinical descriptions.

## Limits

Schneider's approach belongs to a time when standardized operational criteria did not yet exist. It relied on the judgment of an expert physician to distinguish experiences such as "delusional perception," which evaluators do not always agree on with the same precision. His career in German psychiatry during the National Socialist period has also been examined by historians of the field. This entry is educational and not clinical advice.
