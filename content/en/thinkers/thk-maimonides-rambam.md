# Moses Maimonides: Philosopher and Historian

**Moses Maimonides** (1138–1204), known as **Rambam** (an acronym of *Rabbi Moshe ben Maimon*), represents the **meeting point** of **philosophy, religion and history** in the medieval Jewish tradition. He was born in **Islamic Córdoba**, moved to **Fez** and then to **Cairo**, where he became **head of the Jewish community of Egypt** and a physician to the Ayyubid sultan.

## The Philosophical Approach to History

Unlike other Jewish narrators, Rambam applied **Aristotelian logic** to historical reports:
- **Source criticism**: examining the chain of transmitters and verifying their reliability.
- **Logical causality**: not accepting a report that **contradicts the laws of nature** without strong evidence.
- **Precise synchronisation**: attempting to link Jewish chronologies with Greek and Egyptian ones.

## Major Works

### The Mishneh Torah

A **Jewish legal encyclopaedia** organising the whole of Jewish law. It also contains **important historical passages**:
- "The Laws of Kings and Wars", a discussion of legitimate kingship and genealogical succession.
- "The Laws of the Prophets", a discussion of the reliability of revelation and prophecy.

### The Guide for the Perplexed

A philosophical work addressed to educated Jews, which seeks to **reconcile faith and reason**. It includes **historical discussions** on the eternity of the world and of time.

## Influence on Jewish Thought

Rambam established **a tradition of rational criticism** in Jewish historical writing:
- Later Jewish philosophers (Gersonides and Spinoza) followed **his critical approach**.
- **Modern Jewish reform** drew on **his tradition of reinterpreting ancient reports**.

## The Andalusian and Maghrebi Context

Rambam was born in Córdoba in 1138, at the height of the flourishing of philosophy and the sciences in Islamic al-Andalus. However, the Almohad conquest of Córdoba in 1148 and their strict religious policy forced his family to emigrate. They moved to Fez in Morocco, where Rambam lived through critical years in which he was obliged, according to some disputed historical accounts, to conceal his religious identity for a time. This direct experience of religious persecution later shaped his stance on writing religious history. He wrote his famous *Iggeret Teiman* (*Epistle to Yemen*) as consolation to the Jews of Yemen, who faced similar persecution, invoking in it a long historical sequence of the trials of the Jewish Diaspora across the ages.

## His Relationship to Islamic Philosophy and the Prophetic Historiography Method

Rambam was deeply influenced by the method of **al-Farabi** and **Ibn Sina** in reconciling Greek philosophy with prophecy. He carried this influence into his treatment of the question of the "history of prophecy" in the *Guide for the Perplexed*, where he classified the degrees of prophecy in a graded rational scheme, influenced by the Muslim philosophers' explanation of revelation through the theory of intellectual emanation. This led him to read the history of the Biblical prophets in a rationalist manner that attempts to reconcile the religious text with Greco-Islamic philosophical logic.

## The Importance of Arabic in His Writings

Rambam wrote most of his major philosophical and legal works, including the *Guide for the Perplexed* itself, in Arabic (in Hebrew script, in what is known as Judeo-Arabic). The *Guide* was translated into Hebrew only by **Samuel ibn Tibbon**, roughly during the author's own lifetime. This reflects that Arabic was the language of thought and learning in his Judeo-Islamic cultural environment, rather than Hebrew, which remained largely confined to liturgical and legal texts such as the *Mishneh Torah*.

## His Later Influence and the Debate over His Legacy

Rambam's philosophical writings, especially the *Guide for the Perplexed*, provoked a sharp debate within Orthodox Jewish circles in southern France and Spain after his death. This became known as the **Maimonidean Controversy**, which lasted for centuries. Rabbis were divided between supporters of his philosophical rationalism and opponents who rejected it on the ground that it threatened simple popular faith. This controversy itself has become a historical subject in its own right, studied by historians of Jewish thought to understand the dynamics of the conflict between reason and text in religious societies in general. This conflict has a similar pattern in the history of Islamic religious thought itself, between philosophers and jurists, as in al-Ghazali's confrontation with the Peripatetic philosophers in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*.

## Cairo and His Medical and Communal Role

After settling in Cairo towards the end of his life, Rambam worked as a physician at the court of **Saladin** and then of his son **al-Afdal**, and held the office of "Nagid" (head of the Jews) in Egypt. This is an administrative and religious office representing the Jewish community before the Islamic ruling authorities. This dual position, as a physician at an Islamic court and as the senior rabbi of a Jewish community, gave his legal and historical writings direct knowledge of the mechanisms of Islamic rule and of its interactions with religious minorities. This background is reflected in his discussion of "The Laws of Kings and Wars" in the *Mishneh Torah*, and in his practical understanding of how religious minorities preserve their historical continuity within a state with a different religious majority.
