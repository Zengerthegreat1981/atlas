# Alexander Bickel

**Alexander Mordecai Bickel** (1924–1974) was an American constitutional law professor at Yale University. Born in Bucharest, Romania, he emigrated to the United States with his family in 1939. He clerked for Justice Felix Frankfurter in the Supreme Court's 1952 Term and joined the Yale Law School faculty in 1956. He is considered one of the most important theorists of American constitutional law in the mid-twentieth century, and the author of the best-known formulation of a theoretical dilemma that still haunts every discussion of judicial review.

## Formulating the 'counter-majoritarian difficulty'

In his central book *The Least Dangerous Branch: The Supreme Court at the Bar of Politics* (1962), a title borrowed from Hamilton's description of the judiciary in *The Federalist Papers*, Bickel coined a term that became one of the most widely used concepts in American constitutional jurisprudence: **'the counter-majoritarian difficulty'**. He points to a structural paradox at the heart of American constitutional democracy: when the Supreme Court, a body of nine justices who are not elected and are appointed for life, strikes down a law enacted by an elected Congress or a federal state, it exercises a power that **contradicts the basic democratic principle of majority rule**. Judicial review, however justified by the protection of constitutional rights, remains in essence the exercise of a power without an electoral source over decisions made by the representatives of the people.

## Passive virtues: the art of avoiding decision

Bickel did not call for abolishing judicial review. He sought instead to develop a framework that would reduce the severity of this difficulty through what he called **'passive virtues'**: a set of procedural and doctrinal tools (such as the political question doctrine, the rules of standing, and the technique of denying review without explanation, or denial of certiorari) that allow the Court to **avoid deciding** sensitive constitutional questions when society and political institutions are not yet ready for a definitive judicial resolution, and to await the maturing of political and moral consensus on the question through ordinary democratic means first. Bickel held that this considered judicial restraint (judicial prudence) is the most suitable way to reconcile the need for stable constitutional principles with respect for democratic sovereignty.

## His relation to Ely and Waldron

Bickel's diagnosis of the dilemma opened a broad field of later theoretical responses, all of which sought to resolve the tension he had formulated. **John Hart Ely** attempted in *Democracy and Distrust* (1980) to resolve the difficulty by confining the role of judicial review to reinforcing the representative democratic process itself (opening channels of political change and protecting minorities from systematic exclusion), rather than imposing particular substantive outcomes. **Jeremy Waldron** went further than both, rejecting even the attempt to resolve the difficulty by restricting the scope of judicial review and calling into question the democratic legitimacy of judicial review from the start. Nevertheless, Bickel remains the indispensable starting point for all of this debate, having formulated the problem with a sharpness that no one before him had matched in clarity of terminology.

## His position on civil rights cases

Bickel's position on the Supreme Court's decisions during the Warren Court era was marked by notable complexity. Most prominent among these decisions was *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954), which ended racial segregation in public schools. Bickel strongly supported the moral outcome of *Brown*, but remained methodologically cautious about some later judicial decisions that expanded the interpretation of constitutional rights more broadly. He argued that unrestrained judicial zeal, even in the service of just goals, may undermine the legitimacy of the judicial institution over the long term.

## Later work

In his later books, notably *The Supreme Court and the Idea of Progress* (1970) and *The Morality of Consent* (published posthumously in 1975), Bickel moved toward a more Burkean and sceptical view of judicial attempts to enforce abstract principle, stressing the limits of reason in government and the value of consent and gradual, tradition-bound change. He was also sharply critical of aspects of the Warren Court's later jurisprudence. Interpreters differ on how far this represents a break with his earlier position and how far a development of it.

## Criticism

Bickel was accused of making the 'passive virtues' a source of vague discretionary power that allows the Court to avoid decision whenever it wishes, without a clear standard for distinguishing cases in which restraint is required from those that call for immediate and decisive judicial intervention (such as *Brown* itself). Later critics also argued that his diagnosis of the dilemma implicitly presupposes a strong normative authority for parliamentary majority rule that was not itself given adequate philosophical justification within his work.

## His place in American constitutional jurisprudence

Bickel is usually mentioned alongside Felix Frankfurter (the justice for whom Bickel clerked, and with whom he shared a preference for restraint) as representatives of a current sometimes known as **'judicial restraint'** or 'judicial self-restraint', in contrast to a more activist current that sees the Supreme Court as an instrument for achieving social justice directly through bold constitutional interpretation. The term 'counter-majoritarian difficulty' remains to this day a mandatory reference point in any university course on the theory of judicial review in the United States. It is cited both by defenders of judicial review seeking to justify it despite this difficulty and by its critics, who build on it to undermine its legitimacy.
