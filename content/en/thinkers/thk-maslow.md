# Abraham Maslow

Abraham Harold Maslow (1908–1970) was an American psychologist, regarded as the spiritual father of the humanistic psychology movement, and the author of the "hierarchy of needs" and of the theories of self-actualization and peak experiences. He was born in Brooklyn, New York, to a Jewish family that had immigrated from Russia, and died in Menlo Park, California, after a heart attack. His contribution rests on shifting attention from the study of illness and incapacity, which predominated in the psychology of his time, to the study of mental health and the highest potentials of the human being.

## Formation

Maslow studied psychology at the University of Wisconsin, and received his doctorate in 1934 under Harry Harlow in research on dominance and sex in monkeys, a strictly behavioral environment. He then moved to Columbia University, where he worked with Edward Thorndike, and afterward taught at Brooklyn College from 1937 to 1951. There he was influenced by the European émigrés he met in New York, among them Alfred Adler, Erich Fromm and Karen Horney, and by the Gestalt psychologist Max Wertheimer and the anthropologist Ruth Benedict, whom he said were his two models in studying psychological maturity. In 1951 he moved to Brandeis University, where he remained until 1969. In 1938 he visited the Blackfoot (Siksika) reserve in Alberta, Canada, on a field trip that, by more than one account, influenced his thinking.

## The Hierarchy of Needs

The first conception appeared in his article "A Theory of Human Motivation" (Psychological Review, 1943), and he then elaborated it in his book *Motivation and Personality* (1954). The needs are arranged in graded levels: physiological (food and sleep), then safety, then belonging and love, then esteem, and at the top self-actualization. The idea is that the lower needs dominate consciousness when they are unmet, and that the emergence of higher needs depends, relatively speaking, on the satisfaction of those beneath them. In the 1954 edition he added the need to know and understand and the aesthetic needs. It is well known that the shape of the pyramid was not drawn by Maslow but was drawn later in management and marketing books, and that Maslow himself stressed that the order is not rigid and that people may sacrifice a lower need for a higher value.

## Self-Actualization and Peak Experiences

In his study of those he considered "psychologically healthy," among them, as he stated, Lincoln, Jefferson, Einstein and Eleanor Roosevelt, Maslow drew out traits of what he called self-actualization: clear perception of reality, spontaneity, independence, deep humanity, and the capacity for "peak experiences," moments of absorption, joy and a sense of meaning. In *Toward a Psychology of Being* (1962) he distinguished between "deficiency needs" (D-needs), which drive the filling of a lack, and "being needs" (B-needs), or "Being-values," such as beauty, truth and justice, which are sought for their own sake. In *Religions, Values, and Peak-Experiences* (1964) he wrote that the great religious experiences are in origin natural peak experiences. Late in life he added "self-transcendence" above self-actualization, an addition that Koltko-Rivera (2006) brought back into prominence.

## Selected Quotation

"What a man can be, he must be." — Maslow, "A Theory of Human Motivation" (1943); repeated in *Motivation and Personality* (1954).

## The Humanistic Movement

In the 1960s Maslow took part in founding the "American Association for Humanistic Psychology" (1961) and its journal, the *Journal of Humanistic Psychology*, with Anthony Sutich, and he called this current the "third force" in contrast with psychoanalysis and behaviorism. Rollo May was also associated with the movement, and Maslow's outlook is distinguished from his by its optimism: the tragic existential conflict that occupies May is met in Maslow by the assumption that the human being grows spontaneously when his needs are satisfied. Then, in early 1969, he took part in the appearance of the *Journal of Transpersonal Psychology*, which paved the way for what is called transpersonal psychology.

## Reception and Criticism

Maslow influenced management, education and self-help more than he influenced empirical research. The most important criticism directed at him is methodological: he chose his examples on the basis of his impressions, his sample is small and elite, and the criterion of "actualization" in it is circular. In 1976 Wahba and Bridwell reviewed the literature of empirical tests of the hierarchy theory in Organizational Behavior and Human Performance and found weak support for the strict gradation. In a worldwide study published in 2011 in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, Tay and Diener found that basic needs appear across cultures and are linked to well-being, but that their order is not binding as Maslow held. Kenrick and colleagues (2010) proposed a modified pyramid based on an evolutionary logic. Critics from the feminist current also held that "self-actualization" reflects an individualistic masculine vision. In recent years a reading has appeared holding that Maslow's ideas were influenced by what he learned among the Blackfoot without sufficient acknowledgment, a matter still under debate among researchers.
