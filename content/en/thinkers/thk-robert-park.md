# Robert Ezra Park

An American sociologist (1864–1944), a former journalist before becoming a professor at the University of Chicago at a relatively late age, and a principal founder (with Ernest Burgess) of the Chicago school of urban ecology.

## Overview

Park worked for about a decade as a newspaper reporter in several American cities, then studied philosophy and psychology at Harvard and in Germany, where he heard Georg Simmel lecture in Berlin and took his doctorate at Heidelberg in 1904. After working for the Congo Reform Association, he served as a publicist and assistant to Booker T. Washington at the Tuskegee Institute from 1905 to 1914, engaging directly with American race issues. He joined the University of Chicago in 1914 at about the age of fifty, a career path unusual by academic standards.

## Historical Context

Park's arrival in Chicago came at a moment when the city was undergoing an enormous demographic transformation: huge waves of European immigrants, the start of the "Great Migration" of Black Americans from the rural South to the northern industrial cities, and rapid industrial and urban expansion that created new social problems (crime, deviance, family breakdown) requiring new tools of sociological analysis not yet available in American academia at the time.

## Main Ideas and Method

Park developed "Human Ecology", borrowing concepts from plant ecology ("competition", "invasion", "succession", "dominance") to explain how population groups and economic activities are distributed geographically within the modern industrial city in an almost automatic, unplanned way, similar to the distribution of plant species within a natural ecosystem. In doing so he founded an entirely new research field in urban sociology, later developed by his colleague Ernest Burgess in his famous "concentric zones" model. Park called for studying the city in the field through a mix of direct observation, in-depth interviews and social statistics ("the city as a living social laboratory"), and he directed dozens of graduate students in Chicago toward direct field studies of the city's neighborhoods and marginal groups (gangs in Frederic Thrasher, hobos in Nels Anderson, ethnic neighborhoods and the Jewish ghetto in Louis Wirth), which together made up much of the Chicago school's field research. With Burgess he also wrote the textbook *Introduction to the Science of Sociology* (1921), and he proposed a "race relations cycle" (contact, competition, accommodation, assimilation). He also developed the concept of the "Marginal Man" in his pivotal essay "Human Migration and the Marginal Man" (1928) to describe the individual who lives between two cultures (the culture of his immigrant origin and that of the new host society) without full belonging to either. Such a person suffers continuing psychological and identity tension but gains in return a dual critical awareness of both cultures together. In formulating this concept he was influenced in part by his direct journalistic experience with race issues in America during his work with Booker T. Washington.

## Influence and Criticism

Park, together with Ernest Burgess, represented the founding nucleus of the Chicago research program that produced dozens of pioneering field studies on deviance, migration and urban crime throughout the 1920s and 1930s, and he directly influenced the formation of symbolic interactionism through his later students (among them Herbert Blumer, who coined the term itself). His ecological model was later criticized by researchers who held that it portrays the distribution of poverty and race within the city as an almost natural result of neutral ecological competition processes, ignoring the roles of power, discrimination and deliberate public policy (such as discrimination in mortgage lending) in producing this actual distribution. This criticism was later made by researchers in the sociology of race and the city who saw in the ecological approach an obscuring of the dimension of deliberate social conflict behind what appeared environmentally neutral on its face.

## Major Works and Representatives

Besides *The City* (1925), co-authored with Burgess (with contributions by Roderick McKenzie and Louis Wirth), which for decades remained the reference text in American urban sociology, Park supervised a whole series of field studies carried out by his students and published by the University of Chicago Press, from Frederic Thrasher's *The Gang* (1927) to Nels Anderson's *The Hobo* (1923) to Louis Wirth's *The Ghetto* (1928), together forming an influential body of field research in early American sociology. Park is widely regarded as a central figure in shaping the identity of the Chicago school, which blended field research with ecological theory, a legacy that shaped the Chicago department long after his retirement in 1933 and his death in 1944. The department itself had been founded in 1892 by Albion Small.
