# Penelope Brown

## Definition

Penelope Brown is an American linguistic anthropologist who worked for many years as a researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics in Nijmegen, the Netherlands. Together with Stephen C. Levinson, she set out the account of linguistic politeness that became a standard reference in pragmatics for studying how speakers manage social relationships through language.

## Historical context

In developing the theory, Brown drew on anthropological fieldwork on **Tzeltal** (a Mayan language spoken in the Mexican state of Chiapas). She studied address, politeness, apology and requesting in a local community, while Levinson drew on Tamil material from South India; English supplied a third comparison. Their joint essay appeared in 1978, then in book form in 1987 as *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage*, which has been cited very widely in pragmatics.

## Main ideas and method

The Brown and Levinson theory is built on borrowing the concept of "face" from the sociologist Erving Goffman, meaning the general public self-image that each individual seeks to maintain before others. They distinguished between two kinds of face: "positive face" (the individual's desire to be accepted, admired and to have their membership valued) and "negative face" (the desire not to be restricted, and for freedom of action without interference). On this basis, they formulated the concept of "face-threatening acts" (FTAs): speech acts that by their nature threaten the face of the addressee or the speaker (such as requests, commands, criticism and refusals). They proposed a set of graduated strategies that speakers use to mitigate this threat, ranging from indirect hinting to explicit performance without mitigation, passing through strategies of positive politeness (such as displaying closeness and solidarity) and negative politeness (such as reticence, advance apology and softening of imposition). Most importantly, they claimed that these strategies reflect common rational universal principles shared by humans, while allowing variation in the details of their execution according to language and culture, based on their comparison between English, South Indian Tamil and the Mayan language Tzeltal.

## Research on language acquisition and spatial cognition

Besides politeness theory, Brown published research on cross-cultural first-language acquisition, including how Tzeltal children come to use mitigation and politeness. That work widened a literature that had been centered largely on English. She also took part in comparative projects at the Max Planck Institute on how languages encode space and direction. Tzeltal, on her and her colleagues' description, makes heavy use of an absolute frame tied to local terrain (roughly uphill, downhill and across), rather than the relative left/right system familiar from English. That contrast fed a wider debate on language and spatial cognition, a contemporary form of the questions associated with Sapir and Whorf.

## Major works

Her most famous work is her co-authored book with Stephen Levinson, *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1987), alongside early field studies of Tzeltal published in specialized anthropological and linguistic journals, later research on children's acquisition of linguistic politeness strategies across diverse languages and cultures, which helped link politeness theory to the field of first language acquisition, and other important contributions to studies of language, space and cognition within the comparative projects of the Max Planck Institute.

## Continuing research career

Brown continued her research activity at the Max Planck Institute for decades afterward, taking part in large cross-cultural comparative projects that gather field data from many languages. She maintained her original methodological commitment to direct, long-term fieldwork rather than relying on purely desk-based theorizing, a hallmark of her work.

## Impact and critique

The Brown and Levinson theory had a wide influence on pragmatics and sociolinguistics and became a starting point for many studies of politeness in different languages. It has been criticized by researchers such as Yoshiko Matsumoto (1988) and Sachiko Ide (1989), who argued that a universal individual "face," understood in broadly Western terms, fits poorly with Japanese, where social relationships are managed through discernment and hierarchy. Helen Spencer-Oatey later proposed "rapport management" (2000) partly in response to such criticisms. The 1978/1987 model remains a standard reference, read alongside those critiques.
