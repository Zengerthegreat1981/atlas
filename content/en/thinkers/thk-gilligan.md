# Carol Gilligan

Carol Gilligan is an American psychologist and feminist thinker, born in New York on 28 November 1936. She studied at Swarthmore College, then at Radcliffe and Harvard University, where she earned her doctorate in psychology in 1964, and worked as a professor at Harvard and then at New York University. Her book *In a Different Voice* (1982) is regarded as the founding text of care ethics (Care Ethics), and it brought about a shift in moral psychology and in the feminist debate in philosophy.

## Context: Kohlberg and Moral Development

In the 1970s Gilligan worked with Lawrence Kohlberg at Harvard. Kohlberg had presented a theory of moral development consisting of six stages moving from obedience and punishment to universal ethical principles, and built his ladder on a longitudinal study of eighty-four boys. Gilligan observed that women on this scale fall at lower levels than men, for example at the third stage, which is defined by personal relationships and goodness. She concluded that the scale itself is the biased party, not those measured by it, because it was built on a sample of males alone and made reasoning by abstract principles the highest.

## In a Different Voice

In *In a Different Voice: Psychological Theory and Women's Development* (Harvard, 1982) she presented studies based on interviews with women facing real decisions such as abortion, and on children's responses to the "Heinz dilemma" that Kohlberg used, including the response of the girl Amy, who did not consider rights and property as the boy Jake did but rather relationships and what follows from the connection between people. Gilligan introduced the concept of the different voice: that there is a way of moral thinking that proceeds from relationship and responsibility rather than from right and rule, and that is absent from models of moral development. As she underscored in the opening pages of the book (1982):

> "The different voice I describe is characterized not by gender, but theme. Its association with women is an empirical observation, and it is primarily through the voices of women that I have traced its development."

Gilligan herself repeatedly stressed that this voice is distinguished by its theme and not by the sex of its bearer, that is, it is not women's ethics, a distinction that was lost in most of what was written about her.

## Justice Ethics and Care Ethics

She distinguished between justice ethics, which rests on individual rights, abstract principles, and impartiality, and care ethics, which rests on relationships, responsiveness to needs, and responsibility. She regarded the two voices as complementary, both of which should be present in every moral maturity. Her view contrasts with approaches that start from an abstract individual, holding instead that the moral self exists within a network of relationships and commitments from which it cannot be abstracted.

## Later Works

Afterward she continued the study with adolescent girls: in *Meeting at the Crossroads* (1992, with Lyn Mikel Brown) she described what she called the loss of voice in girls on entering adolescence, when they are asked to give up their knowledge of relationships for the sake of socially acceptable femininity. She devised a method of analyzing interviews called the Listening Guide, which listens to the voice several times to identify the multiple voices in a narrative. She wrote *The Birth of Pleasure* (2002), *Joining the Resistance* (2011), *The Deepening Darkness* (2009, with David Richards), and *Why Does Patriarchy Persist?* (2018, with Naomi Snider). She also wrote the novel *Kyra* (2008).

## Influence

Care ethics became an independent field in philosophy, developed by Nel Noddings in *Caring* (1984), Virginia Held in *The Ethics of Care* (2006), Joan Tronto in *Moral Boundaries* (1993), and Eva Kittay, and it influenced political philosophy and legal theory, as Robin West employed the idea of the different voice in the critique of legal theory. It also influenced health care ethics, nursing, moral education, and moral development programs in schools.

## Limits and Criticism

The different voice thesis has endured as a methodological critique of Kohlberg, but not as an empirical claim about a difference between the sexes: the major meta-analyses, the best known being Lawrence Walker's review (1984) and Jaffee and Hyde's analysis (2000), found that the difference between men and women in the pattern of moral reasoning is very small and much smaller than the variation within each sex. Further criticism was directed at her method: her samples are small and of limited social diversity, and her interview data were not published in a form that would permit full reanalysis. Feminists, among them Catharine MacKinnon, accused her of risking, in glorifying care ethics, the codification of the role historically imposed on women as their natural virtue. Seyla Benhabib criticized her dichotomy between the generalized other and the concrete other.
