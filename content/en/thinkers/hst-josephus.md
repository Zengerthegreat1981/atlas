# Josephus

Titus Flavius Josephus (born Yosef ben Matityahu, c. 37–c. 100 CE) was a Jewish priest, military commander, and historian who wrote in Greek under Roman imperial patronage. Having participated directly in the First Jewish-Roman War (66–73/74 CE) as a rebel general before surrendering and aligning with the Flavian dynasty, Josephus occupied a complex, liminal position between Jewish tradition and Roman imperial culture. His voluminous surviving works constitute the principal historical authority for Judea in the Second Temple period, the rise of the Herodian dynasty, and the catastrophic destruction of Jerusalem.

## Biographical and Historical Context

Born into an aristocratic priestly family in Jerusalem with matrilineal ties to the Hasmonean royal house, Josephus received a rigorous education in traditional Jewish law, devoting his youth to exploring the three major Jewish sects: the Pharisees, the Sadducees, and the Essenes. In 64 CE, he traveled to Rome on a diplomatic mission to secure the release of several imprisoned Jewish priests, an experience that gave him firsthand knowledge of Roman imperial power.

Upon the outbreak of the Jewish revolt against Rome in 66 CE, the Jerusalem authorities appointed Josephus military commander of Galilee. He fortified the region and defended the stronghold of Jotapata (Yodfat) against Roman legions led by Vespasian. Following a brutal 47-day siege in 67 CE, Jotapata fell. 

Captured after surviving a mutual suicide pact among his officers, Josephus was brought before Vespasian and boldly prophesied that the general would soon become emperor of Rome. When the prophecy was fulfilled during the civil wars of 69 CE (the Year of the Four Emperors), Vespasian freed him. 

Adopting the Flavian family name, Josephus accompanied Vespasian's son Titus to the siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE, serving as an interpreter, mediator, and eyewitness. Following the war, he relocated to Rome, where he received Roman citizenship, an imperial pension, and living quarters in Vespasian's former private residence, devoting the remainder of his life to writing history.

## Major Works

Josephus composed four major works in Greek:

1. **The Jewish War (*Bellum Judaicum*, 7 books, c. 75–79 CE)**: Originally composed in his native Aramaic for Jewish communities in Mesopotamia and subsequently rewritten in Greek for a broader imperial audience. It offers a detailed military chronicle of the revolt, from the pre-war factional turmoil under Roman procurators to the siege and destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem and the fall of Masada.
2. **Jewish Antiquities (*Antiquitates Judaicae*, 20 books, c. 93–94 CE)**: A monumental universal history of the Jewish people from creation down to the eve of the revolt in 66 CE. Writing for Gentile Greek and Roman readers, Josephus paraphrased the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek rhetorical and historiographical forms, arguing for the antiquity and philosophical dignity of Jewish law.
3. **Against Apion (*Contra Apionem*, 2 books)**: A polemical and apologetic defense of Judaism against Hellenistic anti-Jewish slanders, particularly those circulating in Alexandria, answering allegations about Jewish misanthropy and arguing for the antiquity of Jewish tradition relative to Greek culture.
4. **The Life (*Vita*)**: An autobiographical defense written late in life to counter charges by a rival Jewish historian, Justus of Tiberias, who accused Josephus of inciting the Galilean revolt against Rome.

## Historiographical Method and Theological Causation

Methodologically, Josephus modeled himself on Thucydides and Polybius, emphasizing autopsy, military expertise, and direct interrogation of participants. He had access to the official campaign diaries (*commentarii*) of Vespasian and Titus, which he claims to have consulted and which inform his detailed descriptions of Roman siege engines, legionary organization, and battlefield maneuvers.

Theologically, Josephus interpreted the destruction of Jerusalem through the lens of biblical covenantal theology combined with Greek conceptions of fortune (*tyche*). He argued that the Roman victory was not an indictment of Jewish religion, but rather the execution of divine judgment: God had abandoned His sanctuary because violent Jewish zealots had defiled the Temple with civil murder and lawlessness.

## Critical Assessment and Legacy

Josephus was long regarded with ambivalence within Jewish tradition as an apostate and collaborator. Conversely, his works were carefully preserved by early Christian scholars because of their detailed illumination of the New Testament historical background, his descriptions of John the Baptist, James the brother of Jesus, and the controversial passage known as the *Testimonium Flavianum* (Antiquities 18.63–64). 

Modern historical and archaeological research (such as excavations at Masada and Jerusalem) has confirmed his great value as our primary witness to the religious sects, political factions, and material culture of Greco-Roman Judea.
