# Abu Ya'qub al-Sakkaki

A rhetorician and grammarian from Khwarazm (Central Asia), who lived in the late Khwarazmian period and through the Mongol conquest of Central Asia. He is the author of **Miftah al-Ulum** (*The Key to the Sciences*), an encyclopedia that codified the sciences of the Arabic language (morphology, syntax, semantics, figurative expression and embellishment) into a single rigorous system. It transformed the open-ended arguments of Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani into a three-part scholastic classification (the science of meanings, the science of figurative expression, and the science of embellishment), which became the standard framework for teaching Arabic rhetoric up to the modern era.

## Biography

Al-Sakkaki was born in Khwarazm. Some later accounts state that he began his working life as a blacksmith or locksmith (hence the nisba "al-Sakkaki," which refers to metalwork such as making locks or metal fittings), and only turned to the pursuit of knowledge at a relatively late age. This story is usually transmitted as a biographical anecdote rather than as a report with a firmly established chain of transmission. He worked on grammar, rhetoric, logic and jurisprudence, and composed the *Miftah al-Ulum* as a comprehensive educational encyclopedia. He died in 626 AH (1229 CE) according to the commonly cited date, and the influence of his work subsequently centered on the Arab East (Syria and Egypt) through those who adopted and summarized his method, rather than through direct continuation in his homeland.

## Early Education and Named Teachers

Near-contemporary notices, including that of Yaqut al-Hamawi, praise al-Sakkaki as a jurist and theologian skilled in several sciences, but they say little about how he was trained. Wolfhart Heinrichs links this thin record to the Mongol disruption of Khwarazm. Later Eastern accounts say he worked as a smith and turned to study only around the age of thirty. Heinrichs treats that story as very likely transferred from the biography of the earlier Shafi'i jurist al-Kaffal al-Marwazi, who was also called a locksmith. Hanafi biographical notices name three Hanafi teachers: Sadid al-Din al-Khayyati, Mahmud ibn Ubayd Allah al-Harithi, and Muhammad ibn Abd al-Karim al-Turkistani. A recent study finds that scholarship agrees securely only on Shadid ibn Muhammad al-Khayyati. The same notices say he spoke with respect of a figure known only by the nisba al-Hatimi, whose identity is otherwise unknown. In the linguistic sciences, then, no teacher is securely identified.

## His Contributions

### *Miftah al-Ulum*: Codifying the Rhetorical Sciences

Al-Sakkaki divided the *Miftah al-Ulum* into three parts: morphology; syntax; and the sciences of meanings and figurative expression, followed by material on embellishment, definition and argument (logic), and prosody and rhyme. Later tradition, especially al-Qazwini's *Talkhis*, treated embellishment as a separate third science. In the rhetorical section he recast the ideas of al-Jurjani on composition (*nazm*) and imaginative representation (*takhyil*) in the form of definitions, chapters and rules that could be memorized and taught. These are: **the science of meanings** (matching speech to the situation: brevity and elaboration, inversion and rearrangement, separation and conjunction, and the types of declarative and imperative statements); **the science of figurative expression** (ways of expressing a single meaning in different forms: simile, metaphor, metonymy and figurative language); and, added to these by later scholars, **the science of embellishment** (verbal and semantic ornaments such as paronomasia and antithesis).

### From Theory to Curriculum

The essential difference between al-Sakkaki and al-Jurjani is that al-Jurjani wrote an open argumentative analysis that presents and discusses examples, while al-Sakkaki presented rhetoric in the form of **definitions, boundaries and divisions**, in the manner of the logical and jurisprudential books of his age, making it amenable to summarizing, commenting upon and teaching in schools. This methodological shift is what later enabled al-Khatib al-Qazwini to abridge the rhetorical section of the *Miftah al-Ulum* in the **Talkhis al-Miftah** (*The Summary of the Key*). That work became the canonical text around which commentaries and glosses in Arabic rhetoric revolved (such as the *al-Mutawwal* and *al-Mukhtasar* of al-Taftazani) until the twentieth century.

### His Relationship to Aristotelian Logic

Al-Sakkaki included a full section on logic in the *Miftah al-Ulum*, influenced by the wider movement of integrating Aristotelian logic into Islamic sciences, which developed between the fifth and seventh centuries AH (Ibn Sina, al-Ghazali and Fakhr al-Din al-Razi). This combination of traditional Arabic language sciences with logical method was unusually explicit in rhetorical works, and it gave the *Miftah al-Ulum* a rigorously "scientific" character that distinguished it from al-Jurjani's open argumentative style. The logical definition (defining a thing by its genus and differentia) became the essential tool for fixing the technical terms of rhetoric.

### His Division of the Science of Figurative Expression and Its Later Influence

Within the science of figurative expression, al-Sakkaki distinguished three main ways of expressing a single meaning: simile (stating the point of resemblance explicitly or implicitly between two terms); metaphor and figurative usage (using a word in other than its original meaning, on the basis of a relation together with an indicator that prevents the literal meaning from being intended); and metonymy (mentioning the necessary concomitant while intending the implied attribute). This three-part division, with its fine subdivisions (such as the division of metaphor into explicit and implicit, and of metonymy into metonymy of an attribute, of a qualified thing, or of a relation), passed in almost complete form into al-Qazwini's *Talkhis al-Miftah*, and from there into most later school textbooks of rhetoric up to the present day.

## Impact

The *Miftah al-Ulum* marks the turning point that brought Arabic rhetoric into its "age of codification," following the "age of foundation" represented by al-Jurjani roughly a century and a half earlier. Al-Sakkaki's three-part division (meanings, figurative expression, embellishment) became the framework through which Arabic rhetoric was traditionally taught at al-Azhar and across the other institutions of traditional Islamic education, and it remains present in contemporary curricula for teaching rhetoric. Modern scholars, however (such as Wolfhart Heinrichs and William Smyth), debate the extent to which this scholastic codification impoverished the richness of al-Jurjani's original analysis when it turned it into ready-made classifications.

## Limitation

Critics of al-Sakkaki's project note that he prioritized the logical form (definition and division) over the analytical flexibility that characterized al-Jurjani, so that concepts which had been tools for understanding literary texts became ends in themselves, to be memorized and later applied as templates to texts. Modern rhetoricians and literary critics have regarded this as a partial emptying of the theory of *nazm* of its original critical vitality. Furthermore, his incorporation of Aristotelian logic into the *Miftah al-Ulum* made the book difficult to read directly for non-specialists, which led al-Qazwini to abridge only its rhetorical section and leave the rest aside.

In addition, the absence of documented details about al-Sakkaki's life and early intellectual formation makes it difficult for researchers to connect his rigorous logical method to a clearly defined intellectual trajectory, unlike figures such as al-Jurjani or al-Shafi'i, for whom the intellectual and social context is relatively better documented. As a result, explaining the motives behind his methodological shift toward logical formulation based on definitions and divisions rests more on inference from the text of the *Miftah al-Ulum* itself than on a documented biographical record.

### Reception of the *Miftah al-Ulum* in the East after His Death

His book circulated, despite the upheavals of the Mongol conquest, through copies to the centers of learning in Syria and Egypt, where it found wide acceptance among scholars of the seventh and eighth centuries AH. Later scholars did not merely transmit its content; they reformulated and condensed it (as al-Qazwini did in the *Talkhis al-Miftah*). Commentaries and glosses then accumulated around it (such as the *al-Mutawwal* and *al-Mukhtasar* of Sa'd al-Din al-Taftazani in the eighth century AH), which together form what may be called the "*Miftah al-Ulum* system," extending across centuries of teaching and commentary. It is a paradigmatic example of how the traditional Islamic sciences developed through successive layers of text, commentary and gloss.

## Principal Works

- ***Miftah al-Ulum***: an encyclopedia of morphology, syntax, the sciences of meanings and figurative expression, with material on logic and prosody.

In the first sections of the *Miftah al-Ulum* he relied largely on earlier grammatical tradition more than on original contributions. The historical value of the book therefore lies chiefly in its innovative rhetorical section, in the way it organized the material, rather than in its grammatical content, which was already in circulation before him.

It should be noted that al-Sakkaki was not the first to attempt to organize the sciences of rhetoric into two or more parts. Partial attempts were made earlier, for example by Fakhr al-Din al-Razi in *Nihayat al-Ijaz*. But the *Miftah al-Ulum* was distinguished by its comprehensiveness and methodological rigor, to the extent that it surpassed its predecessors in circulation and school adoption, becoming in practice the "standard summary" against which other contemporary or later attempts were measured.
