# Joel Feinberg

**Joel Feinberg** (1926–2004) was an American philosopher. Born in Detroit, he taught at Brown, Princeton, the Rockefeller University and UCLA before settling at the University of Arizona in 1977. He is regarded as a leading philosopher of the twentieth century to devote a complete project to defining **the legitimate limits of criminalization**: when may the state at all criminalize a particular conduct and punish it?

## The major project: four volumes, four candidate principles

Feinberg's project culminates in his large four-volume work **The Moral Limits of the Criminal Law** (1984–1988). It examines rigorously **every possible principle** that might be used to justify criminalization, one principle at a time. It tests each through hypothetical examples and cases carefully designed to probe the principle's scope and consistency.

## Extending Mill's harm principle

Feinberg starts from the **harm principle** formulated by John Stuart Mill (the liberty of the individual may be restricted only to prevent harm to others), but subjects it to precise analytical dismantling. What counts as "harm" at all, in the sense that justifies criminalization? Feinberg distinguishes harm as a **setback to an interest** from mere injury or passing annoyance. He develops a precise criterion requiring that the act be **wrongful** as well as harmful, in order to satisfy the conditions of legitimate criminalization under the harm principle.

## The offense principle: the intermediate link

Feinberg adds an intermediate principle, the **offense principle**, to which he devotes the second volume, *Offense to Others*. Some conduct, such as public indecent exposure or disgusting acts in front of the public, may not cause real harm to others' interests in the strict sense. It may, however, cause them acute psychological offense and a deep sense of humiliation or disgust that is hard to avoid in public places. Feinberg holds that this may sometimes justify a very limited criminalization, such as regulating the places where certain conduct may be practiced rather than banning it outright. He sets strict criteria, however (the severity of the offense, the ease of avoiding it, and the motive of the perpetrator), to prevent the principle from being extended to justify criminalizing anything that bothers anyone.

## Rejecting legal moralism and legal paternalism

Feinberg devotes the third and fourth volumes to refuting two other common principles. The first is **legal paternalism** (the view that conduct may be criminalized merely because it harms the **actor himself**, such as not wearing a seatbelt). The second is **legal moralism** (the view that conduct may be criminalized because it is immoral in itself, even if it harms no one: "harmless wrongdoing"). Feinberg rejects hard paternalism (interference with competent choices for the actor's own good), though he accepts a "soft" paternalism that permits intervention to determine whether a choice is voluntary and informed. On legal moralism he argues that immorality as such is not a legitimate ground for criminalization, while leaving open a small class of "free-floating evils" that he discusses with caution. He holds that **harm to others**, in the broader sense he developed and together with the offense principle, is the main liberal criterion for justifying state coercion through the criminal law, and he acknowledges borderline cases that test the limits of this position, such as consent to self-injury.

## Action, responsibility and desert: the early foundation

The large project was preceded by an influential early work, **Doing and Deserving** (1970), in which Feinberg analyzed with analytical precision the basic concepts of the theory of criminal responsibility. What is the difference between **causation** and **responsibility**? When is an act "voluntary" in the sense sufficient to hold me accountable for it, and when is the chain of responsibility broken by the free will of another party? The volume includes his essays on responsibility, on justice and desert, and on the expressive function of punishment, which treat punishment as conveying social condemnation and not only as a deterrent device. This prepared his later project of defining the limits of criminalization, as an extension of the theory of individual responsibility he laid out in this early book.

## Impact and critique

Feinberg's project has become a standard reference in contemporary philosophy of criminal law. His analytical framework, which dismantles the four principles of criminalization and examines each separately, became a methodological model even for those who disagree with his final conclusions. Critics, particularly defenders of forms of legal moralism in the tradition of Patrick Devlin's earlier position, objected that the expanded "harm" criterion Feinberg proposes is loose enough to justify much of what he meant to exclude under the name of moralism. They also argued that the sharp separation between harm and pure immorality may be difficult to apply in practice in many cases where the two dimensions are intertwined. Nevertheless, his precise analytical method for dismantling principles of criminalization remains a model taught in philosophy of criminal law to this day.
