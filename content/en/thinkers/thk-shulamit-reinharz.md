# Shulamit Reinharz

Shulamit Reinharz (born 1946) is an American sociologist and a leading figure in feminist methodology and Jewish women's studies. She is a professor of sociology at Brandeis University, where she founded the Women's Studies Research Center and the Hadassah-Brandeis Institute (1997). Her best-known book, *Feminist Methods in Social Research* (1992), became a standard text on how feminist commitments shape the practice of social science, and her later work turned to the history of Jewish women. Although she is a sociologist, her work on fieldwork, reflexivity, and qualitative method is widely read in anthropology.

## Life and Career

Reinharz was born in Amsterdam in 1946, shortly after the Second World War, and grew up in the United States. She took her PhD in sociology at Brandeis University in 1977, taught at the University of Michigan, and then joined the Brandeis faculty, where she became Jacob Potofsky Professor of Sociology. She is married to the historian Jehuda Reinharz, who served as president of Brandeis University.

Her institutional work was as important as her writing. The Hadassah-Brandeis Institute, which she founded in 1997, is a research institute devoted to the study of Jews and gender, and the Women's Studies Research Center at Brandeis brought together scholars, artists, and activists working on women's issues.

## Key Works

- *On Becoming a Social Scientist: From Survey Research and Participant Observation to Experiential Analysis* (1979).
- *Feminist Methods in Social Research* (1992).
- *American Jewish Women and the Zionist Enterprise* (2005), edited with Mark A. Raider.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

*Feminist Methods in Social Research* (1992) is Reinharz's central contribution to methodology. Drawing on sociology, anthropology, and oral history, she argued that feminist research is not a single method but a perspective that informs every stage of inquiry: the questions asked, the relationship between researcher and researched, and the uses to which findings are put. She surveyed a wide range of methods, including interviewing, ethnography, survey research, oral history, content analysis, and action research, and argued that the researcher's own position, including gender, class, and personal history, should be made explicit rather than concealed behind a pretence of objectivity.

Her earlier book, *On Becoming a Social Scientist* (1979), explored the tension between detachment and engagement in research, treating the researcher's experience as part of the research process rather than as a contaminant.

In her later work, Reinharz turned to Jewish women's history, including the lives of Jewish women in the Zionist movement and in the American Jewish community, and argued that the study of Jews and the study of women should inform each other.

## Influence and Critique

*Feminist Methods in Social Research* shaped the training of many sociologists and anthropologists, and her institutional work at Brandeis helped establish Jewish women's studies as a recognised field.

Critics have raised questions that apply to feminist methodology more broadly. Some argue that emphasis on the researcher's standpoint can slide into relativism. Others note that the methods Reinharz describes are shared with other critical traditions, such as ethnography, oral history, and action research, so that what is distinctively feminist is the political and ethical commitment rather than the technique.
