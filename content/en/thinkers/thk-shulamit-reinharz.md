# Shulamit Reinharz

Shulamit Reinharz (born 1946) is an American sociologist and a leading figure in feminist methodology and Jewish women's studies. As the Jacob Potofsky Professor of Sociology at Brandeis University until her retirement in 2017, she founded the Hadassah-Brandeis Institute in 1997 and the Women's Studies Research Center in 2001, and she directed Brandeis's women's studies programme from 1991 to 2001. Her best-known book, *Feminist Methods in Social Research* (1992), became a standard text on how feminist commitments change the practice of social science, and her later work turned to the history of Jewish women and to the story of her own family in the Holocaust.

## Life and Career

Reinharz was born Shulamit Tirzah Rothschild on 17 June 1946 in Amsterdam, the Netherlands, thirteen months after the end of the Second World War, to parents who had survived the war in hiding. When she was one year old, her family emigrated to the United States, settling in Massachusetts, and she grew up in New Jersey. She studied at Barnard College, where she took her BA, and at Brandeis University, where she earned her MA in 1969 and her PhD in sociology in 1977. She taught at the University of Michigan from 1972 to 1982, and then at Brandeis from 1982 until her retirement in 2017, becoming a full professor in 1991 — at the time the only woman to hold that rank in sociology at the university. She is married to the historian Jehuda Reinharz, the former president of Brandeis University.

Her institutional work was as important as her writing. The Hadassah-Brandeis Institute, which she founded in 1997, was the first research institute devoted to the intersection of Jews and gender, and the Women's Studies Research Center, founded in 2001, brought together scholars, artists, and activists working on women's issues. By her retirement, the centre was home to some eighty women scholars.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

*Feminist Methods in Social Research* (1992) is Reinharz's central contribution to methodology. Drawing on sociology, anthropology, and oral history, she argued that feminist research is not a single method but a perspective that changes every stage of inquiry: the questions asked, the relationship between researcher and researched, and the uses to which findings are put. She surveyed the full range of feminist methods — survey research, ethnography, oral history, content analysis, action research — and she insisted that the researcher's own position, including gender, class, and personal history, must be made explicit rather than hidden behind a pretence of objectivity. The book became one of the most widely cited texts in feminist methodology and remains in print.

Her earlier book, *On Becoming a Social Scientist* (1979), had already explored the tension between detachment and engagement in fieldwork, arguing that the researcher's experience — including emotion and personal involvement — is data, not a contaminant. *Observing the Observer* (1997), written with Susan E. Chase, developed this reflexive turn, collecting accounts by researchers who had confronted their own identities in the field.

In her later work, Reinharz turned to Jewish women's history. She edited and wrote on the lives of Jewish women in the Zionist movement and in the American Jewish community, and she argued that the history of Jews and the history of women must be written together, since each has been distorted by the absence of the other. *Hiding in Holland* (2024), her most recent book, is a "piano duet" between her father's extensive writings about his years in hiding during the Nazi occupation and her own historical commentary — a work that is at once a Holocaust memoir, a family history, and a reflection on the ethics of writing about the dead.

## Influence and Critique

Reinharz's influence on feminist social science has been wide and lasting. *Feminist Methods in Social Research* shaped the training of a generation of sociologists and anthropologists, and her institutional work at Brandeis created a model for Jewish women's studies that has been imitated elsewhere. The Hadassah-Brandeis Institute and the Women's Studies Research Center made Brandeis a centre for the study of gender and Jewish life, and her advocacy helped to establish Jewish women's studies as a recognised field.

Critics have raised questions that apply to feminist methodology more broadly. Some have argued that the emphasis on the researcher's standpoint can slide into relativism, in which every perspective is equally valid and the claims of evidence are weakened. Others have noted that feminist methods, as Reinharz describes them, are often methods shared with other critical traditions — ethnography, oral history, action research — and that what is distinctively feminist is the political commitment rather than the technique. Reinharz herself acknowledged these tensions, insisting that feminist research is defined by its purposes — the understanding and transformation of women's lives — and not by any single method, and that rigour and commitment are compatible rather than opposed.
