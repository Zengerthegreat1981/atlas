# Otto Rank

Otto Rank (1884–1939) was an Austrian psychoanalyst and philosopher of culture, one of Sigmund Freud's closest collaborators for two decades before their theoretical split. His work on the trauma of birth, separation, the duality of anxiety, and the creative will is regarded as an important forerunner of themes later taken up in existential and humanistic psychotherapy.

## Life and Career

Rank was born Otto Rosenfeld in Vienna on 22 April 1884 into an impoverished Jewish family and later adopted the surname Rank. A largely self-educated young man, he impressed Freud with an early manuscript on the psychology of the artist (*Der Künstler*, 1907). Freud became his mentor and financed his university education at the University of Vienna, where Rank earned his doctorate in 1912, becoming one of the early non-medical psychoanalysts. From 1906 he served as the salaried secretary of the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society, and from 1919 he directed the International Psychoanalytic Publishing House (*Internationaler Psychoanalytischer Verlag*). His early works applied psychoanalytic concepts to myth, art, and literature, notably *The Myth of the Birth of the Hero* (1909), *The Incest Motif in Poetry and Legend* (1912), and *The Double* (*Der Doppelgänger*, 1914).

## The Break with Freud

In *The Trauma of Birth* (*Das Trauma der Geburt*, 1924), Rank argued that the physiological and psychological experience of birth and separation from the mother is the primary prototype of human anxiety, shifting the focus of therapy from Oedipal conflicts and unconscious sexual drives to pre-Oedipal maternal bonding and the existential shock of individuation. Together with Sándor Ferenczi, Rank also co-authored *The Development of Psychoanalysis* (1924), which advocated an active, emotionally experiential technique over intellectualized reconstructions of the past. These departures provoked fierce resistance from Freud and the inner circle of the Psychoanalytic Committee. By 1926 Rank broke formally with Freud, relocated to Paris, and later settled in the United States in 1935, teaching and lecturing widely until his death in New York on 31 October 1939.

## Theoretical Contributions and Existential Themes

Rank's mature post-Freudian philosophy, formulated in *Art and Artist* (1932) and *Will Therapy* (translated by Jessie Taft, 1936), articulated several concepts foundational to existential therapy:

- **The Creative Will (*der schöpferische Wille*)**: In contrast to Freud's view of the ego as largely passive and defensive against instinctual drives, Rank conceived the will as a conscious, positive, and integrative guiding force. For Rank, individuals are not merely reactive creatures driven by biological determinism; they actively create their own personalities and worldviews through deliberate acts of will.
- **Life Fear and Death Fear (*Lebensangst* and *Todesangst*)**: Rank identified two complementary existential anxieties. *Life fear* is the fear of living as an autonomous, separated individual—the anxiety of stepping into the unknown and bearing the burden of individuation. *Death fear* is the complementary fear of losing one's individuality—the terror of dissolution, absorption, and the obliteration of the unique self in the collective or in another person. Neurosis, in Rank's framework, represents a paralysis between these two poles, wherein the individual can neither embrace individuation nor surrender to connection.
- **Therapeutic Technique and End-Setting**: In clinical practice, Rank abandoned passive, open-ended free association in favor of an active focus on the immediate, "here-and-now" (*Hier und Jetzt*) relationship between therapist and patient. He introduced the practice of time-limited therapy with a fixed termination date, using the inevitable crisis of ending and separation as a therapeutic vehicle to mobilize the patient's will and confront their primary anxiety of autonomy.

## Legacy and Influence

Although Rank did not use the label "existentialist" during his lifetime, he influenced existential and humanistic psychology. Rollo May discussed Rank as a forerunner who emphasized will, creative decision, and separation anxiety. Carl Rogers acknowledged an influence of Rank's relational approach, transmitted through Jessie Taft and Frederick Allen at the Pennsylvania School of Social Work, on the development of client-centered therapy. Furthermore, cultural anthropologist Ernest Becker drew heavily on Rank's ideas in his Pulitzer Prize-winning work *The Denial of Death* (1973) into man's hunger for heroic immortality and the existential terror of mortality.
