# Kate Raworth

**Kate Raworth** is a British economist, a senior associate at the Environmental Change Institute of the University of Oxford and a visiting professor in practice at the London School of Economics. She is known for developing the "doughnut economics" model, which seeks to offer a new visual and conceptual framework for defining what she calls a "safe and just space" for human economic activity.

## From field work to economic theory

Raworth worked for several years on poverty and global development for the international charity Oxfam, where she published the 2012 discussion paper "A Safe and Just Space for Humanity" that first set out the doughnut, before moving to academic research at Oxford. This field background in global poverty gave her an early interest in questions of social justice, alongside her later interest in environmental limits. Together these formed the central duality of her later intellectual model.

## The doughnut model: two boundaries, not one

In her book *Doughnut Economics* (2017), Raworth presented a visual framework that combines two boundaries that appear to contradict each other. The first is an **inner social boundary**, representing the minimum necessary level of human well-being (food, water, education, health and gender equality) that should be guaranteed to every person. The second is an **outer environmental boundary**, drawing on the nine planetary boundaries identified by a team of Earth-system scientists led by Johan Rockström in 2009 (such as climate change, ocean acidification and loss of biodiversity), which should not be exceeded. The precise values of those boundaries are themselves debated among Earth-system scientists. The "doughnut" (the ring-shaped figure) lies in the safe and just space between these two boundaries. The ideal economic goal is therefore to remain within this space, rather than to maximize total growth without any ceiling.

## Critique of seven entrenched economic assumptions

Raworth built her book around a critique of seven traditional ways of economic thinking, which she argues are deeply rooted in university economics education despite not being valid. Among them is the assumption that the economy will inevitably grow in an upward curve without limit, in place of which she proposes the image of a stable doughnut. Another is the assumption that free markets balance themselves automatically, independent of the state and society. She proposes instead a more interconnected view of the economy as embedded within society and the environment, rather than separate from them.

## Her relation to the ecological economics tradition

Raworth explicitly builds on an older intellectual tradition in ecological economics established by Herman Daly and others concerning the physical limits of growth. However, she adds a more explicit social dimension through the inner boundary. This is an attempt to avoid a common criticism of the more abstract calls for degrowth: that they may ignore the need of billions of poor people for genuine economic growth that raises their basic standard of living first.

## Practical civic applications

The impact of Raworth's model went beyond academic circles into concrete policy applications. The city of Amsterdam adopted the "doughnut" as a guiding framework for its post-pandemic recovery policy in 2020, and other cities have used it as well. This makes Raworth a relatively unusual case of an economist whose ideas moved quickly from a book into applied public policy.

## Criticism of her model

Raworth's model has faced criticism from economists who argue that its attractive visual framework lacks the quantitative precision needed to turn it into detailed policies that can be directly applied. They see it as closer to an inspiring illustrative metaphor than to a rigorously analytical economic model in the precise mathematical sense that characterizes traditional economic models. Raworth herself partly acknowledges this criticism when she describes her work as an attempt to change the prevailing "mental picture" of the economy more than as a precise predictive model in the narrow technical sense.

## A balanced position on degrowth

Raworth describes herself as "agnostic" about growth, taking a position between the more radical "degrowth" current and the traditional defence of continuous growth. She holds that the right question is not "Should the economy grow or not?" but rather "Should growth in gross domestic product remain a goal in itself at all?". She proposes that rich societies in particular should abandon their "addiction to growth" as the sole measure of success, while poorer economies still need genuine growth to raise basic living standards. This distinction places her closer to Herman Daly's reconciling position than to the more abstract calls for comprehensive economic contraction.

## Broad media and educational impact

Raworth spread her ideas through public lectures, educational videos and wide media coverage that went beyond the specialist academic audience. She co-founded the "Doughnut Economics Action Lab" (DEAL), which provides practical tools for local communities and cities wishing to apply the model to their own contexts. This reflects an explicit commitment to translating the theoretical framework into adaptable local practice.
