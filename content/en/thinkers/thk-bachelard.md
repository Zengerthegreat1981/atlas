# Gaston Bachelard

A French philosopher (1884–1962), who taught physics and chemistry at Bar-sur-Aube before obtaining the *agrégation* in philosophy (1922), then his doctorate with *Essai sur la connaissance approchée* (1927), then became a professor at Dijon (1930–1940), and then held the chair in the history and philosophy of science at the Sorbonne (1940–1954). He had two distinct paths that are often confused: the epistemology of science, and the poetics of the imagination.

## What He Gave

**The first path: the epistemology of science.** In *Le Nouvel Esprit scientifique* (1934) he read the physics of relativity and quantum mechanics as an overturning of the Cartesian mind, and formulated **"phenomenotechnique"**: the phenomenon in modern science is not a given that is observed, but an effect produced by an apparatus. In *La Formation de l'esprit scientifique* (1938), whose subtitle reads literally "a contribution to a psychoanalysis of objective knowledge," he formulated the **"epistemological obstacle"** (*obstacle épistémologique*): what hinders science is not a lack of data but prior knowledge itself, namely first intuition, familiar images, and hasty generalisation. From this came the **"epistemological break"** (Bachelard's own term is *rupture épistémologique*; *coupure* is Althusser's later variant): the progress of science is not a continuous accumulation but a separation from prior knowledge. In *La Philosophie du Non* (1940) he presented the "epistemological profile" of a single concept (for example mass), showing how different philosophical attitudes coexist in one thinker, and in *Le Rationalisme appliqué* (1949) an "applied" rationalism formulated within scientific practice rather than before it.

**The second path: the poetics of the imagination.** *La Psychanalyse du feu* (1938), then *L'Eau et les rêves* (1942), *L'Air et les songes* (1943), *La Terre et les rêveries de la volonté* and *La Terre et les rêveries du repos* (both 1948), *La Poétique de l'espace* (1957), and *La Poétique de la rêverie* (1960). Here he reads literary images through the four elements (fire, water, air, earth), taking the image as a creation of the poetic imagination rather than as a symptom, and in *La Poétique de l'espace* he develops **"topoanalysis"** (*topo-analyse*): the analysis of intimate spaces (the house, the cellar, the attic, the corner, the shell, and the staircase) as structures of experience rather than decorative metaphors.

**Against Bergson.** In *La Dialectique de la durée* (1936) he opposed Bergson's conception of continuous duration, and proposed in its place a discontinuous duration built by rhythm rather than lived through flux.

## The Limit

In his books on the imagination Bachelard uses the term "psychoanalysis," and then **explicitly turns away from Freud**: he refuses to refer the image to a repressed content or a prior desire, and in the preface to *La Poétique de l'espace* (1957) he adopts a phenomenology of the imagination that takes the image in its present reality, not as an effect of a past cause. The term is shared but the method is different, and this is the point that the hasty conflation of Bachelard and Freud obscures.

As for the **epistemological break**, its history is one of reception: Georges Canguilhem, who succeeded him in the Sorbonne chair and at the Institute for the History of Science (1955), developed it in the direction of a history of biological concepts; then Louis Althusser used it in *Pour Marx* (1965, after Bachelard's death) to establish a "break" between the young Marx and Marx of *Capital*. From Canguilhem's line came Michel Foucault. The attribution of the Althusserian ideological thesis to Bachelard himself is a mistake: his intention was a break with the obstacles of common knowledge within science, not a break between science and ideology.

## His Place in the Current

He represents the "poetic" aspect of twentieth-century French philosophy, in contrast to the ontology of Sartre and Merleau-Ponty. He received the Grand Prix national des Lettres in 1961. His documented influence on the reading of space appears in Juhani Pallasmaa's *The Eyes of the Skin* (published 1996, after his death).

## Life and Context

Bachelard was born in Bar-sur-Aube to a modest family, worked as a clerk in the postal service before continuing his studies in science, and then served in the First World War. He came to philosophy from physics and chemistry, and therefore continued to hold that the philosopher must learn from the actual history of science rather than from abstract theory of knowledge. His daughter, Suzanne Bachelard, became a philosopher who worked on Husserl and the philosophy of science.

## Arabic and Critical Reception

Ghalib Halasa translated *The Poetics of Space* into Arabic, and it became one of the most used references in Arabic literary criticism on the novelistic space; critics such as Hassan Bahrawi relied on it in *Structure of the Novelistic Form*. Bachelard himself did not write on Arabic or Islamic literature, so the link is one of reception, not influence. In English-language studies, Mary Tiles, in *Bachelard: Science and Objectivity* (Cambridge University Press, 1984), offered a critical reading of his epistemology and showed that "applied rationalism" is neither naive realism nor idealism. His poetic thesis is criticised for generalising the experience of a European country house into the experience of space in general.
