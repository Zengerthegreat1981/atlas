# Franz Brentano

A German-Austrian philosopher and psychologist (1838–1917), the direct teacher of Edmund Husserl, and the thinker who revived the scholastic notion of intentionality in modern form, a notion that became central to later phenomenology.

## His Contribution

**Intentionality (1874).** In *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (Psychologie vom empirischen Standpunkte, 1874) he revived a scholastic-Aristotelian thesis: every mental phenomenon is distinguished by being directed toward an object, the "intentional inexistence" (intentionale Inexistenz) of the object within the act. Judgment, presentation and emotion are three classes that he distinguishes by the mode of their directedness and not by their content. This criterion is what he used to separate mental phenomena from physical phenomena.

**Inner perception, not inner observation.** He distinguished between *innere Wahrnehmung*, a consciousness accompanying every mental act of itself, which he held to be certain, and *innere Beobachtung*, the attempt to turn the ongoing act into an object of observation, which he held to be impossible. Hence his thesis that every conscious act includes a secondary consciousness of itself.

**Descriptive versus genetic psychology.** In his later lectures in Vienna he distinguished between "descriptive psychology" (Psychognosie), which isolates the structures of consciousness, and "genetic psychology," which investigates their physiological causes. It was precisely this distinction on which Husserl built his phenomenological project.

**Ethics.** In *Vom Ursprung sittlicher Erkenntnis* (1889) he traced moral judgment to a "correct love" toward its object, thus founding a type of moral objectivity through the structure of emotional acts.

## His Influences and Those He Influenced

His formation was Aristotelian: his dissertation *Von der mannigfachen Bedeutung des Seienden nach Aristoteles* (1862) and then *Die Psychologie des Aristoteles* (1867), and he studied in Berlin under Adolf Trendelenburg. His influence is wider than his books: his direct students were **Edmund Husserl**, **Carl Stumpf**, **Alexius Meinong**, **Christian von Ehrenfels**, **Kazimierz Twardowski**, **Anton Marty** and **Tomáš Masaryk**; from them came the Graz school, the Lwów-Warsaw school and the origin of Gestalt theory (Ehrenfels, "Gestalt qualities," 1890). **Sigmund Freud** attended his lectures in Vienna between 1874 and 1876 while a medical student.

## The Most Famous Dispute

Two paths of dispute. The first is doctrinal-institutional: he was ordained a Catholic priest in 1864, then rejected the dogma of papal infallibility (1870), and resigned from the priesthood and from his chair at Würzburg in 1873; after moving as a professor to Vienna in 1874 he lost the chair a second time in 1880 because he married, and he continued teaching as a private lecturer (Privatdozent) before moving to Florence and dying in Zurich in 1917. The second is theoretical: his student **Twardowski**, in *Zur Lehre vom Inhalt und Gegenstand der Vorstellungen* (1894), read the thesis of the "immanent" object as a confusion, and split content from object, a split from which Husserl and Meinong moved to theories of an object independent of the act, while the later Brentano went in the opposite direction ("thingism"/Reism), rejecting the existence of non-real entities.

## Judgment, Truth and Later Schools

Brentano holds that judgment is not a connection between two concepts as in traditional logic, but an independent mental act that affirms or denies the existence of something. The sentence "man is mortal" means for him the affirmation of the existence of a mortal man, and thus he reformulated the theory of the proposition in the form of "existential propositions." He linked truth to evidence (Evidenz): a true judgment is one apprehended with immediate clarity. In *The Four Phases of Philosophy* (1895) he spoke of historical cycles in which philosophy rises and then declines into skepticism, mysticism and fantastical theories, and saw German idealism as a model of decline. In the last part of his life he worked on the theory of "Reism," which holds that the truly existent are particular things alone, and so he denied the existence of universals and abstract meanings. Important works were published after his death, among them *Descriptive Psychology* and *Truth and Evidence* (1930).

## Echo and Reception

In his youth Martin Heidegger read Brentano's book on the manifold meanings of being in Aristotle, the reading that awakened in him the question of being, according to his own account. The concept of intentionality passed into analytic philosophy through Roderick Chisholm, who translated his texts and defended "Brentano's thesis" that intentionality is the mark of the mental, and into contemporary philosophy of mind through the debate over the natural irreducibility of intentionality.

## Limits

He was not a phenomenologist in the Husserlian sense: he did not develop the phenomenological reduction nor the return to pure consciousness, but was a philosophical psychologist from a renewed Aristotelian school who defines "empirical" in the sense of inner perception, not in the sense of laboratory measurement. He has no direct clinical influence: his connection to existential therapy passes through Husserl and then Heidegger, not through him.
