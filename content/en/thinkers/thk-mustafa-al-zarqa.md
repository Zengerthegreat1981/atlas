# Mustafa Ahmad al-Zarqa

A Syrian jurist, a prominent Islamic jurist of the twentieth century who combined traditional Shariah training with modern legal education (he also held a law degree). He is regarded as a leading figure in restating **legal maxims (qawa'id fiqhiyya)** within a modern theoretical framework that can be directly compared with the general theory of obligations in civil law.

## Life and context

Al-Zarqa was born in Aleppo to a family known for its religious scholarship; his father, Shaykh Ahmad al-Zarqa, was a well-known jurist of Aleppo. In his training he combined traditional Islamic legal study with the study of modern law, a rare combination in his generation, which gave his scholarly method a distinctly comparative character between Islamic jurisprudence and modern civil law. He taught at the University of Damascus for decades, took part in international jurisprudential bodies (such as the International Islamic Fiqh Academy), received the King Faisal International Prize for Islamic Studies (1984), and wrote on issues of the modern Islamic economy such as insurance and banking. He died in Amman in 1999.

## Central ideas

### General introduction to fiqh: a general theory of obligations from Islamic law

In his encyclopedic work **al-Madkhal al-Fiqhi al-'Amm** (General Introduction to Fiqh), al-Zarqa made a major methodical attempt to extract a 'general theory' from the scattered tradition of Islamic jurisprudence, which is historically dispersed across separate fiqh chapters (sales, leasing, agency, and so on), in the manner that the general theory of contract and obligation functions in modern civil law. He gathered chapters on rights, property, ownership, contract, obligation, and liability into a single theoretical framework, a project often compared with that of his contemporary Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri in codification, but approached from the side of classical fiqh.

### Commentary on legal maxims: from the Majalla to a modern theory

Al-Zarqa devoted particular attention to the field of **legal maxims**. His father, Ahmad al-Zarqa, wrote *Sharh al-Qawa'id al-Fiqhiyya*, a commentary on the maxims set out in the introduction to the Ottoman *Majallat al-Ahkam al-Adliyya* (the nineteenth-century codification of Hanafi civil law, whose maxims draw on works such as those of Ibn Nujaym and al-Suyuti); Mustafa edited and published it (Damascus, 1989) and gave the maxims a place in his own general theory of fiqh. This made his work a bridge between classical legal maxims and the needs of contemporary codification.

### Fatwa on modern economic questions: insurance as a case study

Al-Zarqa took part in contemporary Islamic jurisprudential debates on the legitimacy of commercial insurance and banking, which he analyzed through the classical theory of contract and *gharar* (uncertainty). He wrote a study of the insurance system, and his views are discussed in the literature alongside those of other jurists who took divergent positions.

### Education and dual formation

Much of al-Zarqa's ability to build a bridge between fiqh and modern law is attributed to his dual academic formation: a traditional Shariah training in Aleppo and a modern law degree in Damascus. This enabled him to address both audiences (traditional jurists and students of modern law) in a shared language.

## Impact and criticism

Al-Zarqa is regarded as a major bridge between classical Islamic jurisprudence and twentieth-century civil law, and his works are widely cited in the study of Islamic law and modern codification. Some scholars hold that his project in *al-Madkhal al-Fiqhi al-'Amm*, alongside that of al-Sanhuri, represents a leading attempt to show that Islamic jurisprudence already contains an implicit 'general theory' that can be extracted and modernly organized. Critics, however, point out that this extraction is itself a modern process of reconstruction, imposing on the older jurisprudential material classifications that were not necessarily present in the minds of the earlier jurists themselves.

### His institutional role in the Islamic Fiqh Academy

Al-Zarqa served on modern collective jurisprudential bodies, most notably the International Islamic Fiqh Academy of the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation. These institutions sought to replace individual traditional fatwa-giving with an institutional mechanism of collective ijtihad, in which jurists from different schools and countries discuss emerging issues before issuing a joint decision. This is a relatively recent institutional model that reflects the twentieth-century evolution of how fatwas are produced, moving away from isolated individual ijtihad.

## Civil codes and the insurance contract

After Syrian independence he argued for a civil code drawn from the several schools of fiqh, and he opposed the 1949 decision, under Husni al-Za'im, to replace the Ottoman Majalla with a code copied from the Egyptian civil code. Accounts credit him with a close part in Syria's family law and in keeping the civil law as near to fiqh as politics allowed. During the Syrian-Egyptian union he chaired a committee for a single family law for the United Arab Republic. In 1981 the Arab League placed him on a committee for a unified Arab civil law on a fiqh basis, and in Jordan he took part in work on the Jordanian civil code. These reports name the codes. They do not itemise articles.

On insurance he dissented from the Islamic Fiqh Council. He refused to treat commercial insurance as forbidden. As an organised way of spreading loss, he held it lawful in three forms: cover of goods, liability cover, and the contract called life insurance. He denied that it is gambling or usury, and he denied a sound line between "cooperative" and "commercial" insurance, since every such contract pools loss among those who share a risk. The case is set out in his book on the insurance contract.
