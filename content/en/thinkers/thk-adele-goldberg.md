# Adele E. Goldberg

American linguist and professor at Princeton University, and one of the leading theorists of contemporary **Construction Grammar**. She is the author of *Constructions* (1995), which formulated the most influential theoretical framework of this school and is among the most cited works in functional linguistics over the past three decades.

## Central Thesis

Goldberg proposes that **grammatical constructions themselves** (not only individual words) carry independent meaning. For example, the construction "subject + verb + object + second object" (as in "gave him a book") conveys a meaning of "transfer" even with verbs that do not contain this meaning in their lexical entry. A closely related well-known example is "Pat sneezed the napkin off the table": although "sneeze" does not mean "cause to move" in the lexicon, the caused-motion construction itself contributes that meaning. This runs counter to the generative assumption that meaning originates in lexical words and that grammatical rules are abstract and carry no meaning in themselves.

## Usage-Based Approach

Construction grammar intersects with usage-based grammar in rejecting a strict separation between lexicon and grammar, and in stressing that language acquisition proceeds through learning structural patterns from repeated exposure to actual usage. It does not rely on activating innate, pre-existing grammatical standards as generative theory does.

## Statistical Preemption: How Children Learn the Limits of Generalization

Among Goldberg's most important later contributions is her treatment of the "No Negative Evidence Problem." If constructions are productive and can be generalized (such as extending the "subject-verb-object-second object" construction to new verbs), how does a child learn that some verbs (such as "explain" in English) do not accept this generalization, despite their semantic similarity to verbs that do, without anyone explicitly telling the child that the form is wrong? Goldberg proposed the mechanism of "statistical preemption": when a child repeatedly hears a particular verb used exclusively in an alternative construction (for example, "explain to" in place of the double-object form) in contexts where the child would have expected the other construction, this repetition itself implicitly signals that the first construction is ruled out for that specific verb, without any direct correction from adults.

## Academic Training and Career

Goldberg completed her doctorate at the University of California, Berkeley, where she was a student of George Lakoff, one of the pioneers of cognitive linguistics; her work builds on the Berkeley tradition of Lakoff and Charles Fillmore, which rejects the generative separation between grammar and semantics. She later worked at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign before moving to Princeton University, where she continued to develop her experimental and computational research on language acquisition and the structure of grammatical arguments.

## Impact

*Constructions* (1995) became one of the most cited works in contemporary cognitive and functional linguistics, and founded an entire research current that combines theoretical analysis with empirical verification (through child language acquisition experiments and corpus studies). This distinguished her work from many other grammatical theories that were less concerned with direct behavioural testing of their claims.

## The "Giving" Example and the Expansion of the Construction Network

Goldberg illustrates her thesis through a detailed analysis of the ditransitive construction. This construction has a central meaning of "successful actual transfer of something from one party to another" (as in "gave her a flower"), but it expands gradually through metaphorical and figurative relations into related sub-meanings, such as "conditional future transfer" in "promised her a flower," or "negative transfer" (denial of transfer) in "refused her a flower." This network-like expansion from a central meaning to derived sub-meanings forms a key model in Goldberg's account of how grammatical-semantic knowledge is organized in the human mind. It is closer to the organization of natural perceptual categories (as in Eleanor Rosch's research on categorization) than to strict formal logical systems.

## Position in the Debate with Generative Theory

Throughout her career Goldberg has engaged in sustained critical dialogue with Chomskyan generative theses, especially the assumption of general grammatical rules abstracted from any meaning (rules separate from the lexicon of each verb). In contrast, she offered accumulating empirical evidence (from grammaticality judgment experiments, child language acquisition studies, and analyses of large linguistic corpora) supporting the hypothesis that a speaker's linguistic knowledge is a network of partially generalizable "constructions" closely tied to specific lexical examples. On this view it is not a system of purely abstract rules, as the standard generative theory assumes. This dialogue is a standard part of discussions of competing grammatical theories.

Her third book, *Explain Me This* (2019), deepens the understanding of the "partial productivity" of constructions: why certain generalizations are accepted on new verbs while other, superficially similar generalizations are rejected. She argues that speakers' generalizations are shaped by the statistical distribution of verbs across constructions, including preemption by competing constructions that express the same message.
