# Paul Samuelson

**Paul Anthony Samuelson** was an American economist regarded as one of the most influential figures in shaping modern academic economics through his rigorous mathematical style. He was the first American to receive the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, in 1970, and the author of *Economics*, a textbook used very widely for decades, in which he set out a formulation of the "neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis".

## From Harvard to MIT

Samuelson studied at the University of Chicago and then at Harvard (PhD 1941). In 1940 he joined the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), where he helped build one of the strongest economics departments in the world. He became a central reference for a whole generation of American economists who studied under him or were influenced by his rigorous mathematical approach to economic questions. His other contributions include the theory of revealed preference (1938), the theory of public goods (1954), the overlapping-generations model of money (1958), and, in international trade, the Stolper–Samuelson theorem and the factor-price equalisation result of the Heckscher–Ohlin–Samuelson model. With Robert Solow he also popularised the Phillips curve as a policy menu in 1960.

## Foundations of Economic Analysis: rigorous mathematical unification

In 1947 Samuelson published *Foundations of Economic Analysis*, based on the doctoral dissertation he prepared at Harvard. In it he systematically applied the tools of analytical mathematics (differential and integral calculus, and dynamic stability analysis) to several economic fields that had previously been treated with varied descriptive methods. This established a new methodological standard that became dominant in the discipline as a whole: explicit mathematical expression as a requirement of theoretical rigour.

## The neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis in a textbook

In 1948 Samuelson published his textbook *Economics*. It sold in very large numbers and was taught to generations of students. In this book Samuelson set out the formulation of the "neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis", in which Keynes's tools for managing aggregate demand in the short run apply when unemployment and excess productive capacity exist, while neoclassical principles of efficiency regain their force as the economy approaches full employment.

## The Cambridge capital controversy

Together with his colleague Robert Solow at MIT, Samuelson took part in a sharp academic debate known as the **Cambridge capital controversy** against economists at the British University of Cambridge (Joan Robinson and Piero Sraffa). The Cambridge economists criticized the neoclassical concept of capital as a single homogeneous quantity that can be measured independently of distribution prices. In his 1966 article "A Summing Up", Samuelson acknowledged the validity of some of the technical mathematical criticisms directed at it, but defended the continued usefulness of simplified neoclassical models for practical analytical purposes despite these theoretical complications.

## The Nobel Prize and institutional standing

Samuelson received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1970 "for the scientific work through which he has developed static and dynamic economic theory and actively contributed to raising the level of analysis in economic science". Until his death in 2009 he remained a prominent institutional reference in American economics. He also reached a wide public through his *Newsweek* columns.

## His relationship with monetarism and mutual critique

Samuelson engaged in an extended critical dialogue with Milton Friedman about the effectiveness of Keynesian fiscal policy versus monetarist monetary policy. Samuelson defended the continued usefulness of managing aggregate demand with fiscal tools alongside monetary ones. Friedman argued that the traditional Phillips curve offered no stable trade-off, a view that gained support after the stagflation of the 1970s. This disagreement was a major axis of twentieth-century macroeconomics.

## An extended academic family

Samuelson was the uncle of the economist Lawrence Summers, who later served as United States Treasury Secretary and as president of Harvard University. Lawrence's father, the economist Robert Summers, was Samuelson's brother (born Robert Samuelson; he took the surname Summers). Lawrence's mother, Anita Summers, was the sister of Kenneth Arrow.

## A press column that shaped public opinion

Samuelson wrote a regular economics column in *Newsweek* from 1966 for many years, alternating with Milton Friedman, who wrote a column expressing the opposing monetarist view. This arrangement let a general readership follow the dispute between Keynesian and monetarist views in relatively plain language. It made specialist academic debate part of the general economic conversation accessible to non-specialists in that era, at a time when specialist economic journalism was far less widespread than it is today. The columns appeared in the late 1960s and 1970s.
