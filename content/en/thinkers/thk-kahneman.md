# Daniel Kahneman

An Israeli-American cognitive psychologist, born in Tel Aviv in 1934 and died on 27 March 2024. He taught at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, the University of British Columbia, the University of California, Berkeley, and Princeton University, where he spent the later part of his career. He received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2002 (shared with Vernon Smith) for integrating insights from psychological research into economic science, especially concerning human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty; he was a psychologist, not an economist. His career began in the Israeli army, where he worked on personnel assessment systems; he then obtained his doctorate from the University of California, Berkeley in 1961. His **second** wife was the cognitive psychologist **Anne Treisman**, author of the feature integration theory of attention and a first-rank scientist in her own independent field, who died in 2018.

## What He Contributed

With his colleague Amos Tversky, Kahneman formed an unusual scientific partnership in the history of psychology, in the course of which they produced three interwoven lines of results:

- **Prospect Theory**: presented with Amos Tversky in 1979 as an empirical account of choice under risk, it showed that the value of a human decision is computed in terms of losses and gains rather than absolute value, and that people dislike a loss more strongly than they like an equivalent reward. The theory became a cornerstone of behavioral economics and redefined the very idea of "rationality." It was later developed into "Cumulative Prospect Theory" (1992) to extend its scope to multiple outcomes.

- **The research program on judgment under uncertainty (Heuristics and Biases)**: a series of experimental studies he began with Tversky and Paul Slovic in the 1970s and 1980s, which documented how people resort to "rules of thumb" (heuristics) such as availability, representativeness, and anchoring, and how these rules produce systematic, replicable biases. The edited volume *Judgment Under Uncertainty* (1982) became a standard reference for this research program.
- **Systems 1 and 2 in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*** (2011): a long-standing general presentation of a mental map of cognitive processing, in which he divided human cognition into "System 1" (fast, intuitive, automatic, emotional) and "System 2" (slow, analytical, effortful, conscious). The book unified decades of research in a single narrative accessible to the general reader, and so achieved wide circulation and became one of the most influential behavioral-science books of its generation.

## His Place in the Movement

Kahneman stands at the heart of what came to be known as the "cognitive bias revolution," and with Tversky and Thaler he is counted among the actual founders of behavioral economics. His work redrew the boundaries between psychology and economics, and opened the door to "economic psychology" as an independent academic discipline. Wider fields were also influenced by him: design, medicine, law, and public policy (applications of nudging).

His last book, *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* (2021, with Sibony and Sunstein), helped widen the scope of the problem from bias to unjustified variability in professional judgments, and with it proposed "decision hygiene" practices to reduce variability.

Kahneman died on 27 March 2024, leaving a founding research legacy in understanding the limits of human judgment.

## His Most Important Works

- *Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases* (1982, with Tversky and Slovic, editors): the first systematic compilation of research from the Heuristics and Biases program, and still an academic reference in cognitive psychology.
- *Choices, Values, and Frames* (2000, with Tversky): a development of the research program to include "decision framing" and its emotional dimensions.
- *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011): his best-known book for a general audience, and a comprehensive summary of a research program that lasted decades.
- *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* (2021, with Sibony and Sunstein): an expansion of the bias model to include random variability in professional judgments.

## His Impact in Practice

Kahneman's impact goes beyond the academy into three applied domains:

- **Medicine**: research on framing has informed discussion of how risks and treatment options are communicated to patients.
- **Law and institutions**: work on bias and noise has informed discussion of how professional judgments, such as sentencing and insurance underwriting, can be made more consistent.
- **Public policy**: the nudge idea developed by Thaler and Sunstein draws on dual-process accounts of the kind Kahneman popularized. This shift came to be known as "libertarian paternalism."

## Methodological Limits

**First: the two systems are a metaphor, not an anatomy.** Kahneman himself acknowledges this in the book: "System 1" and "System 2" are artificial characters for convenience, not two entities in the brain. Critics hold that the metaphor is read literally in educational and managerial circulation, and that mental processing is a continuous, graded phenomenon, not a binary one.

**Second: the replication crisis.** This is the heaviest limit, and Kahneman acknowledged it publicly while alive. The chapter on "priming" in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* was built on studies that did not withstand replication attempts, among them the famous study showing that reading words relating to old age slows the reader's walking. In 2012 Kahneman wrote an open letter to researchers in social priming warning them of "a train wreck looming," and in 2017 he acknowledged in a public comment that he had placed too much faith in underpowered studies in that chapter. This acknowledgment, from a scientist who had reached the standing he had, is one of the most honorable stances in the replication crisis, and the book may not be presented without mentioning it.

**Third: the limits of "bias."** **Gerd Gigerenzer** has raised a methodological objection since the 1990s: that much of what is called bias is an effect of **how the question is worded** rather than a flaw in the mind; if the same problem is presented in **natural frequencies** (one in a thousand) instead of percentages, a large part of the "errors" disappears. And that **heuristics are not deficiencies** but economical tools that work well in the environments in which they formed. The debate between the two schools has not been settled, and is among the most fertile in the field.

**Fourth: "nudge" applications.** Recent reviews have shown that the effect sizes of nudge interventions in public policy are **much smaller** than the early literature promised, and that part of the published effect is explained by publication bias.
