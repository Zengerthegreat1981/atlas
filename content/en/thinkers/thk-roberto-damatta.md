# Roberto DaMatta

A Brazilian anthropologist, born in Niterói in 1936. He studied history at the Federal Fluminense University and earned his doctorate in anthropology from Harvard University. He taught at the Federal Fluminense University, the Pontifical Catholic University of Rio de Janeiro and the National Museum of Brazil, and is Emeritus Professor at the University of Notre Dame in the United States. He is considered one of the most prominent theorists of **Brazilian national identity** from an anthropological perspective.

## Contributions

### "Carnivals, Rogues, and Heroes" (*Carnavais, Malandros e Heróis*, 1979)

His best-known work, and an influential anthropological attempt to read Brazil through **carnival** and the major popular celebrations (parades, religious processions, official holidays) as "distinctive windows" for understanding the deep structure of Brazilian society. In it he set out what he called **the Brazilian dilemma** (*o dilema brasileiro*): a structural tension between a highly hierarchical and authoritarian society on one hand (the official sphere, with "the street" and "the house" as spaces distinct in their values), and a deep cultural desire for equality, harmony and democracy, which erupts festively in carnival, where the daily hierarchies are temporarily suspended. He also analysed the figure of the *malandro* (the charming trickster) as a cultural model in Brazil that mediates between the official order and chaos.

### The "House" and "Street" Dichotomy (*A Casa e a Rua*, 1985)

He developed an analytical framework based on the distinction between two socio-moral spaces in Brazilian culture: the **house** (the space of intimate personal relations, loyalty and tolerance) and the **street** (the space of impersonal relations, formal law and conflict). He showed how the behaviour and moral expectations of Brazilians move between the two logics according to context, and how this tension explains several Brazilian social and political phenomena (such as the informal distinction before the law between "someone you know" and "the stranger").

### Structural Anthropology Applied to the "National Self"

DaMatta drew on the structural tools of Lévi-Strauss (binary oppositions, ritual as a symbolic system), but directed them toward the study of his own society rather than distant "primitive" communities, which made him a pioneer of what is known in the Brazilian context as "anthropology at home".

## Impact

- The "house/street" dichotomy is a standard analytical tool in studies of Brazilian national identity and in local political sociology.
- His analysis of carnival influenced cultural and folklore studies across Latin America.
- He is regarded as one of the leading figures of **modern Brazilian anthropology**, notably for turning ethnographic method on Brazilian society itself.

## Limitations

- His framework has sometimes been criticised for relying on broad cultural generalisations about the "Brazilian personality" that may overlook the vast regional, class and racial diversity within Brazil itself.
- Some of his readings of carnival as a social "outlet" that regulates the existing order have been criticised by left-wing researchers for underplaying the protest and conflictual character that some forms of popular carnival practice actually carry.

## "Do You Know Who You Are Talking To?"

Among DaMatta's best-known analyses is his essay in *Carnavais, Malandros e Heróis* on the Brazilian phrase "*Você sabe com quem está falando?*" ("Do you know who you are talking to?"). It is a phrase used by a person in a situation where a general rule applies to everyone, in order to remind the other party that his special status exempts him from that rule. DaMatta regarded it as a ritual of hierarchy: the speaker tries to turn an impersonal relationship in the street into a personal one governed by social rank, which reveals the duality between the written law that applies to all and the networks of loyalty and connections. In this way he showed how the logic of individual citizenship and the logic of the person who enjoys privilege coexist in Brazil.

## Method: Making the Familiar Strange

In *Relativizando: Uma Introdução à Antropologia Social* (1981), DaMatta formulated a twofold principle: the anthropologist must make the strange familiar by interpreting it, and must make the familiar strange by subjecting it to questioning. He applied the second part to his own society, treating carnival, football matches, religious processions and national holidays as rituals that represent the structure of relations. The first part was expressed in his fieldwork among the Apinayé people of central Brazil, which produced the book *Um Mundo Dividido: A Estrutura Social dos Índios Apinayé* (1976; English translation *A Divided World*, Harvard University Press, 1982), an analysis of their kinship system and dual organisation following Lévi-Strauss and David Maybury-Lewis, under whose supervision he wrote his thesis at Harvard.

## The Three-Races Myth and Racism

In an essay in *Relativizando* (1981), later developed in *O que Faz o Brasil, Brasil?* (1984), DaMatta discussed what he called the fable of the three races: the idea that Brazil was formed by a smooth fusion of Indigenous people, Africans and Portuguese. This image is associated with Gilberto Freyre's *Casa-Grande e Senzala* (1933; *The Masters and the Slaves*). He argued that this image performs an ideological function: it conceals racism by portraying society as integrated, and keeps hierarchy in place by assigning everyone to their place. Brazilian researchers who followed him discussed what is known as racial democracy.

## Critical Reflections

His argument about "house and street" is more accurate in describing the urban middle classes of Rio and São Paulo than in describing rural and indigenous societies, as critics have observed. And his interest in ritual presents a harmonious picture of a society that is full of class struggle and violence.
