# Stephen W. Porges

An American neuroscientist (born 1945), a Distinguished University Scientist at Indiana University and a professor of psychiatry at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. He is the founder of Polyvagal Theory, which redrew the map of the autonomic nervous system and opened a new horizon for understanding the relationship between the body, social safety and emotion.

## Formative context

Porges received his doctorate in psychology from Michigan State University in 1970 and specialized in physiological psychology, that is, the study of the relationship between autonomic nervous system activity and behavior. He worked for a long time in laboratories researching the heart and the autonomic nervous system, and at universities including Maryland, the University of Illinois at Chicago, Indiana and North Carolina, before he set about building a coherent theoretical model. His ideas crystallized gradually between the mid-1990s and the first decade of the 2000s, and he published his foundational book *The Polyvagal Theory* in 2011, followed by *Polyvagal Safety* (2021).

In his work Porges sought to go beyond the classical view of the autonomic nervous system as merely dual (sympathetic / parasympathetic), showing that the vagus nerve itself has two distinct evolutionary levels that regulate emotional and social responses in different ways.

## What he contributed

- **Polyvagal Theory**: a thesis holding that the mammalian autonomic nervous system has three evolutionarily distinct states: (1) the Ventral Vagal Complex, associated with the face, expression and social communication; (2) the Sympathetic system, associated with arousal, movement and fight or flight; and (3) the Dorsal Vagal system, associated with immobilization, collapse and numbness.
- **The social-physiological concept of "safety"**: safety is not a cognitive decision but a neural state in which the ventral vagal system can distinguish safe cues from others. Porges distinguishes between "perceived safety" and "neural safety."
- **Neuroception**: a term specific to Porges describing the process by which the autonomic nervous system detects, without awareness, whether a situation is safe, dangerous or life-threatening. This process precedes conscious cognitive processing.
- **"Safe-and-Social Engagement"**: the individual's ability to use the muscles of the face, the middle ear and the voice to regulate relationships with others, in the state of the ventral vagal system. In Porges's view this is the "healthy" state.
- **Physiological measures of social interaction**: development of tools for measuring heart rate variability (RSA) and its parameters in assessing emotion regulation, which found their way into research on attachment and relationships.

## Timeline of the theory

Porges announced his hypothesis in his presidential address to the Society for Psychophysiological Research in 1994 and published it in *Psychophysiology* in 1995 under a title concerned with orienting in a defensive world. From there it developed in three phases. In the first he focused on the "vagal brake," that is, the role played by the myelinated vagus nerve in inhibiting heart rate and releasing it rapidly, and he linked it to the amplitude of respiratory sinus arrhythmia as an index of cardiac regulation. In the second he broadened the explanation to include the link of this branch with the muscles of the face, larynx and middle ear within what he called the "social engagement system," and he investigated this in studies of infants and attachment. In the third he coined the term neuroception in an article published in 2004 in the journal *Zero to Three*, and then published a comprehensive summary of the idea in *Biological Psychology* in 2007.

## From the laboratory to the clinic

Practitioners carried the theory from the field of physiological research into the clinic. Porges designed the "Safe and Sound Protocol," an auditory intervention based on filtered music with frequencies close to the range of the human voice, intended to train the middle ear to pick up the cues of social voice, and it is used with autistic children and trauma survivors. In 2018 the volume *Clinical Applications of the Polyvagal Theory* appeared, which he edited with Deb Dana and in which he gathered practitioners from different currents who applied the theory in therapy. There is a gap between the published evidence for these protocols and their wide use, as most of the evidence is preliminary and based on small samples.

## The physiological controversy

The sharpest scientific criticism of the theory came from researchers in cardiac and respiratory physiology. Peter Grossman and Edwin Taylor published, in the same issue of *Biological Psychology* in 2007, a critical paper in which they argued that respiratory sinus arrhythmia does not measure cardiac vagal tone with the precision the theory assumes, and that the claim that the myelinated vagus is specific to mammals conflicts with what is known about some reptiles and fish. Grossman returned in 2023 to review the five premises of the theory, considering that they do not withstand the comparative data. Supporters of the theory respond that Porges describes it as a generative framework that produces testable hypotheses, and that its spread in therapy does not depend on the correctness of every anatomical detail, a response that critics see as conflating metaphorical value with scientific proof.

## His place in the tradition

Polyvagal Theory has become one of the most influential neurological frameworks in psychotherapy over the last two decades, especially in the treatment of trauma and attachment. It directly influenced the work of Bessel van der Kolk (in his book *The Body Keeps the Score*) and body-oriented therapies such as Somatic Experiencing, Sensorimotor Psychotherapy and relational therapy.

The theory has faced criticism from neuroscience researchers, some of whom believe that the distinction between the two vagal systems is less sharp than Porges describes, and that direct evidence for some of its conclusions remains limited. Porges and his collaborators respond that the theory has sometimes been misunderstood, and that it was framed as a "testable hypothesis" more than as a final truth.

## Major works

- *The Polyvagal Theory: Neurophysiological Foundations of Emotions, Attachment, Communication, and Self-regulation* (2011). The foundational book.
- *The Pocket Guide to the Polyvagal Theory: The Transformative Power of Feeling Safe* (2017). An accessible text.
- *Polyvagal Safety: Attachment, Communication, and Self-regulation* (2021). An application of the idea of safety to relationships and self-regulation.
- *Clinical Applications of the Polyvagal Theory: The Emergence of Polyvagal-Informed Therapies* (2018, edited with Deb Dana).
