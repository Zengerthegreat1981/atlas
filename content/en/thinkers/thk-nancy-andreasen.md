# Nancy Andreasen

An American psychiatrist and researcher, born in 1938 in Lincoln, Nebraska. She holds a doctorate in English literature (specializing in the Renaissance) before turning to the study of medicine and specializing in psychiatry. She holds the Andrew H. Woods Chair of Psychiatry at the University of Iowa College of Medicine, and is one of the world's leading researchers on the neurobiological basis of schizophrenia.

## From Literature to Psychiatry

Andreasen studied English literature at the University of Nebraska, specializing in Renaissance poetry and literature, and taught it at the University of Iowa before deciding in the late 1960s to study medicine, earning her medical degree from Iowa in 1970. This literary dimension remained present in her work: she was concerned with describing patients' subjective experiences precisely, and with the relationship between disordered language and disordered thought. In 1984 she published *The Broken Brain*, which called for viewing mental illnesses as brain diseases and helped change the outlook of the public and of physicians after decades in which analytic explanations had prevailed. In the 1980s she also moved from descriptive phenomenology toward quantitative tools and imaging.

## Her Contributions

### Scales for the Positive and Negative Symptoms of Schizophrenia

In the early 1980s Andreasen developed two widely used instruments for measuring the symptoms of schizophrenia: the **Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS)** in 1983, and the **Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS)** in 1984. This established the clinical distinction between "positive symptoms" (such as hallucinations and delusions) and "negative symptoms" (such as flattened affect and social withdrawal), a distinction that remains a cornerstone of diagnosis and clinical research on schizophrenia. Earlier, in 1979, she had published the Scale for the Assessment of Thought, Language, and Communication to measure formal thought disorder. Together these instruments completed a toolkit for describing the two main dimensions of schizophrenia, psychotic symptoms and negative symptoms, along with a third dimension, disorganization of thought.

### Pioneering Neuroimaging in Schizophrenia

She was among the first to use computed tomography, and then **magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)**, in a quantitative manner to study brain structure in patients with schizophrenia. In 1986 she published a study in the *Archives of General Psychiatry* on the frontal lobe in schizophrenia using MRI, and in 1990 a broader study with a larger sample, reporting reduced brain and frontal lobe volume and enlarged ventricles in patients as a group. This research helped establish the understanding of schizophrenia as a **neurodevelopmental disorder** rather than merely an acute chemical imbalance. Her subsequent studies used positron emission tomography in patients who had not received treatment, and she introduced the concept of "cognitive dysmetria" to explain the impaired coordination between the prefrontal cortex, thalamus, and cerebellum in schizophrenia, meaning that the circuit disruption affects the coordination of mental operations more than any single region. She followed patients in longitudinal studies to determine whether tissue loss progresses over time and how medications affect it.

### Research on Creativity and the Brain

In 1987 she published in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* a study of writers from the Iowa Writers' Workshop, reporting higher rates of mood disorders, particularly bipolar disorder, among them and their first-degree relatives compared with a control group. In a later phase she extended her research to the relationship between **creativity** and mental illness, using neuroimaging techniques to study the cognitive performance of writers, artists, and scientists, and discussed this work in her book *The Creating Brain: The Neuroscience of Genius* (2005). She suggested that creativity is related to flexible, free-associative thinking involving the association cortices, rather than to the illness itself.

### Scientific Honors and Editorship

Andreasen received the **National Medal of Science** of the United States (2000) in recognition of her contributions to research on schizophrenia and neuroimaging, and is a member of the National Academy of Medicine. She served as editor of the *American Journal of Psychiatry* between 1993 and 2005, and took part in the work on the fourth edition of the diagnostic manual (DSM-IV), including its schizophrenia work group.

## Impact

Andreasen's research changed the way schizophrenia is understood in contemporary psychiatry, moving from a model based on clinical symptoms alone to one that integrates neuroimaging and brain development. Her diagnostic instruments (SANS and SAPS) remain widely used in clinical research around the world.

## Limits and Critique

The Iowa writers study has been criticized for its small sample, for the fact that the investigator was also the interviewer without blinding the groups, for not revealing the direction of causality between creativity and illness, and for not including scientific or musical creativity. Some researchers argue that the SANS and SAPS overlap in some items, and that the structure of the negative symptoms later produced more recent scales that distinguish between lack of motivation and lack of expression. The hypothesis of "cognitive dysmetria" remains an explanatory theory that is difficult to test precisely and does not account for the wide variability among patients. This entry is educational and not clinical advice. Moreover, differences in brain volume between groups do not mean there is a diagnostic marker usable for an individual, and diagnoses of schizophrenia still rest on clinical examination rather than on imaging.
