# Nancy Andreasen

An American psychiatrist and neuroscientist, born 11 November 1938 in Lincoln, Nebraska. She holds the Andrew H. Woods Chair of Psychiatry at the University of Iowa Carver College of Medicine. Her research concerns the clinical description and brain imaging of schizophrenia.

## From Literature to Psychiatry

Andreasen took a B.A. at the University of Nebraska (1958), an M.A. at Radcliffe College (1959), and a Ph.D. in English at Nebraska (1963), with a specialty in the poetry of John Donne. She published *John Donne: Conservative Revolutionary* (1967) and taught Renaissance literature at the University of Iowa before entering medicine there. She received her M.D. in 1970 and completed psychiatry residency in 1973. The literary training remained visible in her attention to patients' own descriptions and to disordered language. In 1979 she published the Scale for the Assessment of Thought, Language, and Communication. In 1984 *The Broken Brain: The Biological Revolution in Psychiatry* argued that major mental illnesses should be studied as brain diseases, at a time when psychodynamic accounts still dominated much teaching. Through the 1980s she moved from descriptive phenomenology to quantitative scales and imaging.

## Her Contributions

### Scales for the Positive and Negative Symptoms of Schizophrenia

In a 1982 paper she set out a definition and reliability study of negative symptoms, issued as the **Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS)**; the **Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS)** followed in 1984. The scales made operational a distinction, already discussed by earlier clinicians, between "positive symptoms" (hallucinations, delusions) and "negative symptoms" (flattened affect, poverty of speech, avolition, social withdrawal). With the 1979 thought-disorder scale they gave research a way to score psychotic symptoms, negative symptoms, and disorganization separately. Later factor studies found that some SANS items overlap and that negative symptoms themselves split into diminished expression and diminished motivation, which newer scales try to separate.

### Pioneering Neuroimaging in Schizophrenia

She was among the first to use computed tomography, and then **magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)**, in a quantitative manner to study brain structure in patients with schizophrenia. In 1986 she published a study in the *Archives of General Psychiatry* on the frontal lobe in schizophrenia using MRI, and in 1990 a second, larger series in the same journal, which did not replicate the earlier findings of smaller frontal, cerebral and cranial size but did find enlarged ventricles in patients as a group, mostly in men. This research helped establish the understanding of schizophrenia as a **neurodevelopmental disorder** rather than merely an acute chemical imbalance. Her subsequent studies used positron emission tomography in patients who had not received treatment, and she introduced the concept of "cognitive dysmetria" to explain the impaired coordination between the prefrontal cortex, thalamus, and cerebellum in schizophrenia, meaning that the circuit disruption affects the coordination of mental operations more than any single region. She followed patients in longitudinal studies to determine whether tissue loss progresses over time and how medications affect it.

### Research on Creativity and the Brain

In 1987 she published in the *American Journal of Psychiatry* a study of writers from the Iowa Writers' Workshop, reporting higher rates of mood disorders, particularly bipolar disorder, among them and their first-degree relatives compared with a control group. In a later phase she extended her research to the relationship between **creativity** and mental illness, using neuroimaging techniques to study the cognitive performance of writers, artists, and scientists, and discussed this work in her book *The Creating Brain: The Neuroscience of Genius* (2005). She suggested that creativity is related to flexible, free-associative thinking involving the association cortices, rather than to the illness itself.

### Scientific Honors and Editorship

Andreasen received the United States **National Medal of Science** in 2000 for work joining behavioral research, neuroscience, and imaging. She is a member of the National Academy of Medicine. She was editor-in-chief of the *American Journal of Psychiatry* from 1993 through 2005 and took part in the DSM-IV schizophrenia work group. A later general account of imaging and plasticity appeared as *Brave New Brain* (2001).

## Impact

Andreasen's research changed the way schizophrenia is understood in contemporary psychiatry, moving from a model based on clinical symptoms alone to one that integrates neuroimaging and brain development. Her diagnostic instruments (SANS and SAPS) remain widely used in clinical research around the world.

## Limits and Critique

The Iowa writers study has been criticized for its small sample, for the fact that the investigator was also the interviewer without blinding the groups, for not revealing the direction of causality between creativity and illness, and for not including scientific or musical creativity. Some researchers argue that the SANS and SAPS overlap in some items, and that the structure of the negative symptoms later produced more recent scales that distinguish between lack of motivation and lack of expression. The hypothesis of "cognitive dysmetria" remains an explanatory theory that is difficult to test precisely and does not account for the wide variability among patients. This entry is educational and not clinical advice. Moreover, differences in brain volume between groups do not mean there is a diagnostic marker usable for an individual, and diagnoses of schizophrenia still rest on clinical examination rather than on imaging.
