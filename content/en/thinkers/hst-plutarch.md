# Plutarch

Lucius Mestrius Plutarchus (c. 46–c. 120 CE), known universally as Plutarch, was a Greek biographer, Middle Platonist philosopher, and priest of Apollo at Delphi. He developed biography (*bios*) into a sophisticated genre distinct from conventional annalistic historiography. 

His monumental *Parallel Lives* (*Bioi Paralleloi*)—pairing prominent Greek and Roman statesmen and commanders to illuminate character, moral choice, and statecraft—became one of the most influential works of historical literature in Western civilization, shaping classical humanism, Renaissance drama, and Enlightenment political thought.

## Biographical and Cultural Context

Born into an affluent, cultured family in Chaeronea in Boeotia, Plutarch studied mathematics and Middle Platonic philosophy in Athens under Ammonius. He was deeply embedded in the Mediterranean intellectual networks of the Second Sophistic, traveling to Egypt, Asia Minor, and Rome, where he lectured on ethics and formed close friendships with prominent Roman senators. 

Through the sponsorship of his friend Lucius Mestrius Florus, he gained Roman citizenship, adopting the *gentilicium* Mestrius, and according to the Suda, later received honorary consular rank (*ornamenta consularia*) from Trajan or Hadrian.

Despite his imperial connections, Plutarch maintained his primary residence in his small ancestral hometown of Chaeronea, serving as a municipal magistrate and, for the final three decades of his life, as one of the two senior priests at the Panhellenic sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi. His intellectual mission was syncretic and pedagogical: harmonizing Greek cultural and philosophical heritage with the political realities of the Roman Empire, demonstrating that Greeks and Romans shared common ethical standards of civic virtue.

## Methodological Distinction: Biography versus History

Plutarch articulated his methodological manifesto in the celebrated opening of his *Life of Alexander* (chapter 1), explicitly demarcating biography from formal history:

Plutarch states there that he is writing lives rather than histories, and that a small matter, a saying or a jest, often reveals character better than battles with thousands of casualties, great armaments, or sieges of cities.

While the political historian recounts grand military movements, public treaties, and institutional changes, Plutarch's objective was ethical portraiture. Like a painter capturing likeness through the expression of the eyes and face, the biographer examines personal anecdotes, private habits, emotional responses to crisis, and intimate choices to reveal the underlying moral character (*ethos*) of the individual.

## The *Parallel Lives* and Comparative Analysis

The surviving *Parallel Lives* comprise 22 pairs of biographies (each pairing a Greek figure with a Roman counterpart of similar career type), plus four standalone lives (Artaxerxes II, Aratus of Sicyon, Galba, and Otho). Most pairs conclude with a formal comparative essay (*synkrisis*) that systematically evaluates the merits and defects of both subjects:
- **Founders and Lawgivers**: Theseus and Romulus; Lycurgus of Sparta and Numa Pompilius.
- **Imperial Conquerors**: Alexander the Great and Julius Caesar.
- **Orators and Statesmen**: Demosthenes and Cicero.
- **Military Strategists in Republics**: Pericles and Fabius Maximus; Themistocles and Camillus.

Plutarch's comparative method functioned as a cross-cultural analytical tool. By juxtaposing Greek and Roman careers, he highlighted universal human patterns of ambition, political envy, leadership, and moral hubris, while illuminating the distinct institutional constraints of Greek poleis versus the Roman Republic.

## Sources, Historical Preservation, and Reception

Beyond his biographical focus, Plutarch was an extraordinarily erudite scholar who cited hundreds of historical sources, many otherwise completely lost to antiquity—including memoirs, official letters, political pamphlets, and the lost works of Hellenistic historians such as Theopompus, Callisthenes, Duris of Samos, and Hieronymus of Cardia.

In addition to the *Lives*, Plutarch authored the *Moralia* (*Ethika*), a diverse corpus of more than seventy treatises exploring ethics, religion, political counsel, and literary criticism (including his critical essay *On the Malice of Herodotus*). 

Translated into French by Jacques Amyot (1559) and into English by Sir Thomas North (1579), Plutarch's *Lives* served as the primary historical source for William Shakespeare's Roman plays (*Julius Caesar*, *Antony and Cleopatra*, *Coriolanus*) and profoundly inspired Montaigne, Rousseau, and the founding fathers of the American Republic.
