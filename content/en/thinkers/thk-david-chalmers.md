# David Chalmers

The philosopher of mind who formulated the "hard problem of consciousness" and called for naturalistic property dualism.

## What He Gave

- **The "hard problem of consciousness" (1995)**: the distinction that reshaped the contemporary debate in philosophy of mind. The "easy" problems (how the brain discriminates stimuli, how it integrates information, how it produces a verbal report about its states) are easy **in the methodological sense**, not the practical one: we know the form their solution will take, which is a functional mechanism. The hard problem is: why does this functional operation come accompanied at all by **experience from the inside**? Why is it not "darkness" within while behavior stays as it is? Chalmers's argument is that any functional description, however complete, leaves this question standing, because it explains performance, not feeling.
- **The zombie argument (Philosophical Zombie)**: a being identical to us atom for atom in physical structure and behavior, but with no experience at all. Chalmers holds that conceiving of such a being is logically possible without contradiction, and that its logical possibility suffices to refute the claim that experience **follows** logically from the physical facts. The argument is a matter of sharp controversy; its best-known opponent is Daniel Dennett, who holds that what we imagine here is not coherent in the first place.
- **Naturalistic dualism (Naturalistic Dualism)**: his positive alternative. He does not propose a separate spiritual substance like Descartes, but proposes that **conscious experience is a fundamental property of nature** like mass and charge: not derived from anything else, but linked to the physical by regular psychophysical laws. Hence his openness to **panpsychism**: the possibility that some experiential properties have a primitive presence at lower levels of organization.
- **The principle of organizational invariance and "fading qualia"**: a thought experiment in which your neurons are replaced one by one with a silicon equivalent that preserves the same functional role. If experience faded gradually, you would reach a state in which you lose the color of what you see while still saying confidently that you see it, a situation Chalmers regards as implausible. The result: **functional organization, not matter, determines experience**, which makes his position compatible with the possibility of machine consciousness.
- **The extended mind (1998, with Andy Clark)**: the thesis that cognitive processes do not stop at the boundary of the skull; the notebook used by a person with memory loss plays the same functional role that biological memory plays, and so deserves to be counted as part of his cognitive system.

## His Place in the Current

Chalmers stands directly opposite **eliminative materialism** (Paul and Patricia Churchland), which sees conscious states as folk categories that will vanish with the progress of neuroscience, and opposite **Dennett**, who sees the "hard problem" as an illusion arising from a misdescription. But at the same time he is not an enemy of science: his formulation of the problem helped frame the **scientific study of consciousness**, including the search for the neural correlates of consciousness (NCC), a research program associated especially with Francis Crick and Christof Koch.

## Biography

David John Chalmers was born in Sydney on 20 April 1966 and grew up in Adelaide, studied mathematics and computer science at the University of Adelaide, then went to Oxford as a Rhodes scholar before turning to philosophy and the sciences of mind. He received his doctorate from Indiana University in 1993 under the supervision of Douglas Hofstadter with a thesis entitled "Toward a Theory of Consciousness." He taught at Washington University in St. Louis, the University of California at Santa Cruz, and Arizona, then worked as a professor at the Australian National University, and since 2009 has worked at New York University, where he founded with Ned Block the Center for Mind, Brain, and Consciousness. At the first Tucson conference, "Toward a Science of Consciousness," in 1994, he presented the hard problem, then published his founding article a year later.

## The Precise Formulation of the Thesis

His book *The Conscious Mind* (1996) presents the structure of the whole argument: that conscious experience does not follow logically from the physical facts, and that natural science needs new fundamental laws linking the physical to the experiential. In it he uses the zombie argument, the knowledge argument, and the inverted spectrum argument, distinguishing between logical possibility and metaphysical possibility with the tools of two-dimensional semantics. He also presents two principles that make the project scientific: the principle of coherence between consciousness and functional awareness, and the principle of organizational invariance, and at the close of the book he proposes a preliminary conception of information as having two aspects, a physical aspect and an experiential aspect. In *The Character of Consciousness* (2010) he collected his later essays on consciousness and semantics. In *Constructing the World* (2012) he attempted to build a philosophy based on defining being in relation to a number of fundamental truths, including the idea of "scrutability," which holds that all truths can be derived from a small base set, drawing inspiration from Carnap's project in *The Logical Structure of the World*. In *Reality+* (2022) he adopted the thesis that virtual worlds are real and not illusions.

## Influence and Reception

The 1995 article shaped the contemporary debate on consciousness, so that the distinction between the easy problems and the hard problem became a common term in neuroscience and philosophy. Recent theories of consciousness, among them integrated information theory (IIT) and global workspace theory, have helped define their position on the problem. In 1998 the neuroscientist Christof Koch bet that the neural correlates of consciousness would be settled within twenty-five years, and Chalmers bet the opposite, and in 2023 Koch conceded that the bet went to Chalmers. He is also credited with opening the philosophical debate on the technological singularity (The Singularity: A Philosophical Analysis, 2010) and on the consciousness of artificial intelligence.

## Limits and Criticism

Dennett objected in "The Unimagined Preposterousness of Zombies" (1995) that the conception of the zombie is incoherent, and Chalmers replied that conceiving of a logical possibility does not require us to imagine it in detail. The illusionism of Keith Frankish offered the opposite answer, which says that the "hard problem" is an illusion generated by mechanisms of introspection. Other philosophers criticized the move from logical possibility to the metaphysical conclusion, and held that the conceivability argument is not decisive. As for his theory of the existence of fundamental psychophysical laws, some considered it a primitive explanation that does not explain how the correlation occurs. In the case of naturalistic dualism, the relation of experience to physical causation remains in question, since saying that experience has no causal effect (epiphenomenalism) leaves the report about it unexplained, one of the old objections that he tried to confront.
