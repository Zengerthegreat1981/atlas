# Abu Yusuf Ya'qub ibn Ibrahim

An Iraqi jurist from Kufa, a leading student of Abu Hanifa and, according to the tradition, the first to hold the office of **chief judge (qadi al-qudat)**, under the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid. He is a central figure in the transmission of Abu Hanifa's largely oral opinions into an institutional legal school with a presence in the state's apparatus. He is also the author of an early and major Islamic work on fiscal and administrative policy, the *Kitab al-Kharaj*.

## Life and context

Abu Yusuf grew up in Kufa in a family of modest means and remained a close student of Abu Hanifa for a long time, becoming one of his most intimate and most influential pupils in shaping Hanafi fiqh after his teacher's death. He later moved to Baghdad, the seat of the Abbasid caliphate, where he rose through judicial posts until he reached the office of chief judge of the Abbasid state under Harun al-Rashid. In this office he was reportedly responsible for the appointment of judges across the state, which gave him considerable influence in spreading the legal opinions of the school of Abu Hanifa throughout the entire Abbasid judicial network. Abu Yusuf died in Baghdad in 182 AH.

## Early years in Kufa

The biographical tradition places Abu Yusuf's birth in Kufa around 113 AH and traces his Arab lineage to the Ansar of Medina through an ancestor counted among the Companions. His household was poor, and a frequently repeated story relates that a parent wished him to earn a living instead of attending study circles, until Abu Hanifa offered financial support. Such anecdotes come from later biographical literature and may be embellished.

His education was not confined to one teacher. He collected prophetic traditions from Kufan and other authorities, among them al-A'mash and Hisham ibn Urwa, and acquired a reputation as a transmitter of hadith as well as a jurist. He also studied law with Ibn Abi Layla, the judge of Kufa, before attaching himself to Abu Hanifa. He later preserved the differences between these two masters in a work known as *Ikhtilaf Abi Hanifa wa Ibn Abi Layla*, which reflects the competing legal circles of the city in which he was formed.

## His role in founding the Hanafi school as an institution

Before Abu Yusuf, the opinions of Abu Hanifa were transmitted orally by circles of his students without comprehensive systematic written codification. Abu Yusuf contributed substantially to converting these opinions into an organized written tradition, whether by recording the juristic views of his teacher or by adding opinions of his own that sometimes differed from Abu Hanifa's (as shown by the Hanafi 'disagreement' (khilafiyyat) books comparing the views of Abu Hanifa, Abu Yusuf, and Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani). This documentary and organizing role, combined with his official judicial position, made him the link through which Hanafi fiqh passed from the stage of individual oral ijtihad to the stage of an institutional school with formal influence in the state.

## The Kitab al-Kharaj: fiqh of revenue and administration

The **Kitab al-Kharaj** is the best known of Abu Yusuf's works. He wrote it at the direct request of Harun al-Rashid to serve as a practical guide to managing the public finances of the state. It covers the rules of the kharaj tax levied on conquered lands, the ushr, the jizya, the principles of distributing the public treasury (bayt al-mal), the regulations governing the treatment of the dhimmis, and the rules for managing agricultural land according to its classification (taken by force, 'anwatan, or by treaty, sulhan). From the perspective of legal theory, this work is one of the earliest Islamic texts that approaches fiqh as an instrument for formulating the fiscal and administrative policy of the state, not merely as devotional rulings or individual transactions, which gives it exceptional significance in studies of early Islamic political and administrative jurisprudence. The book combines pure jurisprudential argument with direct political advice addressed to the caliph, in a manner that sometimes approaches the early Islamic genre of 'counsel to kings' (nasiha lil-muluk).

## Methodology of usul

Abu Yusuf generally followed the method of Abu Hanifa, based on analogy (qiyas) and juristic preference (istihsan), but he showed a greater inclination than his teacher to take account of the public interest (maslaha) and the practical circumstances of government, as is clearly seen in the Kitab al-Kharaj, where he balances strict textual jurisprudence against the requirements of the practical administration of the state. He also disagreed with Abu Hanifa on many questions recorded in the books of Hanafi disagreement, which shows that the Hanafi school from its beginnings was not a homogeneous bloc but an internal dialogue among multiple juristic views.

## Legacy and critical assessment

Abu Yusuf is considered one of the two principal disciples of Abu Hanifa (together known as *al-Sahibayn*, "the two companions"), who with Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani shaped the Hanafi school. Orientalists such as Joseph Schacht have observed that early Hanafi legal literature (including the works of Abu Yusuf) reflects a transitional phase from uncodified individual opinion to a more systematically organized juristic formulation, although scholars differ on how accurately some of these texts can be historically attributed. His judicial office is also an institutional precedent that influenced the later shape of Islamic judiciary, as it linked a particular legal school systematically to the state's judicial apparatus.

## Key works

- **Kitab al-Kharaj**: on the fiscal and administrative policy of the state.
- Contributions to recording the juristic opinions of Abu Hanifa and transmitting them to the next generation of students.
