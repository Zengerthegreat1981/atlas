# John Austin (Legal Positivist, Command Theory)

An English jurist (1790–1859), a friend and follower of **Jeremy Bentham** and deeply influenced by him, who is considered the academic founder of legal positivism as a distinct, methodical field of study in the English-speaking world, through his book *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832).

## Command Theory of Law

Austin defines law as a **general command** issued by a **political sovereign**: a person or body that receives habitual obedience from the majority of the members of a political society, and that does not itself owe habitual obedience to any other authority. This command is backed by a threat of a **sanction** for violation. Austin was also a utilitarian: he held that the content of law can be assessed by the standard of utility (which he linked to divine commands), while insisting that law's existence is a separate question from its merit. In this formulation Austin sharply separates positive law from morality: the validity of a legal rule depends on its issuance from an actual political sovereign, not on the justice of its content. Austin carefully distinguishes between "law in the strict sense" (issued by the political sovereign) and "law in a metaphorical sense" (such as the laws of honor or social customs not backed by an organized political sanction). He refuses to treat the latter as law in the true sense, however binding their actual social force may be.

## His Life and Academic Project

Austin was born in Suffolk in 1790. He served in the army (1806-1812) before studying law and being called to the bar in 1818. He was not successful in practice, so he turned to academia. In 1826 he was appointed professor of jurisprudence at the newly founded University of London (later University College London), and spent time in Bonn studying German law before beginning his lectures in 1828. His lectures did not attract many students, he stopped lecturing in 1832, and he resigned the chair in 1835. *The Province of Jurisprudence Determined* (1832) grew out of the first part of the course. The scale of his influence was recognized mainly after his death in 1859, when his wife Sarah Austin edited his lectures and published them as *Lectures on Jurisprudence* (1863). That work restored the standing of his project and established it as a basic reference in teaching general jurisprudence at British universities throughout the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

## A Comparison with the Theory of Ruling in Usul al-Fiqh

Setting out Austin's theory alongside the theory of the Sharia ruling in Islamic usul al-fiqh reveals a fundamental difference in the source of obligation. Whereas Austin ties the validity of law exclusively to its issuance from an actual **human political sovereign**, the usulists (such as al-Amidi in *al-Ihkam* and al-Shatibi in *al-Muwafaqat*) tie the Sharia ruling to the address of the Lawgiver (God) concerning the acts of the obligated persons, regardless of whether a political authority exists to enforce it or compel compliance. Nevertheless, jurists of political Sharia (*fiqh al-siyasa*) discussed, in a parallel way, a question close to Austin's: from where do the ruler's commands (such as discretionary punishments and unscripted regulatory systems) draw their binding force? They answered that obedience to the ruler is obligatory within the limits of what does not contravene Sharia, not because he is a sovereign enjoying habitual obedience as in Austin, but because Sharia itself conditionally obligated obedience to those in authority.

## Impact and Later Criticism

Austin's theory dominated the teaching of jurisprudence in British universities for decades, and formed the basis from which most classical "Jurisprudence" courses proceeded until the mid-twentieth century. **H.L.A. Hart** later criticized it decisively in *The Concept of Law* (1961). The command model, Hart argues, fails to explain **power-conferring** laws (such as laws of marriage contracts, which do not oblige but enable individuals to arrange their legal affairs). It fails to explain the **continuity** of legal obligation across changes in the identity of the sovereign itself (the death of a king does not automatically invalidate all of his kingdom's laws). And it reduces the entire legal relationship to the model of the "gunman situation," which fails to distinguish between a highwayman who orders someone at gunpoint and a lawful legal system that enjoys internal acceptance from those subject to it. Yet the command theory remains the starting point against which every later development in legal positivism is measured, from Kelsen to Hart to Raz.
