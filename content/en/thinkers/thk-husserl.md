# Edmund Husserl

Edmund Husserl (1859-1938) was a German-Moravian philosopher and the founder of phenomenology. He was not an existentialist, yet the existential and phenomenological traditions that followed him, and the clinical approaches derived from them, rely heavily on his method.

## Life

He was born in Prossnitz (Prostějov) in Moravia on 8 April 1859 into a Jewish family, studied mathematics, and received his doctorate in Vienna in 1883 with a dissertation on the calculus of variations. In Vienna he attended Franz Brentano's lectures, which turned him toward philosophy; he completed his habilitation at Halle in 1887 and published *Philosophy of Arithmetic* in 1891. He taught at Göttingen (1901-1916) and then at Freiburg (1916-1928), where Martin Heidegger was his assistant and later his successor in the chair. After the Nazi laws of 1933 he was barred from university activity as a person of Jewish descent. He died in Freiburg on 27 April 1938.

## What He Gave

A method: return to "the things themselves." Suspend your theories and judgements, and describe what is given in experience as it is given, before interpreting it. In the *Logical Investigations* (1900-1901) he first argued against psychologism, the view that the laws of logic are merely laws of thought as a psychological process, and then developed descriptive analyses of meaning, perception and knowledge.

## Intentionality

Taking the idea from Brentano, he held that consciousness is always consciousness of something. There is no closed consciousness that then meets a world; the direction toward the world is the structure of consciousness. This undercuts the picture of an inner mind facing an outer reality. He analysed the act of consciousness (noesis) and its intended object as meant (noema).

## Epoché (Bracketing) and Reduction

The epoché is the methodological suspension of the "natural attitude," the unreflective assumption that the world simply exists. In *Ideas I* (1913) this leads to the phenomenological and transcendental reduction, in which the world is studied as it appears to consciousness. In clinical settings the idea is often borrowed in a simplified form, as description before interpretation, which is a core skill in some British existential training.

## Later Work

In his later writings he turned to intersubjectivity (the fifth of the *Cartesian Meditations*), to the analysis of internal time-consciousness, and to the "lifeworld" (*Lebenswelt*), the pre-scientific world of everyday experience that he held to be the forgotten foundation of the sciences in *The Crisis of European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology*.

## His Disagreement with Heidegger

Heidegger was his student and assistant and then diverged from his project: Husserl wanted to ground knowledge in the structures of transcendental consciousness, and Heidegger asked the question of the meaning of being, starting from the existence of the human being in the world. The distance between them was both personal and philosophical.

## What Reached the Clinic

Mainly the method, not the transcendental project. This is a legitimate use but narrower than the original, and not all practitioners are aware of the difference.

## His Most Important Works

- *Logical Investigations* (*Logische Untersuchungen*, 1900–1901).
- *Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology and to a Phenomenological Philosophy*, Book One (*Ideen I*, 1913).
- *Cartesian Meditations* (*Cartesianische Meditationen*, French 1931, German 1950).
- *The Crisis of European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology* (*Die Krisis der europäischen Wissenschaften und die transzendentale Phänomenologie*, 1936/1954).

## Selected Quotations

"We want to go back to the 'things themselves'" (*Logical Investigations*, Introduction to the second volume). The phrase "To the things themselves!" became the slogan of the phenomenological movement.
