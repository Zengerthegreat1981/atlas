# Arthur Kleinman

An American psychiatrist and medical anthropologist born in 1941. At Harvard University he has combined psychiatry and medical anthropology as Esther and Sidney Rabb Professor of Anthropology; he also chaired the Department of Social Medicine at Harvard Medical School and directed the Harvard University Asia Center. He is considered a leading figure in contemporary medical anthropology, and is known for distinguishing between disease and illness, for the model of explanatory models, and for his long fieldwork in Taiwan and China.

## Training and Context

Kleinman trained as a psychiatrist in the United States in the 1960s, at a moment when the Western psychiatric clinic assumed that diagnostic categories were universal and that cultural difference was merely a surface layer over a uniform biological disorder. Working in hospitals and in medical markets in Taipei led him to observe that patients describe their ailments in different languages, and that the clinic coexisted with popular and religious healing methods and traditional Chinese medicine alongside biomedicine. From this encounter he formulated a twofold project: on one hand precise cultural description, and on the other an attempt to change the practice of the physician in the examination room.

## Disease and Illness

Kleinman distinguished between disease, the objective biological process diagnosed by biomedicine according to its standard measures, and illness, the lived experience as it is interpreted culturally by the patient, his family and his community, including the meanings, suffering and anxiety associated with the condition. He argued that effective care needs attention to both dimensions, and that a physician who treats the disease while neglecting the illness may cure the organ and fail to treat the patient. This framework was shared by colleagues such as Byron Good and Leon Eisenberg in the article "Culture, Illness, and Care" published in *Annals of Internal Medicine* in 1978, which carried the concept from anthropological literature into internal medicine literature.

## Explanatory Models

In his book *Patients and Healers in the Context of Culture* (University of California Press, 1980), Kleinman developed the idea that each party in an illness situation (patient, family, traditional or biomedical practitioner) holds an explanatory model of what is happening: the cause, the time of onset of symptoms, the pathological process, the severity of the condition and the appropriate treatment. Clinical miscommunication often arises from a silent collision between these models, without the physician being aware that they exist. Together with two colleagues he proposed a set of practical questions, eight of them in their common form, for eliciting the patient's model, which became a standard teaching tool in multicultural medical education. He also divided health care into three overlapping sectors (popular, professional and folk), so that the researcher could understand the physician's own place within a wider system.

## China and Neurasthenia

In *Social Origins of Distress and Disease* (Yale University Press, 1986), Kleinman studied clinic patients in the city of Changsha in China, and noticed that the widespread diagnosis of neurasthenia there corresponds in the West to conditions classified as depression. He showed that many of these patients express psychological and social distress linked to the Cultural Revolution, to loss and to working conditions in the language of the body and physical symptoms, and that this embodiment is not an escape from acknowledging depression but a socially acceptable language for expressing pain. This supported his hypothesis that the psychiatric diagnostic categories themselves are culturally conditioned, and it was a critique of what he called the "category fallacy", which assumes the validity of Western classification outside its context.

## Illness Narratives

In *The Illness Narratives: Suffering, Healing, and the Human Condition* (Basic Books, 1988), Kleinman made illness narratives a central subject of research: how patients build a coherent story of their chronic suffering that gives it meaning, how this story takes shape within different cultural frameworks, and how the physician can listen to it as part of treatment rather than as mere side chatter. In his later book *Writing at the Margin* (University of California Press, 1995), he collected essays on the relationship between anthropology and medicine, and developed the concept of social suffering, which he shared in a joint volume with Veena Das and Margaret Lock published in 1997.

## Care and Ethics

Over the last two decades Kleinman has turned to the question of ethics and care. He wrote *What Really Matters: Living a Moral Life Amidst Uncertainty and Danger* (Oxford, 2006), and then *The Soul of Care* (Viking, 2019), drawing on his experience caring for his wife Joan, who had Alzheimer's disease. In it he argued that family and medical care belong to what gives life its meaning, and that health systems that turn care into procedural transactions empty it of its moral dimension.

## Impact

- The distinction between disease and illness, and the eight-question model, became a standard part of medical education curricula around the world, especially in training physicians in cultural competence.
- His work helped establish multicultural medicine as an applied field that links anthropology to clinical practice.
- His Chinese studies influenced the understanding of how psychiatric diagnostic categories are shaped culturally, and the debate in cultural psychiatry about expressions of distress.

## Limits of the Project

- The model of explanatory models has been criticised for assuming more coherence and stability in patients' conceptions than actual observation shows, since patients' explanations are often contradictory and shift according to circumstance.
- Critical medical anthropology has argued that the focus on the dyadic physician–patient relationship may reduce the weight of structural factors such as poverty and health policy, although Kleinman addressed these dimensions in his work on social suffering.
- Some researchers have also taken issue with his tendency to interpret meaning at the expense of political-economic analysis of the distribution of disease.
