# Marilyn Strathern

Dame Marilyn Strathern (born 1941) is a British social anthropologist best known for her work on gender, kinship, and personhood in Melanesia and for her application of feminist thought to the basic theoretical problems of anthropology.

## Biographical Context

Strathern was born in 1941 in Wales and studied at Girton College, Cambridge, where she later received her doctorate. She carried out her first fieldwork among the Hagen people of the Mount Hagen region of Papua New Guinea in the mid-1960s, returning in later years, and she also studied kinship in an English village, Elmdon in Essex. She held academic posts at the University of Manchester, where she became professor of social anthropology in 1985, and from 1993 at the University of Cambridge, where she held the William Wyse Chair of Social Anthropology (as its first woman holder) until 2008. She was Mistress of Girton College from 1998 to 2009 and was made a Dame in 2001. Her work has influenced feminist anthropology, gender studies, kinship theory, and science and technology studies.

## Theoretical Contributions

Strathern's central contribution is the argument that gender is not a secondary dimension of kinship and exchange but a constitutive part of how Melanesian sociality is imagined and enacted. In *Women in Between* (1972) and her Hagen work she examined how women's roles in marriage and exchange are articulated within the competitive exchange of pigs and valuables known as *moka*, in which men (and the "big men" among them) build prestige. Rather than asking what women do in a male-dominated economy, she asked how gendered images, relations, and persons make exchange possible.

Her concept of **dividual** or partible personhood, drawing in part on the earlier work of McKim Marriott and Roy Wagner, is a major reconceptualization. In much Western thought persons are imagined as bounded individuals who own their property and relations. Strathern argued that in many Melanesian contexts persons are better understood as composites of relationships, who are made through exchange and can be "divided" across the social contexts in which they act. She related this to a critique of Western assumptions about society and the individual, and she emphasized **relations** and the "partial connections" through which analysis itself is built. Her style, dense, reflexive, and demanding, is part of the argument.

## Major Works

*The Gender of the Gift: Problems with Women and Problems with Society in Melanesia* (1988) is her most important book. It is not a monograph on a single community but a re-reading of ethnography from Hagen and many other Melanesian societies. Strathern uses it to contrast Melanesian ideas of gender, exchange, and personhood with the assumptions of Western feminist and anthropological analysis, and to question the application of Western concepts of "society," "gender," and "gift" to Melanesia. Other books include *Women in Between: Female Roles in a Male World, Mount Hagen* (1972), *Partial Connections* (1991), *After Nature: English Kinship in the Late Twentieth Century* (1992), *Reproducing the Future: Anthropology, Kinship and the New Reproductive Technologies* (1992), *Property, Substance and Effect: Anthropological Essays on Persons and Things* (1999), *Kinship, Law and the Unexpected: Relatives Are Always a Surprise* (2005), and *Learning to See in Melanesia* (2013). She also edited *Audit Cultures* (2000), on the spread of audit in universities and other institutions.

## Influence and Debate

Strathern's work had a foundational impact on feminist anthropology and on the study of kinship, personhood, and the effects of new reproductive technologies. Her concept of dividual personhood has been widely used, extended, and contested, including in studies of personhood outside Melanesia, in the anthropology of ontologies, and in work on property, body parts, and genetic materials. Her work on relations influenced science and technology studies and the study of assisted reproduction.

Critics have raised several concerns. Some argue that the contrast between Western individuals and Melanesian dividuals is too sharp and that it overstates the unity of both. Some feminist scholars have criticized the book for sacrificing attention to women's agency and experience, while others praise it for avoiding essentialist accounts of "women's experience." Others note the difficulty of her prose. Questions have also been raised about whether the Hagen- and Melanesia-based argument can be extended to other regions, and whether a focus on relationality obscures structural inequality.

## Legacy

Strathern is regarded as one of the most influential anthropologists of the late twentieth century. Her work continues to inform debates on kinship, gender, relationality, intellectual property, and the anthropology of knowledge.
