# Rabi'a al-'Adawiyyah

Rabi'a bint Isma'il al-'Adawiyya al-Qaysiyya, known as Rabi'a al-'Adawiyyah, was an ascetic and Sufi from Basra. She died in 185 AH / 801 CE according to the most probable accounts, and she is the most famous woman in the history of early Islamic Sufism. What remains of her life is not so much a documented biography as a Sufi memory that accumulated over three centuries. Her life should therefore be read in the light of the sources that preserved it, not in the light of the literary and cinematic image it has since become.

## What the Arabic Sources Say

The oldest surviving mention of her is in Abu Talib al-Makki (d. 386 AH / 996 CE), in *Qut al-Qulub* (*Nourishment of Hearts*), which transmits her sayings on love and contentment. Next comes al-Sulami (d. 412 AH / 1021 CE), in his account of female Sufi worshippers and his *Tabaqat al-Sufiyya* (*Generations of the Sufis*); then Abu Nu'aym al-Isfahani (d. 430 AH / 1038 CE), in *Hilyat al-Awliya'* (*The Adornment of the Saints*); then al-Qushayri (d. 465 AH / 1072 CE), in *al-Risala al-Qushayriyya*; and then Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 597 AH / 1201 CE), in *Sifat al-Safwa*, which devotes a chapter to female worshippers. None of these sources is free of moral refinement that serves its author's purpose. Farid al-Din al-'Attar (d. c. 618 AH / 1221 CE) wrote about her in Persian in *Tadhkirat al-Awliya'* (*Memorial of the Saints*), and this is the source that shaped many of her marvellous stories and added what became her famous image in the West. A comparison of the sources shows that the stories furthest from reality are the most recent in date.

## The Probable Biography

The reports state that she was born into a poor family in Basra, that she was orphaned, and that famine scattered her family, so that she was sold as a slave and then freed. She lived in Basra in an age when asceticism was a strong current after al-Hasan al-Basri (d. 110 AH). Some reports count among her contemporaries Sufyan al-Thawri and Malik ibn Dinar. Her meeting with al-Hasan al-Basri himself, however, is distant in time, since he died in 110 AH when she would have been a child. This may suggest that some anecdotes were created by a desire among narrators to link chains of asceticism together. The sources agree that she chose not to marry, that she lived in ascetic poverty, and that her words remained in circulation in the assemblies of Basra.

## Pure Love

What is new in her thought is not the denial of fear and hope in themselves, since both are present in the Qur'an and in the asceticism of those who preceded her. It is rather the placing of love (*mahabba*) as a station higher than both. The sources attribute to her the famous line of a poet in which she says she loves her Lord with two loves: a love of longing and a love because He is worthy of it. They also attribute to her the story of the water and the fire, in which she says she wishes to extinguish the fire of Paradise and burn Paradise so that people would not worship God out of fear or hope. In this idea, which links worship with love, Rabi'a stands on a line that continues after her with al-Harith al-Muhasibi, Dhu al-Nun al-Misri, al-Junayd and al-Hallaj, and then al-Ghazali in the quarter on love in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*. On the other side, some strict jurists judged that talk of absolute divine love might lead to laxity regarding the Sharia, and they expressed reservations about the expressions attributed to the Sufis.

## What Modern Scholarship Says

Margaret Smith, in her book on Rabi'a published in 1928, collected what was reported about her in Arabic and Persian sources. Rkia Cornell in 1999 presented a translation of what al-Sulami reported about female Sufi worshippers. Annemarie Schimmel, in her study of the mystical dimensions of Islam, holds that Rabi'a was among the first to express divine love, although she was not the author of a complete doctrine. Feminist studies read her as evidence that spiritual authority was not the monopoly of men, while other historians warn against projecting contemporary concepts onto a woman whose sources preserve only short sayings.

## Limits of Knowledge

Rabi'a left no written text, and most of the poetry attributed to her was preserved in later references in varying forms. Three layers should therefore be distinguished: short sayings on asceticism and reliance on God, which most probably belong to the earliest layer; expressions of love that spread in the fourth and fifth centuries AH; and the marvellous stories written by al-Attar. In addition, some reports may confuse her with other female worshippers who bore the same name, including Rabi'a al-Shamiyya, wife of Ahmad ibn Abi al-Hawari. This makes the attribution of some reports to al-Adawiyya doubtful, and it means that every citation of one of her sayings must be accompanied by a reference to its source.
