# Rabi'a al-'Adawiyyah

Rabi'a al-Adawiyya of Basra was an ascetic whom later Sufism remembered as a teacher of divine love. Biographical dictionaries give her fuller name as Rabi'a bint Isma'il al-'Adawiyya al-Qaysiyya. She should be kept apart from Rabi'a al-Shamiyya, wife of Ahmad ibn Abi al-Hawari, who is another woman. Rabi'a al-Adawiyya's death is often placed in 180 or 185 AH (796–801 CE). What remains is a Sufi memory built up over several centuries. It has to be read through the texts that preserved it.

## What the Arabic Sources Say

She is mentioned earlier than the great Sufi handbooks. al-Jahiz (d. 255 AH / 868–869 CE) refers to her in *al-Hayawan* and *al-Bayan wa-l-Tabyin*. The fuller portrait comes later. Abu Talib al-Makki (d. 386 AH / 996 CE) cites her on love and contentment in *Qut al-Qulub*. al-Sulami (d. 412 AH / 1021 CE) includes her in *Dhikr al-Niswa al-Muta'abbidat al-Sufiyyat*, a book on women worshippers, not in the male *Tabaqat al-Sufiyya*. Abu Nu'aym al-Isfahani (d. 430 AH / 1038 CE) writes of her in *Hilyat al-Awliya'*, al-Qushayri (d. 465 AH / 1072 CE) in *al-Risala*, and Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 597 AH / 1201 CE) in *Sifat al-Safwa*. Each of these reports serves its author's purpose. Farid al-Din Attar (d. c. 618 AH / 1221 CE), in the Persian *Tadhkirat al-Awliya'*, supplied many of the marvellous stories that later fixed her image. In general, the more marvellous the story, the later the source.

## The Probable Biography

The reports state that she was born into a poor family in Basra, that she was orphaned, and that famine scattered her family, so that she was sold as a slave and then freed. She lived in Basra in an age when asceticism was a strong current after al-Hasan al-Basri (d. 110 AH). Some reports count among her contemporaries Sufyan al-Thawri and Malik ibn Dinar. Her meeting with al-Hasan al-Basri himself, however, is distant in time, since he died in 110 AH when she would have been a child. This may suggest that some anecdotes were created by a desire among narrators to link chains of asceticism together. The sources agree that she chose not to marry, that she lived in ascetic poverty, and that her words remained in circulation in the assemblies of Basra.

## Pure Love

What the later tradition presents as distinctive is not a denial of fear and hope. Both are Qur'anic, and both were already part of Basran asceticism. It is the placing of love (*mahabba*) above them. Later authors attribute to her verses on two loves, a love of longing and a love of God because He is worthy of love, and a story in which she wishes to burn Paradise and put out the Fire so that God is worshipped neither from hope nor from fear. That story is especially prominent in Attar, and the verses on two loves are a later layer, not an early saying. Writers on divine love after her time, among them al-Muhasibi, Dhu al-Nun al-Misri, al-Junayd, al-Hallaj and al-Ghazali in the book of love in *Ihya' Ulum al-Din*, were placed by the tradition on the same path. Some jurists warned that unbounded talk of divine love could loosen observance of the Sharia.

## What Modern Scholarship Says

Margaret Smith, in her book of 1928, collected the Arabic and Persian reports. Rkia Cornell in 1999 translated al-Sulami's accounts of early Sufi women, including the notice on Rabi'a. Annemarie Schimmel treats her as an early voice in the literature of divine love, and not as the author of a system. Feminist readings have taken her as evidence that spiritual authority was not confined to men. Other historians warn against reading a modern idea of authorship back into short sayings preserved by later men.

## Limits of Knowledge

Rabi'a left no written text, and most of the poetry attributed to her was preserved in later references in varying forms. Three layers should therefore be distinguished: short sayings on asceticism and reliance on God, which most probably belong to the earliest layer; expressions of love that spread in the fourth and fifth centuries AH; and the marvellous stories written by al-Attar. In addition, some reports may confuse her with other female worshippers who bore the same name, including Rabi'a al-Shamiyya, wife of Ahmad ibn Abi al-Hawari. This makes the attribution of some reports to al-Adawiyya doubtful, and it means that every citation of one of her sayings must be accompanied by a reference to its source.
