# T. H. Marshall

A British sociologist, author of the most influential classical classification of "citizenship" into three complementary dimensions, which formed the theoretical basis of the British welfare state after the Second World War.

## The Central Idea

In his landmark lecture "Citizenship and Social Class" (1949), Marshall proposed that full modern "citizenship" consists of three elements that developed historically in succession in Britain: "civil rights" (the eighteenth century: individual liberty, property, justice before the law), "political rights" (the nineteenth century: the right to vote and to take part in politics), and "social rights" (the twentieth century: the right to a minimum of economic welfare and security, such as education, health, and social security). He argued that the modern welfare state is necessary to achieve full citizenship in practice and not merely in form: civil and political rights that are formally equal remain practically deficient without a minimum of social and economic equality that enables the poor citizen to actually exercise them.

## Context: Post-War Britain

Marshall delivered his landmark lecture in 1949 at the University of Cambridge (the Alfred Marshall Lectures, named after the famous Cambridge economist), at a decisive historical moment: Britain had just established the comprehensive "welfare state" system after the Second World War (the Beveridge Report of 1942, the creation of the National Health Service in 1948). Marshall's theorizing came as a sociological-historical attempt to explain and justify this enormous institutional transformation as the logical completion of a long historical course of the expansion of citizenship, not a radical socialist rupture as its conservative opponents then described it.

## Further Detail: The Tension between Citizenship and Capitalism

Marshall recognized a fundamental structural tension between the principle of "equality" that citizenship presupposes (every citizen enjoys the same three rights regardless of economic position) and the principle of "inequality" on which the capitalist economy is by nature based (inequality of income, property, and opportunity). He argued that social rights (free education, comprehensive health care, a minimum income) do not aim to abolish this economic inequality entirely, but to guarantee a common minimum of full "social status" that enables every citizen, regardless of income, to take actual part in the social and political life of society, regarding this balance between civil equality and economic inequality as a distinctive and relatively stable feature of modern Western welfare societies.

## Impact and Critique

This framework provided the most famous theoretical justification for the British post-war welfare state and became a classic reference in comparative political sociology for studying the development of citizenship. It has been criticized on several grounds. Feminist scholars (for example Carole Pateman and Ruth Lister) argued that its linear sequence reflects the experience of the white British working man and overlooks how women and ethnic minorities were excluded from rights long after their formal extension to men, so that multiple, uneven paths of citizenship need to be recognized. Others have argued that the account is too evolutionary and tied to a single national case, treating the expansion of rights as cumulative when social rights in particular can be cut back.

## Later Reception

Tom Bottomore published Marshall's lecture again in 1992 (with Marshall's own essay) with a commentary assessing it in light of the later retrenchment of the welfare state, including under Margaret Thatcher's government, when some social rights that Marshall had treated as settled were rolled back. The framework has also been used to compare welfare-state building in continental Europe and Scandinavia, where the three kinds of rights were combined in different ways. Marshall himself, a professor of sociology at the London School of Economics, also had practical experience in government service (including the Foreign Office during the Second World War) and later in international social science work.
