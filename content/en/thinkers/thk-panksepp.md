# Jaak Panksepp

An Estonian-American neuroscientist (1943–2017), born in Tartu, Estonia, and raised in the United States. A professor at Bowling Green State University, he is regarded as the de facto founder of the field of **affective neuroscience** as an independent academic discipline, rather than merely an application of neuroimaging techniques to the subject of emotions. He received his doctorate from the University of Massachusetts in 1969 and spent most of his career at Bowling Green, later also holding a chair at Washington State University. He was also known for his advocacy for animal welfare and for his campaign to establish that animals have affective consciousness.

## What He Contributed

- **Founding affective neuroscience as a field**: his book *Affective Neuroscience* (1998) offered the first systematic framework linking subcortical circuits, behavior, and affective experience, and became the founding reference of the discipline. Before this book, "emotions" were a subject of social and personality psychology, rarely discussed in a strict neural framework. Panksepp insisted that they are empirically measurable like perception and movement.
- **Seven primary affective systems** in the brain, which he describes as ancient evolutionary circuits shared among mammals:
  1. **SEEKING** (seeking/curiosity/anticipation): the mesolimbic dopamine system, linked to anticipation and reward.
  2. **RAGE** (anger): a system linked to threat and frustration, activated in situations of restraint or loss.
  3. **FEAR** (fear): the amygdala and brainstem, a response to acute threat.
  4. **LUST** (sexual desire): ancient hormonal circuits linked to reproductive motivation.
  5. **CARE** (nurturing): the oxytocin system, activated in caring for the young.
  6. **PANIC/GRIEF** (panic and sorrow at separation): the separation and social-anxiety system (called PANIC in his 1998 book and PANIC/GRIEF in later work).
  7. **PLAY** (play/social joy): the system of rough-and-tumble and play, necessary for social development.
- **A methodological distinction between emotions and the feeling of them**: emotions are brain-body processes that can be measured behaviorally and neurally, while "feeling" is the phenomenal dimension that adds consciousness to them. This distinction became central to every later discussion of animal consciousness, and it reopened the question "do animals feel?" with experimental tools.
- **The discovery of "rat laughter"** through ultrasonic vocalizations at a frequency of 50 kilohertz, and the research that followed on the social role of tickling and play in mammals. This discovery changed the understanding of play behavior in rodents and restored its standing as a basic evolutionary function, not merely a "marginal" behavior.

## His Place in the Field

Panksepp reframed emotions as "objective neural structures" measurable in rodents, which put him at the center of the debate between evolutionary psychology and cognitive neuroscience, and between reductionism and behaviorism. He was sometimes considered an "advocate of affect-centrism," and he influenced the development of neuroscience-based therapies (neuropsychotherapy) and studies of addiction, depression, and developmental disorders. His work was also presented on platforms of popular culture, especially in his writings on love and laughter.

The main points of criticism of his work can be summarized in three axes: (1) excessive generalization from rodents to humans, especially regarding the full affective significance of the systems in humans; (2) a relative neglect of cortical and cognitive influences on the activation of the systems; (3) the use of the term "primary" systems opens a difficult philosophical question about whether evolution "chose" precisely these seven emotions, or whether they are an analytic construction. His later defense, especially in *The Archaeology of Mind* (2012), showed flexibility in dealing with these criticisms.

Panksepp's influence helped reopen the door to a "bodily approach to emotion" (somatic markers), an idea Damasio developed in a different context, though both stress that body and brain are one functional unit in affective experience.

## Major Works

- *Affective Neuroscience: The Foundations of Human and Animal Emotions* (1998): the founding book of the field.
- *A Textbook of Biological Psychiatry* (2004, as co-editor): a specialized clinical reference on the neural foundations of mental disorders.
- *The Archaeology of Mind: Neuroevolutionary Origins of Human Emotions* (2012, with Lucy Biven): a later development of the theory of the seven systems, with a focus on the evolutionary roots of human affect.

## His Intellectual Approach: Why It Matters Today

In an age when clinical psychology has turned to "evidence-based" practice and "quantitative measurement," Panksepp's legacy reminds us that the inner experience of the child, the animal, or the patient is not merely an item on a questionnaire, but has neural foundations open to reflection. The seven systems have become a tool used by psychotherapists to understand "what is this client activating at this moment?" before deciding which technique to use. His books continue to be read in biological psychology and psychiatry.
