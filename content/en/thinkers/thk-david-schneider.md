# David Schneider

An influential American anthropologist, specializing in the study of kinship and American society, who argued that **kinship is not a natural biological fact but a cultural symbolic system**. His work provoked sharp debate among anthropologists about the nature of kinship itself. He argued that most anthropological studies of kinship assumed that **blood and sex are the foundation** of kinship relations, and that these assumptions must be thoroughly critiqued.

## Biographical Context

David Schneider was born in Brooklyn, New York, in 1918. He studied at Cornell University and received his PhD from Harvard in 1949. He did fieldwork on the island of Yap in Micronesia in 1947–1948. He taught at the University of California, Berkeley, in the 1950s, and from 1960 at the University of Chicago, where he spent the rest of his career. He died in 1995.

## Core Theoretical Framework

Schneider developed a revolutionary model for studying kinship:

**1. Kinship as a symbolic system**: Schneider argued that what we call **kinship** is not a direct reflection of biological facts but a **cultural symbolic system**, a set of meanings and symbols that a society produces to interpret relations among relatives. This means that **what counts as a relative** in one culture may not count as one in another.

**2. Separating the biological and the cultural**: Schneider firmly rejected the classical assumption that kinship is built on **blood and sex** as biological facts. He showed instead that this is a purely **American** assumption, not a universal one. He argued that other peoples need not define kinship through biogenetic ties at all, and that anthropologists should not assume they do.

**3. Critique of genealogy**: Schneider criticized the classical approach of anthropologists who draw **genealogical charts** and treat them as the basis for understanding a kinship system. He said that genealogy is a **Western representation** of kinship that may not reflect how people actually understand their kinship relations.

**4. American kinship as a case**: Schneider used **American society itself** as an anthropological case study, which was revolutionary at the time. Drawing on interviews with middle-class informants in Chicago and elsewhere, he argued that Americans distinguish "relatives in nature" (blood relatives) from "relatives in law" (relatives by marriage), and that sexual intercourse is the central symbol joining the two.

## Key Works

- ***American Kinship: A Cultural Account*** (1968; second edition 1980): his main work, an analysis of the symbols and norms through which middle-class Americans understand kinship. Its central terms are "relative in nature" (shared biogenetic substance) and "relative in law," together with a "code for conduct" of enduring, diffuse solidarity.

- ***"Kinship Terminology and the American Kinship System"*** (1955, with George C. Homans): an earlier article on American kinship terms.

- ***Matrilineal Kinship*** (1961, edited with Kathleen Gough): a major comparative volume on matrilineal systems.

- ***"Notes Toward a Theory of Culture"*** (1976): an essay arguing for the analysis of culture as a system of symbols and meanings.

- ***A Critique of the Study of Kinship*** (1984): his sustained argument that "kinship" as an analytical category rests on European folk assumptions about biological relatedness, drawing partly on his Yap material.

## Ethnographic Focus

Schneider spent most of his academic career studying **American culture itself** from an anthropological perspective. This was bold because most anthropologists in the past studied **other peoples** rather than their own societies. Earlier he had done fieldwork on Yap in Micronesia, but his best-known focus was **how middle-class Americans, and anthropologists drawing on their own folk categories, understand kinship**.

## Impact and Debates

Schneider's work had a deep influence on anthropology:

- **The debate on the nature of kinship**: Schneider provoked a long debate among anthropologists about **what kinship actually is**. Is it a universal phenomenon that can be measured, or a concept that differs from one culture to another?

- **Critique of the classical model**: Schneider pushed anthropologists to re-examine their assumptions that **blood and sex** are the natural basis of kinship.

- **Debate and later work**: Feminist and kinship scholars such as Sylvia Yanagisako and Jane Collier built on his critique, and later work on new reproductive technologies (for example, Franklin and McKinnon's *Relative Values*, 2001) examined how biological and social relatedness are being redefined.

- **Studying American society anthropologically**: He opened the way for a new approach in which anthropologists study **their own societies** as anthropological case studies, contributing to **anthropology at home**.

## Legacy and Contemporary Use

Schneider's work remains a basic reference in:

- **Kinship theory**: his ideas about **kinship as a cultural system** became a starting point for most modern studies of kinship.
- **American kinship studies**: his work is used as a basic reference in any study of American kinship and the American family.
- **Philosophical critique of anthropology**: his critique of the discipline's basic assumptions influenced the next generation of anthropologists who engaged in **epistemological critique**.

## Open Gaps and Issues

Despite the importance of his work, several criticisms remain:

- **Practical application**: while his theory of kinship as a cultural system is strong, it is not always clear how it should be **applied** to other kinship systems in non-American contexts.

- **Gender and power**: insufficient attention was given to how **gender** and **hierarchy** shape the American kinship system.

- **Contemporary changes**: his account was based on mid-twentieth-century American informants and has been revisited in light of reproductive technologies and changing family forms.
