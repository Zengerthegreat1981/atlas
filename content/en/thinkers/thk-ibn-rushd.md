# Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad ibn Rushd

An Andalusian philosopher, jurist and physician, the most famous commentator on Aristotle in the Islamic heritage, known in Latin Europe as Averroes and by the title "the Commentator," to distinguish him from Aristotle, "the Philosopher." He combined Maliki juridical judgeship, medicine and philosophy, and wrote, in his *Fasl al-Maqal* (*The Decisive Treatise*), one of the most important classical defenses of the legitimacy of philosophy in Islam.

## Biography

Ibn Rushd was born in Córdoba in 520 AH/1126 CE into a distinguished Cordoban family of Maliki judges (his grandfather had also been judge of Córdoba). He studied jurisprudence, hadith, medicine and philosophy (it is thought that he worked from the Arabic translations of Aristotle rather than from the Greek), and took up the judgeship in Seville (565 AH/1169 CE) and then the judgeship of the community (chief judge) in Córdoba, and came into contact with the Almohad court, where the caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf drew him close and asked him, according to what 'Abd al-Wahid al-Marrakushi relates in *al-Mu'jib*, reporting from one of Ibn Rushd's students, to explain Aristotle's books because of the difficulty of their style. He worked as physician to the caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf and then to his son Abu Yusuf Ya'qub al-Mansur. Late in his life, for intertwined political and religious reasons, al-Mansur ordered him exiled to Lucena near Córdoba and some of his philosophical books burned in 591 AH/1195 CE (historians debate the precise causes), before he was summoned to Marrakesh and the restriction was lifted shortly before his death. He died in Marrakesh in 595 AH/1198 CE, and his remains were later transferred to Córdoba.

## His Contribution

### The Three Kinds of Commentary on Aristotle

On most of Aristotle's works Ibn Rushd wrote three kinds of commentary: the "Jawami'" (brief summaries), the "Talkhisat" (middle commentaries that restate the text), and the "Great Commentaries" (line-by-line analysis of the full Aristotelian text, which no other commentator did in such detail). These Great Commentaries (especially his commentary on the *Physics*, the *Metaphysics* and *On the Soul*) are what earned him in Latin Europe the absolute title of "the Commentator," after they were translated into Latin and Hebrew and became a basic reference for reading Aristotle in the universities of Paris and Bologna from the thirteenth century.

### *Tahafut al-Tahafut*: The Reply to al-Ghazali

In this book Ibn Rushd replied chapter by chapter to al-Ghazali's *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (*The Incoherence of the Philosophers*), defending al-Farabi and Ibn Sina on some questions and criticizing their interpretations on others, and presenting his own reading, closer to Aristotle than to the Plotinian emanationist reading that predominated in Ibn Sina. Ibn Rushd holds that many of al-Ghazali's criticisms strike Ibn Sina's own philosophy and not the authentic Aristotelian philosophy that he himself adopts, and on the question of the eternity of the world he attempts a middle interpretation that reads the Aristotelian "eternity" in a way that does not radically conflict with the idea that the world is eternally caused by God without a time prior to creation.

### *Fasl al-Maqal*: The Relation between Philosophy and the Shari'a

In this short juridical treatise Ibn Rushd builds a juridical-usuli argument (not a purely philosophical one) for the obligation of philosophical inquiry: he cites Qur'anic verses calling for "consideration" (i'tibar) and "reflection" (nazar) to conclude that the Shari'a itself makes obligatory the study of logic and philosophy for those with the intellectual capacity for it. He establishes a threefold conception of the modes of persuasion (demonstrative for the philosophers, dialectical for the theologians, and rhetorical for the common people), so that each scriptural text addresses its audience in the manner appropriate to it, without this meaning the existence of two conflicting "truths," but one truth presented at different levels of exposition.

### Medicine: *al-Kulliyyat fi al-Tibb*

His book *al-Kulliyyat fi al-Tibb* (*Colliget* in Latin) is an encyclopedic work on the general principles of medicine (intended to be complemented by Ibn Zuhr's *al-Taysir* on particular treatments), in which he treated anatomy, physiology, diseases and the principles of treatment; it was translated into Latin and used as a teaching reference in Europe alongside the works of Ibn Sina and al-Razi.

### Comparative Jurisprudence: *Bidayat al-Mujtahid*

Alongside his philosophical work, Ibn Rushd wrote a reference work in Maliki jurisprudence, *Bidayat al-Mujtahid wa Nihayat al-Muqtasid*, in which he reviews in a comparative method the opinions of the different juridical schools on practical questions together with their evidence, explaining the causes of juridical disagreement with a method resembling in its logical rigor his philosophical method, which made the book a continuing textbook in comparative jurisprudence to this day, apart from the controversy over his philosophy.

## Influence

Ibn Rushd's influence in Latin Europe was deeper than in the Islamic world after his death: the school of "Latin Averroism" arose at the University of Paris in the thirteenth century at the hands of Siger of Brabant and Boethius of Dacia, who adopted radical readings (such as the unity of the active intellect in all human beings) that provoked ecclesiastical condemnations in 1270 and 1277. Later Jewish philosophers such as Moses of Narbonne and Gersonides wrote commentaries drawing on his work; Maimonides (1138-1204) was an older contemporary and was not influenced by him. In the Islamic heritage, Ibn Rushd was regarded as the last of the great Peripatetic philosophers, since after him no Peripatetic current of the same strength appeared in the Islamic East or West, in contrast to the Illuminationist and Sufi current, which continued to flourish.

## Limits

Scholars (such as Majid Fakhry) observe that Ibn Rushd's project of returning to a "pure Aristotle" against the Plotinian emanationist reading was not completely achieved, since he remained influenced by earlier interpretive frameworks (such as Alexander of Aphrodisias and Themistius) on issues such as the active intellect and its unity in all people, a matter that provoked sharp controversy because it appears to deny the individual immortality of the human soul, a position later rejected by both Thomas Aquinas in Latin and most Muslim theologians. Likewise, his defense in *Fasl al-Maqal* of the priority of rational demonstration over the textual apparent sense in cases of conflict, while restricting it with strict methodological conditions, has remained a matter of interpretive dispute: is it a defense of the independence of philosophical reason, or a conciliatory formulation that keeps the Shari'a the final arbiter over philosophy? The question has not been settled by consensus among contemporary scholars.

## His Key Terminology

Ibn Rushd is known for his precise distinction among three kinds of commentary (the brief Jawami', the middle Talkhisat, and the literal Great Commentaries), a methodological classification that has become a reference for students of the history of Arabic philosophical commentaries on Aristotle, since it reflects the development of his personal method across the stages of his intellectual life, from quick summary to precise line-by-line analysis.
