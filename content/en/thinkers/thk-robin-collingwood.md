# R.G. Collingwood

Robin George Collingwood (1889–1943) was a British philosopher, historian, and archaeologist who served as Waynflete Professor of Metaphysical Philosophy at the University of Oxford from 1935 until 1941. Uniquely combining rigorous philosophical inquiry with frontline archaeological fieldwork on Roman Britain, Collingwood formulated an influential idealist philosophy of history centered on the doctrine of "re-enactment" (*re-thinking*), arguing that the study of human history is an autonomous form of knowledge fundamentally distinct from the natural sciences.

## The Dual Career: Romano-British Archaeology and Philosophy

Unlike many philosophers who engaged with history purely as an abstract epistemological problem, Collingwood was an accomplished field archaeologist. He was a leading scholar of the archaeology and epigraphy of Roman Britain of his generation, took part in excavations on Hadrian's Wall, wrote *The Archaeology of Roman Britain* (1930), co-authored *Roman Britain and the English Settlements* (1936, with J. N. L. Myres), and laid the groundwork for *The Roman Inscriptions of Britain* (published in 1965 with R. P. Wright).

Collingwood maintained that his archaeological practice directly informed his philosophy: digging for antiquities taught him that historical knowledge does not emerge from passive observation of artifacts or uncritical reading of chronicles, but from active questioning. He formulated this as the **logic of question and answer** in *An Autobiography* (1939) and *An Essay on Metaphysics* (1940): any historical proposition or text can only be understood by reconstructing the specific question it was designed to resolve.

## Core Historiographical Concepts

### Re-enactment of Past Thought

Collingwood's central thesis in *The Idea of History* (published posthumously in 1946) is that "all history is the history of thought." For Collingwood, a historical event is not merely a physical occurrence in nature, but an intentional human action. Consequently, the historian's task is not simply to record that an event happened, but to grasp the thought that animated it by **re-enacting** (*re-thinking*) that thought in their own mind. 

Collingwood illustrated this with the death of Julius Caesar in 44 BCE: the "outside" of the event is the stabbing of a man in the Senate, while the "inside" is the thought of the conspirators, their reasons for acting, and their view of the political situation. For him, to explain the event is to reconstruct that thought in its historical situation.

### The Inside and Outside of Events

Collingwood drew a strict methodological boundary between the natural sciences and history:
- **The Natural Sciences**: Deal with events that possess only an "outside"—physical movements, chemical reactions, or biological processes observed without attributing subjective purpose or thought to the phenomena themselves. Natural phenomena are explained by subsuming them under universal inductive laws.
- **History**: Deals with actions that possess both an "outside" (physical manifestations) and an "inside" (the thought, intention, and decision of the historical agent). History explains actions not through general laws, but by uncovering their internal rationality.

### The A Priori Historical Imagination

In his 1935 inaugural lecture, "The Historical Imagination," Collingwood rejected the positivist "scissors-and-paste" approach that treated history as a mere compilation of testimonies from authoritative sources. Instead, he argued that the historian uses an *a priori* historical imagination to construct a coherent, continuous narrative bridging the fragmentary gaps left by surviving relics and documents. This imagination is not arbitrary fantasy; it is strictly disciplined by the constraints of empirical evidence and the demand for internal consistency.

## The Explanation Debate: Collingwood vs. Positivism

Collingwood's philosophy of history became a focal point of debate in twentieth-century analytic philosophy of history. When logical positivist Carl G. Hempel proposed the "covering law model" (deductive-nomological explanation) in 1942, asserting that historical explanation must rely on general empirical laws just like physics, Collingwood's theory of re-enactment became a principal philosophical alternative. Canadian philosopher William H. Dray (*Laws and Explanation in History*, 1957; *History as Re-enactment*, 1995) drew on and refined Collingwood's position, arguing that historical actions are explained by exhibiting their "rational appropriateness" from the actor's perspective rather than by subsuming them under covering laws.
