# Sigmund Freud

Austrian neurologist (1856-1939), the first founder of psychoanalysis (Psychoanalyse), and the source of the theoretical authority from which most of the thinkers listed in this section later broke away, including those among them who developed an explicitly existential perspective in therapy (Rank, Jung, and later Rollo May); and one of the most influential thinkers in the history of psychology, psychiatry, and Western culture in general.

## Biography

He was born in Freiberg in Moravia to a Jewish family and moved with his family to Vienna in 1860, where he spent most of his working life, before being forced to flee to London in 1938 after Nazi Germany annexed Austria; he died there in 1939. He studied medicine at the University of Vienna and graduated in 1881, and worked at the start of his career on neurology. In 1885 he traveled to Paris to study hysteria under **Jean-Martin Charcot** at the Salpêtrière hospital, which redirected him from organic medicine to the study of psychological disorders. In collaboration with **Josef Breuer**, he published in 1895 the book *Studies on Hysteria* (Studien über Hysterie), which proposed that hysterical symptoms result from painful repressed memories and established the "talking cure" technique that later developed into free association.

He founded the "Wednesday Psychological Society" in Vienna in 1902, which developed into the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society (1908) and then the International Psychoanalytical Association in 1910. The movement saw prominent schisms: **Carl Jung**, who founded "analytical psychology," and **Alfred Adler**, who founded "individual psychology"; both differed with Freud over the centrality of the sexual drive.

## What He Contributed

- **The unconscious (Unbewusste)**: he established the concept of the unconscious as an independent psychic structure open to examination, in which a large part of mental life takes place outside immediate awareness and drives behavior and symptoms without the individual's knowledge. In his first ("topographical") model, set out in the last chapter of *The Interpretation of Dreams*, the mind is divided into the conscious, the preconscious, and the unconscious; the structural model of 1923 (below) later complemented it.
- **The interpretation of dreams**: in *The Interpretation of Dreams* (Die Traumdeutung, issued in November 1899 with a fixed publication date of 1900), which he considered his most important work, he presented a systematic interpretation of the dream through free association as the "royal road to the unconscious" and as a disguised, symbolic attempt to fulfill a repressed wish.
- **Sexual theory and developmental stages**: in *Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality* (1905) he proposed the stages of psychosexual development (oral, anal, phallic, followed by latency and the genital stage). He had formulated the **Oedipus complex**, first in his correspondence of 1897 and then in *The Interpretation of Dreams*, as the prototype for the organization of childhood desire.
- **Defense mechanisms and repression**: the concept of **repression** (Verdrängung) as the basic mechanism that keeps painful contents out of consciousness, and the basis for the formation of neurotic symptoms.
- **The tripartite psychic structure**: in *The Ego and the Id* (1923) he divided the psychic apparatus into the **id** (Es, instinctual drives), the **ego** (Ich, the realistic mediator), and the **superego** (Über-Ich, conscience and internalized standards), a structural model that provided the ground on which everything after it arose, whether in the directions of schism or in the directions of integration with phenomenological and existential philosophy.
- **The two instincts**: in his later works, especially *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920), he distinguished between the **life drive** (Eros) and the **death drive**, the latter often called "Thanatos," a term Freud himself did not use.

## His Place in This Atlas

Freud is not described here as an existential thinker (he did not explicitly adopt any of the school's slogans), but he is listed as the **first institutional and theoretical source** from which most of the thinkers whose biographies appear in the section "The Founding Generation and the Early Freudians" branch off. Many elements of this Atlas, such as Rank's separation anxiety, Ferenczi's return to trauma, Tausk's "influencing machine," and Spielrein's destructive instinct, can be understood only in relation to Freud's theory.

## Influence

Freud deeply influenced psychiatry, literature, philosophy, and cultural criticism, and his concepts of the unconscious, psychological defenses, and the talking cure remain present in the foundations of modern psychiatry and dynamic psychology.

## Limitation

He did not describe himself as existential; his inclusion here is referential/foundational, not an affiliation. And many of his specific hypotheses (such as the Oedipus complex or penis envy) have not been supported by empirical research or are difficult to test, and were subjected to wide methodological criticism from philosophers of science such as **Karl Popper**, who considered psychoanalysis an unfalsifiable theory.

## Major Works

- *Studien über Hysterie* (with Breuer, 1895).
- *Die Traumdeutung* (1900).
- *Drei Abhandlungen zur Sexualtheorie* (1905).
- Five Lectures on Psychoanalysis (Über Psychoanalyse, delivered at Clark University in 1909, published 1910).
- *Jenseits des Lustprinzips* (1920).
- *Das Ich und das Es* (1923).
- *Das Unbehagen in der Kultur* (1930).
