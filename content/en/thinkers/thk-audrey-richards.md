# Audrey Richards

Audrey Isabel Richards (1899–1984) was a British social anthropologist who helped found the anthropology of food and nutrition and opened functionalist anthropology to the study of women's lives. A student of Bronisław Malinowski at the London School of Economics, she carried his method of intensive fieldwork to Northern Rhodesia — present-day Zambia — where her study of the Bemba people linked diet, agriculture, labour migration, and social structure in a single analysis. She was one of the first women to hold a leading position in British anthropology, and her work was rediscovered by feminist anthropology in the 1970s as a pioneering account of women's experience.

## Life and Career

Richards was born in London in 1899 into a family of lawyers and civil servants, and studied at Newnham College, Cambridge, before moving to the London School of Economics to work under Malinowski. She was part of the first generation of his students who took his method — long fieldwork, conducted in the local language — to colonial Africa. Her principal fieldwork was among the Bemba of Northern Rhodesia in the early 1930s, where she lived in villages, learned the language, and recorded household food consumption day by day. She taught at the University of the Witwatersrand in South Africa from 1938 to 1940, held a readership at the University of London from 1946 to 1950, and then directed the East African Institute of Social Research at Makerere in Uganda from 1950 to 1956. In 1956 she returned to Cambridge as a fellow of Newnham College, founded and directed the university's African Studies Centre, and later held the Smuts Readership in Commonwealth Studies. She was also active in the International African Institute and in the development of applied anthropology, advising on nutrition and development policy. She died on 29 June 1984, recognised as a leading figure of British social anthropology.

## Key Works

*Land, Labour and Diet in Northern Rhodesia: An Economic Study of the Bemba Tribe* (1939) is Richards's central work and one of the first systematic anthropological studies to connect a people's diet with their whole economic, social, and ecological system. *Chisungu: A Girl's Initiation Ceremony Among the Bemba of Northern Rhodesia* (1956) documents in rare detail a girls' initiation rite, treating it as a comprehensive education in the sexual, marital, and economic values of Bemba society. She edited *East African Chiefs: A Study of Political Development in Some Uganda and Tanganyika Tribes* (1960), a comparative study of African political systems under colonial rule. Her earlier work included *Hunger and Work in a Savage Tribe* (1932), a study of food and nutrition that already showed her characteristic concern with the material conditions of life. She also wrote on kinship, chieftainship, and the social consequences of labour migration in Central Africa.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Richards's distinctive contribution was to extend functionalist anthropology — the study of how institutions meet human needs and maintain social life — from politics and kinship to food, nutrition, and the environment. Where Malinowski had treated food primarily as a biological need, Richards showed that diet is a social fact: among the Bemba, the shifting cultivation system known as chitemene, the division of labour between men and women, and the seasonal hunger that followed the rains could only be understood together, and only in the context of colonial labour migration, which drew men away to the copper mines and left women to farm. Her method was unusually quantitative for her time: by tracking what households actually ate, day by day, she documented seasonal variation in nutrition and its effects on health and family relations — an early form of nutritional anthropology that anticipated later work on food security. In *Chisungu* she showed that a girls' initiation ceremony lasting weeks functioned as a complete education, transmitting the values of womanhood, marriage, and economic life in a single concentrated ritual — a subject male anthropologists of her generation had largely neglected. She thus brought women's experience into the centre of social anthropology decades before feminist anthropology made it a programme.

## Influence and Critique

Richards's influence was both substantive and institutional. She helped found nutritional anthropology as a field, and her framework for linking food systems to social structure is still used in studies of food security in Africa and elsewhere. As director of the East African Institute of Social Research at Makerere and founder of the African Studies Centre at Cambridge she shaped the research agenda of African anthropology, and her students carried her concerns into later studies of conflict, urbanisation, and social change. Her Smuts Readership and her service as the first woman president of the Royal Anthropological Institute marked the arrival of women in the senior ranks of British anthropology. Feminist anthropologists in the 1970s rediscovered *Chisungu* as a classic of the ethnography of women. Critics note, however, that her functionalism sometimes treated colonial conditions as background rather than as the product of a political system: her analyses of hunger among the Bemba describe the effects of labour migration without always pressing the critique of the colonial economy that produced it. Her framework also assumes a degree of social equilibrium that later conflict-oriented anthropology challenged. Even so, her combination of careful ethnography, quantitative method, and attention to women's lives remains a model for anthropology that takes everyday material life seriously.
