# Alexander Chayanov

**Alexander Vasilievich Chayanov** was a Russian-Soviet agricultural economist who set out a distinctive theory of the independent peasant economy. He saw the family farm as an economic unit with its own internal logic, not necessarily subject to the conventional calculations of capitalist profit. This theory was rediscovered and widely adopted in development economics and economic anthropology in the second half of the twentieth century. Chayanov himself paid with his life for the conflict between his ideas and Stalin's policy of forced collectivisation.

## An Agrarian World in the Service of the Russian Peasant

Chayanov was trained academically at the Moscow Agricultural Institute, and devoted most of his professional life to studying the actual Russian agricultural economy through extensive field surveys of peasant farms, rather than confining himself to abstract theorising. In the early Soviet period he held prominent research and administrative positions, and became one of the leading agricultural economics advisers to the new state during the 1920s. That decade saw the flourishing of his most important theoretical output, before the campaign of Stalin against independent peasant economists brought him down.

## The Theory of the Peasant Labour Organisation

In his book *Organizatsiya krestyanskogo khozyaistva* ("Peasant Farm Organisation", 1925; known in English as *The Theory of Peasant Economy*, 1966), Chayanov developed an analytical model completely different from that of the capitalist farm, which is based on hiring labour and maximising cash profit. He argued that the peasant family farm operates according to a fundamentally different logic, based on balancing the drudgery of additional labour against satisfying the family's consumption needs. It does not operate on the marginal profit compared with an alternative market wage. He called this logic the "labour-consumer balance." He explained that the size of production and labour on a peasant farm is determined by the size of the family and the number of dependants relative to the number of workers, not by the logic of maximising cash profit alone, as both classical and Marxist economic theory assume.

## An Implicit Critique of Orthodox Marxism

Chayanov's theory carried a serious implicit challenge to the orthodox Marxist analysis prevailing at the time. That analysis predicted the inevitable disintegration of the peasantry into two distinct classes: rich peasants (kulaks) who employed wage labourers, and poor peasants who sold their labour power, in line with the classical capitalist class differentiation. Chayanov argued instead that the apparent inequality between peasant farms more often reflects the different stages of the family life cycle (the number of children and dependants relative to workers), rather than a real, stable class differentiation. This analysis clashed directly with the theoretical justification Stalin used to justify his campaign to "liquidate the kulaks as a class" in the early 1930s.

## Agricultural Cooperation as a Middle Path

Chayanov advocated voluntary agricultural cooperation (cooperatives for marketing, finance and shared agricultural equipment) as a middle form that preserves the autonomy of the peasant family farm while granting it the advantages of scale in marketing, finance and technology. He rejected both the absolute fragmentation of peasant farming and the complete forced collectivisation that began to take shape as official Soviet policy in the late 1920s. This conciliatory vision was regarded as "rightist" and suspect by the Stalinist leadership, which was moving rapidly towards comprehensive forced collectivisation by 1929–1930.

## Arrest, Execution and Rediscovery

Chayanov was arrested in 1930 in the same repressive campaign that targeted Kondratiev, and was falsely accused of belonging to a fictitious "Toiling Peasant Party". He was executed in 1937 at the height of the Great Stalinist Purge, and was not formally rehabilitated until 1987. Western development economists and economic anthropologists, led by Daniel Thorner, who edited with Basile Kerblay and R. E. F. Smith the English translation of his works published in 1966, later rediscovered his theory as a useful analytical tool for understanding contemporary agrarian economies in Asia, Africa and Latin America, where the family farm still escapes full explanation in terms of capitalist profit logic.

## A Distinctive Literary Dimension

Alongside his rigorous academic output, Chayanov also wrote fictional literary works under a pseudonym, including the short novel *The Journey of My Brother Alexei to the Land of Peasant Utopia* (1920, under the pseudonym Ivan Kremnev), in which he imagined a utopian peasant Russia, with Moscow in 1984. Written in a literary style, it reflected his vision of a decentralised, cooperative agricultural society as an alternative to the model of centralised forced industrialisation that was then imposing itself in his time. This literary aspect reveals the depth of Chayanov's intellectual commitment to his alternative vision of organising the agrarian economy. He did not confine himself to dry academic analysis, but also tried to imagine the concrete social form that his cooperative vision could take if it were given the full opportunity to be applied, away from the pressures of rapid forced industrialisation that were mounting around him in real life, outside the pages of his novel.
