# Niklas Luhmann

A German sociologist (1927–1998) who began his career as a government administrative official before his relatively late academic turn, and who developed one of the most abstract and complex social theories of the twentieth century: the theory of autopoietic social systems.

## What He Contributed

- **Social autopoiesis**: In *Social Systems* (1984) he borrowed the concept of "autopoiesis" (self-making) from the Chilean biologists Maturana and Varela and applied it radically to society. A social system, unlike almost the whole sociological tradition before him, is not composed of human individuals but only of "communications," which reproduce themselves according to their own internal logic, operationally closed (though cognitively open to their environment), including individuals themselves, who in this conception become an "environment" of the social system rather than an internal component of it.
- **The functional differentiation of modern society**: He developed a conception of modern society as differentiated into independent functional subsystems (economy, law, politics, science, religion, education), each subsystem operating according to its own binary "code" (economy: payment/non-payment; law: legal/illegal; science: true/false) that cannot be translated directly into the logic of another subsystem, so that each subsystem observes the others only through its own distinctions, although subsystems can be "structurally coupled" (as the economy and law are through contract and property).

## His Place in the Current

Luhmann's theory has been regarded as one of the most abstract and complex attempts in the history of sociological theory, and it provoked a sharp and lengthy intellectual debate with Jürgen Habermas over the possibility of critical rationality and genuine moral communication in a society conceived as a set of operationally closed systems. Habermas criticized Luhmann's theory as emptying the possibility of social critique and ethical communicative action of its content, while Luhmann defended the realism of his analytical conception of a modern society that is indeed complex.

## Historical Context

Niklas Luhmann trained in law and worked in German public administration in Lower Saxony before spending 1960-61 at Harvard University, where he studied with Talcott Parsons, the leading figure of American structural functionalism. In 1968 he became professor of sociology at the newly founded University of Bielefeld, where he remained until his retirement. Luhmann initially adopted Parsons's functional framework, but later developed a radical critique of it. He saw that Parsons had assumed that the social subsystems all serve the "needs" of society as a whole, conceived as a unified, integrated system, whereas Luhmann held that each subsystem in modern society (economy, law, politics) develops according to its own entirely independent internal logic, without a guiding central coordination or a unified collective purpose that society as a whole serves. This was the starting point from which he later developed his original theory of social autopoiesis.

## Main Ideas (In Detail)

Luhmann elaborated the concept of "communication" (Kommunikation) as the only basic unit of which the social system is composed. Every communication includes three inseparable elements (information, the uttering of information, and understanding), and generates a subsequent communication that responds to it, so that a chain of successive communications forms the social system, without any human individual as such being directly a "part" of this system (for humans themselves are independent psychic and biological systems that interact with the social system from outside it, not from within). Luhmann also described how the functional "subsystems" (law, economy) always regard one another solely from the angle of their own code: when the legal system tries to regulate the economic market, it cannot in fact "see" the internal economic logic directly, but reformulates it according to its own legal code (legal/illegal), always producing an approximate translation rather than a complete correspondence between the two systems' logics.

## Critique and Assessment

Jürgen Habermas sharply criticized Luhmann's theory in their famous debate (Theorie der Gesellschaft oder Sozialtechnologie, 1971), holding that Luhmann's portrayal of society as operationally closed systems deprives social theory of its capacity for ethical critique and for defending a communicative rationality freed from domination, and turns sociology into a neutral descriptive instrument that in practice serves to preserve the existing order rather than question it. Luhmann defended himself by arguing that his theory is necessarily descriptive rather than normative, and that the claim that complex modern society can be unified under a single all-encompassing communicative rationality (as Habermas aspires) is in the first place an unrealistic assumption about the nature of highly functionally differentiated modern societies. This debate remained one of the most important intellectual debates in the history of German social theory in the second half of the twentieth century, and it is still widely discussed in debates on social critique.
