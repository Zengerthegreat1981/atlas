# Bernard Comrie

A British linguist (born 1947 in Sunderland, England) and typologist who extended the statistical universals approach of Joseph Greenberg into precise typological classifications in the domains of tense, aspect, mood (TAM), and grammatical relations across the world's languages. He is also a co-editor of the 2005 print edition of the *World Atlas of Language Structures* (WALS).

## Historical Context

Comrie studied at the University of Cambridge and turned early to typological linguistics, influenced by Joseph Greenberg's method of searching for linguistic universals through the comparison of broad samples of the world's languages. He later held posts at Cambridge and the University of Southern California, and from 1997 to 2015 was director of the Department of Linguistics at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, Germany, where WALS was compiled; he later moved to the University of California, Santa Barbara.

## Central Ideas and Method

Comrie started from a basic premise: deep regularities in the human capacity for language can be discovered through systematic comparison of broad and geographically and historically diverse samples of the world's languages, rather than by focusing on a single language (such as English) as much early generative grammar did. He carefully distinguished between "absolute universals" (properties that apply to all languages without exception) and "statistical or tendency universals" (common patterns that are not absolutely necessary), stressing that most reliable typological generalizations belong to the second type. This distinguishes his approach from the ambition to formulate strict universal grammatical rules, as in generative grammar.

## Aspect Across Languages

In his landmark book *Aspect* (1976), Comrie offered a systematic classification of the difference between "tense" (the location of an event relative to the moment of speech) and "aspect" (different ways of viewing the internal temporal constitution of a situation, such as perfective and imperfective, habitual or progressive). His companion volume *Tense* followed in 1985. He showed how different languages encode these semantic distinctions with radically different grammatical tools, from inflectional forms to periphrastic constructions.

## The Accessibility Hierarchy

Comrie developed, with Edward Keenan, an influential typological classification of how relative clauses are formed across the world's languages. There is a regular hierarchy of grammatical roles that can be relativized (subject, then direct object, then indirect object, then oblique, then genitive, then object of comparison). On their formulation, if a language can form a relative clause on a role lower in the hierarchy, it can generally form one on every higher role. The hierarchy is an implicational tendency of the same broad kind as Greenberg's universals, and it has become a standard reference point in typology. Some later work has discussed apparent exceptions and the need to define the roles carefully.

## Major Works

Besides *Aspect* (1976) and his reference article with Keenan (1977), Comrie published his comprehensive book *Language Universals and Linguistic Typology* (1981, revised 1989), which became a standard textbook in typology courses around the world. He was a co-editor of the print *World Atlas of Language Structures* (Haspelmath, Dryer, Gil and Comrie, 2005). The later online edition (Dryer and Haspelmath, 2013) maps structural features across more than 2,600 languages; Comrie wrote several of its chapters, including those on case alignment. He also published on the languages of the Caucasus and of the former Soviet Union, including *The Languages of the Soviet Union* (1981), and edited *The World's Major Languages* (1987). *Tense* (1985) complements *Aspect*.

## Impact and Criticism

Comrie provided classificatory tools that became standard in comparative linguistics. As a co-editor of the 2005 *World Atlas of Language Structures*, he helped extend the mapping of structural features to a very large language sample. Typological and generativist approaches differ on whether widespread tendencies or innate constraints best explain cross-linguistic patterns. His textbooks remain widely used introductions to typology.

## Fieldwork on the Caucasian and Turkic Languages

Alongside his general theoretical work, Comrie conducted direct descriptive fieldwork on less-studied languages, especially those of the Caucasus region, including the Daghestanian languages, which he studied with local linguists, with attention to features such as their complex case systems. This reflected his commitment to grounding typological generalizations in detailed language description.

## His Style of Teaching and Reference Writing

Comrie's textbooks set out typological distinctions in clear instructional prose. *Language Universals and Linguistic Typology* has long been used as an introduction in graduate programs. *The World's Major Languages* gives non-specialists a comparative sketch of a selection of widely spoken languages and their basic structures.
