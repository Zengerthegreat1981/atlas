# Mustafa Emirbayer

An American sociologist, author of the founding manifesto of "contemporary relational sociology," who called for reorienting sociology from the study of "fixed substances" (such as individuals or classes as pre-given units) to the study of changing "processes and relations."

## The Central Idea

In his landmark article "Manifesto for a Relational Sociology" (1997), Emirbayer put forward a radical critique of what he called "substantialism," which dominates sociology, whether in its individualist form (the economic methodology that assumes rational individuals with fixed interests prior to interaction) or its collectivist form (the structuralism that assumes essential social totalities such as class or the state). In its place he proposed a "relational" perspective holding that the basic social units are not individuals or structures but "transactions" and the changing relations themselves, and that the identity of any actor (an individual or an institution) is continually formed and changed through the network of its moving relations, not as a fixed substance prior to the relation.

## Historical Context

Emirbayer wrote his landmark article in an American academic context that in the 1990s had seen a growing accumulation of diverse research currents, all of which shared, without an organized awareness of it, an anti-substantialist tendency: the quantitative social network analysis developed by Harrison White and his students; classical American pragmatism (John Dewey, George Herbert Mead), which stressed that the meaning of any action is determined by its changing interactional context rather than by essential properties prior to it; and the sociology of Pierre Bourdieu, which sought to transcend the structure/agency dualism through the concepts of "habitus" and "field." Emirbayer saw that these scattered currents needed an explicit unifying manifesto that would gather them under a single self-aware theoretical-methodological umbrella.

## Main Ideas and Method

Emirbayer, drawing on Dewey and Arthur Bentley's *Knowing and the Known* (1949), distinguished three modes of sociological explanation: "self-action", in which independent, self-moving entities (individuals, classes, states) are the starting point; "inter-action", in which pre-formed entities with fixed attributes interact without being changed by it; and "trans-action", in which the entities themselves derive their meaning, identity and capacities from their changing positions in flows of relations. He adopted the third, most radical position and applied it to several basic sociological concepts, among them power, equality, freedom, and the self, which he treated as formed through relations and narratives over time rather than as fixed substances. He later developed with Ann Mische, in a joint article (1998), an influential analysis of "agency" itself as a complex temporal phenomenon combining an orientation toward the past (repeated habit), an orientation toward the present (momentary practical evaluation), and an orientation toward the future (the creative imagining of possible alternatives), rejecting any one-dimensional conception of human agency.

## Key Works and Representatives

Mustafa Emirbayer is a professor of sociology at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. Harrison White, whose work in structural network analysis informed this current, was among its important predecessors. Other researchers such as François Dépelteau later helped develop a broader research current under the name "relational sociology," notably through edited volumes such as *The Palgrave Handbook of Relational Sociology* (2018).

## Impact and Critique

This framework provided a unified theoretical basis for several currents that had been scattered (social network analysis, American pragmatism, Bourdieu's sociology), and influenced the reorientation of sociological theoretical debate away from the traditional structure/agency dualism. Criticism was directed at it by researchers who held that the philosophical radicalism of Emirbayer's transactional position (the rejection of any fixed essence of any actor) makes its concrete empirical application difficult, since any actual research needs a kind of provisional stability in defining the units of analysis in order to carry out any measurement or comparison, while others regarded it as a necessary philosophical correction even if its practical application still requires provisional methodological simplifications that do not fundamentally conflict with the original thesis. The manifesto has remained among the most cited theoretical articles in American sociology and contributed to a growing international relational-sociology network.
