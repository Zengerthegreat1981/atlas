# Gottlob Frege

Gottlob Frege was a German philosopher, logician, and mathematician, born in Wismar on 8 November 1848 and died in Bad Kleinen on 26 July 1925. He is regarded as the founder of modern symbolic logic and of analytic philosophy of language, although he received only limited recognition in his lifetime. He taught at the University of Jena between 1874 and 1918, and by his work he brought about a turn in logic not seen since Aristotle.

## Formation and Career

Frege studied mathematics, physics, chemistry, and philosophy in Jena and Göttingen, and received his doctorate from Göttingen in 1873 on the geometrical representation of imaginary numbers, then received his habilitation at Jena in 1874. He became an extraordinary professor in 1879 after the publication of his first book, an honorary ordinary professor in 1896, and retired in 1918. His university life was quiet, while his works remained unknown until Bertrand Russell made them known after 1903.

## The Concept Script

In the *Concept Script* (Begriffsschrift, 1879) Frege founded modern symbolic logic. He introduced the theory of quantification with its quantifiers and bound variables, the tool that made it possible for the first time to formulate a sentence such as "every person loves some person" in a precise formal formulation, something that Aristotle's logic was unable to do for centuries. He transferred the concept of function (Funktion) from mathematics to logic, replacing the duality of subject and predicate, and presented the first complete formal system for the calculus of propositions and predicates with axioms and rules of inference.

## The Foundations of Arithmetic and Logicism

In *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik, 1884) he presented his program called logicism, that is, the reduction of arithmetic to logic. He criticized psychological conceptions of number, which he saw as misleading, and laid down three methodological principles: the separation of the psychological from the logical, the distinction between concept and object, and the context principle: never ask for the meaning of a word in isolation from the sentence. He highlighted that number belongs to the concept and not to the thing: one who says that the emperor's carriage is drawn by four horses ascribes the number to the concept "horse drawing the emperor's carriage," not to the horses singly.

## Sense and Reference

In "On Sense and Reference" (Über Sinn und Bedeutung, 1892) he made his founding distinction between sense (Sinn), that is, the mode of presentation of a thing, and reference (Bedeutung), that is, the thing itself. His example is "the morning star" and "the evening star": they have the same reference, the planet Venus, and different senses, which is why "the morning star is the evening star" was an astronomical discovery and not a tautology. He said that the reference of a sentence is its truth value, and its sense is the thought it expresses (Gedanke). In "Function and Concept" and "On Concept and Object" (1891–1892) he elaborated the difference between concepts and objects. In his essay "The Thought" (Der Gedanke, 1918) he held that thoughts reside in a third realm that is neither the world of external things nor the world of subjective ideas.

## The Basic Laws and the Paradox

*The Basic Laws of Arithmetic* (Grundgesetze der Arithmetik, two volumes, 1893 and 1903) is his great attempt to derive all of arithmetic from logic alone in a formal system. While the second volume was in press in 1902, a letter reached him from Bertrand Russell revealing a contradiction in the fifth law of his foundations, namely the paradox of the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. Frege added an appendix acknowledging that one of the foundations of his construction had been shaken after the work was finished, one of the most famous moments of admitting error in the history of philosophy. In his last years he abandoned logicism and proposed that the foundation of arithmetic lies in geometry.

## Influence

Frege influenced Russell through correspondence rather than as a teacher, Wittgenstein, who visited him in Jena in 1911 and directed him to Russell in Cambridge, Rudolf Carnap, who attended his lectures, and Quine, Dummett, and Kripke. Michael Dummett in *Frege: Philosophy of Language* (1973) offered a reading that makes him the father of analytic philosophy. In recent decades scholars have returned to Frege to study his theory of number and Hume's principle, and the "neo-Fregean" school has come to revive his program.

## Limits and Criticism

Russell's paradox brought down the fifth law of the *Basic Laws* and he was unable to repair it, and so his logicist program failed in its original form. Critics of his distinction between sense and reference held that it explains proper names only with difficulty, as Kripke did in *Naming and Necessity* (1972). His talk of a third realm of thoughts is criticized as a metaphysics that is hard to justify. His political diaries, published in 1994, revealed right-wing, antisemitic positions in his last years, an aspect unrelated to his logical work but one that raises questions about his biography. His direct influence in his own time was cut off by the density and difficulty of his symbolism.
