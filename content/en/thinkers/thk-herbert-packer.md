# Herbert L. Packer

**Herbert Packer** (1925-1972) was an American law professor at Stanford University. He is the author of one of the most influential frameworks for understanding comparative American criminal procedure: his distinction between the **crime control model** and the **due process model**, presented in his influential 1964 article and later developed in his major book *The Limits of the Criminal Sanction* (1968).

## The two competing models: crime control versus due process

Packer argues that the criminal justice system of any society moves between two competing ideal poles. The **crime control model** assumes that the most important function of the criminal system is to suppress crime efficiently and swiftly. It gives priority to speed and administrative decisiveness, places great trust in the capacity of police and prosecutors to distinguish the innocent from the guilty at early stages, and tends to minimize procedural safeguards that might slow down or obstruct effective prosecution. Its symbolic image is the **assembly line**, which moves cases quickly toward conviction.

By contrast, the **due process model** doubts the reliability of official agencies and their capacity to avoid error and bias. It sets up multiple procedural barriers (the right to silence, the right to counsel, the presumption of innocence, the exclusion of illegally obtained evidence) that limit state power even if they slow the process or let guilty people go free. Its symbolic image is the **obstacle course**.

## The nature of the two models: analytical tools, not normative prescriptions

Packer presents the two models as competing value systems that underlie the operation of the criminal process, not as a literal description of any system actually in existence. Every real criminal system mixes elements of both in varying proportions. The framework's importance lies in its diagnostic function. It allows one to understand the tensions behind particular procedural reforms, such as the expansion of the rights of accused persons by the US Supreme Court in the 1960s, which can be read as a movement toward the due process model.

## The limits of the criminal sanction: toward restrained criminalization

In his broader book *The Limits of the Criminal Sanction* (1968), Packer extends his analysis to a deeper question: **when should the criminal tool be used at all**, rather than regulatory or civil instruments? He offers a strict restrictive standard for justifying criminalization, warning against **overcriminalization** that results from using the criminal law to enforce morality or regulate private behaviors (such as some drug offenses and consensual sexual offenses). This paved the way for later extensive jurisprudential debates about the limits of criminalization, among scholars such as Joel Feinberg and, after him, Douglas Husak.

## An implicit comparison with criminal procedure in Islamic jurisprudence

A comparative observation: the tension Packer describes between efficiency and guarantees can be set beside the classical Islamic juristic principle of **averting hudud penalties by doubts** (dar' al-hudud bi-l-shubuhat), which restricts the power to impose hadd penalties as soon as even a minimal reasonable doubt exists. This comes close to the logic of the due process model, despite the radically different reference framework. Likewise, the Islamic jurists' requirement of a number of witnesses in certain hadd offenses (such as adultery) reflects a similar procedural caution in its general logic, although it differs in detail from the safeguards of modern criminal procedure.

## Packer's six criteria for criminalization

In *The Limits of the Criminal Sanction* Packer sets out six criteria that should be satisfied before conduct is made criminal. In summary: the conduct must be generally regarded as threatening to society; subjecting it to criminal sanctions must be consistent with the goals of punishment; suppressing it must not inhibit socially desirable conduct; it must be capable of evenhanded and non-discriminatory enforcement; controlling it through the criminal process must not expose that process to qualitative or quantitative strain; and there must be no reasonable alternative to the criminal sanction for dealing with it. This restrictive framework made the book a basic reference for later discussions of the limits of criminalization.

## Critique of the criminalization of "victimless crimes"

Packer applied his criteria skeptically to offenses sometimes called **victimless crimes** (such as drug offenses, gambling and consensual sexual offenses). Writers in this tradition argue that the absence of a direct victim weakens the justification for criminalization and that criminalizing such conduct has practical costs: black markets and the organized crime associated with them, the burden on enforcement agencies, and broad discretionary police powers.

## Impact and reception

Packer's distinction became a core part of the teaching of criminal procedure in American universities, and was used as an analytical framework in comparative studies of criminal systems around the world. Critics have offered methodological observations. Some argue that the two-model dichotomy is simplified to the point of hiding a greater diversity of actual dynamics within the criminal system, such as plea bargaining, which fits neither model exactly. Others propose additional models, such as John Griffiths's "family model" (1970) and Kent Roach's victims'-rights models (1999).
