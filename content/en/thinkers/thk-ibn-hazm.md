# Ibn Hazm al-Andalusi

A Zahiri jurist, traditionist, theologian, and man of letters from al-Andalus, born in Cordoba in 384 AH (994 CE) into a family of ministers whose ancestry the sources trace to freedmen (mawali) of the Umayyads. He lived through a period of sharp political upheaval that saw the fall of the Umayyad Caliphate in Cordoba and the rise of the Taifa kingdoms. He moved between political activity and devotion to learning. He became a leading representative of the Zahiri school in Islamic jurisprudence and the author of a significant work of comparative religious study in the Arabic heritage.

## Biography

Ibn Hazm grew up in a family of ministers. His father served as a minister to al-Mansur ibn Abi Amir, and Ibn Hazm received a broad education in literature, jurisprudence, logic, and medicine. In the early years of his life he took part in the political conflicts that followed the fall of the Umayyad Caliphate. He held the vizierate briefly under Umayyad claimants to the caliphate, such as Abd al-Rahman IV and Abd al-Rahman V, and he was imprisoned more than once because of these political stances.

After the Umayyad Caliphate ended definitively in 1031 CE, Ibn Hazm withdrew from political activity and devoted himself to writing and teaching. He began by studying the Maliki school and then the Shafi'i school, and moved to the Zahiri school, which he adopted and defended so vigorously that he became its foremost representative in al-Andalus. He faced strong opposition from the Maliki jurists who dominated the Andalusian juristic scene, and some of his books were burned in Seville by order of the authorities. He died in the village of Mant Lisham, near Niebla (Huelva), in 456 AH (1064 CE).

## Contributions

### Zahiri Jurisprudence and the Rejection of Analogy

In his book *al-Ihkam fi Usul al-Ahkam*, Ibn Hazm developed a complete methodological theory for the Zahiri school, which had been founded earlier by Dawud ibn Ali al-Isfahani. Its core was the rejection of juristic analogy (qiyas) in all its forms, which he regarded as reasoning by opinion without textual foundation. It also rejected juristic preference (istihsan), unrestricted public interest (maslaha mursala), and school-based imitation (taqlid). The school relied only on the apparent meaning of the texts of the Qur'an and the Sunna and on certain consensus. Ibn Hazm held that analogy opens the door to an unlimited multiplicity of rulings according to each jurist's reasoning. The legal texts, by contrast, had already specified all that the accountable person needs, whether by general wording or by the indication of language rather than by analogy.

### Comparative Religious Studies: *al-Fasl fi al-Milal wa al-Ahwa' wa al-Nihal*

This book is an early and significant Arab-Islamic attempt to study religions and sects comparatively. Ibn Hazm treated Judaism and Christianity in particular with detailed textual criticism, examining internal contradictions in the Torah and the Gospels according to the chains of transmission available to him. He also treated Islamic sects, including the Mu'tazila, the Ash'aris, the Shi'a, the Sufis, and the philosophers. His style is marked by sharp polemical intensity. He adopted a meticulous textual method in tracing the differences among copies of the Bible, which made the book a later reference in the literature of inter-religious debate.

### Ethics and Love: *Tawq al-Hamama*

In this literary work, which departs from the pattern of his juristic and polemical writings, Ibn Hazm analyzes the phenomenon of human love in psychological and social terms. He draws on his personal observations and the experiences of his contemporaries in Andalusian society. He discusses topics such as the signs of love, its causes, and its ailments, in a refined literary style that made the book a well-known text of medieval Arabic love literature.

### Theology and Logic

Ibn Hazm was among the few who used the tools of Aristotelian logic (as it had been translated into Arabic) in the service of a Zahiri approach opposed to juristic analogy. This appears to be a contradiction, and it was later used as a starting point for an academic discussion of the relation between Zahirism and rationality. He also wrote a treatise on logic, *al-Taqrib li-Hadd al-Mantiq*. He also wrote on distinguishing the certain matters of the fundamentals of religion, which do not admit independent reasoning, from the speculative matters of jurisprudence, in which disagreement is permissible.

## Impact

The Zahiri school remained a minority after Ibn Hazm and never spread institutionally like the four great schools. However, his usul ideas in criticizing analogy influenced later usul debates within the other schools. Some modern reformers found in him a reference for calling for the freeing of jurisprudence from school-based imitation. His book *al-Fasl* became a founding text in an Arab-Islamic tradition of comparative religious study. Al-Shahrastani wrote a later work on religions and sects, *al-Milal wa al-Nihal*, with a different method and tone. In modern scholarship, Ibn Hazm received early attention from Ignaz Goldziher, who wrote the classic study of the Zahiri school (1884).

## Limitations

Ibn Hazm was sharply criticized by jurists of other schools, especially the Malikis of al-Andalus. He was accused of harshness and rigidity in his polemical style, to the point that a saying was attributed to his critics that "Ibn Hazm's tongue and al-Hajjaj's sword are brothers," describing the harshness of his polemics. From a usul standpoint, most jurists objected that his absolute rejection of analogy paralyzes the treatment of new cases for which no explicit text exists. For them, analogy is the deduction of the rationale ('illa) of a ruling from the text, not an addition to it. Therefore, rejecting it outright narrows the scope of legal reasoning more than it preserves commitment to the text. Some modern scholars have observed that Ibn Hazm's literal adherence to the apparent meaning sometimes clashes with his practical need, in particular in specific juristic questions, for a kind of reasoning that does not differ much in substance from what he rejects in his opponents. Tensions thus exist within his system that were never fully resolved.

As for his book *al-Fasl*, despite its historical value, some contemporary scholars (see Adang's study) have described its high polemical tone toward Judaism and Christianity as bringing it closer to a defensive debate than to a neutral descriptive study by modern academic standards. Some of his judgments on contradictions in the sacred texts rely on the limited copies and sources available to him in al-Andalus rather than on a comprehensive survey.

## Major Works

- **al-Fasl fi al-Milal wa al-Ahwa' wa al-Nihal**: on the comparative study of religions and sects.
- **al-Ihkam fi Usul al-Ahkam**: on Zahiri legal theory.
- **al-Muhalla bi al-Athar**: an applied jurisprudential encyclopedia in the Zahiri school.
- **Tawq al-Hamama fi al-Ulfa wa al-Ullaf** (*The Ring of the Dove*): on the analysis of love.
- **Jamharat Ansab al-Arab**: on the genealogies of the Arabs.

## Ministerial posts under the Umayyad claimants

The order of his political offices is reconstructed from his own remarks and from later biographical notices, which do not all agree in detail. After exile from Almeria, he went to Valencia and supported Abd al-Rahman al-Murtada (Abd al-Rahman IV), who had been proclaimed caliph in 408/1018, and was rewarded with a vizierate. When al-Murtada was killed, Ibn Hazm was again pressed and imprisoned. He returned to Cordoba during the caliphate of al-Qasim ibn Hammud (409/1018) and remained until 412/1021. He then served as vizier to Abd al-Rahman V al-Mustazhir, proclaimed in Ramadan 414/December 1023. That caliphate lasted only weeks: al-Mustazhir was killed in 414/1024, and his successor, Muhammad III al-Mustakfi (1024–1025), had Ibn Hazm arrested. Ibn Hazm himself ties that arrest to his friendship with al-Mustazhir. One reading of the sources takes the detention as lasting until Hisham III al-Mu'tadd took the caliphate (1027–1031). Yaqut adds that Ibn Hazm also held the vizierate briefly under Hisham III. He wrote *Tawq al-Hamama* at Xativa in 417/1026, and from about that time he largely left politics.

## The scale of his writings

A note transmitted from his son Abu Rafi' al-Fadl, and recorded by Ibn Bashkuwal, says that what his father wrote, some 80,000 folios in about 400 volumes, passed to him. That figure is a report of bulk, not a catalogue. Notices preserve the titles of about 150 of those works. No surviving list accounts for all 400 volumes, and a large part of the corpus is lost. Modern bibliographies, including the inventory in the 2013 Brill volume on Ibn Hazm, collect the titles that the classical sources happen to name, across fiqh, theology, history, genealogy and literature.
