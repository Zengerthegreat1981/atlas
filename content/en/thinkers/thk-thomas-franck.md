# Thomas M. Franck

**Thomas Franck** (1931–2009), born in Europe and later a Canadian and American citizen, was a professor of international law at New York University. He is regarded as a prominent theorist of **legitimacy and compliance** in contemporary international law, through his seminal book *The Power of Legitimacy Among Nations* (1990). That book posed a central question: why do states comply with international law despite the absence of a coercive central authority that imposes it on them?

## Legitimacy as an independent source of binding force

Franck proposes that states' compliance with international rules is not explained only by calculations of self-interest or fear of sanctions, but by an inner sense of **legitimacy** that makes the rule appear binding in itself. He identifies four main determinants of the legitimacy of any international legal rule: **determinacy** (how clear the content of the rule is, and how capable it is of application without ambiguity), **symbolic validation** (its connection to rituals and symbols that lend it institutional prestige), **coherence** (its consistency with other recognized rules and principles), and **adherence** (its inclusion within a recognized procedural system for making and amending rules). The more these four elements are present in a particular rule, the more likely states are to comply with it voluntarily.

## Critique of political realism in international relations

Franck directly critiques the **realist** school in international relations, which reduces states' behavior to calculations of power and abstract self-interest. He points to the widespread, routine compliance of states with international legal rules even when doing so does not serve their immediate interest. On his account, any explanation of this compliance is incomplete without assuming an independent normative force of legitimacy itself, not merely a reflection of the political balance of power.

## Democracy as an emerging right in international law

In his influential article "The Emerging Right to Democratic Governance" (1992), Franck argued that international law is witnessing the crystallization of an **emerging right to democratic governance**. He based this on the growing international requirement that governments hold free and fair elections in order to be regarded as legitimate, as reflected in international election-monitoring practices and United Nations resolutions. This thesis provoked wide debate about the limits of international law's intervention in states' choice of their internal systems of government.

## Procedural and distributive justice among nations

Later, in *Fairness in International Law and Institutions* (1995), Franck drew a distinction between **procedural justice** (the integrity of the process by which international rules are themselves made) and **distributive justice** (the fairness of distributing resources and opportunities between rich and poor states). He argued that the sustainability of the international legal system requires addressing both dimensions together, rather than being satisfied with a merely formal procedural legitimacy that ignores the deep economic power differences among states.

## Humanitarian intervention and the problem of legitimacy without explicit legality

Franck addressed the problem of unauthorized humanitarian military intervention, such as NATO's action over Kosovo in 1999 (not authorized by the Security Council), in his essays on the lessons of Kosovo. The formula that such an action may be "illegal but legitimate" is closely associated with the debate over Kosovo (notably the report of the Independent International Commission on Kosovo, 2000); Franck's discussion explored how far extreme moral necessity, such as preventing mass atrocity, could justify departing from the literal rules of the United Nations Charter, and what this does to the authority of those rules. This position provoked wide debate about the risks of weakening the authority of explicit formal rules in favor of loose moral judgments open to political misuse.

## Interdisciplinary method

Franck's intellectual project was distinguished by its methodological openness to sociology, anthropology, and social psychology in analyzing legal compliance. He rejected confinement to purely formal legal tools that limit themselves to analyzing texts without examining the actual social and psychological dynamics that make a rule appear binding in the eyes of international actors. This approach is associated with later interdisciplinary work in international law and international relations.

## Enduring legacy in the study of international legitimacy

Franck's theses on legitimacy have been widely used in analyzing later international crises, and he himself wrote on the United Nations after the Iraq intervention of 2003. His four determinants have been used by scholars to assess how far the international community accepts particular measures, regardless of their purely formal conformity with existing legal texts.

## Impact and critique

Franck's book contributed to the study of compliance with international law by moving beyond the realist and purely positivist reduction toward an analysis that combines law and social political science. Some scholars criticized it for the difficulty of measuring "legitimacy" empirically with sufficient precision, and because its four determinants may sometimes conflict with one another, with no clear criterion for weighting one over another when they clash. Others regarded it as a bridge between pure legal theory and the actual international political reality.

## Practice as counsel and adviser

Alongside scholarship, Franck advised governments and appeared in inter-state disputes. During decolonization he helped draft constitutions for African states emerging from British rule, including Sierra Leone, Rhodesia (later Zimbabwe), and the arrangements that joined Tanganyika and Zanzibar as Tanzania, and he served as legal adviser to the governments of Chad, Kenya, Mauritius, the Solomon Islands, and El Salvador. He took part in early negotiations that fed into the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea. Before the International Court of Justice he was counsel for Chad in its territorial dispute with Libya and, from 1994 until the 2007 judgment, for Bosnia and Herzegovina in the genocide case against Serbia and Montenegro. In 2001 Indonesia named him judge ad hoc in the Ligitan and Sipadan case with Malaysia, the first time a state other than the United States had appointed an American to that role. At his death he was leading Macedonia’s effort to challenge Greece’s objection to its NATO membership. He treated litigation as a way to settle political disputes in legal language.
