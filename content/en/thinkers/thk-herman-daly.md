# Herman Daly

**Herman Daly** was an American economist. He taught at Louisiana State University, worked as a senior economist in the environment department of the World Bank (1988–1994), and then became a professor at the University of Maryland's School of Public Policy. He was a leading figure in ecological economics and a persistent critic of treating unlimited economic growth as a policy goal without physical constraint.

## From Inside the World Bank to a Critic of Growth

In the late 1980s and early 1990s Daly worked as an environmental economist at the World Bank, a position that gave him direct insight into how the largest international development institution designed its policies on the basis of continuous growth as the sole standard of economic success. This experience reinforced his conviction that the assumption ignores a basic physical fact: the economy is a subsystem within a larger, resource-limited ecological system, not an isolated system that can expand indefinitely.

## The Steady-State Economy

In his book *Steady-State Economics* (1977), Daly presented an explicit alternative to the idea of unlimited economic growth: an economy that maintains a relatively constant size of physical capital and population over time. In such an economy, the rate of natural resource consumption and waste generation remains within the capacity of the surrounding ecological systems to regenerate and absorb them without cumulative degradation. This replaces the continuous pursuit of ever-increasing material accumulation, which implicitly assumes unlimited resources and absorptive capacity for the planet.

## The Second Law of Thermodynamics as the Basis of Analysis

Daly grounded his position in the second law of thermodynamics (entropy). Drawing on Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen, under whom he studied at Vanderbilt University, he argued that economic activity transforms low-entropy matter and energy (ordered natural resources) into high-entropy waste and dissipated heat. That transformation is physically irreversible, whatever the technology used.

## Critique of the Idea of "Decoupling" Growth from Resource Use

Daly confronted economists who argue that technological innovation can gradually "decouple" economic growth from the consumption of physical resources. He and other ecological economists contend that such absolute decoupling has not been demonstrated on the global scale required to address environmental crises such as climate change, and that relative efficiency gains are often offset by an increase in total consumption (sometimes called the "Jevons paradox"), so that the effect of efficiency can disappear at the aggregate level.

## Distinguishing Growth from Development

Daly carefully distinguished conceptually between "growth" (a quantitative increase in the physical size of the economy) and "development" (a qualitative improvement in the quality of life, without necessarily any physical increase). He argued that an economy stable in physical size could continue to "develop" qualitatively (better education, higher arts, deeper social relations) without any need for further continuous material growth. He drew this distinction to avoid having his position read as a call for comprehensive civilisational stagnation.

## Influence on the Later Degrowth Movement

Daly's work laid the intellectual groundwork for the later European degrowth movement, though it differed from it in emphasis. Daly preferred "steady state" (stabilisation of size at a certain level) to explicit "degrowth" (deliberate contraction). He held that the most suitable goal is to reach a sustainable level and then stabilise there, not to continue deliberate contraction indefinitely. This is a subtle but important distinction between his current and the more radical degrowth current.

## An academic platform for ecological economics

In 1989 Daly was among the founders of the International Society for Ecological Economics. The society's journal, *Ecological Economics*, began the same year, with Robert Costanza as its first editor. With the theologian John B. Cobb Jr. he also wrote *For the Common Good* (1989), which proposed an Index of Sustainable Economic Welfare as an alternative to GDP. This provided an independent academic platform that distinguished the field from the mainstream environmental economics, which treats the environment primarily as a source of external distortions that can be corrected through pricing within the standard neoclassical framework, without questioning the assumption of continuous growth itself, which Daly rejected.

## Critique of International Development Institutions

Daly levelled a direct critique at his former institution, the World Bank, and at the International Monetary Fund. He argued that their development policies, based on maximising economic growth as a higher goal, ignore hard physical ecological limits. He called for a radical institutional reform that would redefine "development success" by standards that take environmental sustainability into explicit account, rather than treating it as a marginal addition to the core growth objective.

## A Legacy Extending to Raworth and Later Generations

Daly's conceptual framework has directly influenced the work of later researchers such as Kate Raworth, who subsequently developed a visual model ("doughnut economics") that shares Daly's basic idea of ecological limits on economic activity, and adds an explicit social dimension concerning the guarantee of a minimum level of human well-being. This has made Daly a foundational reference, cited continuously in the later literature of ecological economics and the degrowth movement, despite his precise differences from both in the details.
