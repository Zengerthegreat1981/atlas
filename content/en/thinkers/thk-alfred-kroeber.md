# Alfred L. Kroeber

Alfred Louis Kroeber (1876–1960) was an American anthropologist and the most influential of Franz Boas's first students. As the founder of anthropology at the University of California, Berkeley, he built one of the great departments of the discipline, documented the Indigenous cultures of California before many of their languages disappeared, and developed the concepts of the "superorganic" and of culture areas that shaped American anthropology for half a century. His life also carries one of the discipline's most troubling episodes: his relationship with Ishi, the last surviving member of the Yahi people.

## Life and Career

Kroeber was born in Hoboken, New Jersey, in 1876, to a family of German immigrants, and studied at Columbia University, where he became Franz Boas's first doctoral student in anthropology. His dissertation on Arapaho decorative art earned him in 1901 the first PhD in anthropology awarded by Columbia. That same year he moved to the University of California, Berkeley, where he founded the anthropology department and its museum, and where he taught until his retirement in 1946. Over four decades he conducted fieldwork among Native peoples of California — the Yurok, Karok, Mojave, and many others — recording languages, myths, and social organisation at a moment when Californian Indigenous communities were under severe pressure. From 1911 to 1916 he worked closely with Ishi, a Yahi man who had emerged from hiding in northern California after his people were destroyed; Ishi lived at the university museum in San Francisco, where Kroeber and his colleague Thomas Waterman recorded his language and stories. Kroeber was twice married; his second wife, Theodora Kroeber, wrote the celebrated biography *Ishi in Two Worlds* (1961). His daughter was the novelist Ursula K. Le Guin. Kroeber died in Paris in 1960 while travelling in Europe.

## Key Works

*Anthropology* (1923), revised in an expanded edition in 1948, was the standard American textbook of the discipline for decades. *Handbook of the Indians of California* (1925) remains a monumental reference work, assembling ethnographic and linguistic data on the Indigenous groups of California. *Configurations of Culture Growth* (1944) is his most ambitious comparative study, tracing waves of creative achievement in philosophy, science, and art across civilisations including Greece, China, India, and the Islamic world. His essay "The Superorganic" (1917) argued that culture has a logic of its own, irreducible to individual psychology or biology. With Clyde Kluckhohn he wrote *Culture: A Critical Review of Concepts and Definitions* (1952), a landmark survey of more than 160 definitions of culture. His early monographs include *The Arapaho* (1902–1907) and studies of Californian basketry, kinship, and religion.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Kroeber's central idea was that culture is "superorganic": it exists above the level of the individual organism, follows its own patterns of growth and change, and cannot be explained by reducing it to psychology or biology. From this followed his interest in configurations — the distinctive patterns of value, style, and achievement that characterise a civilisation at a given period — and in culture areas, the geographic regions within which neighbouring peoples share traits through diffusion and common history. He was a leading exponent of diffusionism in American anthropology, arguing that cultural traits spread from centres of innovation, and he mapped the culture areas of North America in detail. His work on the growth of civilisations was an early form of comparative history: he treated the flourishing of Greek philosophy, Chinese thought, and Islamic science as comparable waves of creativity, each with its own rhythm of rise and decline. He also helped institutionalise the four-field structure of American anthropology — archaeology, linguistics, physical anthropology, and cultural anthropology — through his teaching and his students, who included Julian Steward.

## Influence and Critique

Kroeber's influence on American anthropology was institutional as well as intellectual: Berkeley under his leadership trained generations of anthropologists, and his *Handbook* remains indispensable for Californian Indigenous studies. His concept of culture areas shaped archaeology and ethnography, and his defence of culture as an autonomous order fed into the cultural relativism associated with Boas. His comparative study of civilisations anticipated later world-historical approaches. But his work has also drawn serious criticism. The "superorganic" was attacked as metaphysical — Leslie White and others argued that it reified culture and removed human agency from history. More gravely, his treatment of Ishi has become a byword for the ethics of salvage anthropology: Ishi was exhibited to the public as the "last wild Indian," his brain was removed after his death in 1916 for study, and his remains were only repatriated to his descendants decades later. Kroeber's own writings show ambivalence about the episode, but the criticism stands: the man who documented the destruction of Californian peoples was also, however unwillingly, part of the institutions that profited from it. His daughter's fiction, shaped by the anthropological household in which she grew up, remains an unexpected part of his legacy.
