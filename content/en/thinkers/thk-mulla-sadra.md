# Mulla Sadra (Sadr al-Din Shirazi)

The greatest Muslim philosopher of the Safavid era, and one of the greatest philosophers of Islam after Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd. He was born in Shiraz and studied under "Mir Damad" (the master of the School of Isfahan), then withdrew for years to the village of Kahak near Qom, and afterwards returned to Shiraz to teach.

## Life and Formation

Muhammad ibn Ibrahim al-Shirazi was born in Shiraz around 1571–1572 into a well-off family, and received in Isfahan, the capital of the Safavid state, lessons in jurisprudence and hadith from Baha' al-Din al-Amili and in philosophy from Mir Damad. He then returned to Shiraz at the invitation of its governor and established teaching at the Khan madrasa. He died in Basra in 1635/1636 on his way to the pilgrimage, according to the best-known accounts.

His environment was Safavid Isfahan, which brought together philosophers, gnostics and jurists in open competition, and on its soil arose a school that combined the heritage of Ibn Sina, Suhrawardi and Ibn Arabi; Mulla Sadra was the fruit of this environment.

## What He Offered

- **"The Four Journeys" (Al-Hikma al-Muta'aliya fi al-Asfar al-Aqliyya al-Arba'a)**: a huge work, in nine volumes in the current edition, organized around four intellectual journeys: from creation to the Real, in the Real by the Real, from the Real to creation by the Real, and in creation by the Real. It is an Arabic book in the original.
- **"Existence is the principle of existents" (Asalat al-Wujud)**: existence is "principial" (Asl) and quiddity (Mahiyya) is "derivative" — Sadra presents this as a reversal of the quiddity-centred position of the earlier tradition, including (in his reading) Suhrawardi and Ibn Sina.
- **"Substantial motion" (al-Haraka al-Jawhariyya)**: substance changes "in itself," not merely accidentally. By this he explains the change of the natural world in an existential way, and time is understood as an extension of this motion and a measure of it.
- **"Gnostic knowledge" ('Irfan)**: inward (intuitive) knowledge is necessary for understanding existence; rational demonstration alone is not enough.
- **Reconciliation of philosophy, Sufism and gnosis**: "Transcendent Wisdom" gathers together Peripatetic philosophy (Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd), Illuminationism (Suhrawardi) and Gnosticism (Ibn Arabi).

## Method: Demonstration, Unveiling and Text

In the introduction to the "Asfar," Mulla Sadra declares that he gathers three sources: rational demonstration, mystical unveiling, and the religious text of Qur'an and hadith. He sees no contradiction among them, for the text for him supports what reason and unveiling reach if it is understood inwardly, and this explains the breadth of his Qur'anic exegesis and his attempt to read the verses of creation and resurrection existentially.

## The Ideas in the Balance

The affirmation of the primacy of existence is the most important thing that distinguished him from Suhrawardi, who held the primacy of quiddity, and from Ibn Sina, who made existence accidental to quiddity. He holds the gradation (tashkik) of existence, that is, that existence is a single reality with degrees of intensity and weakness, and thereby explains multiplicity without squandering unity. From substantial motion follows the view that the corporeal world originated in time, and that the soul is corporeal in origination and spiritual in subsistence, a position that contradicts the apparent view of the Peripatetics and founds his conception of bodily resurrection.

## His Place in the Current

- **Founder of Transcendent Wisdom** as an independent Islamic philosophical current, the most important since Ibn Sina.
- **His opponents**: literalist jurists in Isfahan and Shiraz in his time, who accused him of Sufism and of departing from the literal sense.
- **Impact**: on all later Muslim philosophers (Abd al-Razzaq Lahiji, Mulla Hadi Sabzawari, Tabataba'i), and on twentieth-century Iranian religious thought (Khomeini, for example, studied and taught in the school of Transcendent Wisdom).

## Reception and Influence in the Arab World

Sadrian thought was received in the religious seminaries, especially Najaf, where the "Asfar" is taught and commented upon, and Tabataba'i wrote "Bidayat al-Hikma" and "Nihayat al-Hikma" in Arabic, mixed with the Sadrian method. The "Asfar" has been published in Arabic in several editions, among them the Dar Ihya' al-Turath al-Arabi edition in Beirut (1981). Westerners took an interest in him thanks to Henry Corbin, then the studies of Fazlur Rahman and Seyyed Hossein Nasr.

## Critique and Limits

His traditional opponents criticize his mixing of philosophy with gnostic intuition in a way that weakens demonstrative reasoning, and some moderns criticize him for being a closed metaphysical system that does not open onto the empirical sciences. Scholars observe that Sadrian language is dense with technical terms, so the reader needs the commentaries of Tabataba'i and Sabzawari to understand it.

## Chronology of His Works and Late Period

Scholars (such as Seyyed Hossein Nasr and Sajjad Rizvi) divide Mulla Sadra's intellectual output into three main phases: an early preparatory period of study in Isfahan under Mir Damad and Shaykh Baha'i (up to c. 1605); a prolonged middle phase of spiritual seclusion and philosophical formulation in Kahak near Qom (c. 1605–1620), during which the foundational drafts of the *Asfar* were composed; and a final mature teaching period in Shiraz at the Khan Madrasa (c. 1620 until his death in 1635/1636).

His late Shiraz period witnessed the completion and refinement of his masterworks and a concentrated turn toward scriptural hermeneutics:
- **Metaphysical syntheses**: He authored mature summaries of his system, notably *Al-Shawahid al-Rububiyya* (Divine Witnesses)—composed as a definitive textbook of Transcendent Theosophy—alongside *Al-Hikma al-Arshiyya* (The Wisdom of the Throne) focusing on eschatology, and *Mafatih al-Ghayb* (Keys to the Unseen).
- **Philosophical commentaries**: During this late phase he composed detailed marginal glosses (*Hashiya*) on Suhrawardi's *Hikmat al-Ishraq* (specifically on Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi's commentary) and on the metaphysics of Ibn Sina's *Kitab al-Shifa'*.
- **Scriptural hermeneutics and Hadith commentary**: His final years were dominated by extensive Qur'anic exegesis (*Tafsir al-Qur'an al-Karim*, including commentaries on Ayat al-Kursi, Surah Ya-Sin, and Surah al-Jumu'a) and his profound, uncompleted philosophical commentary on al-Kulayni's Shi'i hadith compendium, *Sharh Usul al-Kafi*.

## Most Important Works

- The Four Intellectual Journeys (Al-Asfar al-Arba'a)
- Al-Shawahid al-Rububiyya
- Mafatih al-Ghayb
- Iksir al-Arifin
- Al-Mabda' wal-Ma'ad
- Commentaries on Qur'anic suras and verses (tafsir)
- Glosses on Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi's commentary on Suhrawardi's "Hikmat al-Ishraq"
- Commentary on al-Kulayni's Usul al-Kafi
