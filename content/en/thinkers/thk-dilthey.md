# Wilhelm Dilthey

A German philosopher, who gave the classic formulation of the distinction between the "natural sciences" (Naturwissenschaften) and the "human sciences / sciences of spirit" (Geisteswissenschaften), and the first reference for the idea that understanding the human being (Verstehen) differs radically from explaining natural phenomena.

## What He Contributed

He argued that psychological and historical life is not grasped by mechanical causal explanation (Erklären) as in the natural sciences, but by hermeneutic understanding (Verstehen), which reconstructs meaning from within. This distinction became the cornerstone of every later phenomenological or hermeneutic method in the human sciences and psychiatry.

## His Place in the Current

He was an older contemporary of Husserl, and his influence extended directly to Karl Jaspers (who adopted the distinction of understanding/explanation literally in *General Psychopathology*) and then to the whole line of phenomenological psychopathology that branched from Jaspers.

## Limitation

He did not use the term "existentialism" nor practice phenomenology in the strict Husserlian sense; he is included here as a direct methodological forerunner, not a member of the school.

## Major Works

*Introduction to the Human Sciences* (Einleitung in die Geisteswissenschaften, 1883) · *The Formation of the Historical World in the Human Sciences* (Der Aufbau der geschichtlichen Welt in den Geisteswissenschaften, 1910).

## Biography

Wilhelm Dilthey was born on 19 November 1833 in Biebrich near Wiesbaden, Germany, into the family of a Protestant pastor, and studied theology in Heidelberg and then philosophy and history in Berlin, where he was influenced by the historical school and by the heritage of Schleiermacher. He worked as a professor in Basel (1867), then Kiel (1868), then Breslau (1871), and moved in 1882 to Berlin as Lotze's successor in the chair of philosophy. He began by studying Schleiermacher and published the first volume of *The Life of Schleiermacher* (1870), then devoted his life to a methodical foundation for the human sciences. He died on 1 October 1911 in Seis am Schlern in South Tyrol.

## The Philosophy of Life and Lived Experience

Dilthey makes "life" the starting point: human life is not empirical matter external to consciousness but is "lived experience" (Erlebnis), in which thoughts, feelings and wills are joined in one whole. From this experience comes expression (Ausdruck) in language, art and institutions, and understanding (Verstehen) consists in moving from the expression to the experience that produced it. For this reason the human sciences rest on the capacity of the human being to understand the human being, because the two sides are of one nature. He linked this to history: the individual understands himself only through what is carried by the works, texts and institutions formed in the past.

## Descriptive Psychology and Interpretation

In 1894 Dilthey published *Ideas Concerning a Descriptive and Analytical Psychology*, in which he criticized explanatory psychology based on causal hypotheses and held that psychology should describe psychic life in its unity as it is lived, which provoked a reply from Hermann Ebbinghaus, who accused him of abandoning experiment. He then turned in *The Rise of Hermeneutics* (1900) to making interpretation the general method of the human sciences, tracing the line from Schleiermacher. Among his literary works is *Poetry and Experience* (1905), and among his late works is *The Formation of the Historical World in the Human Sciences* (1910), in which he tried to ground what he called the "critique of historical reason" on the concepts of significance, structure, value and purpose.

## The Theory of Worldviews

Dilthey set out models of the great worldviews, and held that philosophies express three recurring types, namely naturalism, idealism of freedom and objective idealism, and that each type springs from an attitude to life rather than from a conclusive demonstration. In this view was a recognition that metaphysics is not a science in the strict sense but an expression of the meaning of life in a given age.

## Influence and Reception

Dilthey influenced Heidegger, who praised him in *Being and Time* (1927) and built part of his analysis of historicity on his reading; Gadamer, who developed hermeneutics in *Truth and Method* (1960) and at the same time criticized the psychologism of understanding in Dilthey; Jaspers, who carried the dyad of understanding and explanation over to psychiatry; and Max Weber, Georg Misch and others. His works appeared in the *Gesammelte Schriften*, which continues to expand, and in English selections edited by Makkreel and Rodi.

## Limits and Critique

Husserl in "Philosophy as Rigorous Science" (1911) criticized what he saw as historical relativism in Dilthey that ends in discarding the objective value of knowledge. Others held against him that the critique of historical reason remained incomplete, so that the project remained fragments and drafts. The positivists criticized the sharp distinction between the natural sciences and the human sciences, arguing that causal explanation can encompass human phenomena. His emphasis on lived experience and psychological empathy as the basis of understanding also carries the risk of reducing interpretation to the author's psychological states, which hermeneutics after him has moved beyond.
