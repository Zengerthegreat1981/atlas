# Joan Bresnan

An American linguist, co-founder with Ronald Kaplan of Lexical-Functional Grammar, and one of the earliest prominent critical voices against the complexity of transformations in Chomsky's generative grammar.

## Critique of transformational complexity

Bresnan began from a critical methodological observation: the transformational model of Chomsky (especially in its early phase) assumes complex syntactic movement operations that are difficult to justify processually. From the perspective of psycholinguistics, the question is how the brain actually processes these transformations in real time. In her 1978 paper "A Realistic Transformational Grammar" and later work she proposed instead a "monostratal" model that assumes no transformational movement between successive structural levels.

## Functional structure

Lexical-Functional Grammar represents a sentence through two parallel representations: the constituent structure (the surface word order) and the functional structure (grammatical relations such as subject and object, independent of surface order and derived from information contained in the lexicon for each verb). This model made it possible to describe typologically diverse languages (including languages with free word order) without assuming movement transformations.

## Lexical mapping theory

A further contribution is Lexical Mapping Theory, a formal framework that explains how thematic roles (such as agent and patient) are linked to grammatical functions (subject and object) through general principles that systematically govern this mapping. It explains phenomena such as the passive voice (in which the patient role is mapped to the subject function rather than the object) through general morpho-syntactic rules instead of movement transformations.

## Computational linguistics and the move to Stanford

Bresnan, who earned her doctorate at MIT in 1972, later moved to Stanford University, where she was associated with work in computational linguistics. The parallel structure of Lexical-Functional Grammar (representing both the constituent structure and the functional structure without complex computational transformations) made it easier to build practical computational parsers more efficiently than transformational models, which were harder to implement computationally. The framework has been used in several research natural language processing projects, such as the ParGram grammar-writing effort.

## Fieldwork on Bantu languages

Bresnan worked on Bantu languages, especially Chichewa (with Sam Mchombo), using agreement and relatively free word order to test Lexical-Functional Grammar on languages typologically distant from English. Those analyses describe the phenomena through lexical information and functional structure rather than through movement transformations.

## Impact

With Ronald Kaplan she developed Lexical-Functional Grammar as a formal alternative to transformational accounts of grammatical relations. The framework has been used in computational parsing (including the ParGram project) and in descriptions of languages outside Europe. An international LFG conference still meets.

## Glue semantics and the integration of grammar with meaning

"Glue Semantics," developed within the LFG community chiefly by Mary Dalrymple and colleagues, is a formal mechanism for linking the grammatical representation in Lexical-Functional Grammar (the functional structure) to semantic interpretation through a flexible linear logic. This allows meaning to be composed without assuming a strict literal parallelism between the surface grammatical structure and the semantic composition step by step. It extended the application of the framework to advanced semantic analyses that go beyond the purely grammatical representation that characterized the framework's earliest formulations.

## Probabilistic linguistics and grammatical variation

In a later phase of her career, Bresnan developed an interest in probabilistic (stochastic) models for explaining grammatical variation within a single language (such as the preference for one grammatical structure over another equivalent in meaning in some contexts but not others). She combined the formal tools of Lexical-Functional Grammar with statistical methodologies drawn from computational linguistics. This was regarded as an important bridge between theoretical formal grammar and the statistical modeling of actual language use.

## Academic supervision and establishing a research school

Bresnan supervised generations of graduate students at Stanford who continued developing Lexical-Functional Grammar and its computational applications. Together with Ronald Kaplan, she founded an international research network (which meets regularly at the annual LFG conference) that still produces research analyzing diverse languages within this framework today, four decades after its first formulation.
