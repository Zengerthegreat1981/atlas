# Claude Lévi-Strauss: Structural Anthropology and Mythological Analysis

Claude Lévi-Strauss (1908–2009) was a French anthropologist, born in Brussels, who founded structural anthropology and became one of the most influential intellectuals of the twentieth century. Drawing on the structural linguistics of Ferdinand de Saussure and Roman Jakobson, he treated kinship systems, myths, and classificatory schemes as systems of relations governed by underlying mental structures, rather than as products of history or as functional solutions to social needs. His major works are *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949) and the four volumes of *Mythologiques* (1964–1971).

## Life and Career

Lévi-Strauss was born in Brussels on 28 November 1908 to a French Jewish family and grew up in Paris. He studied law and philosophy at the Sorbonne, passed the agrégation in philosophy in 1931, and taught briefly in secondary schools. In 1935 he took up a post in sociology at the University of São Paulo, and between 1935 and 1939 he made field expeditions in Brazil, working among the Caduveo and Bororo and later among the Nambikwara and Tupi-Kawahib. These stays were short by later standards and were not the basis of extended ethnographic monographs, but they informed his later work and his memoir *Tristes Tropiques* (1955).

During the Second World War he was in New York, teaching at the École libre des hautes études, where he met Roman Jakobson; the encounter with structural linguistics shaped his thought. His formation also drew on Jean-Jacques Rousseau and on the Durkheimian tradition associated with Marcel Mauss. After returning to France he worked at the Musée de l'Homme and the École pratique des hautes études, held the chair of social anthropology at the Collège de France from 1959, and was elected to the Académie française in 1973. He died in Paris in 2009, a few weeks short of his 101st birthday.

## Method: Structural Analysis

Lévi-Strauss held that cultural phenomena such as kinship terms or myths resemble linguistic phenomena in that their meaning derives from relations among elements, not from the elements in isolation. He analysed cultural material by identifying contrasts and transformations among terms, for example between nature and culture, raw and cooked, or fresh and rotten. He often presented these relations in formal or quasi-mathematical schemes, such as his formula for myth. He argued that this method disclosed the unconscious logic of the human mind, and that "primitive" and "modern" thought operate with the same intellectual capacities, a claim he developed in *La pensée sauvage*. He treated myth as a way of restating contradictions that ordinary thought cannot settle, through further oppositions and transformations. Much of the material was comparative: myths, kinship terminologies, and classifications published by other ethnographers, read across regions rather than taken only from his own Brazilian stays.

## Major Works

- **The Elementary Structures of Kinship** (1949; revised edition 1967; English translation 1969): argues that the incest prohibition and the exchange of women (understood as the exchange of relationships among groups) underlie kinship systems, and analyses elementary systems of prescribed marriage. It builds on Marcel Mauss's theory of reciprocity.
- **Tristes Tropiques** (1955): a memoir and meditation on his Brazilian travels and on anthropology.
- **Structural Anthropology** (1958; English translation 1963): essays setting out his programme, including "The Structural Study of Myth."
- **Totemism** (1962) and **The Savage Mind** (1962): the first argues that totemism is better understood as a way of classifying the social world through natural differences than as a distinct religion; the second analyses the "science of the concrete" and the bricoleur's way of thinking with signs drawn from the natural environment.
- **Mythologiques** (1964–1971): *The Raw and the Cooked*, *From Honey to Ashes*, *The Origin of Table Manners*, and *The Naked Man*. A vast analysis of several hundred myths, mostly of the indigenous peoples of the Americas, showing them as transformations of one another.

## Influence

Structuralism spread from anthropology into literary theory, semiotics, psychoanalysis, and philosophy, and was associated in France with thinkers such as Roland Barthes, Jacques Lacan, and Louis Althusser. Within anthropology, Edmund Leach introduced and critiqued Lévi-Strauss's ideas in Britain, and Mary Douglas drew on structural contrasts in her work on classification and purity.

## Critiques

Critics argued that the search for universal structures neglects history, practice, power, and individual agency, and that the binary oppositions identified in myth are partly imposed by the analyst. British anthropologists such as Leach and Rodney Needham questioned his treatment of kinship and alliance, and ethnographers of Amazonia pointed out that his Brazilian material sometimes oversimplifies local complexity. Later scholars, including Pierre Bourdieu, argued for a theory of practice instead of structure, and postcolonial critics noted that his analysis of indigenous mythology drew on collected texts rather than sustained dialogue with the communities concerned. Feminist readings, notably Gayle Rubin's "The Traffic in Women" (1975), used his model of the exchange of women as the starting point for a critique of how kinship organises sex and gender. Nonetheless, a number of later anthropologists, including Philippe Descola and Eduardo Viveiros de Castro, have returned to his work on Amerindian thought.
