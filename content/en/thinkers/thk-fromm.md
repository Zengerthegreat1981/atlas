# Erich Fromm

A prominent German-American psychoanalyst, social philosopher, and humanist (1900–1980), born in Frankfurt to an Orthodox Jewish family. He studied sociology at the University of Heidelberg and trained analytically at the Berlin Psychoanalytic Institute, before emigrating to the United States in 1934 to escape the rise of Nazism. He was one of the leading figures of the "Frankfurt School" of critical theory in its first phase, and a leading exponent of a current that combined psychoanalysis with Marxist social critique and existential philosophy and humanism.

## Historical Context

Around 1930 Fromm joined the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt (Institut für Sozialforschung), where he worked with Max Horkheimer and other colleagues on a collective project to understand how individuals identify psychologically with authority even though it oppresses them. This was the question that framed all of his later work. After emigrating to America, he gradually separated from the Institute and developed an independent line that blends Freud's analysis of the unconscious with Marx's critique of alienation. He rejected both Freud's determinism of the sexual instinct and the determinism of economic structure held by some Marxists, in favor of a humanist conception of basic existential needs (belonging, transcendence, identity, a frame of orientation).

## Main Ideas

In his seminal book *Escape from Freedom* (1941), Fromm presented a psychological and historical analysis of the "dilemma of human freedom". As the primary ties (family, sect, traditional community) dissolve in modern society, the individual gains negative freedom (freedom from constraints) but loses the security those ties provided. An anxiety of isolation arises that drives him either to "mechanisms of escape" (authoritarian submission, destructiveness, or automaton conformity) or to positive freedom through love and authentic creative work that connect him to the world without losing his individuality. He applied this framework to analyzing the rise of Nazism as a collective flight from the burden of modern individual freedom. In *The Art of Loving* (1956) he developed a conception of love as a capacity (an art) acquired through practice and discipline, not a spontaneous emotion one happens upon, distinguishing mature giving love from selfish childish attachment. In *Man for Himself* (1947) he formulated a classification of non-productive personality orientations (receptive, exploitative, hoarding, marketing) as against the "productive orientation" that unites reason, love, and creative work. Late in his career, in *To Have or to Be?* (1976), he advanced a pivotal distinction between the "having mode of existence", in which a person's worth is measured by the things, titles, and even ideas he possesses, and the "being mode of existence", which is realized in action and living relationship rather than in possession and accumulation.

His theory of "social character" holds that every society has a psychic structure shared among its individuals that makes them want what the social order requires of them, so that social necessity becomes personal desire. He tested this idea in the field in the study *Social Character in a Mexican Village* (1970, with Michael Maccoby), on the village of Chiconcuac, where he studied the peasants and the transformations brought about by the entry of the market and the modern economy. Among his other works are *The Sane Society* (1955), in which he criticized consumer capitalism as an alienated society, and *The Heart of Man* (1964), in which he distinguished between "love of life" (biophilia) and "love of death" (necrophilia) as opposing orientations in personality.

## His Place in the Movement

Fromm represents the broadest theoretical and social bridge between Freudian psychoanalysis, Marxist social critique, and the existential analysis of freedom, isolation, and alienation. This led to his classification among the "Neo-Freudians" alongside Karen Horney and Harry Stack Sullivan, although he was reserved about this narrow classification given the breadth of his political, religious, and philosophical interests.

His relationship with the Frankfurt School was severed in the late 1930s, when Horkheimer and his colleagues rejected his conception, which excluded libido theory and gave priority to social factors. A public dispute then followed with Herbert Marcuse in the journal *Dissent* (1955–1956), in which Marcuse, in the context of his reading of *Eros and Civilization*, accused him of emptying psychoanalysis of its critical force through his "revision" of Freud, and Fromm replied with a critique of Marcuse's view of instinctual liberation.

## Limitations

His focus on broad social and cultural critique led him to be occupied with the civilizational and political analysis of personality more than with microscopic individual clinical techniques, which led some orthodox analytic circles to classify his work as closer to social philosophy than to purely clinical psychoanalysis.

Among the limits of his work is that his field study in Mexico used an interpretive method relying on questionnaires and projective tests and then on the analysts' interpretation of them, so it is not free of researcher bias, and that his major concepts, such as social character and the having mode, were formulated in a moral, evaluative language that brings his books closer to humanist advocacy than to a testable theory.
