# Hyman Minsky

**Hyman Philip Minsky** was an American economist in the post-Keynesian tradition. For decades he remained relatively marginal in the dominant academic circles. The global financial crisis of 2008 then revived wide interest in his ideas on the financial fragility inherent in capitalism, and the term "Minsky moment" became common in economic and financial media to describe points of sudden financial collapse. The phrase was coined in 1998 by the investment strategist Paul McCulley, not by Minsky himself.

## Intellectual formation: from Keynes to Schumpeter

Minsky studied mathematics at the University of Chicago and then took his doctorate at Harvard University (1954), where his teachers included **Joseph Schumpeter** and **Wassily Leontief**. He was deeply influenced by a reading of Keynes's *General Theory* that focused on its less famous chapters concerning finance, speculation and decisions under uncertainty, rather than on the simplified tools of demand management that became widespread in the neoclassical–Keynesian synthesis dominant in academia.

## The financial instability hypothesis

Minsky developed his best-known contribution, the **financial instability hypothesis**. It holds that long-run economic stability carries the seeds of its own instability. As a period of apparent stability lengthens, borrowers and lenders gradually come to accept higher levels of risk and leverage. The financing structure of the economy then shifts through three stages: from "hedge" financing, which is safe, to "speculative" financing, and finally to "Ponzi" financing, which depends entirely on the continued rise of asset prices to service debts. Eventually the financial system becomes fragile enough to collapse from a relatively simple shock.

## Stability is destabilizing

Minsky formulated a famous principle: "stability is destabilizing". The longer apparent financial calm lasts, the more financial institutions and borrowers relax their assessment of risk. This makes financial crises an internal outcome of the dynamics of financial capitalism itself, not merely accidental external shocks. This conclusion fundamentally contradicts the assumption of efficient markets prevailing in traditional financial economics.

## Stabilizing an unstable economy: practical recommendations

Minsky gathered his theoretical ideas in *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986). In it he called for an active role for the central bank as "lender of last resort", for a large government sector whose spending and deficits cushion downturns ("Big Government" and "Big Bank"), and for careful government regulation of the financial system that limits the buildup of excessive leverage before a crisis occurs, rather than only after it. He held that the stability of the capitalist system requires permanent institutional intervention, not merely periodic management of aggregate demand.

## Revival after the 2008 crisis

Minsky's ideas remained relatively marginal in mainstream academic economics throughout his life and in the years after his death in 1996. The global financial crisis of 2008, however, which arose from a housing mortgage bubble driven by credit expansion and rising financial fragility over several years, seemed to many commentators to resemble Minsky's scenario closely. This revived academic and media attention to his work strongly, and the term "Minsky moment" came to describe the moment when a financial system that had seemed stable for a long time suddenly collapses.

## Later influence on financial economics

Minsky's ideas contributed to the development of a subfield of economics known as **monetary post-Keynesian economics**. It focuses on the role of banks, credit and financial fragility as central variables in macroeconomic analysis, rather than as a secondary and "neutral" factor, as some traditional neoclassical models assumed. Those models had largely neglected the financial sector before the 2008 crisis.

## A marginal institutional position throughout his career

Minsky taught at Brown University and the University of California, Berkeley, and spent most of his career (1965–1990) at Washington University in St. Louis, rather than at the elite universities dominated by reconciling Keynesian and monetarist economists. This partly reflects the marginality of his institutional position throughout his working life, despite the depth of his ideas. Later recognition after his death in 1996 gave him an academic standing that he had not received to the same extent during his lifetime.

## A renewed presence in contemporary financial debate

After 2008, Minsky's name went beyond academic economics into financial practice itself. Financial commentators and some investment professionals began citing his analysis when discussing credit cycles, and the term "Minsky moment" entered the vocabulary of market commentary. This was an unusual transition of a marginal academic idea into wider financial discussion, even though Minsky's theses were originally directed at criticizing that sector's excessive risk-taking rather than at providing investors with an additional predictive tool.
