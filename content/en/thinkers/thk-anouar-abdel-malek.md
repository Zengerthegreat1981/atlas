# Anouar Abdel-Malek

Anouar Abdel-Malek (1924-2012) was an Egyptian-born sociologist and political theorist who worked largely in France. He is best known for his early critique of Orientalism and for his work on civilizations, nations and the post-colonial world.

## Life and Context

Born in Egypt into a Coptic family, Abdel-Malek was active in Egyptian politics and left the country in the 1950s, settling in France. There he worked for many years at the Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique (CNRS). His thought was shaped by anti-colonial movements, Marxism and the rise of the Third World after 1945.

## Main Ideas

- **Critique of Orientalism.** In the essay "Orientalism in Crisis" (1963) he argued that European Orientalist scholarship treated Eastern societies as fixed essences, set apart from history and defined by the West. Edward Said later acknowledged this essay in *Orientalism* (1978).
- **Civilizations and nations.** He argued that societies should be studied through their own historical, national and civilizational paths, not through a single Western model of development.
- **Social dialectics.** He sought a sociology of the Global South that combined the study of national movements, class structure and culture, and that treated social science itself as shaped by hegemonic power.
- **Military society.** His study of Egypt after 1952 analysed the role of the army in the state and society.

## Major Works

- *Egypte, societe militaire* (1962)
- "Orientalism in Crisis," *Diogenes* (1963)
- *La pensee politique arabe contemporaine* (1970)
- *Social Dialectics*, vol. 1 *Civilizations and Social Theory* and vol. 2 *Nation and Revolution* (1981)

## Method

Abdel-Malek combined historical analysis, political sociology and comparative study of civilizations. He insisted that sociologists should be aware of their own position and that Western categories should not be assumed to be universal.

## Influence and Criticism

His critique of Orientalism helped open the way for postcolonial studies, although his name is less widely known than Said's. Critics have said that his notion of civilizational specificity risks essentialism of its own, and that his political commitments sometimes shape his analysis. His work remains most cited in debates on Orientalism, decolonizing knowledge and the sociology of the Arab world.
