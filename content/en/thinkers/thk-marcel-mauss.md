# Marcel Mauss

Marcel Mauss (1872–1950) was a French sociologist and anthropologist, the nephew and closest collaborator of Émile Durkheim, and the author of *The Gift* (1925), one of the most fertile short texts in the history of anthropology. Mauss never conducted fieldwork of his own, yet his comparative reading of other people's ethnography produced theories — of the gift, of the total social fact, of techniques of the body — that still organise research in economic anthropology, the study of ritual, and the anthropology of the person. He was the bridge by which the Durkheimian school crossed from sociology into anthropology.

## Life and Career

Mauss was born on 10 May 1872 in Épinal, in the Lorraine region of France, into a cultivated Jewish family committed to the secular Republic and the social sciences. He studied philosophy at Bordeaux under his uncle Émile Durkheim, who was then remaking French sociology, and later studied comparative religion and Sanskrit in Paris. From the founding of *L'Année Sociologique* in the late 1890s he was one of its principal contributors, writing reviews and essays that helped establish sociology as a rigorous comparative science. He was also active in the French socialist and cooperative movements.

Unlike his contemporaries, Mauss built his expertise from published ethnography, missionary reports, and traders' accounts rather than from fieldwork — a methodological choice that both enabled and limited his work. He served in the army during the First World War, and he taught at the École Pratique des Hautes Études and from 1931 held a chair in sociology at the Collège de France. The *Manuel d'ethnographie* (published posthumously in 1947 from his lecture courses) preserves his teaching on ethnographic method. He died in Paris on 10 February 1950, having trained a generation of French ethnologists. Claude Lévi-Strauss wrote the introduction to the collection of his essays published in 1950.

## Key Works

Mauss's most important work is the *Essai sur le don* (1925), published in the *Année Sociologique* (new series, 1923–24) and translated as *The Gift: The Form and Reason for Exchange in Archaic Societies*. In about a hundred pages it analyses gift-giving among Pacific and North American Indigenous peoples, with comparative material from ancient Roman, Indian, and Germanic law, and argues that gifts create obligations — to give, to receive, and to repay — that bind people together more durably than market exchange. Its central cases are the potlatch of the Northwest Coast, described by Franz Boas, and the kula ring of the Trobriand Islands, described by Bronisław Malinowski.

His earlier essay on sacrifice (1899), written with Henri Hubert, analysed ritual killing as a mechanism for establishing communication between the sacred and the profane worlds. The collection *Sociologie et anthropologie* (1950), introduced by Lévi-Strauss, gathered his scattered essays, including "Les techniques du corps" (a 1934 lecture) and "Une catégorie de l'esprit humain: la notion de personne, celle de 'moi'" (a 1938 lecture), which argued that personhood is a historically and culturally variable category, not a universal essence.

## Main Ideas: The Gift and the Total Social Fact

Mauss's central discovery was that economic behaviour is fundamentally social, not merely utilitarian. A gift is never free: it carries an obligation to return it, and the failure to repay brings shame and loss of status. Gift exchange therefore creates lasting social bonds, redistributes goods and prestige, and sustains alliances — functions that market exchange, with its immediate settlement of accounts, cannot perform. The three obligations of the gift — giving, receiving, repaying — became one of anthropology's most durable analytical tools.

Building on Durkheim, Mauss developed the concept of the *fait social total*, the total social fact: the idea that certain practices, such as the potlatch or the kula, mobilise economic, legal, religious, aesthetic, and moral dimensions all at once. No single analytical lens can explain them; they must be understood holistically, in their full social context. This move challenged the boundaries between economics, law, religion, and kinship studies.

Mauss also pioneered the study of *techniques du corps*, techniques of the body — the insight that bodily practices such as walking, eating, swimming, and working are culturally shaped and socially transmitted, not natural givens. The idea anticipated the modern anthropology of embodiment and material culture by half a century.

## Influence and Critique

Lévi-Strauss made *The Gift* a founding text of structural anthropology, reinterpreting gift exchange as a form of symbolic communication akin to language. In the 1960s and 1970s, economic anthropologists returned to Mauss during the substantivist–formalist debate: substantivists such as Marshall Sahlins argued that non-market economies run on a different logic from capitalist markets, and that Mauss had demonstrated this difference, while formalists replied that economic principles apply everywhere. Mauss became the touchstone for the claim that economies are always embedded in social relations.

The most searching critique came from Annette Weiner, who conducted fieldwork in the Trobriands and argued in *Women of Value, Men of Renown* (1976) that Mauss and Malinowski had both overlooked women's valuable exchanges — bundles of banana-leaf skirts and decorated cloth — which were as central to the status system as the men's kula ring. Mauss's framework, she showed, was blind to gender. Other critics noted that he generalised from a narrow ethnographic base, that he never tested his claims in the field, and that his categories carried an evolutionary assumption, treating gift economies as "archaic" in contrast to "modern" markets. Jacques Derrida, in *Given Time* (1992), pressed the logic further, arguing that a truly free gift, given without expectation of return, is impossible — since the very act of giving creates a debt.

## Legacy

Mauss remains foundational for contemporary economic anthropology, for debates about alternative economies, the solidarity economy, and non-market exchange. The language of the "gift economy" recurs in discussions of open-source software, volunteering, and organ donation — contexts in which work is given without immediate payment, creating obligations that are moral as well as economic.
