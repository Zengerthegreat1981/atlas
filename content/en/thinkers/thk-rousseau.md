# Jean-Jacques Rousseau

A philosopher, writer and musician from Geneva (1712–1778), who lived most of his life in France and wrote in French. He is regarded as one of the pillars of Enlightenment philosophy and at the same time one of its fiercest critics, and he paved the way for the Romantic movement by placing sincere feeling and inner conscience above abstract rational argument. He formulated questions that are still alive: how does a human being who is free by nature become subject in society? What legitimacy makes authority acceptable? How is the soul to be educated without civilization corrupting it?

## His Life

He was born in Geneva on 28 June 1712, and his mother died days after his birth, so he grew up with his father Isaac, a watchmaker, who left the city when Jean-Jacques was ten. He was apprenticed to an engraver and then left Geneva in 1728, and Madame de Warens took him in at Annecy and under her he converted to Catholicism before later returning to Calvinist Protestantism. He reached Paris in 1742 and worked in music and wrote for the *Encyclopédie* articles on music and on political economy, and became associated with Denis Diderot. After he visited Diderot in the prison of Vincennes in autumn 1749 he saw what he called the "illumination," and wrote the *Discourse on the Sciences and Arts*, which won the prize of the Academy of Dijon in 1750 and made him known overnight.

Then came the major works in the early 1760s. When *The Social Contract* and *Émile* appeared in 1762 both books were condemned in Paris and Geneva, and he fled to Switzerland and then to England at the invitation of David Hume, before quarreling with him and returning to France. He spent his last years in isolation and a sense of persecution, and wrote the *Confessions* and the *Reveries of the Solitary Walker*, and died at Ermenonville on 2 July 1778. His remains were transferred to the Panthéon in Paris in 1794 at the height of the Revolution that had adopted his ideas.

## His Contribution

In the *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality among Men* (1755) Rousseau distinguished between "love of self" (Amour de soi), the natural instinct of survival that harms no one, and "love of superiority" or "vanity" (Amour-propre), an inclination that arises in society when a person measures his worth by the way others look at him. He held that property, competition and comparison turn the good natural disposition into a mask and falsity, and this is the seed of the concept of social alienation in modern thought. It should be said that the phrase "noble savage" does not occur in his writings, and tradition attributes it to his depiction of natural man as free of institutional evil, though not of need.

In *The Social Contract* he treated the following dilemma: how can we submit to law without losing our freedom? His answer is that true freedom is obedience to a law we have laid down for ourselves, and that the contract creates a "general will" directed to the common good, which differs from the "will of all," which is the sum of private interests. Sovereignty for him belongs to the people, and is inalienable and cannot be represented, which is why he criticized the English representative system. He used his provocative phrase "forced to be free" to say that whoever violates the law he has accepted violates his true will. He added the idea of the "legislator" and "civil religion" to secure the unity of the community.

*Émile* is an educational novel in which he proposes a negative education that lets the child grow according to his nature and learn from things before books. In the section called the "Profession of Faith of the Savoyard Vicar" he presented a natural religion based on conscience, which angered the clergy. In the *Confessions* and the *Reveries* he founded a new kind of autobiography based on inner sincerity and the search for the authentic self.

## His Place in the Tradition

Rousseau stands at the edge of the Enlightenment: he shares with its philosophers the pursuit of freedom and equality and the rejection of despotism, but differs from them in their optimism about progress and the sciences. Kant was influenced by him, saying that Rousseau set him right in respect for the human being, and built on the idea of the general will his conception of the autonomous moral law. Politically, he was adopted by the Jacobins headed by Robespierre, and was read as a critic by Hegel, Marx and Hannah Arendt. In respect of Romanticism he paved the way for Wordsworth, Goethe and Schiller. His ideas entered the Arab world in the nineteenth century; Rifa'a al-Tahtawi mentioned him in *Takhlis al-Ibriz* (1834) among the writers of France, and the Nahda elite read him as the author of *The Social Contract*, and 'Adil Zu'aytir translated *The Social Contract* into Arabic in the mid-twentieth century.

### Influence on Kierkegaard and Nietzsche

Rousseau's defense of inward sincerity against social conformity is often compared with, and was a point of reference for, two precursors of existentialism:
- **Søren Kierkegaard**: Kierkegaard's emphasis on inwardness, the single individual (*den Enkelte*), and personal conscience is sometimes compared with Rousseau's in the *Confessions*, though direct influence is not well established, and Kierkegaard grounded his view in Christian faith rather than in natural innocence.
- **Friedrich Nietzsche**: Nietzsche maintained an intensely ambivalent relationship with Rousseau. In *Twilight of the Idols* and *Daybreak*, Nietzsche fiercely attacked Rousseau's egalitarianism, moral sentimentalism, and romantic idealization of "nature," diagnosing them as the ideological root of modern democracy, socialism, and the French Revolution's *ressentiment*. Yet Nietzsche simultaneously recognized Rousseau as a formidable kindred spirit in rebelling against cultural decadence, hypocrisy, and artificial convention, acknowledging the disruptive power of his call for authentic self-overcoming.

## Limits and Critique

Edmund Burke accused him of inspiring revolutionary madness, and Jacob Talmon, in *The Origins of Totalitarian Democracy* (1952), linked the general will to the despotism that came in the era of the Terror. Feminism has faulted him for the figure of Sophie in *Émile*, in which he made the education of woman subordinate to her role in relation to man. His optimism about the possibility of recovering innocence through the social contract also distinguishes him from later existentialism, which sees alienation as an ontological structure that does not disappear. Scholars also note that among his works there is a tension between the authentic individual and the citizen whose will dissolves in the community, a tension he did not resolve.

## His Principal Works

*The Social Contract* (Du contrat social, 1762) · *Émile, or On Education* (Émile, ou De l'éducation, 1762) · *The Confessions* (Les Confessions, written 1765–1770, published posthumously 1782) · *Reveries of the Solitary Walker* (Les Rêveries du promeneur solitaire, written 1776–1778, published posthumously 1782).

## Selected Quotations

"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." — *The Social Contract* (Du contrat social), 1762
