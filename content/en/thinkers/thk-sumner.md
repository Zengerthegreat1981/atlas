# William Graham Sumner

An American sociologist and economist (1840–1910), a professor at Yale University and one of the first to teach sociology in American universities. He combined an extreme conservative political position (social Darwinism) with an enduring conceptual contribution to the analysis of collective customs.

## Contributions

- **Social Darwinism**: In *What Social Classes Owe to Each Other* (1883), he argued that natural competition over scarce resources is an inevitable social law, and that any government intervention to protect the "weaker" from its results (social welfare) obstructs social natural selection and harms society in the long run.
- **Folkways and mores**: In *Folkways* (1907), he distinguished between "folkways," spontaneous, unintended customs that arise from the collective repetition of behavior without conscious planning, and "mores," customs that have acquired binding moral force and whose violators are socially punished.
- **Ethnocentrism**: He introduced this term into English-language sociology (it had been used earlier by the Austrian sociologist Ludwig Gumplowicz) in *Folkways*; it became a basic concept in sociology and anthropology, to describe the tendency to evaluate other cultures by the standards of one's own culture and to regard it as a universal measure.

## Further Context

Sumner taught at Yale from 1872 and offered one of the first university sociology courses in the United States, using Herbert Spencer's *The Study of Sociology* as a text. In essays such as "The Forgotten Man" (1883) he defended laissez-faire and the independent self-supporting middle-class citizen against what he saw as paternalistic reform and special-interest legislation, and he opposed protective tariffs and, later, American imperialism ("The Conquest of the United States by Spain", 1899). In *Folkways* he argued that mores are slow to change and cannot easily be overridden by legislation. With his student Albert G. Keller he developed the large comparative project published as *The Science of Society* (1927).

## Place in the Current

- The concepts of "folkways" and "ethnocentrism" separated from the broader social Darwinist framework and remained alive in contemporary sociology after the popularity of social Darwinism itself declined by the mid-twentieth century.
- He represents a conservative counter-voice within the American classical founding of sociology, as against the progressive reformers contemporary with him (such as Jane Addams and the early Chicago School).

## Limitations

- His extreme conservative political position (opposition to any government social welfare) was used historically to justify discriminatory social policies, which calls for presenting his thesis together with its critique and not as purely scientific neutrality.
- His social Darwinism was not subjected to rigorous systematic empirical testing; it was a philosophical-ideological conclusion projected onto social observation more than the result of field research.

## Major Works

- *What Social Classes Owe to Each Other* (1883).
- *Folkways: A Study of the Sociological Importance of Usages, Manners, Customs, Mores, and Morals* (1907).
- *The Science of Society* (4 vols., 1927, with Albert G. Keller).
