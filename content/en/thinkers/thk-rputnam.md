# Robert D. Putnam

An American political scientist (born 1941) who became one of the most cited authors in contemporary sociology through a single concept: **social capital**. His central thesis is that networks of relationships, norms and mutual trust are not social ornament but a **productive resource**, explaining why democracy and the economy work in one place and falter in another. From this concept he built an influential and highly controversial diagnosis of the disintegration of American collective life in the second half of the twentieth century.

## What He Contributed

- **"Making Democracy Work" (1993)**: a twenty-year comparative study of the Italian regions after the decentralizing reform of 1970. The question: why did the same institutions succeed in the north and fail in the south? His answer: the density of **civic traditions** and voluntary associations that predated the institutions. Here Putnam's use of social capital as a variable explaining institutional performance was born.
- **"Bowling Alone"**: it began as an article in the *Journal of Democracy* in 1995 and became a book in 2000. The central image: Americans were still bowling in growing numbers but had stopped playing in **organized leagues**, so the practice remained while the bond withered. He gathered indicators of a long-term decline in union, party, church and association membership and in home visiting.
- **The distinction between bonding and bridging capital**: bonding strengthens cohesion within a homogeneous group (the family, the sect, the closed club), while bridging crosses the divides between different groups. This distinction is his most important analytical contribution: it prevents the reading that "all bonding is good," since bonding capital alone may produce insularity and exclusion.
- **Explaining the decline by generational replacement and television**: he judged that the largest cause is not a change in individuals but a **replacement of generations**. The "long civic generation" shaped by the Depression and the Second World War is passing away, succeeded by a generation raised on individual home entertainment. For him television is a major explanatory variable.
- **"E Pluribus Unum" (2007)**: a result that troubled him personally. In the short run, ethnic diversity is associated with lower trust, **not only toward those who are different but toward those who are similar as well** ("hunkering down" like a turtle). He ran additional analyses before publishing it, and argued that in the long run societies can build new, broader shared identities that restore trust.
- **"Our Kids" (2015) and "The Upswing" (2020)**: he moved the question from associations to the **opportunity gap** between children of different classes, and then to a century-long curve running from individualism to collectivism and back (the "I–we–I" curve), co-authored with Shaylyn Romney Garrett.

## His Place in the Current

- **A third formulation of social capital**, always read against two earlier ones: Bourdieu (social capital as one form of capital deployed in **the reproduction of class inequality**) and Coleman (as a resource within the rational-choice framework). For Bourdieu the concept is an instrument of struggle; for Putnam it is a **public good** measured at the level of the region or society. This difference is the entry point to the whole dispute.
- **Heir of Tocqueville**: the thesis of voluntary associations as a school for democracy is taken explicitly from *Democracy in America*.
- **Functionalist logic**: civic cohesion is treated as a resource that improves institutional performance, and its absence as a cost to the system, a construction closer to functionalism than to conflict theory.
- **Wide political impact beyond academia**: "social capital" became a term in documents of the World Bank and the OECD and in British government discourse in the 1990s. This impact itself calls for a sociological analysis, a sociology of fashionable concepts.

## Limitations

- **Theda Skocpol's methodological critique**: the decline in membership is not an automatic behavioral phenomenon but the result of a **structural transformation in the form of organization**, from mass-membership associations with local chapters to professional advocacy organizations without members ("advocates without members"). Putnam measures the old form and concludes that the phenomenon is dead, whereas it was the institution and the state that changed, not individuals.
- **The causal inference about television is weak**: a temporal correlation between the spread of television and the decline of the indicators does not establish causation, and parallel variables (women's entry into the labor market, suburbs and commuting time, job insecurity, the decline of unions) explain a large part of it.
- **Neglect of political economy and inequality**: a recurrent objection is that the diagnosis treats "trust" and "bonding" in isolation from the concentration of wealth and the dismantling of social protection, thereby turning a distributional problem into a cultural-moral one. (He partly responded to this in *Our Kids*.)
- **The concept stretched until it lost its edge**: "social capital" came to be used for trust, networks, norms, participation, volunteering and happiness, and each researcher measures it with different indicators, which makes it hard to refute. This is an epistemological critique, not a detail.
- **The diversity finding is a double-edged sword**: *E Pluribus Unum* was used in anti-immigration discourse in a way that Putnam explicitly rejected, and the methodological debate around it (the definition of trust, the conflation of diversity with urban poverty, the limits of the cross-section) has not been settled.
- **The cultural limits of the model**: its indicators are built on one particular American form of voluntary organization. Transferring them to societies where the extended family, the kinship network and the sect perform the same function measures **the absence of the form** and not the absence of bonding, which is a caution for applying the concept in other societies.

## Major Works

- *Making Democracy Work: Civic Traditions in Modern Italy* (1993, with Robert Leonardi and Raffaella Nanetti).
- *Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community* (2000; originating in the article "Bowling Alone: America's Declining Social Capital" in the *Journal of Democracy*, 1995).
- *Better Together: Restoring the American Community* (2003, with Lewis M. Feldstein and Don Cohen).
- *E Pluribus Unum: Diversity and Community in the Twenty-first Century* (an article in *Scandinavian Political Studies*, 2007).
- *Our Kids: The American Dream in Crisis* (2015).
- *The Upswing: How America Came Together a Century Ago and How We Can Do It Again* (2020, with Shaylyn Romney Garrett).
