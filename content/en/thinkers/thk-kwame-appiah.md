# Kwame Anthony Appiah

Kwame Anthony Appiah (born 1954) is a Ghanaian-British-American philosopher, cultural theorist, and novelist, and one of the most widely read public intellectuals of his generation. His work moves between philosophy, African studies, and anthropology, and it is unified by a single question: how should we live with identities — race, nation, religion, culture — that we know to be constructed, yet which shape our lives profoundly? From *In My Father's House: Africa in the Philosophy of Culture* (1992) to *The Lies That Bind: Rethinking Identity* (2018), he has argued for a cosmopolitanism that takes both universal values and local attachments seriously, and he has become one of the most influential critics of racial essentialism.

## Life and Career

Appiah was born on 8 May 1954 in London, the son of Joe Appiah, a Ghanaian lawyer and politician who became a leading figure in the independence movement, and Peggy Cripps, the daughter of the British Labour politician Sir Stafford Cripps. He grew up in Kumasi, Ghana, in a household that was at once deeply Asante and deeply cosmopolitan, and he was educated at Bryanston School in England and at Clare College, Cambridge, where he took his doctorate in philosophy (1982). He taught at Yale, Cornell, Duke, Harvard, and Princeton, where he was the Laurance S. Rockefeller University Professor of Philosophy, before moving to New York University in 2014, where he is Professor of Philosophy and Law. He has also been president of the American Philosophical Association's Eastern Division and of PEN American Center, and since 2015 he has written "The Ethicist" column for *The New York Times Magazine*.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

*In My Father's House* was Appiah's first major intervention, and it remains his most important book for African studies. Against both the nativist celebration of an authentic African essence and the wholesale rejection of African traditions, he argued that African identities are historically made, not given, and that the task of African philosophy is to think critically about the traditions it inherits. His critique of "African" as a racial category — inherited from European racial science and then embraced by pan-Africanists — made him one of the earliest and most forceful critics of racial essentialism in the humanities.

In *The Ethics of Identity* (2005) and *Cosmopolitanism* (2006), Appiah developed his positive position. He defended the moral importance of identity — the projects, practices, and solidarities through which people give their lives meaning — while insisting that no identity is fixed by biology or by history. Cosmopolitanism, for Appiah, is not the erasure of difference but the habit of conversation across difference: the recognition that we can learn from strangers without either romanticising them or demanding that they become like us. He describes this stance as a kind of "cosmopolitan patriotism": attachment to a particular home combined with obligations to humanity as a whole.

*The Honor Code* (2010) offered a theory of moral change, arguing that practices such as duelling, foot-binding, and the Atlantic slave trade ended not primarily because people were persuaded by abstract arguments but because the practices came to be seen as dishonourable. Honour, he argued, is a powerful moral mechanism, and moral revolutions happen when a practice loses its honour. In *The Lies That Bind* (2018) he returned to identity, showing how the five great modern identities — creed, country, colour, class, and culture — are all built on fictions that we nevertheless live by, and arguing that we should treat them as useful stories rather than as essences.

## Influence and Critique

Appiah's influence extends well beyond philosophy. In anthropology, his critique of race and his defence of a historically grounded understanding of culture have been widely adopted, and his cosmopolitanism has shaped debates about multiculturalism, global justice, and the ethics of fieldwork. His public writing, especially "The Ethicist," has made him one of the few academic philosophers with a large general readership, and he has received the National Humanities Medal in the United States.

Critics have raised two main objections. Some African philosophers have argued that his scepticism about African identity underestimates the political value of pan-African solidarity, and that his cosmopolitanism is too comfortable with the inequalities of the global order. Others have questioned whether honour, as he describes it, can really explain moral revolutions, or whether economic and political forces do the heavier work. Appiah has responded that his aim is not to deny the reality of identity or the force of material conditions, but to insist that identities are things we make — and therefore things we can remake, more justly.
