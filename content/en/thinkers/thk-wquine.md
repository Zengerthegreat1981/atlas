# Willard Van Orman Quine

An American philosopher and logician (1908–2000), considered one of the most prominent philosophers of the twentieth century in the analytic tradition. He was born in Akron, Ohio, studied at Oberlin College, received his doctorate from Harvard in 1932 under the supervision of Alfred North Whitehead, and taught at Harvard from 1936 until his retirement in 1978, continuing to write and lecture until his death in Boston. He is viewed as the bridge between logical positivism and contemporary naturalism: he learned from it the sharpness of logic and the caution of empiricism, and then undermined a number of its foundations from within.

## His formation

In 1932–1933 Quine traveled to Europe, where he met the members of the Vienna Circle in Vienna, Carnap in Prague (who remained the closest of his teachers to him and his friendly adversary), and Polish logicians in Warsaw, among them Tarski. He contributed to introducing American philosophy to the post-Frege and post-Russell logic, and wrote *A System of Logistic* (1934) and *Mathematical Logic* (1940).

## What he contributed

- **"On What There Is" (1948)**: he dealt with the problem of speaking about what does not exist, and formulated the criterion of ontological commitment: "to be is to be the value of a bound variable", that is, what a theory commits to is what must exist for its statements to be true.
- **"Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951)**: an article published in the *Philosophical Review*. In it he attacked the distinction between analytic and synthetic propositions, and the idea that every sentence has an isolated empirical content that can be tested on its own. The result is "holism": theories face experience as a whole, and are revised where revision costs least, which became known as the Duhem–Quine thesis.
- ***Word and Object* (1960)**: in it he formulated the "indeterminacy of translation": however much behavioral evidence is gathered, no single correct translation of the sentences of a foreign language is determined, and he formulated the "inscrutability of reference" through the "gavagai" example. The point is that meaning does not exist as an independent entity behind linguistic behavior.
- **The naturalization of epistemology (1969)**: in his essay "Epistemology Naturalized" he called for epistemology to be regarded as a chapter of empirical psychology, not a first philosophy that precedes science and judges it. He pictured knowledge in the "web of belief" (*The Web of Belief*, 1970, with J. S. Ullian) and in the metaphor of Neurath's boat.
- **Logic and ontology**: he rejected modal logic in its common form, and preferred a parsimonious ontology based on first-order logic and set theory, and later contributed to the "indispensability argument", which gives mathematical concepts an ontological status because of their role in our best scientific theories.

## His place in the current

Quine stands at a crossroads: heir to empiricism and a rebel against it. He influenced generations of philosophers, among them Donald Davidson, who studied at Harvard and was strongly shaped by him, David Lewis, Dagfinn Føllesdal, Hao Wang, and Hilary Putnam, who shared with him the formulation of the indispensability argument. His legacy enters the philosophy of language, the philosophy of science and ontology.

## The influence on thinking about translation

The thesis of "indeterminacy of translation" matters to the Arab reader engaged in translation and in transferring philosophical concepts between Arabic and other languages, because it warns against assuming an identity of "meaning" between two languages, and draws attention to the role of theoretical structure and cultural background in every translation. It is a study that Quine wrote from a strictly behaviorist perspective, not from the perspective of translation studies, so it should not be made to carry what it does not say.

## Limits and critique

- **Behaviorism**: his thesis depends on a behaviorist conception of language that denies reliance on inner mental states, a position criticized by Chomsky and others, who held that language requires an innate faculty.
- **Circularity**: critics held that his critique of analyticity falls into a circle, because it uses concepts close to it, such as "stimulus synonymy" and "revisability", and his supporters replied that the aim is to expose the failure of definitions, not to offer alternatives.
- **The naturalization of knowledge**: some philosophers, among them Jaegwon Kim, asked what becomes of the normative dimension in epistemology if it becomes a descriptive science.
- **Parsimonious ontology**: his rejection of modal concepts and properties led some of his opponents to see him as reductive and unfaithful to lived experience.
