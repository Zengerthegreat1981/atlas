# Muhyi al-Din Ibn Arabi

An Andalusian mystic and philosopher, born in Murcia in 560 AH / 1165 CE, and regarded as a highly influential theorist of philosophical Sufism in Islam. He was titled "the Greatest Master" (al-Shaykh al-Akbar), and he set out a complete metaphysical system around the concept of "unity of being" that exerted a deep and long-lasting influence on Islamic Sufism from al-Andalus to Persia, India, and Southeast Asia.

## Biography

Ibn Arabi grew up in Seville and received a traditional education in jurisprudence and hadith before turning in his youth to the Sufi path after a spiritual experience he later described in his writings. He traveled in al-Andalus and the Maghreb, and, according to his account, met the philosopher Ibn Rushd in Cordoba in a famous encounter he related in the "Meccan Revelations". He left al-Andalus for good in 1200 CE as a pilgrim and moved among Mecca, Baghdad, Mosul, Anatolia, and Cairo, before settling in Damascus in 1223 CE, where he spent the last part of his life writing and teaching under the patronage of the Ayyubids, and he died there in 638 AH / 1240 CE.

## What He Gave

### The Meccan Revelations

His great encyclopedia, which he began after a spiritual vision in Mecca (1202 CE) and continued to expand until near his death. It runs to many volumes covering jurisprudence, esoteric Quranic interpretation, cosmology, ethics, and the science of the ranks of being. In it Ibn Arabi presents a comprehensive system of God's relation to the world through the concept of "self-disclosure" (Tajalli), according to which all existents are manifestations of the self-disclosure of the Absolute Real, not existents independent of it in themselves.

### The Bezels of Wisdom

A condensed book which he wrote, according to his account, after a vision in which he saw the Prophet Muhammad giving him the book. It consists of twenty-seven chapters, each attributed to one of the prophets who represents a "word" or a particular manifestation of divine wisdom (the bezel of Adamic nature, the bezel of Noahic nature, the bezel of Abrahamic nature...). It is among the most condensed and difficult texts of Islamic Sufism, and has received dozens of commentaries over the centuries, the most famous being the commentary of Dawud al-Qaysari and that of Abd al-Razzaq al-Kashani.

### The Concept of "Unity of Being" and the Perfect Human

Although Ibn Arabi did not use the term "unity of being" in its later form exactly as his followers formulated it, his system rests on the idea that true being is one, namely the being of God, and that the being of contingent things is shadowy and relative, with no independence. Tied to this is the concept of the "Perfect Human" (al-Insan al-Kamil): the being that gathers in himself all the divine names and attributes, whose highest model is realized in the Muhammadan Reality as the cosmic intermediary between the Real and creation.

### Esoteric Interpretation and the Unity of Religions

Ibn Arabi developed a method of esoteric interpretation of religious texts based on the multiplicity of levels of meaning, and statements were attributed to him on the multiplicity of manifestations of true worship that are sometimes read as a call to the unity of the essence of religions, though scholars (William Chittick especially) warn against reading them outside their precise metaphysical context as a relativist pluralism in the modern sense.

## Influence

Ibn Arabi's ideas spread through a network of students and commentators that formed what is known as the "Akbarian School", the most prominent being Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi (his stepson and closest student), who organized his ideas in a more systematic philosophical form, then Abd al-Karim al-Jili, Dawud al-Qaysari, and Abd al-Rahman Jami. His influence extended to Persia and India, where it interacted with Persian Sufism and with Sufi thought in the Ottoman Sultanate, and it reached Southeast Asia through Sufi networks. In the twentieth century, the French Orientalist Henry Corbin helped introduce his thought to the Western reader through his works on the "creative imagination", and William Chittick studied his metaphysical system with broad academic rigor in the West.

## Limits

Ibn Arabi's ideas, especially the theory of unity of being and the concept of Pharaoh, as an example in which his faith at the moment of drowning is discussed in the "Bezels", aroused sharp controversy in the later Islamic tradition. Ibn Taymiyya accused him of holding that the theory of unity of being amounts in practice to a kind of incarnation or union between Creator and creature, which Ibn Taymiyya considers contrary to Islamic monotheism, which requires a categorical essential distinction between the essence of God and the essences of His creatures; he returned to the critique in several fatwas collected in his *Majmu' al-Fatawa*. Other jurists also objected to his symbolic interpretation of some stories of the prophets, and to statements in the "Bezels" on Pharaoh's position at drowning, considering them close to justifying unbelief.

On the other hand, later Sufis such as Abd al-Wahhab al-Sha'rani defended Ibn Arabi; he interpreted the problematic statements of the "Bezels" as particular Sufi technical terms that are misunderstood if taken in their literal linguistic sense, and distinguished between "ecstatic utterances" (shathat) issuing from the state of the gnostic and what is an intended theoretical statement. Contemporary scholars such as Chittick hold that much of the disagreement arose from reading Ibn Arabi's condensed, multi-level texts outside their particular rhetorical and symbolic context, while others hold that the doctrinal objection to unity of being is a fundamental objection that cannot be reduced to linguistic misunderstanding.

## The Significance of Terminology and Method

Ibn Arabi's style is distinguished by a particular terminological density, as he coined or re-employed dozens of technical terms (such as "the divine presences", "the fixed entities", and "the absolute imagination"), so that his texts cannot be understood apart from this internal lexicon. He adopted a method that blends rational argument and symbolic interpretation of Quranic and hadith texts with what he described as "unveiling" (kashf), or direct experiential knowledge, which he claims is the source of much of what he wrote, especially in the "Bezels of Wisdom", which he says in its introduction he received complete in a single vision. This claim of a non-inferential source of knowledge gave his texts, among his followers, a status almost immune from direct logical criticism, which partly explains the intensity of the controversy around them.

## His Most Important Works

- **The Meccan Revelations**: the great Sufi encyclopedia.
- **The Bezels of Wisdom**: a condensed system of prophetic wisdom.
- **The Creation of the Circles**: a treatise on cosmic symbolism.
- **The Divine Governances**: on the spiritual path and spiritual politics.
- **The Interpreter of Desires**: a Sufi poetic collection.
