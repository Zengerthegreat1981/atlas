# Konrad Zweigert

**Konrad Zweigert** (1911–1996) was a German jurist, professor of comparative private law (at Tübingen from 1948, then Hamburg), judge of the Federal Constitutional Court (1951–56) and director of the Max Planck Institute for Foreign and International Private Law in Hamburg. Together with his co-author Hein Kötz, he is regarded as the author of one of the most influential and widely used works of comparative law in academic teaching worldwide, owing to his rigorous formulation of what is today known as the **functional method** in comparative legal studies.

## The Functional Method: The "Supreme Principle" of Comparative Law

In their landmark reference book *Einführung in die Rechtsvergleichung auf dem Gebiete des Privatrechts* (English translation: *Introduction to Comparative Law*), Zweigert and Kötz name **functionality** as the basic methodological principle of comparative law, and add a "**presumption of similarity**" (*praesumptio similitudinis*) about the results. Since all human societies face similar basic social and economic problems (such as compensating harm, regulating a contract of sale, or protecting property), the different legal systems, despite their different vocabularies, formal concepts and historical sources, tend to arrive at **functionally similar solutions** to these shared problems. This method requires the comparative researcher to begin from the **practical social problem**, not from a local formal legal concept (such as "contract" or "tort" in its technical sense within a particular system), since formal legal concepts differ between systems, while practical functional solutions to identical problems may converge regardless of the conceptual wrapping.

## Criticism of Formal Conceptual Comparison

Zweigert and Kötz clearly distinguish between **conceptual comparison**, which compares legal institutions on the basis of the internal formal classifications of each system (a comparison they consider potentially misleading, since a concept such as "contract" may cover a completely different scope in one system than in another), and **functional comparison**, which asks how each legal system actually deals with a specific social problem, regardless of the formal conceptual category under which it places the solution. This precise methodological distinction made their book a standard educational tool for training law students to get past the barriers of local terminology and reach a real practical understanding of comparative legal solutions.

## The Classification of Legal Families According to Zweigert and Kötz

The book also offers a special classification of world "legal circles" (*Rechtskreise*), partly overlapping with René David's classification but using more detailed criteria that include origin and historical development, the distinctive manner of legal thinking, unique institutions, the recognized sources of law, and the underlying ideology. Within this classification they distinguish between the Romanistic family, the Germanic family, the common law family, the Scandinavian circles, the socialist circle, the Far Eastern circle, and the religious circles (including Islamic and Hindu law).

## The Max Planck Institute and the Global Comparative Documentation Project

Zweigert directed the Max Planck Institute for Foreign and International Private Law in Hamburg (from the early 1960s until 1979), which became one of the world's leading research libraries and documentation centres for comparative law, with extensive collections of legislation and judicial decisions from many legal systems. This made primary sources accessible to generations of comparative researchers.

## Functionalism as an Educational Method

The distinctive pedagogical approach of the Zweigert and Kötz book, which presents a single practical problem (such as compensation for psychological harm, or the voidness of a contract for error) and then surveys its actual solutions in several legal systems side by side, turned the teaching of comparative law from a static descriptive account of foreign legislation into a live analytical exercise that trains the student to think across legal boundaries. This pedagogical approach is precisely what made the book translated into dozens of languages and adopted as a core textbook in law faculties around the world.

## Legacy

The Zweigert and Kötz book remains one of the most widely used texts for teaching comparative law worldwide to this day. Their functional method has influenced all subsequent comparative studies that seek to go beyond the barriers of local terminology.

## Continuity of the Book across Generations

Hein Kötz, who co-authored the book from its first edition, continued to update it after Zweigert's death in 1996, preserving the functional methodological structure that Zweigert and Kötz established. This reflects the enduring strength of this method as a normative framework that does not easily age, despite the continuous changes in the content of the comparative legal systems themselves.
