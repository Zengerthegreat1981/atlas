# Ralf Dahrendorf

Ralf Dahrendorf (1929-2009) was a German-British sociologist, political thinker and liberal politician. He is best known for a conflict theory of class that locates the source of conflict in authority relations rather than in property ownership alone.

## Life and Context

Born in Hamburg, Dahrendorf studied philosophy, classical philology and sociology in Hamburg and then at the London School of Economics. He taught at several German universities, including Tubingen and Konstanz, and sat briefly in the West German parliament and served as a European Commissioner in the early 1970s. He directed the London School of Economics from 1974 to 1984 and later led St Antony's College, Oxford. He became a British citizen and a member of the House of Lords.

## Main Ideas

In *Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society*, Dahrendorf argued that Marx's analysis, based on ownership of the means of production, no longer fit industrial societies in which ownership and control had separated. He proposed that society is composed of imperatively coordinated associations, in which some positions hold authority and others are subject to it. Conflict arises from this unequal distribution of authority, and classes are groups defined by their shared interests in preserving or challenging it. He saw conflict as a source of social change and as something to be regulated through institutions rather than eliminated. In *Homo Sociologicus* he examined the tension between individual freedom and the social roles that constrain people. Later, in *Life Chances*, he framed liberty and social opportunity as a combination of options and ligatures.

## Major Works

- *Homo Sociologicus* (1958)
- *Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society* (German original 1957; English 1959)
- *Society and Democracy in Germany* (1965)
- *Life Chances* (1979)
- *The Modern Social Conflict* (1988)
- *Reflections on the Revolution in Europe* (1990)

## Method

Dahrendorf worked in a comparative, theoretical and historical mode, building general models of conflict while engaging with empirical questions about German and European society.

## Influence and Criticism

His work became a central statement of conflict theory in contrast to the structural functionalism of Talcott Parsons. Critics argued that authority relations are not always the primary source of class division, that his model fits organizational hierarchies more than broad class structures, and that his account of post-capitalist society was overstated.
