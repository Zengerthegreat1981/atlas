# Esther Duflo

**Esther Duflo** is a French-American economist and professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). She shared the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2019 with Abhijit Banerjee and Michael Kremer. She is the second woman in the prize's history to win it in economics, after Elinor Ostrom (2009), and, at 46, the youngest winner in the field.

## From Paris to the Laboratory of Poverty Alleviation

Duflo studied history and economics at the École normale supérieure in Paris, before moving to the United States to complete her PhD in economics at MIT (1999), where Abhijit Banerjee was among her advisers and later became her long-term research partner and husband. In 2003 she, Banerjee and Sendhil Mullainathan founded the Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab (J-PAL), which systematised the use of randomised controlled trials to evaluate dozens of development programmes around the world.

## Leading the Methodology of Randomised Trials

Duflo is among the most influential figures in establishing randomised controlled trials as the gold standard for evaluating development policies. With her team she designed and managed large-scale field trials in India, Kenya and other countries, covering diverse areas from education to public health to microfinance. In each case she focused on a specific, practical question (for example, which interventions raise school attendance and learning) rather than on a general theoretical debate about the overall causes of poverty.

## Critique of Traditional Macro Approaches

Like Banerjee, Duflo criticised the traditional tendency in development economics to pose highly general macro questions that are hard to test empirically with precision (are institutions, geography or culture the cause of national poverty?). She called for a more modest and testable approach to anti-poverty policy, which treats poverty as a series of specific problems amenable to cumulative partial solutions, rather than as a single mass requiring one grand unified theory to explain and solve it all at once.

## Special Interest in Gender and Policy

Duflo devoted particular research attention to the effects of policies on women in developing countries. Through field studies she examined the impact of women's representation in Indian local councils on public spending priorities, and the effect of economic empowerment programmes designed specifically for women on household decisions. In doing so she added an explicit gender dimension to the randomised trial methodology she developed with Banerjee.

## Scientific Resistance and Engagement with It

Duflo publicly engaged with the critique of William Easterly and other critics of randomised trial methodology. She defended the value of precise empirical evidence even when it is geographically limited in scope, as opposed to relying on untested theoretical intuition or on grand generalisations lacking firm evidence. At the same time she acknowledged that partial trials cannot replace a broader understanding of the institutional and political contexts that determine the success of any policy in practice.

## The Nobel Prize and Its Symbolic Impact

Duflo's Nobel Prize in 2019, awarded "for their experimental approach to alleviating global poverty", was a symbolically important event: she was only the second woman to win the prize in economics since its establishment, and the youngest winner. This reopened a broader debate about the representation of women in academic economics. That debate goes beyond the scope of her direct contribution to development economics, but it is noted here because of its direct connection to her professional career.

## Institutional Impact Beyond Academia

Duflo's influence extended beyond purely academic circles into direct advisory roles for governments and many international organisations seeking to apply the principle of "what the evidence actually proves" in designing development programmes. She served on the US Global Development Council under President Obama. J-PAL, which she helped found, has partnered with governments and international organisations to evaluate and scale programmes, and its methodology is cited well beyond development economics.

## A Debate on the Limits of Methodological Neutrality

As with any experimental methodology, there is a debate over the choice of the questions themselves that are tested with randomised trials. The decision to test the effectiveness of distributing free mosquito nets, rather than testing broader structural policies (such as international tax reform or sovereign debt), is in itself a methodological choice with implicit political consequences. Some critics of the methodology point this out, without implying doubt about the validity of the experimental results themselves within the scope they were designed to measure.

## An Inseparable Link with Banerjee's Work

It is difficult to separate Duflo's contribution from her long research partnership with Abhijit Banerjee. The two co-designed most of the major trials on which the field's experimental methodology was built, and co-authored the book *Poor Economics* (2011). The Nobel Prize in 2019 was awarded jointly to the two of them and Michael Kremer.
