# Harvey Whitehouse

A British anthropologist and cognitive scientist, Professor of Social Anthropology at the University of Oxford, and one of the founding names of the cognitive science of religion alongside Pascal Boyer, Justin Barrett and Scott Atran. What distinguishes him from them is his reliance on direct ethnographic fieldwork (rather than theoretical armchair reflection alone) to test his hypotheses.

## The Theory of Modes of Religiosity

On the basis of his field observations of a religious movement in Papua New Guinea, Whitehouse developed a theory that distinguishes between **two structurally distinct modes** of transmitting religiosity and its experience across cultures, detailed in the entry on the theory of modes of religiosity. One is the "doctrinal" mode, which recurs with high frequency and relies on semantic memory and organized verbal instruction. The other is the "imagistic" mode, which is rarely repeated but highly emotionally arousing, and relies on episodic memory of events rather than verbal repetition.

## Method: Combining Ethnography and Cognitive Experimentation

Whitehouse is methodologically distinguished from Boyer and Barrett, who relied primarily on theoretical inferences from experimental cognitive psychology in the laboratory, by his insistence on grounding his theory first in long-term ethnographic fieldwork. He then tests the hypotheses derived from it in later comparative quantitative studies across many cultures. This makes him a methodological bridge between traditional anthropology and experimental cognitive science. The details of this fieldwork itself (late 1980s, concerning the Pomio Kivung movement) are treated in `stu-pomio-kivung-fieldwork-whitehouse-1995`.

## The Foundational Fieldwork

Whitehouse drew on his fieldwork in the late 1980s among followers of the Pomio Kivung movement in New Britain Province, Papua New Guinea. This was a local religious movement combining elements of Christianity with Melanesian millenarian beliefs about the return of the ancestors. Whitehouse observed a ritual schism within the movement between the main movement, with its routinised, frequently repeated teachings and rituals (closer to the "doctrinal" mode), and a splinter group that developed rare but highly arousing rites (closer to the "imagistic" mode). This direct field observation was the basis on which he later built his broader comparative theory, documented in more detail in his separate field study.

## The Critique of the Theory

The doctrinal/imagistic dichotomy faced methodological criticism from E. Thomas Lawson and Robert McCauley, who developed an alternative, the "Ritual Competence Theory." They argued that the decisive distinction between rituals is not their frequency or emotional intensity, but their internal attributional structure: who is presumed to be the highest agent (a god or supernatural being) in the chain of delegation of ritual action. This debate remained one of the most fruitful methodological discussions within the cognitive science of religion during the first two decades of the new millennium.

## His Later Project: The Seshat Database

Whitehouse later widened the scope of testing his theory through his co-founding (with Peter Turchin and Pieter François) of the "Seshat: Global History Databank" project, which collects structured data on many past societies in order to test hypotheses about the relationship between social complexity, religion and the emergence of large states. It is an ambitious extension of his original method from the individual ethnographic case to quantitative global history.

## Connection to Islamic Contexts

The theory of modes of religiosity has later been applied by other researchers to diverse Islamic practices. High-frequency collective rituals (such as the five daily prayers and regular communal recitation) are usually classified under the "doctrinal" mode, which depends on repetition and organized teaching. Some exceptional Sufi rituals (such as intense dhikr circles or the rare spiritual experiences of disciples) are classified closer to the "imagistic" mode, which rests on emotional intensity and singular experience. This application, however, remains an inference from Whitehouse's general framework, not part of his direct field research, which focused on Melanesia and non-Islamic contexts.

## Academic Standing

Whitehouse is Professor of Social Anthropology at the University of Oxford and has directed its Institute of Cognitive and Evolutionary Anthropology, a research centre at the intersection of anthropology, cognitive psychology and the study of religion.

## Additional Works

Together with Luther H. Martin, Whitehouse edited *Theorizing Religions Past: Archaeology, History, and Cognition* (2004), which applies cognitive approaches to religion in the deep past, and with James Laidlaw he edited *Ritual and Memory* (2004).

## Conclusion

Whitehouse is a major figure in the contemporary cognitive science of religion. By combining long-term fieldwork with comparative quantitative theorizing, he has helped close a long-standing methodological gap between traditional anthropology and experimental cognitive science.
