# Maurice Dobb and Paul Sweezy

Maurice Dobb (1900–1976), a fellow of Trinity College, Cambridge, and Paul Sweezy (1910–2004), an American Marxist economist and co-founder of *Monthly Review*, were the principal protagonists of an influential twentieth-century debate on the historical mechanisms driving the transition from feudalism to capitalism in Western Europe.

## The Transition Debate

The debate originated with the publication of Maurice Dobb's *Studies in the Development of Capitalism* (1946). Dobb mounted a rigorous historical-materialist critique of classical commercialization models. Drawing on Karl Marx's analysis in *Capital* (Volume III), Dobb argued that feudalism was characterized by serfdom and the direct extra-economic extraction of surplus labor by landlords. The collapse of European feudalism in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, Dobb maintained, was driven by **internal contradictions** within the feudal mode of production: the over-exploitation of peasant labor, declining agricultural productivity, and the consequent peasant flight and resistance, which made the traditional manorial system economically unviable.

In his 1950 critique published in *Science & Society*, Paul Sweezy challenged Dobb's internalist explanation. Drawing on the historical thesis of Henri Pirenne, Sweezy argued that Western European feudalism was inherently conservative, self-perpetuating, and oriented toward production for use rather than exchange. In Sweezy's view, internal contradictions alone could not dissolve manorial relations. Instead, an **external prime mover** was required: the resurgence of long-distance trade, urban merchant enclaves, and merchant capital from the eleventh century onward. Sweezy maintained that market commerce commodified agricultural output, introduced monetization, stimulated consumption demands among the nobility, and lured serfs away from manorial estates into self-governing cities.

Dobb published a detailed rejoinder arguing that trade alone could not dissolve feudal relations without preexisting internal labor crises; where market pressures grew upon resilient servile structures, as in Eastern Europe, trade caused not capitalism but the "second serfdom."

## International Expansion and Legacy

The exchange between Dobb and Sweezy catalyzed contributions from a wider circle of Marxist and economic historians between 1950 and 1954:
- **Kohachiro Takahashi** introduced comparative perspectives from the Japanese transition and the Prussian path, reinforcing Dobb's focus on relations of production and emphasizing Marx's "really revolutionary way" (Way I), in which direct producers become merchants and industrial capitalists.
- **Rodney Hilton** examined the dynamics of feudal rent, peasant revolts, and agrarian class struggle in medieval England.
- **Christopher Hill** examined the ideological and political dimensions of the English Civil War as a bourgeois revolution.

The complete exchange was edited by Rodney Hilton in the 1976 collection *The Transition from Feudalism to Capitalism*, which established the terms of historical-materialist economic history. It was followed by the Brenner Debate of the late 1970s—in which Robert Brenner criticized both Dobb and Sweezy for differing forms of economic determinism—and influenced Immanuel Wallerstein's formulation of modern world-systems theory.

## Broader Intellectual Careers

Beyond the transition debate, both scholars shaped twentieth-century political economy:
- **Maurice Dobb** served as a central figure in the Communist Party Historians Group in Britain and published influential analyses of Soviet economic planning, including *Soviet Economic Development since 1917* (1948) and *Welfare Economics and the Economics of Socialism* (1969). He also collaborated with Piero Sraffa on the standard eleven-volume edition of *The Works and Correspondence of David Ricardo* (1951–1973).
- **Paul Sweezy**, educated at Harvard and the London School of Economics, wrote *The Theory of Capitalist Development* (1942), introduced the kinked-demand-curve theory of oligopoly, and founded the independent socialist magazine *Monthly Review* in 1949 with Leo Huberman. Together with Paul A. Baran, Sweezy authored *Monopoly Capital: An Essay on the American Economic and Social Order* (1966), analyzing modern corporate capitalism and stagnation.
