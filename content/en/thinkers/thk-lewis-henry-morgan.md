# Lewis Henry Morgan

## Definition

Lewis Henry Morgan was an American lawyer and amateur anthropologist (in the sense that he held no specialized academic post). He is considered one of the founding fathers of modern American anthropology and of the school of classical evolutionism. He became known for his extended study of the Iroquois tribes of New York State and for his ambitious theories about the stages of human social evolution.

## Historical Context

Morgan began his interest in the Iroquois (the Haudenosaunee, especially the Seneca) in the 1840s, through a Rochester, New York, fraternal society he helped found, which took Iroquois institutions as a model, and through his legal work on behalf of the Seneca in land disputes. His friendship with the young Seneca scholar Ely S. Parker gave him rare access to the details of Iroquois social life, and he was adopted into the Seneca Hawk clan in 1847. This took place in an era of strong evolutionary and progressive thinking in Europe and America. His first major book, *League of the Ho-de'-no-sau-nee, or Iroquois* (1851), is often called the first scientific ethnography of a Native American people.

## Main Ideas and Method

In his central work *Ancient Society* (1877), Morgan developed a unilinear evolutionary model that divided the history of humanity into three major successive stages: "savagery," then "barbarism," then "civilization." He subdivided each stage into sub-stages according to technical development (the discovery of fire, agriculture and writing). He based this in part on his earlier, more specialized study of comparative kinship systems in *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family* (1871), in which he distinguished between "classificatory" kinship terminologies, such as that of the Iroquois, and "descriptive" European systems, treating this difference as an indicator of different evolutionary stages in the structure of the human family.

## Key Works

Another of his works is *Houses and House-Life of the American Aborigines* (1881), in which he studied the collective residential architecture of the Iroquois (the "longhouses"), and linked it to the system of the extended collective family. *Ancient Society* had a direct and major influence on Friedrich Engels, who relied on it in *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884) to build a Marxist analysis of the development of private property and the state; Marx himself had left extensive notes on Morgan's book in his last years.

## Impact and Critique

Morgan was a major pioneer of comparative kinship studies in anthropology, and his distinction between classificatory and descriptive systems is still used today, although its broader evolutionary framework has been superseded. His linear evolutionary model was subjected to decisive criticism by the generation of Franz Boas at the start of the twentieth century, who rejected the idea of a single compulsory evolutionary path through which all societies pass. Boas held that the cultural history of each society is particular and cannot be reduced to a single universal ladder. Morgan's detailed ethnographic work on the Iroquois, however, remained a valuable historical reference even after his broader theoretical framework was rejected.

Alongside his anthropological work, Morgan was a practising lawyer who invested in railroad and mining ventures in Michigan's Upper Peninsula. This gave him personal wealth, part of which supported his independent research, at a time when anthropology had not yet been established as a university discipline in the United States.

Morgan remains a compulsory historical reference in any course on the history of anthropological theory, where he is usually taught as a starting point to show students how the field developed from the ambitious evolutionary aims of the nineteenth century toward the cautious relativism of the Boasian twentieth century, and then toward a more flexible neo-evolutionism with Julian Steward and Leslie White. Studying his contradictory legacy (between his enduring ethnographic value and the rejection of his theoretical framework) is therefore itself a methodological lesson.

He was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and served as president of the American Association for the Advancement of Science in 1880, which reflects his standing in American science despite his lack of a university post.

Today Morgan is the subject of renewed critical readings from the perspective of Indigenous studies. Some researchers praise his real personal relationship with the Seneca community and his legal defense of their rights, while others point to the limits of his evolutionary framework, which classified living contemporary societies as "primitive" on a single one-directional civilizational scale.
