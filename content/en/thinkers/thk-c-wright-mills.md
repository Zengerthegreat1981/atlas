# C. Wright Mills

C. Wright Mills (1916-1962) was an American sociologist known for his critical analysis of power in the United States, his study of the middle class, and his call for a sociology that connects personal troubles to public issues, which he termed the sociological imagination.

## Life and Context

Mills was born in Waco, Texas, in 1916. He studied at the University of Texas and received his doctorate at the University of Wisconsin-Madison. He taught at the University of Maryland and from 1946 at Columbia University, where he remained until his death from heart disease in 1962. Working in the early Cold War, he was a prominent independent radical voice, influenced by Weber, Marx, Veblen, and American pragmatism.

## Main Ideas

- **Sociological imagination:** the capacity to see the relationship between individual biography and history, and between "personal troubles of milieu" and "public issues of social structure."
- **The power elite:** Mills argued that the United States was dominated by an interlocking elite drawn from the military, corporate, and political spheres, whose decisions shaped national life.
- **White-collar society:** he described the new middle class of clerks, managers, and salespeople as occupationally dependent and politically passive.
- **Critique of abstracted empiricism and grand theory:** he opposed both survey-driven data collection detached from theory and the abstract system building he saw in Talcott Parsons.
- **Intellectual craft:** scholars should connect scholarship with public life.

## Major Works

- *From Max Weber: Essays in Sociology* (1946, translated and edited with Hans Gerth)
- *The New Men of Power* (1948)
- *White Collar: The American Middle Classes* (1951)
- *Character and Social Structure* (1953, with Hans Gerth)
- *The Power Elite* (1956)
- *The Causes of World War Three* (1958)
- *The Sociological Imagination* (1959)
- *Listen, Yankee* (1960)

## Method

Mills favored historically informed, structural analysis combined with attention to biography. He advised scholars to practice "intellectual craftsmanship" and to avoid methodological fetishism.

## Influence and Criticism

*The Power Elite* inspired later work on elites and influenced the New Left, and the book remains widely read. Pluralist critics such as Robert Dahl argued that power in America was more dispersed. Others criticized the book as lacking systematic evidence about decision-making. His relation to professional sociology was often antagonistic.

## Applications and Cross-References

The sociological imagination is widely taught as an introduction to the discipline. Elite studies by G. William Domhoff drew on Mills. Compare Weber on bureaucracy, Marx on class, and Parsons's systems theory, which Mills criticized.
