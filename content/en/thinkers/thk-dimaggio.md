# Paul DiMaggio

An American sociologist (born 1951), long a professor at Princeton University and later at New York University, and a co-founder (with Walter Powell) of the "new institutionalism" in the sociology of organizations, explaining why organizations become structurally similar even when this similarity does not serve economic efficiency.

## What He Contributed

- **Institutional isomorphism**: Together with Walter Powell, in their pivotal article "The Iron Cage Revisited" (1983), he formulated the concept of "Institutional Isomorphism." Organizations operating in the same field tend to become structurally similar over time through three distinct mechanisms: coercive isomorphism, resulting from formal legal or regulatory pressures; mimetic isomorphism, resulting from imitating successful organizations under conditions of uncertainty; and normative isomorphism, resulting from the spread of uniform professional norms through shared training.
- **Legitimacy instead of efficiency**: He presented a direct challenge to traditional economic theory, which assumes that organizations become alike because the most economically efficient solution wins out competitively in the market. He argued that organizational similarity often arises from the organization's need for social and institutional "legitimacy" in the eyes of significant others (regulators, funders, partners), and not from purely technical efficiency considerations alone.

## His Place in the Current

Thanks to this joint founding with Powell, the new institutionalism became a basic reference framework in contemporary organizational sociology. It explains widespread phenomena such as completely different companies and organizations adopting similar managerial, marketing or governance standards, even though their operating contexts and actual goals differ radically.

## Work on Culture and the Arts

DiMaggio also made major contributions to the sociology of culture. His 1982 article "Cultural Capital and School Success" (*American Sociological Review* 47) tested Bourdieu's idea of cultural capital on US high-school students, and he studied the arts and nonprofit cultural organizations, editing *Nonprofit Enterprise in the Arts* (1986). Later work addressed culture and cognition (a 1997 review in the *Annual Review of Sociology*), polarization of social attitudes in the United States, and social inequality in internet use.
