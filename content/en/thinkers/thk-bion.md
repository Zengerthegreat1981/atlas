# Wilfred Bion

British physician, psychoanalyst and philosopher (1897–1979), born in India and educated in England, president of the British Psychoanalytical Society (1962–1965), a pioneer of group dynamics and analytic cognitive psychology, and author of the theory of "containment and thinking."

## What he contributed

He put forward an influential theory of thinking and of psychosis. He formulated the concept of "containment" (Container/Contained) and the mother's task of transforming raw, unprepared sensory impressions (beta elements) into elements capable of being thought and dreamed (alpha elements, through the alpha function and maternal reverie, Rêverie). He also pioneered the study of "group dynamics and their three basic assumptions" (dependency, fight/flight, and pairing) and the theory of transformation toward the unknown (O).

## His place in the movement

He is regarded by many analysts as one of the most original psychoanalytic theorists after Freud and Klein, and a major influence on contemporary psychoanalysis in Britain, Latin America and Italy.

## Limitations

His complex mathematical and symbolic language (the Grid) and his rigorous philosophical abstraction mean that reading his texts requires advanced analytic preparation.

## His military and early analytic career

Bion was born in India in 1897 to a British engineer father and was sent to study in England as a child. He served as a tank officer in the First World War and was awarded the Distinguished Service Order (DSO) for his courage at the Battle of Amiens, a war-trauma experience that later influenced his thinking about psychotic anxiety and groups under pressure. His personal analysis was conducted by Melanie Klein, which made him one of the leading heirs of the British object-relations school, although he later developed a conceptual language of his own that goes beyond Klein in its abstraction.

## The Tavistock experience and groups

During the Second World War Bion worked at Northfield Military Hospital on experiments in group treatment for psychologically traumatized soldiers, the experience that later led him to the Tavistock Institute of Human Relations in London (founded 1947), where he formulated his theory of the three "basic assumptions" that govern the behavior of groups unconsciously in their members: the dependency assumption (Dependency), the fight-or-flight assumption (Fight-Flight), and the pairing assumption (Pairing) — each of them a collective defensive mechanism that takes the place of the group's manifest "work task."

## The theory of containment in detail

Bion's container-contained model assumes that the infant is exposed to an enormous quantity of raw sensory and emotional impressions that cannot be digested psychologically, which he called "beta elements," and that the infant gets rid of them by projecting them onto the mother through the mechanism of "projective identification," which Melanie Klein had originally described as a defensive mechanism, but which Bion reinterpreted also as a primitive form of "communication": the mother who receives these projections with a calm mind capable of "dreaming" them (Rêverie) transforms them into meaning that can be thought about ("alpha elements") and returns them to the infant in a bearable form, so that he gradually learns to "think" by himself. When this process fails (a mother unable to contain), the psychic experience remains "undigested," which Bion linked to the roots of deep psychotic disorders.

## His later influence in Latin America and Italy

After his death Bion's ideas moved to active analytic centers in Argentina, Brazil and Italy, where analysts such as Antonino Ferro and Giuseppe Civitarese developed them in new directions (such as the analytic field theory), until Bion came to be regarded in these circles as a major theoretical reference, although his fame in English-speaking circles remained for decades relatively confined to specialized circles because of the difficulty of his language and his high symbolic abstraction.

## The concept of "transformation toward the unknown"

In his last and most abstract theoretical phases, Bion developed the concept of "O" (a symbol he chose deliberately to avoid any prior verbal connotation) to refer to the "absolute truth" of any moment of experience, which he held cannot be known directly or fully thought about but can only be "become" through directly lived experience that transcends rational understanding. This quasi-mystical orientation in his late thinking has been compared by some readers to contemplative traditions, and it provoked debate among his followers over whether this development was a logical extension of his early project or a deviation into obscurity that cannot be applied in practical clinical work.

## His major works

Learning from Experience (1962) · Experiences in Groups (1961) · Elements of Psycho-Analysis (1963) · Transformations (1965) · Attention and Interpretation (1970).
