# Lucy Mair

Lucy Philip Mair (1901–1986) was a British social anthropologist and one of the founders of applied anthropology in Britain. A student of Bronislaw Malinowski at the London School of Economics (LSE), she spent a long career there as a teacher and, eventually, professor of applied anthropology. Her comparative studies of African political systems, her ethnography of the Ganda kingdom of Buganda, and her textbooks on government, marriage, and social change shaped generations of students and helped define how anthropology could speak to questions of colonial administration and, later, of development in newly independent states.

## Life and Career

Mair was born in 1901 and educated at the University of Cambridge before moving to the London School of Economics, where she joined Malinowski's famous research seminar alongside contemporaries such as Edmund Leach, Raymond Firth, and Audrey Richards. The seminar's insistence on intensive fieldwork, rather than armchair speculation, shaped her whole career. She carried out extended fieldwork among the Ganda people of Buganda in Uganda, living in the kingdom and studying its political institutions at close range.

She spent most of her academic life at the LSE, where she became professor of applied anthropology — a chair that reflected her enduring conviction that anthropological analysis should be useful to practical problems of government and social policy. She was active in the professional life of British anthropology through the decades when the discipline was moving from the study of colonial societies to the study of development, and she continued to write and teach into old age. She died in 1986.

## Key Works

Mair's first major books came from her Ugandan fieldwork and from the comparative questions it raised. *An African People in the Twentieth Century* (1934) described the Ganda kingdom and the changes colonial rule had brought to it, while *Native Policies in Africa* (1936) compared how African political systems — centralised kingdoms such as Buganda and decentralised, so-called acephalous societies — adapted to colonial administration under indirect rule. Together they offered one of the earliest comparative frameworks for African political systems, predating the classic volume *African Political Systems* (1940), edited by Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard.

Her later work turned increasingly to teaching and synthesis. *Primitive Government: A Study of Traditional Political Systems in Eastern Africa* (1962) surveyed the variety of forms of political authority in East Africa before colonial impact and became a standard student text. *New Nations* (1963) addressed the challenges of state-building in Africa and Asia after independence, and *Anthropology and Social Change* (1969) examined how traditional societies respond to rapid externally imposed transformation. Her comparative study *Marriage* (1971) ranged across cultures to analyse the institution of marriage, drawing on her wide knowledge of African and Oceanic ethnography. She also wrote on millenarian and prophetic movements in Melanesia, including the cargo cults, comparing them with prophetic movements in Africa as responses to the shock of colonial encounter.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Mair's central contribution was a comparative political anthropology grounded in fieldwork. She insisted that African political systems could not be understood through a single model: kingdoms, chiefdoms, and stateless societies each had their own logic, and each responded differently to colonial rule. Her early comparison of centralised and decentralised systems gave later scholars a vocabulary for describing political diversity that remains influential.

She was also a pioneer of applied anthropology in the British tradition. Where many of her contemporaries treated anthropology as a purely academic pursuit, Mair argued that ethnographic knowledge should inform administration and policy — first colonial administration, then the development problems of independent states. Her writings on new nations applied her long experience of traditional political systems to the question of how those systems interact with imported modern institutions such as political parties, parliaments, and bureaucracies, making her an early voice in what would become the anthropology of development.

A third thread in her work was the study of social change itself. Her analyses of cargo cults and prophetic movements treated religious innovation not as irrationality but as a creative social response to sudden contact with Western technology and wealth — a perspective that connected her African and Oceanic interests within a single comparative project.

## Influence and Critique

Mair's textbooks educated generations of LSE students and helped establish applied anthropology as a recognised British tradition linking theory to public policy. Her comparative framework for African politics influenced the political anthropology that followed, and her work on post-independence state-building was an early reference point for development anthropology.

Her career has also drawn criticism, above all for her role as an adviser to colonial administration. A later generation of postcolonial anthropologists argued that applied anthropology in the colonial period was knowledge placed in the service of control, and that even sympathetic advisers worked within structures of domination. The criticism was directed at her generation as a whole rather than at Mair alone, and her defenders note that she used her advisory position to argue for a deeper understanding of local political systems rather than to justify direct domination. The tension between her commitment to practical usefulness and the politics of the colonial setting in which she worked remains the central debate about her legacy.
