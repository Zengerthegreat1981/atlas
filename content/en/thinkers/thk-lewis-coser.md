# Lewis Coser

Lewis A. Coser (born 1913 in Berlin) was a German-born American sociologist who made conflict a central topic of functionalist sociology. His book The Functions of Social Conflict (1956) argued that conflict can integrate and strengthen groups, and he was also an influential historian of sociological thought and a sociologist of intellectuals.

## Life and Context

Coser left Germany after the Nazi rise to power, lived in Paris, and emigrated to the United States in 1941. He completed his doctorate at Columbia University, where he studied with Robert K. Merton. He taught at Brandeis University and later at Stony Brook, and served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1975. In 1954 he co-founded the journal Dissent with Irving Howe, reflecting his democratic-socialist politics.

## Main Ideas

- Functions of conflict: building on Georg Simmel, Coser argued that conflict is not always disruptive; it can define group boundaries, reinforce internal cohesion and bring about adaptation.
- Realistic versus nonrealistic conflict: the former seeks specific goals, while the latter releases tension and is directed at whatever object is at hand.
- Safety valves and flexibility: societies that tolerate conflict can adjust rather than accumulate pressure.
- Greedy institutions: organizations such as sects and total institutions that demand exclusive commitment from members.
- Sociology of intellectuals and ideas.

## Major Works

- The Functions of Social Conflict (1956)
- Men of Ideas: A Sociologist's View (1965)
- Continuities in the Study of Social Conflict (1967)
- Masters of Sociological Thought: Ideas in Historical and Social Context (1971)
- Greedy Institutions: Patterns of Undivided Commitment (1974)
- Refugee Scholars in America (1984)

## Method

Coser worked through theoretical synthesis and historical-sociological interpretation, placing ideas within their social contexts, rather than through original survey research.

## Influence and Criticism

His work bridged functionalism and conflict theory and was important in textbooks and in the sociology of knowledge. Critics, among them Ralf Dahrendorf, argued that treating conflict through its social functions leaves out its coercive and structural sources, and that his account remains tied to functionalist assumptions of equilibrium.
