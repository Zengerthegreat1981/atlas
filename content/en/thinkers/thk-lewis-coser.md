# Lewis Coser

Lewis A. Coser (1913-2003, born in Berlin) was a German-born American sociologist who made conflict a central topic of functionalist sociology. His book The Functions of Social Conflict (1956) argued that conflict can integrate and strengthen groups, and he was also an influential historian of sociological thought and a sociologist of intellectuals.

## Life and Context

Coser left Germany after the Nazi rise to power, lived in Paris, and emigrated to the United States in 1941. He completed his doctorate at Columbia University, where he studied with Robert K. Merton. He taught at Brandeis University and later at Stony Brook, and served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1975. In 1954 he co-founded the journal Dissent with Irving Howe, reflecting his democratic-socialist politics.

## Main Ideas

- Functions of conflict: building on Georg Simmel, Coser argued that conflict is not always disruptive; it can define group boundaries, reinforce internal cohesion and bring about adaptation.
- Realistic versus nonrealistic conflict: the former seeks specific goals, while the latter releases tension and is directed at whatever object is at hand.
- Safety valves and flexibility: societies that tolerate conflict can adjust rather than accumulate pressure.
- Greedy institutions: groups such as sects that demand exclusive and undivided commitment, relying mainly on voluntary compliance and symbolic boundaries, unlike the physical enclosure of Goffman's total institutions.
- Sociology of intellectuals and ideas.

## Case Studies

*Men of Ideas* examined institutions of intellectual life such as the Parisian salons and cafes, publishers and little magazines. *Greedy Institutions* gave examples ranging from religious orders and sects to political parties and domestic service. *Refugee Scholars in America* studied the intellectual migration from Nazi-controlled Europe to the United States.

## Method

Coser worked through theoretical synthesis and historical-sociological interpretation, placing ideas within their social contexts, rather than through original survey research.

## Related Concepts

Coser's analysis connects with several concepts. Social conflict is read alongside social cohesion and group boundaries, following Georg Simmel's view that opposition is itself a form of association. His functional analysis draws on Robert K. Merton's attention to manifest and latent functions and to dysfunctions, so a conflict may have consequences its participants never intended. The distinction between realistic and nonrealistic conflict is related to scapegoating, in which hostility is redirected to a convenient target. Safety-valve institutions bear on social control and social change, since they may release tension while leaving its sources in place. Conflict theory, in the versions developed by Ralf Dahrendorf and others from partly Marxian and Weberian roots, offers a contrast by emphasising domination and structural interests. Greedy institutions relate to commitment, role demands and Goffman's total institution, and the study of intellectuals belongs to the sociology of knowledge.

## Influence and Criticism

His work bridged functionalism and conflict theory and was important in textbooks and in the sociology of knowledge. Critics argued that treating conflict through its social functions underplays its coercive and structural sources, and that his account remains tied to functionalist assumptions of equilibrium. Ralf Dahrendorf's conflict theory, by contrast, centres on authority relations.
