# Carl Menger

**Carl Menger** (born in Nowy Sącz, Galicia, in 1840; died in Vienna in 1921) was an Austrian economist, one of the three independent pioneers of the marginal revolution and the founder of what later became known as the **Austrian School** of economics. Unlike Jevons and Walras, who formulated the principle of marginal utility in explicit mathematical language, Menger chose a qualitative, non-mathematical verbal style. In doing so he founded an independent Austrian methodological tradition that remained distinct within the marginal school throughout the twentieth century.

## From economic journalism to a university chair

In his youth Menger worked as a journalist, including reporting on markets for the *Wiener Zeitung*. This hands-on experience with actual pricing in markets led him to doubt the capacity of the classical labour theory of value to explain how prices are formed day to day. He published his founding book, *Principles of Economics*, in 1871, and then obtained a professorship at the University of Vienna (extraordinary professor in 1873, full professor in 1879). From 1876 to 1878 he tutored Crown Prince Rudolf in political economy and statistics and travelled with him in Europe and Britain. The association continued until Rudolf's death in 1889. Menger retired from his Vienna chair in 1903.

## The subjective theory of value

In *Principles of Economics*, Menger put forward a central idea: the value of a good is not an objective property inherent in it. It is a subjective judgment made by the individual, based on how much the good contributes to satisfying his needs, which are ranked in descending order of urgency. Menger arranged an individual's needs in a descending table (known as Menger's table) and showed that the value of the last available unit of a good is determined by the least urgent need that this unit can satisfy, not by the total of all the needs the good as a whole satisfies.

## The theory of orders of goods: from consumption to production

Menger made a further original contribution by distinguishing between "goods of the first order" (final consumer goods that satisfy needs directly) and "goods of higher orders" (factors of production, such as machines and raw materials, which do not satisfy a need directly but contribute to producing consumer goods). Menger showed that the value of these production factors is derived from the value of the final consumer goods they help produce, not the reverse, as theories based on production cost had assumed. This reversal in the direction of economic causation would become a cornerstone of later Austrian analysis in capital theory.

## The spontaneous origin of money

In *Principles* and later in "On the Origin of Money" (1892), Menger offered an influential account of the origin of money as a social institution that developed spontaneously, without deliberate central design. Individuals began by exchanging goods directly for one another (barter). They then gradually discovered that some goods, such as precious metals, were more readily marketable than others, so traders began to accept them as a medium of exchange even when they did not need them for direct consumption. This "spontaneous evolutionary" explanation of the origin of money became a standard reference in the history of monetary thought, and later paved the way for other Austrian monetary theories developed by Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek.

## The methods dispute with the German Historical School

Menger entered into a sharp methodological confrontation with Gustav Schmoller and the leaders of the German Historical School. In the dispute, which began after Menger's *Untersuchungen über die Methode der Socialwissenschaften* (1883), Schmoller and his followers held that economics should be built inductively from detailed historical study of each national economy in its particularities, rather than deductively from abstract general theoretical principles, as Menger did. This dispute became known as the **Methodenstreit** (methods dispute). It effectively split German academic economics into two rival camps for decades afterward, and neither side fully settled the argument.

## Establishing the Austrian School

Menger left an institutional legacy that extended through his direct students, most notably Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk and Friedrich von Wieser, who developed his ideas on capital and interest. It then passed through a later generation (Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek) that carried the Austrian tradition into the twentieth century as a distinct current within the broader marginal economics. It is known for its insistence on the complete subjectivity of value and its relative rejection of the formal mathematical formulations preferred by Walras and his followers.

## Independence from Jevons and Walras

Historians of economic thought generally regard the three works of Jevons (1871), Menger (1871) and Walras (1874) as independent discoveries. The resemblance among them, despite Menger's verbal formulation differing from his colleagues' mathematical one, is taken as evidence that the classical labour theory of value had reached an impasse by the 1870s in more than one European intellectual tradition at once, rather than the result of direct mutual influence among the three men.
