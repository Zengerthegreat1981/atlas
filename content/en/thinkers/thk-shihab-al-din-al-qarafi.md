# Shihab al-Din Ahmad ibn Idris al-Qarafi

A Maliki jurist and usuli who lived in Egypt in the early Mamluk period. He is one of the most original usuli scholars of the Islamic heritage in precise comparative thinking between jurisprudential cases that are superficially similar, and in the theoretical distinction between the functions of the ruler, the judge and the mufti. This distinction approaches an early theory of the separation of powers.

## Life and Context

Al-Qarafi was born in Egypt (some sources associate him with Bahnasa in Upper Egypt) and bears the nisba al-Sanhaji, indicating descent from the Berber tribe of Sanhaja; the details of his early upbringing are not precisely documented. He moved to Cairo, where he studied with leading scholars of his time from both the Maliki and Shafi'i schools. Most prominent among them was Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam (a Shafi'i), from whom he learned usul al-fiqh and legal maxims, and Ibn al-Hajib (a Maliki), who summarized usul al-fiqh in *Muntaha al-Sul wa al-Amal*, and whose summary had a profound effect on al-Qarafi's own method. He lived in the religiously plural environment of Mamluk Cairo, and died there in 684 AH / 1285 CE, after leaving several works on fiqh, usul, creed and language.

## Central Ideas

### The Theory of Differences: Precise Distinction between Similar Cases

Al-Qarafi is best known for his book **Anwar al-Buruq fi Anwa' al-Furuq** (The Lights of the Lightnings on the Categories of Differences), in which he founded an analytical method based on comparing two jurisprudential questions that look similar, in order to show the **'illa** (effective cause) or *manat* (ground) that distinguishes them in ruling despite their surface resemblance. Later scholars called this method the "science of differences" ('ilm al-furuq). It is not merely a compilation of jurisprudential questions, but a theoretical exercise in **the precision of distinguishing the grounds of rulings**. It shows how a small difference in description can produce a complete difference in the sharia ruling. This is what makes the book closer to a theory of the logic of legal classification than to a mere encyclopedia of fiqh.

### Distinguishing Fatwa, Judgment and Political Action

In his book **al-Ihkam fi Tamyiz al-Fatawa 'an al-Ahkam wa Tasarrufat al-Qadi wa al-Imam** (The Ruling on Distinguishing Fatwas from Judgments and the Actions of the Judge and the Imam), al-Qarafi put forward an important theoretical distinction between three types of sharia action: the **fatwa** (an announcement of God's ruling which does not in itself bind), the **judicial ruling** (a binding act in a specific dispute between two parties), and the **political/imamic action** (what issues from the ruler on the basis of considering the public interest). This distinction has precise practical consequences: a judge's ruling in one case does not bind other judges in a similar case, whereas a fatwa merely informs the questioner of the ruling and does not bind in the way a judgment does; and the imam's action may admit of specification and restriction that would not be permitted in an unrestricted fatwa. This theorization is today compared to attempts to separate the legislative, judicial and executive powers in general legal theory, although al-Qarafi himself did not use these modern terms.

### His General Usuli Method

In his commentary *Sharh Tanqih al-Fusul* (a commentary on his own abridgement of al-Razi's *al-Mahsul*), al-Qarafi showed an ability to reconcile the method of the theologians (abstract and theoretical) with that of the jurists (inductive and applied) in the codification of usul al-fiqh. He discussed in detail the questions of the 'illa and the ways of identifying it, the conditions for the validity of analogy, and questions of public interest.

## Impact and Critique

Al-Qarafi's method in the *Furuq* profoundly influenced the development of "legal maxims" as a field independent of usul al-fiqh. It became a model for distinguishing what appears to be a general rule from what is in reality an exception disguised by an appearance of similarity. Modern researchers (among them Sherman Jackson, in his study of al-Qarafi's jurisprudence of the state) have regarded him as ahead of his time in theorizing the relationship between the judicial and political authorities. Al-Qarafi, however, wrote within an Islamic jurisprudential framework, not within a modern constitutional theory, so imposing the modern concept of the "separation of powers" on him without reservation may load his text with more than it can bear. On another front, al-Qarafi's method in the *Furuq* has remained a subject of debate about how far it can be generalized beyond the Maliki school from within which he wrote.

### Other Works: al-Dhakhira as an Encyclopedia of Fiqh

Alongside his usuli works, al-Qarafi left a vast Maliki jurisprudential encyclopedia entitled **al-Dhakhira**, which gathered the fiqh of the Maliki school in wide detail with comparisons to the other schools. It is one of the most important sources of later Maliki jurisprudence. This shows that his theoretical contribution in *al-Furuq* and usul was joined by a deep, detailed jurisprudential mastery rather than being separate from it.
