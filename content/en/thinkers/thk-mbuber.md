# Martin Buber

An Austrian-Palestinian-Israeli Jewish philosopher, professor at the University of Jerusalem, one of the most important religious existentialists.

## His biography

Martin Buber was born in Vienna on 8 February 1878 and, after his parents' separation, moved as a child to the home of his grandfather Solomon Buber in Lemberg (present-day Lviv). His grandfather was a great scholar of the Midrash and a publisher of its texts, so the grandson grew up in a world that combined the Hasidic traditions of Eastern Europe with German culture. He studied philosophy and art history in Vienna, Leipzig, Berlin and Zurich, and joined the Zionist movement early, editing the journal "Die Welt" in 1901, then founding the journal "Der Jude", which he published between 1916 and 1924. But he distanced himself from the political line of the Zionist leadership and called for a "cultural Zionism" that sees Palestine as a center of spiritual renewal and not merely a state project.

In the 1920s he began, with Franz Rosenzweig, to translate the Hebrew Bible into German, a project that lasted decades and that Buber completed alone after Rosenzweig's death in 1929, until 1961, in a style that imitates the rhythm and repetition of the original text. He taught religion at the University of Frankfurt from 1923 until 1933, when he was forced out by the Nazi regime, then emigrated to Palestine in 1938 and worked as professor of social philosophy at the Hebrew University in Jerusalem until his retirement in 1951. He died in Jerusalem on 13 June 1965.

## What he gave

- **"I and Thou" (Ich und Du, 1923)**: the founding book, which distinguishes between the "I-Thou relation" and the "I-It relation".
  - "I-Thou": a direct, whole, present relation, which cannot be exploited or analyzed.
  - "I-It": an objective, analytic relation that can be exploited, which treats the other as a thing.
  - "The Eternal Thou": God is present in every genuine "I-Thou" relation.
- **"Dialogue" (Zwiesprache, 1930)**: in his later works he expanded the philosophy of dialogue, and his dialogical essays appeared in English in the collection "Between Man and Man" (1947).
- **"The Prophetic Faith" (Torat ha-Nevi'im, 1942; English 1949)**: a study of the Hebrew prophets.

## The philosophy of dialogue and its meaning

Buber starts from the claim that the human being does not exist first and then enter into a relation, but that the relation precedes the self: he opened "I and Thou" with a sentence saying that the basic words are not single words but pairs of words, "I-Thou" and "I-It". This means that the I that says "I" in the dialogical relation differs from the I that says it while using things. There is no final separation between the two worlds, for every "Thou" turns into an "It" when the moment of encounter passes, and this is not a sin but a condition of practical life; the danger is that the whole world becomes a world of "It", so that the human being loses his humanity.

He then applies this method to several fields: education, which he holds rests on an encounter between teacher and pupil and not on the transmission of content; art, which begins with the artist's dialogue with the material; and politics, which he criticizes when it turns the community into an instrument. In his book on Hasidism he collected the tales of the Hasidim and recast them in a literary spirit, and these tales were for him witness that piety means sanctifying everyday life and not fleeing from it.

## His political position on the conflict in Palestine

Buber was well known for a binationalist position: in 1942 he took part in founding the "Ihud" association with Judah Magnes, president of the Hebrew University, which called for a state shared by Arabs and Jews instead of partition. He opposed the partition resolution in 1947, and after 1948 criticized the treatment of Palestinian refugees and urged that their claims be taken seriously. He held that a Zionism that ignores the existence of the Arabs betrays its spiritual essence. This position remained a minority one in the Zionist movement, but it made his name a subject of interest to Arab writers who examined the possibilities of coexistence, and also made him a target of attack by Israeli nationalists.

## Limits of his philosophy and criticism of it

Emmanuel Levinas criticized him for making the relation reciprocal and symmetrical, whereas Levinas holds that the other precedes me and is higher than I, without reciprocity. In his dialogue with Carl Rogers in 1957 he held that the relation of therapist to patient is unequal by virtue of the roles and does not reach full reciprocity, while Rogers saw in it an encounter between two persons equal in dignity. Other critics hold that his poetic language leaves his concepts obscure, and that his interpretation of Hasidism is selective and neglected the ritual and legal side. The link of the Eternal Thou with God also led some philosophers to see that he offers a religious meditation and not a philosophical theory that can be tested.

## Main works

- *Die Geschichten des Rabbi Nachman* (1906).
- *Die Legende des Baalschem* (1908).
- *Ich und Du* (1923).
- *Zwiesprache* (1930).
- *Torat ha-Nevi'im* (1942; English: *The Prophetic Faith*, 1949).
- *Der Weg des Menschen nach der chassidischen Lehre* (1948).

## Influence

Buber influenced the philosophy of dialogue in Emmanuel Levinas, and humanistic psychology through his famous dialogue with Carl Rogers at the University of Michigan in 1957 on the nature of the therapeutic relationship.
