# Jacques Lacan

A famous French psychiatrist and psychoanalyst who reread Freud structurally and philosophically; his formative period was deeply influenced by Hegel (through Kojève), Heidegger and French existentialism.

## What he contributed

- **The return to Freud**: the slogan of his whole project. He held that psychoanalysis after Freud — especially American **ego psychology** — had deviated into strengthening the "ego" and helping the patient adapt, and that this is the **opposite** of Freud, who showed that the ego itself is the site of illusion. So he called for rereading Freud's text literally anew, with the tools of **structural linguistics** (Saussure and Jakobson), **anthropology** (Lévi-Strauss) and **Hegelianism** (through Kojève's lectures).
- **The mirror stage** (1936, 1949): the child between six and eighteen months — who does not yet have coordination of movement — sees his image in the mirror as **complete and unified**, and identifies with it jubilantly. Here the "ego" arises: **on an image**, that is, on something **outside** the child and prior to him, its unity borrowed from outside while his bodily experience is fragmented. So the ego for him is not the center of the subject but its **first alienation** — an imaginary structure that is attributed to a person and is not the person.
- **The three registers**: the apparatus through which everything is read in his work. **The Imaginary** (the world of images, identifications and illusory unity, whose home is the mirror stage); **the Symbolic** (the system of language, law and kinship, which precedes the individual, who enters it and has his position determined in it); and **the Real** (which does not mean "reality," but **what resists symbolization**: what cannot be said or imagined and always returns to the same place — like trauma, death, and sex as impossible).
- **The unconscious is structured like a language**: his most famous phrase. It does not mean that the unconscious speaks a language, but that it works by the **mechanisms of language** itself: **metaphor** (which for Freud is condensation) and **metonymy** (which is displacement). So the symptom is a metaphor, and desire is a metonymy — it slides from object to object and does not settle.
- **Man's desire is the desire of the Other**: a formula deliberately double in meaning — we desire what others desire, and we desire **that the Other desire us**, that is, recognize us. It follows that desire **cannot be satisfied**, because it is not a need for a thing but a demand for recognition with no limit.
- **Need, demand and desire**: the infant is hungry (**need**), so he cries, and the cry becomes a **demand** addressed to the mother; when she feeds him the need is satisfied, and what the demand was truly asking for — **love and presence** — remains unsatisfied. This remaining surplus is **desire**.
- **The object petit a** (objet petit a): the cause of desire, not its aim. What seems that it would fill the lack if obtained — and never fills it because it is not a thing but the **place of the loss** itself.
- **The Name-of-the-Father** and the paternal metaphor: the symbolic function that introduces a third law between mother and child and so opens the symbolic order; and on its failure — **foreclosure** (forclusion) — he built his explanation of psychosis.

## His place in the current

In his beginnings Lacan intersects with phenomenology and existentialism — he translated Heidegger's article on "Logos," and engaged with Sartre and Merleau-Ponty — then moved in a structuralist direction that makes the subject an **effect** of the symbolic order and not a source of meaning, so that he became a critical interlocutor of existentialism and not one of its members.

His institutional position is the key to understanding his effect: he practiced the **variable-length session** — ending it within minutes if he judged that the moment had reached a significant juncture, instead of the standard fifty minutes. His argument was that a fixed length allows the patient to fill the time with talk that protects himself, and that the sudden cut produces an **effect** that prevents this. This was the cause of his recurring crisis with the international institution: he broke away from the Paris Society (1953), then was **struck from the list of training analysts of the International Psychoanalytical Association in 1963** — and he described that event as an "excommunication." So he founded the **École Freudienne de Paris** (1964), in which he later introduced (1967) a special qualification procedure he called **"the pass"** (la passe), then **dissolved it himself** in 1980, a year before his death, and created in its place "the Freudian Cause."

A whole generation descended from his school: **Jacques-Alain Miller** (his son-in-law and the editor of the seminars), **Jean Laplanche** and **J.-B. Pontalis** (who split from him), **Françoise Dolto** in child analysis, **Luce Irigaray** and **Julia Kristeva** — and his influence extended to **Slavoj Žižek** and to literary, film and political theory more widely than it extended to the clinic.

## Limitations
Four limitations on Lacan:

**First — obscurity.** His text is among the hardest ever written, and he intended that: he said the reader must **work**, and that simplification betrays a subject that is itself a sliding of meaning. His opponents reply that deliberate obscurity prevents both **testing and criticism**, and that it moves the argument from its strength to the authority of its author. It is observed in practice that mutually hostile Lacanian schools cite the same text.

**Second — the use of mathematics.** Lacan borrowed from topology and mathematical logic (the Möbius strip, the Borromean knot, and the "matheme") as precise formulations of psychic structures. **Alan Sokal** and **Jean Bricmont**, in *Fashionable Nonsense* (published after his death, in 1997), showed that these uses **are not mathematically valid** and that they are metaphors adorned with technical language without content. The Lacanians reply that they are declared metaphors and not mathematical claims — a reply that does not hold together with his insistence that the "matheme" is what makes the transmission of psychoanalysis possible.

**Third — the short session.** It was not a procedural matter but the subject of the heaviest ethical accusation: patients paid the fee for a full session for a session that lasted minutes, and the whole decision was in the analyst's hands with no external criterion. The question remains: how is the cut that produces an effect to be distinguished from the cut that serves the analyst's convenience or the number of his appointments?

**Fourth — evidence.** There are no controlled trials of Lacanian treatment, and it is among the therapeutic traditions most resistant to the very idea of measurement — since it holds that the criterion of "efficacy" is taken from a discourse foreign to the aim of analysis. This is an internally consistent position, and its price is this tradition's absence from every clinical guideline in the world.

## His most important works

His doctoral thesis: ***On Paranoiac Psychosis in Its Relations to the Personality*** (De la psychose paranoïaque dans ses rapports avec la personnalité, 1932) · ***Écrits*** (1966) · **The Seminar** (twenty-seven books, 1953–1980, the most famous being: Book I *Freud's Papers on Technique*, Book III *The Psychoses*, Book VII *The Ethics of Psychoanalysis*, Book X *Anxiety*, Book XI *The Four Fundamental Concepts of Psychoanalysis*, Book XVII *The Other Side of Psychoanalysis* which contains the four discourses, and Book XX *Encore*).
