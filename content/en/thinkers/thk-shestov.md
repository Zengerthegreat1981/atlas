# Lev Shestov

A Russian-Jewish philosopher (born Yehuda Leyb Schwarzmann in Kiev, 1866; died in Paris, 1938), a critic of rationalism and of the claim that reason and necessity have the last word. He wrote in Russian and, after emigrating in 1920, taught at the Sorbonne and published largely in French. He was one of the first to present Kierkegaard seriously to French readers, and a precursor of existentialism.

## Life

Shestov was born into a prosperous Jewish merchant family in Kiev and studied law at Moscow and Kiev. He was drawn to literature and philosophy, writing a first book on Shakespeare and his critic Brandes, and became part of the Russian religious-philosophical milieu alongside Nikolai Berdyaev. After the revolution he left Russia in 1920, settled in Paris, and lectured at the Sorbonne. He was a friend and critic of Edmund Husserl, whom he met in 1928 and whose phenomenology he attacked as a last form of the faith in rational evidence; Husserl in turn introduced him to Kierkegaard's work, which he found had anticipated his own themes.

## Main Works

- *Dostoevsky and Nietzsche: The Philosophy of Tragedy* (1903).
- *The Apotheosis of Groundlessness* (1905), an aphoristic attack on systematic thought.
- *Potestas Clavium* (1923).
- *In Job's Balances* (1929).
- *Kierkegaard and Existential Philosophy* (French, 1936).
- *Athens and Jerusalem* (1938), his summary statement.

## What He Contributed

Shestov contrasted "Athens" with "Jerusalem": Greek philosophy, which seeks universal truths bound by necessity and logic, and the biblical faith in a God for whom, as he put it, all things are possible. He argued that philosophy from Socrates to Hegel and Spinoza submits to a necessity that even God must obey, and that this submission is a flight from the particular suffering of the individual human being. Against it he set Job, Abraham and the biblical patriarchs as models of a faith that is an absurd and free leap beyond reason rather than a conclusion reached by reason. His readings of Dostoevsky, Nietzsche, Tolstoy, Pascal and Plotinus returned repeatedly to the individual who is crushed by general laws and who is the real subject of philosophy.

## Influence and Reception

His work influenced French thinkers such as Camus, who discusses him in *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), and Benjamin Fondane, his disciple, as well as Georges Bataille, and it helped shape the reception of Kierkegaard and of existential themes in French thought before the postwar existentialism of Sartre. His influence is largely that of a provocateur and transmitter rather than a systematic founder, and his aphoristic, polemical style has made him less central than Berdyaev or Marcel.

## Criticisms

Critics note that his opposition between reason and faith is too stark and that he leaves little room for argument, ethics, or shared public standards. His reading of the philosophers he attacks is often selective, and his religious position, a faith that overcomes even logical and moral necessity, is difficult to state without self-contradiction, as he himself accepted.
