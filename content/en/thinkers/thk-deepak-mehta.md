# Deepak Mehta

Deepak Mehta is an Indian anthropologist and sociologist, long associated with the Delhi School of Economics, University of Delhi, known for his ethnographic studies of communal violence in urban India. Working closely with the sociologist Roma Chatterji, he studied Dharavi, the vast Mumbai neighbourhood, in the aftermath of the Hindu–Muslim riots of 1992–93 that followed the demolition of the Babri mosque at Ayodhya. His work shows how violence is lived, remembered, and narrated in everyday life — through rumour, naming, housing, and the long aftermath of rehabilitation — and it has become a central reference for the anthropology of violence in South Asia.

## Life and Career

Mehta has taught sociology and anthropology in Delhi, beginning at the Delhi School of Economics, and has been a teacher of students working on violence, Islam, and Muslim communities in India. His earlier ethnographic book, *Work, Ritual, Biography: A Muslim Community in North India* (1997), studied a community of weavers in a small town in Uttar Pradesh.

His research career was shaped by the communal violence of the early 1990s. Mehta and Roma Chatterji, both sociologists in Delhi, conducted fieldwork in Dharavi, a large Mumbai settlement, studying the rioting of December 1992 and January 1993. Their research continued over many years, following residents through the long aftermath of the violence and the procedures of rehabilitation that accompanied it. The work was conducted in dialogue with the wider anthropology of violence and social suffering associated with Veena Das, Arthur Kleinman, and their collaborators.

## Key Works

Mehta's central work is *Living with Violence: An Anthropology of Events and Everyday Life* (2007), written with Roma Chatterji and published by Routledge. Based on narrative accounts of Dharavi residents, the book explores how the violence of 1992–93 was experienced and remembered, and how language, housing, and rehabilitation shaped the lives of people living with its consequences. An earlier chapter, "Boundaries, Names, Alterities: A Case Study of a 'Communal Riot' in Dharavi, Bombay," written by Chatterji and Mehta for the volume *Remaking a World: Violence, Social Suffering, and Recovery* (2001), edited by Veena Das, Arthur Kleinman, Margaret Lock, Mamphela Ramphele, and Pamela Reynolds, presented the core of the argument to an international audience.

Mehta and Chatterji also wrote on housing and rehabilitation in Dharavi and on how the state's procedures of compensation and redevelopment shape community.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Mehta's work begins from a refusal of the standard account of communal riots as the eruption of ancient religious hatreds. In Dharavi, he and Chatterji showed, the violence of December 1992 and January 1993 has to be understood through local relations as well as the larger religious conflict — and through the specific ways in which the demolition of the Babri mosque on 6 December 1992 was narrated locally. The Srikrishna Commission, appointed to inquire into the Mumbai riots, recorded some 900 deaths between December 1992 and January 1993; Mehta's ethnography asks what such numbers mean for the people who lived through them.

A central idea is that violence is not only an event but a condition of everyday life. In *Living with Violence*, Mehta and Chatterji trace how the riot continues to organise social relations long after the killing stops: in the names people give to places and to each other, in the boundaries drawn between communities and in the bureaucratic procedures of compensation and rehabilitation. Rehabilitation itself becomes part of the ethnographic story — a set of state practices that reshape community even as they promise repair.

Methodologically, Mehta's work is an anthropology of events. Rather than treating the riot as a bounded episode to be explained from outside, he follows how an event is made and remade in narration, attending to the words residents use and to the silences around them. This approach, developed in conversation with Veena Das's work on social suffering, forms part of a wider body of Indian anthropological work on violence, memory, and the state.

## Influence and Critique

Mehta's work with Chatterji is widely cited in the anthropology of South Asia and in the comparative study of communal violence, urban life, and post-violence recovery. It is read alongside the work of Veena Das on the aftermath of violence in Delhi, Thomas Blom Hansen on identity and violence in postcolonial Bombay, and the larger literature on social suffering. For students of Indian anthropology, Mehta is also significant for his work on Muslim communities in India.

The work has also been discussed for the limits of its scale: an intensive study of one neighbourhood cannot by itself settle how far its findings travel to other sites of communal violence.
