# Deepak Mehta

Deepak Mehta is an Indian anthropologist and sociologist at the University of Delhi, known for his ethnographic studies of communal violence in urban India. Working closely with the sociologist Roma Chatterji, he spent a decade studying Dharavi, the vast Mumbai neighbourhood, in the aftermath of the Hindu–Muslim riots of 1992–93 that followed the demolition of the Babri mosque at Ayodhya. His work shows how violence is lived, remembered, and narrated in everyday life — through rumour, naming, housing, and the long aftermath of rehabilitation — and it has become a central reference for the anthropology of violence in South Asia.

## Life and Career

Mehta taught sociology and anthropology at the Delhi School of Economics, University of Delhi, where he was a professor and a formative teacher for a generation of students of anthropology and the study of violence. His colleagues and students remember him as a teacher who opened the world of anthropology through his writing and teaching on violence, and he served on the governing board of the Institute for Socio-Economic Research on Development and Democracy (ISERDD) in Delhi.

His research career was shaped by the communal violence of the early 1990s. In 1994 and 1995, Mehta and Roma Chatterji, both then sociologists at the University of Delhi, conducted fieldwork in four parts of Dharavi, a Mumbai settlement of some 600,000 people, studying the rioting of December 1992 and January 1993. Their interviews and fieldwork continued over a ten-year period, from 1995 to 2005, with some forty informants, following residents through the long aftermath of the violence and the procedures of rehabilitation that accompanied it. The work was conducted in dialogue with the wider anthropology of violence and social suffering associated with Veena Das, Arthur Kleinman, and their collaborators.

## Key Works

Mehta's central work is *Living with Violence: An Anthropology of Events and Everyday Life* (2007), written with Roma Chatterji and published by Routledge. Based on narrative accounts of Dharavi residents, the book explores how the violence of 1992–93 was experienced and remembered, and how language, housing, and rehabilitation shaped the lives of people living with its consequences. An earlier chapter, "Boundaries, Names, Alterities: A Case Study of a 'Communal Riot' in Dharavi, Bombay," written with Chatterji for the volume *Remaking a World: Violence, Social Suffering, and Recovery* (2001), edited by Veena Das, Arthur Kleinman, Margaret Lock, Mamphela Ramphele, and Pamela Reynolds, presented the core of the argument to an international audience.

Mehta and Chatterji also wrote on the politics of housing and slum redevelopment in Dharavi, including "Plans, Habitation and Slum Redevelopment: The Production of Community in Dharavi, Mumbai" (*Contributions to Indian Sociology*, 2005), which examined how redevelopment plans produce — and disrupt — community. Mehta co-edited *Riot Discourses* with Chatterji, a collection on the languages through which riots are narrated and understood.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Mehta's work begins from a refusal of the standard account of communal riots as the eruption of ancient religious hatreds. In Dharavi, he and Chatterji showed, the violence of December 1992 and January 1993 was shaped as much by neighbourhood gang rivalries, family feuds, and local coalitions as by the larger religious conflict — and by the specific ways in which the demolition of the Babri mosque on 6 December 1992 was narrated locally, street by street and house by house. The Srikrishna Commission, appointed to inquire into the Mumbai riots, recorded some 900 deaths and more than 21,000 injured between December 1992 and January 1993; Mehta's ethnography asks what such numbers mean for the people who lived through them.

A central idea is that violence is not only an event but a condition of everyday life. In *Living with Violence*, Mehta and Chatterji trace how the riot continues to organise social relations long after the killing stops: in the names people give to places and to each other, in the boundaries drawn between Hindu and Muslim lanes, in the stories told to children, and in the bureaucratic procedures of compensation and rehabilitation. Rehabilitation itself becomes part of the ethnographic story — a set of state practices that reshape community even as they promise repair.

Methodologically, Mehta's work is an anthropology of events. Rather than treating the riot as a bounded episode to be explained from outside, he follows how an event is made and remade in narration, attending to the words residents use and to the silences around them. This approach, developed in conversation with Veena Das's work on social suffering, has influenced how anthropologists in India study violence, memory, and the state.

## Influence and Critique

Mehta's work with Chatterji is widely cited in the anthropology of South Asia and in the comparative study of communal violence, urban life, and post-violence recovery. It is read alongside the work of Veena Das on the aftermath of violence in Delhi, Thomas Blom Hansen on identity and violence in postcolonial Bombay, and the larger literature on social suffering. For students of Indian anthropology, Mehta is also significant as a teacher at the Delhi School of Economics who shaped a generation of scholars working on violence, Islam, and Muslim communities in India.

The work has also been discussed for the limits of its scale: an intensive study of one neighbourhood cannot by itself settle how far its findings travel to other sites of communal violence.
