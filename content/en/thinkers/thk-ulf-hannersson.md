# Ulf Hannerz

Ulf Hannerz (born 1942) is a Swedish anthropologist, professor emeritus of social anthropology at Stockholm University, and a leading figure in the anthropology of globalisation. From an early ethnography of a Black neighbourhood in Washington, D.C., to a study of foreign correspondents around the world, his work has traced a single question: what happens to culture when people, ideas, and images move constantly across borders? His answers — cultural flows, creolisation, cosmopolitans and locals — became the working vocabulary of the anthropology of globalisation and of the study of transnational life.

## Life and Career

Hannerz was born in 1942 in Sweden. He studied at Stockholm University, where he received his doctorate in 1969 with a thesis based on fieldwork in a Black neighbourhood of Washington, D.C. He spent his career at Stockholm University, where he became professor of social anthropology, and he has taught at universities in the United States, Europe, Asia, and Australia.

He is a member of the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences. His fieldwork has taken him from a poor African-American neighbourhood in Washington, D.C., to the Cayman Islands, a small Nigerian town, and, for his study of foreign correspondents, to news bureaus in cities such as Jerusalem, Johannesburg, and Tokyo.

## Key Works

His first book, *Soulside: Inquiries into Ghetto Culture and Community* (1969), was based on fieldwork in Washington, D.C., in the late 1960s. It studied the cultural and social survival strategies of residents of poor Black neighbourhoods, and it established his reputation as a perceptive observer of urban life. *Exploring the City: Inquiries Toward an Urban Anthropology* (1980) drew on that experience to argue for a unified anthropology of the city, bringing together the perspectives of anthropology, sociology, and geography.

His essay "Cosmopolitans and Locals in World Culture" (1990) became one of the most cited texts in the study of globalisation, distinguishing between two orientations toward cultural difference: the cosmopolitan, open to other cultures and willing to engage with them, and the local, rooted in a single place. *Cultural Complexity: Studies in the Social Organization of Meaning* (1992) developed his theory of culture as a flow of meanings rather than a bounded whole. *Transnational Connections: Culture, People, Places* (1996) is his best-known book, arguing that national frameworks are inadequate for understanding culture in an interconnected world. Later works include *Foreign News: Exploring the World of Foreign Correspondents* (2004), an ethnography of the transnational profession of journalism, and *Anthropology's World: Life in a Twenty-First-Century Discipline* (2010).

## Main Ideas: Cultural Flows, Creolisation, and Cosmopolitanism

Hannerz's central argument is that culture should not be imagined as a set of bounded, homogeneous wholes, each belonging to a nation or a people. Instead, culture moves: it flows across borders through migration, media, trade, and communication, and it is continually recombined in new places. His concept of creolisation — borrowed from the study of Caribbean languages — describes how imported cultural forms are taken up locally, mixed with existing practices, and transformed into something new. The result is not a uniform global culture, nor a set of separate local cultures, but a continuous process of mixing and remaking.

His distinction between centres and peripheries added a political dimension to the model. Global cultural centres — New York, London, Tokyo — export forms and standards on a large scale, while peripheries receive these flows and recombine them locally. But this is not a one-way transmission: local actors select, reinterpret, and resist, so that the flow is always a negotiation rather than an imposition. His earlier fieldwork in the Cayman Islands, published as *Caymanian Politics* (1974), examined structure and style in the politics of a small island society.

His work on cosmopolitanism asked what kind of person thrives in this world of flows. The cosmopolitan, in Hannerz's account, is not simply a frequent traveller but someone with a willingness to engage with the Other — an openness to cultural difference as a value in itself. The concept became central to debates about globalisation, migration, and identity.

## Influence and Critique

Hannerz is counted among the founding generation of the anthropology of globalisation, alongside Arjun Appadurai, whose work on global cultural "scapes" developed in parallel. His concepts of flows, creolisation, and cosmopolitanism are now standard terms in the study of transnational life, and his ethnography of foreign correspondents opened a new field: the anthropology of transnational professions, of people whose work is itself the movement of information across borders.

Critics have argued that his model of flows sometimes underplays the power relations that shape them — that the movement of culture is not a neutral circulation but is structured by colonial histories, economic inequality, and the dominance of particular centres. Others have questioned whether cosmopolitanism, as he describes it, is an orientation available mainly to privileged travellers rather than to the migrants and refugees whose mobility is forced rather than chosen. Hannerz himself acknowledged these limits, insisting that flows are always shaped by centres and peripheries, and that openness to difference is a practice, not a property of the privileged alone.

His legacy is a discipline that no longer takes the bounded local community as its natural object, but studies connections: the networks, media, and movements through which culture is made.
