# Buddha

The title "Buddha" ("awakened one") is given to Siddhartha Gautama, also called Shakyamuni, the teacher from whose preaching Buddhism arose in the Ganges plain of northern India. His traditional dates are disputed: older Theravada chronologies placed him around 563–483 BCE, while many modern scholars favour a later span, with his death somewhere in the late fifth century BCE. He taught that suffering has causes, that it can end, and that a practical path leads to its end.

## Life and Context

No contemporary document records his life; the biography is assembled from texts composed and compiled by his followers, which blend memory with legend. According to tradition he was born into the Shakya clan, in the region of Lumbini on the present Nepal-India border, the son of a ruling family. Dissatisfied with a sheltered life, he left home as a young man, studied under teachers of meditation, practised severe asceticism, and then abandoned extremes. After attaining awakening at Bodh Gaya he gave his first teaching at Sarnath, near Varanasi, and for some decades taught in the kingdoms of Magadha and Kosala, founding a community of monks and later nuns. He is said to have died at Kushinagar.

His world was one of urban growth, new kingdoms and a wave of wandering ascetics (*shramanas*) who questioned the authority of the Vedic priests. The Buddha is one of these teachers, alongside figures such as the Jain leader Mahavira.

## Main Ideas

The Four Noble Truths summarize the teaching: life involves *dukkha* (suffering, unsatisfactoriness); it arises from craving and attachment; it can cease; and the Noble Eightfold Path (right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration) leads to that cessation, called *nirvana*.

Three marks characterize conditioned existence: impermanence (*anicca*), suffering, and non-self (*anatta*), the claim that no unchanging, independent self can be found among the five aggregates of body, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness. Dependent origination explains how phenomena arise in conditioned relation to one another, including the cycle of rebirth driven by action (*karma*) and ignorance. The "Middle Way" rejects both sensual indulgence and extreme self-mortification, and, in philosophical readings, both eternalism and annihilationism. The Buddha also reportedly declined to answer certain metaphysical questions, such as whether the world is eternal, because they do not conduce to liberation.

## Sources of Our Knowledge

The Buddha wrote nothing. His teachings were remembered and recited orally by the community and written down centuries later, in the Pali Canon of the Theravada school (the *Sutta*, *Vinaya* and *Abhidhamma* collections) and in parallel collections preserved in Sanskrit, Chinese and Tibetan. Mahayana traditions later added further sutras attributed to him, which historians do not regard as his words. The Pali discourses, such as the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta* and the *Mahaparinibbana Sutta*, are the usual starting points, but scholars debate how much of them goes back to the Buddha himself and how much was shaped by later councils and editors.

## Method

The teaching is presented as practice-oriented and diagnostic: like a physician, the Buddha identifies a problem, its cause, its cure and a course of treatment. Knowledge is to be tested by one's own experience, and the texts record encouragement to examine teachings critically instead of accepting them on authority. Practice combines ethical conduct (*sila*), meditation (*samadhi*) and wisdom (*prajna*). The discourses often use analysis (breaking experience into aggregates or sense bases), dialogue, simile and parable.

## Influence and Reception

Buddhism spread across South, Southeast and East Asia, developing the Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions. Philosophers such as Nagarjuna (emptiness), Vasubandhu and Dignaga, and Chinese and Japanese schools such as Chan and Zen, developed the ideas with great sophistication. King Ashoka's patronage in the third century BCE helped establish its reach. In the modern period Schopenhauer, among others, drew on it, and from the twentieth century it has become a worldwide religion and a topic of interest in philosophy of mind, ethics and cognitive science, including mindfulness practices detached from their original context.

## Criticisms and Debates

Within India, Hindu schools such as Nyaya and Vedanta criticized the denial of a self (atman), and the Buddhist account of rebirth without a persisting self has drawn continuing debate: what is it that is reborn? Modern scholars dispute the historical Buddha's real doctrine, arguing about whether early Buddhism was primarily a soteriology, a philosophy, or a monastic discipline, and about whether the Pali Canon represents the earliest layer. Some critics find the emphasis on renunciation world-denying, while sympathetic interpreters stress the social ethics of compassion and non-harm. Secular and "Buddhist modernist" readings that minimize rebirth and karma are themselves contested as departures from traditional teaching.
