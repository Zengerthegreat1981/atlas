# James M. Buchanan

**James M. Buchanan** was an American economist and a co-founder, together with Gordon Tullock, of the field of public choice theory. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1986 for "his development of the contractual and constitutional bases for the theory of economic and political decision-making." He is the central figure of what became known as the Virginia school of political economy.

## From a Tennessee farm to Chicago

Buchanan grew up in a modest family in Tennessee and studied economics at the University of Chicago, where he was influenced by Frank Knight's ideas on the limits of economic rationality. He also drew on the Swedish economist Knut Wicksell's idea that fiscal decisions should be judged by the consent of those affected. He then held posts at several American universities, most notably the University of Virginia, Virginia Polytechnic Institute and George Mason University, where he established research centres devoted to the study of public choice and constitutional economics.

## Rejecting the assumption of the benevolent planner

Buchanan argued that traditional welfare economics made a fundamental methodological error by implicitly assuming that government is a neutral apparatus that by nature seeks to correct market failures and maximize the public interest. From his perspective, the same methodological assumption used in market analysis (the individual pursuing his own self-interest) should also be applied to analysing the behaviour of politicians, bureaucrats and voters, without an arbitrary exemption of political science from this logic.

## The Calculus of Consent with Tullock

In 1962 Buchanan and Gordon Tullock published their founding book *The Calculus of Consent*, which analysed democratic collective decision-making from the perspective of decision-making costs. Every rule for collective decision (such as full unanimity or simple majority) involves a trade-off between "decision-making costs" (the time and effort needed to reach agreement) and "external costs" (the harm a collective decision may impose on a dissenting minority). The book sought to identify the optimal constitutional rules that balance these two kinds of cost according to the nature of the decision at hand.

## Constitutional economics

Buchanan later developed what he called "constitutional economics": an approach that focuses analysis on the level of higher constitutional rules (rather than day-to-day individual policies), as the level at which to restrain the tendency of political actors to expand public spending and chronic budget deficits. His other major works include *The Limits of Liberty* (1975). He proposed permanent constitutional constraints (such as balanced-budget rules) that a transient political majority would find difficult to amend easily to serve short-term interests.

## Critique of chronic deficits in democracies

With Richard Wagner, Buchanan presented in *Democracy in Deficit* (1977) an explanation of why modern democracies are structurally prone to chronic deficits in public budgets. Elected politicians face short-term incentives to increase public spending (which pleases voters immediately) and to avoid raising taxes (which angers voters immediately), while the burden of the resulting debt can be deferred to future generations that do not vote in current elections.

## Nobel Prize and late recognition

Buchanan received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1986, after decades in which public choice sat outside the centre of mainstream economics. By the 1980s the field was widely taught and cited.

## Contractarian philosophy and methodological individualism

Buchanan's political analysis is contractarian. He asked which constitutional rules rational individuals could agree to when they are uncertain about their future positions. Commentators often compare that device with John Rawls's veil of ignorance. The two projects differ sharply in the distributive outcomes they defend.

## Move to George Mason

Buchanan moved with his research centre from Virginia Polytechnic Institute to George Mason University in 1983. George Mason thereafter housed both public-choice research and a substantial Austrian-economics group, two traditions that remained distinct.

## Criticism from outside the tradition

Buchanan faced criticism from political scientists and economists who held that reducing political motives entirely to material self-interest ignores the genuine ideological and value-based motives of some political actors. He also faced criticism from leftist critics, who regarded his demand for strict constitutional limits on public spending as effectively serving to shrink social welfare programmes more than serving a purely analytical neutrality toward all forms of state intervention on an equal footing.

## Death and enduring legacy

Buchanan died in 2013 at the age of 93, leaving an institutional legacy at George Mason University, where a centre was later named in his honour, and an analytical method that has become a fundamental part of political economy curricula in Western universities. Public choice is now widely taught as part of political economy.
