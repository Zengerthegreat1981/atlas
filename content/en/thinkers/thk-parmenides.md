# Parmenides of Elea

A Greek philosopher of Elea (Magna Graecia, southern Italy), founder of the **Eleatic School** and often called the **father of ontology** (the study of being) in Western philosophy. His dates are inferred: Diogenes Laertius places his prime in the 69th Olympiad (504–501 BCE), which yields a birth around 515 BCE, while Plato's dialogue *Parmenides* imagines him, aged about sixty-five, visiting Athens around 450 BCE.

## His Contribution

He wrote a single philosophical poem in hexameters, traditionally titled *On Nature* (Περὶ φύσεως). It does not survive complete: roughly 150 lines are preserved as quotations in later authors, above all Simplicius, along with Sextus Empiricus, Plutarch, Proclus and Clement. It has three parts:

1. **The Proem**: the narrator is carried in a chariot drawn by mares, escorted by the daughters of the Sun (the Heliades), through the gates of Night and Day, which are guarded by Dike (Justice), and is received by an unnamed goddess who reveals the way of truth.
2. **The Way of Truth (Aletheia)**: a rational inquiry whose starting point is that what is, is, and what is not, is not and cannot be thought or spoken of. What is has these features:
   - **Ungenerated and imperishable**: it neither came to be nor passes away.
   - **Whole and unique** (often read as monistic): there is not a plurality of things.
   - **Unchanging and unmoved**: it undergoes no change or motion.
   - **Continuous and indivisible**: there are no gaps in it.
   - **Complete**: described as like the mass of a well-rounded sphere.
3. **The Way of Opinion (Doxa)**: an account of the world as mortals suppose it, given in a deceptive ordering, built on the opposition of Light and Night (fire and darkness) and covering cosmology and the origin of living beings. Interpreters disagree about its status: whether it is a deliberately inferior likeness of the world, a dialectical exercise, or the best possible account of appearances.

## Parmenides' Concepts

### 1. The Exclusion of Non-Being

The poem does not state the principle of non-contradiction in Aristotle's later formulation (*Metaphysics* Γ 3, 1005b), but its insistence that "it is not" cannot be a path of inquiry is a major precursor of the idea that being and non-being cannot be combined.

### 2. "What Is" (to eon)

The Greek participle *to eon*, "what is," is the subject of the Way of Truth. Much scholarly debate concerns whether "is" there has an existential, predicative or veridical sense, and whether the poem's subject is a single entity or the conditions of any inquiry.

### 3. The Three Ways

The goddess distinguishes three ways: the way that "is" (and cannot not be), the way that "is not" (declared unthinkable and unlearnable), and the way of mortals who treat being and non-being as both the same and not the same, wandering with "two heads." The poem rejects the second and third.

## His Place in the Tradition

- **The Eleatic School**: with **Zeno of Elea** (his follower, famous for paradoxes defending the denial of plurality and motion) and **Melissus of Samos**, a later Eleatic.
- **The Presocratics**: a figure of the generation after Xenophanes, regarded as a turning point in the move from mythical explanation to argument.
- **A source for Plato**: Plato's dialogues *Parmenides* and *Sophist* discuss him, and Plato's Eleatic Stranger speaks of "father Parmenides."

## His Intellectual Network

- **Xenophanes**: named in some ancient sources as his teacher, though the link is doubtful; Diogenes Laertius also reports a Pythagorean associate, Ameinias.
- **Heraclitus (c. 540–480 BCE)**: the "two-headed mortals" of the poem are sometimes taken as a criticism of Heraclitus, though this is disputed. In Plato's *Theaetetus* (152e, 180d–e) the two are set against each other as champions of flux and of stability.
- **Zeno of Elea (c. 490–430 BCE)**: defended his thesis against critics, according to Plato's *Parmenides*, through the famous paradoxes (Achilles and the tortoise, the arrow, and others).
- **Empedocles (c. 494–434 BCE)** and **Anaxagoras (c. 500–428 BCE)**: pluralists who responded to the Eleatic challenge, with four "roots" and with Mind (Nous) as a principle of order.
- **Plato (c. 428–348 BCE)**: re-read Parmenides in the *Parmenides* and *Sophist*.
- **Aristotle (384–322 BCE)**: criticized the Eleatics in the *Physics* and the *Metaphysics*.

## His Life

Reliable biography is sparse. Elea was a colony founded by Phocaeans around the middle of the sixth century BCE. Later reports preserved by Diogenes Laertius (drawing on Speusippus) say that Parmenides gave laws to his city, and that he was associated with a Pythagorean circle; these reports are of uncertain reliability. The exact year of his death is unknown.

## His Legacy

- **Western ontology**: the study of being was shaped by his thesis; Plato and Aristotle respond to it directly, and Hegel and Heidegger returned to it.
- **The perennial debate**: the opposition between a world of change and a world of stable being, often framed as Heraclitus against Parmenides.
- **Argument form**: his poem is one of the earliest sustained deductive arguments in Western thought.
- **The One**: Neoplatonism engaged with Parmenides, especially through Plato's dialogue, in its doctrine of the One.
