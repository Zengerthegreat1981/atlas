# Medard Boss

Medard Boss (1903-1990) was a Swiss psychiatrist and psychoanalyst. He is the figure in existential therapy who worked most closely with Martin Heidegger over many years, and he produced a form of *Daseinsanalysis* that the philosopher helped shape. Most English readers who learn about "Daseinsanalysis" are in effect reading his version.

## Life and Context

Born in Switzerland, Boss studied medicine and trained in psychoanalysis, in the circle of Freud's followers and with other analysts of several schools, before settling as a psychiatrist in Zurich. Early on he was close to Ludwig Binswanger's project of existential analysis, but he came to differ with him on method. Boss concluded that Binswanger's blend of Husserl, Heidegger and other sources was insufficiently faithful to Heidegger's own thought, and that Heidegger's account of human existence as openness to the world offered a better foundation for medicine and psychology.

## The Zollikon Seminars

From 1959, Boss hosted seminars in Zollikon, near Zurich, where Heidegger met Swiss psychiatrists and analysts over roughly ten years, until 1969. Heidegger tried to carry concepts from *Being and Time*, such as being-in-the-world, openness and temporality, into the practical vocabulary of physicians, and discussed clinical cases in that light. The seminar protocols and related correspondence were published as *Zollikoner Seminare* (1987), edited by Boss after Heidegger's death in 1976; an English translation appeared in 2001. The collection remains the most direct record of Heidegger's engagement with psychiatry.

## Main Ideas

**The unconscious.** Boss rejected the Freudian unconscious as a theoretical construct that is not given in experience. In his view, what is not seen is not hidden away inside but simply not yet disclosed, not yet opened up to the person.

**Dreams.** In *Der Traum und seine Auslegung* (1953; English *The Analysis of Dreams*, 1958) and later *I Dreamt Last Night* (1977), he proposed treating dreams as experiences with their own world-disclosing character, to be taken at face value rather than decoded into hidden latent content.

**Technique.** He was wholly opposed to techniques conceived as a method imposed on the person: no preset therapeutic goals, no exercises, no measurement. The therapist lets what shows itself show itself, and helps the patient become open to the possibilities of existence that were closed off. Boss stayed personally respectful of Freud while insisting that the Freudian conceptual machinery misdescribes human existence as a closed psychic apparatus.

**Comparative interests.** He traveled to India in the 1950s and wrote on his encounter with its contemplative traditions, *Indienfahrt eines Psychiaters* (1959; English *A Psychiatrist Discovers India*, 1965), an early attempt at dialogue between European phenomenology and Asian practice.

## Major Works

- *Sinn und Gehalt der sexuellen Perversionen* (1947; Meaning and Content of Sexual Perversions)
- *Der Traum und seine Auslegung* (1953; English *The Analysis of Dreams*, 1958)
- *Psychoanalyse und Daseinsanalytik* (1957; Psychoanalysis and Daseinsanalysis)
- *Grundriss der Medizin und der Psychologie* (1971; English *Existential Foundations of Medicine and Psychology*, 1979)
- *Zollikoner Seminare* (1987, with Heidegger; published after Heidegger's death)

## Influence and Criticism

Boss is less famous than Binswanger but is often considered more philosophically precise, since he adhered closely to Heidegger. Students and colleagues, among them Gion Condrau, continued and edited his work and helped found training institutes in Switzerland. His influence in the English-speaking world was modest during his life, partly because of the difficulty of his philosophical idiom, and rose after the Zollikon Seminars were translated.

Critics point out that his refusal of any technique or preset goal makes the approach hard to teach and difficult to evaluate empirically. Others argue that his strict reading of Heidegger neglects the social, historical and biological dimensions of suffering, and, more broadly, that Heidegger's own political record complicates the use of his philosophy as a foundation for therapy. Some also ask whether denying the unconscious discards useful clinical observations.
