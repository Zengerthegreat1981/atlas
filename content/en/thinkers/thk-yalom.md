# Irvin D. Yalom

Irvin D. Yalom (born 1931) is the door through which most readers enter existential therapy — and the door that gives the most misleading impression that the school is one thing. An American psychiatrist, therapist, and novelist, and professor emeritus of psychiatry at Stanford University, he combined academic clinical writing with storytelling and the novel, and became more widely read than any other existential therapist. His achievement was to turn existential philosophy into a framework a working therapist can actually use.

## Life and Career

Yalom was born on 13 June 1931 in Washington, D.C., to a family of Russian Jewish immigrants, and grew up in a poor neighbourhood of the city. He studied medicine at Boston University, graduating in 1956, and trained in psychiatry at Johns Hopkins University, where he was influenced by Jerome Frank, known for his study of the common factors in psychotherapy. In 1962 he joined Stanford University, where he remained for decades. In the 1960s and 1970s he worked in group therapy and researched it, and was influenced by the existential thought of Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger, and Sartre, and by the writings of Rollo May and Viktor Frankl.

## The Four Ultimate Concerns

His most important contribution, in *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980), is to organise existential anxiety into four **ultimate concerns**: death, freedom with responsibility, isolation, and meaninglessness. Psychological conflict, he argues, arises from the individual's confrontation with these givens of existence, and symptoms can sometimes be understood as defences against this anxiety. The book's strength lies in combining an exposition of the philosophy with examples from actual sessions, so that a clinician can take the framework into the consulting room without committing to a whole philosophical system.

The four concerns recur through all his later work. In *Staring at the Sun: Overcoming the Terror of Death* (2008) he returns to the fear of death and to the idea of the "rippling effect" — the influence a person leaves behind in the lives they touch, which continues after the body is gone.

## Group Therapy and the Therapeutic Relationship

Yalom's other classic is *The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy* (1970), which sets out the curative factors of the group: instilling hope, universality, imparting information, altruism, the development of socialising techniques, and interpersonal learning. The book was revised through successive editions, including the fifth edition of 2005 with Molyn Leszcz, and became a standard text in training programmes.

At the centre of his practice is the conviction that the therapeutic relationship itself is the instrument of healing. He encourages the therapist to be honest and present, and to address what is happening between therapist and patient in the here and now. He distilled this stance into brief advice for therapists in *The Gift of Therapy* (2002), whose most quoted line — "the relationship itself heals" — captures his whole orientation.

## Storytelling and the Novels

Yalom reached his widest audience through clinical stories and novels. *Love's Executioner and Other Tales of Psychotherapy* (1989) collects case stories told with novelistic candour. His novels — *When Nietzsche Wept* (1992), imagining a meeting between Nietzsche and the physician Josef Breuer; *Lying on the Couch* (1996); *The Schopenhauer Cure* (2005); and *The Spinoza Problem* (2012) — usually turn on a therapist and a philosopher or patient, and convert existential questions into dialogue between characters. His memoir, *Becoming Myself* (2017), looks back over a long clinical life, and in 2021 he published *A Matter of Death and Life*, written with his wife Marilyn, who died in 2019.

## Influence and Critique

Yalom represents one current among several in existential therapy — the American current, which stresses the ultimate concerns and the relationship — and it differs from the European school of Binswanger and Boss and from Frankl's logotherapy. Presenting him as "existential therapy" in general is a common error, especially in Arabic writing, where several of his books, above all the novels, have been translated.

Critics charge his framework with a circularity that is hard to falsify: every anxiety can be traced back to one of the four concerns, so little could count against the hypothesis. He sometimes moves from description to causation, presenting the concerns as causes of particular symptoms, and his core case sample is narrow — mostly educated, middle-class American patients — which raises questions about cultural generalisation. Others see his stance as individualistic, giving too little weight to the social and political dimensions of suffering. What endures is an excellent phenomenological map of what people do with their awareness of the conditions of their existence — not a proven causal theory of the origin of symptoms. Research on group therapy has confirmed the curative factors he described, even if to varying degrees, and that empirical support should not be confused with the philosophical claims of his other books.
