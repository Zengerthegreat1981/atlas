# Duncan Kennedy

An American law professor (born 1942) at Harvard University, one of the founders of **Critical Legal Studies** and among its most influential theorists in shaping its analytic tools.

## 'Form and Substance in Private Law Adjudication' (1976)

In his founding article *Form and Substance in Private Law Adjudication* (1976), Kennedy analyzes how adjudication in private law (contracts and torts) oscillates between **rules**, which are applied mechanically regardless of the fairness of the outcome in the individual case, and **standards**, which permit the assessment of fairness in each case. He shows that the choice between the two forms is not a neutral technical matter but reflects an implicit ideological commitment: a tendency toward **individualism** or toward **altruism/solidarity**.

## The thesis of 'fundamental contradiction'

In *Form and Substance* and then, more fully, in *The Structure of Blackstone's Commentaries* (1979), Kennedy develops a deeper thesis: that modern liberal legal thought is built on a **fundamental contradiction** that cannot be ultimately resolved. Our relations with others are necessary for our individual freedom and at the same time conflict with it: the other is necessary for the realization of the self, but is also a source of threat to its autonomy. According to Kennedy, this contradiction is what makes the resolution of many legal questions (such as the limits of freedom of contract versus the protection of the weaker party) **logically indeterminate in the end**, however precisely the rule is formulated. In the Blackstone article he reads the structure of Blackstone's *Commentaries* as an attempt to manage this contradiction. See **legal indeterminacy**.

## His life and intellectual path

Duncan Kennedy was born in 1942, studied at Harvard College and Yale Law School, and has taught at Harvard Law School since the early 1970s. He was one of the leading participants in the founding of the **Critical Legal Studies** (CLS) movement, whose first conference was held in 1977, alongside figures such as Roberto Unger, Morton Horwitz, Karl Klare and Mark Tushnet. Kennedy is distinguished by combining abstract theoretical analysis with direct political engagement in criticizing legal education itself. He wrote a controversial essay titled *Legal Education and the Reproduction of Hierarchy* (published in 1982 and as a pamphlet in 1983), in which he attacked American law schools as instruments for reproducing class and professional hierarchy under the cover of neutral technical training.

## The method of 'paired opposites' in analyzing rules

Kennedy developed an influential analytic tool based on identifying pairs of opposing principles latent in each legal field (such as 'freedom of contract' versus 'protection of the weak party' in contract law, or 'absolute ownership' versus 'neighbor rights' in property law). He argues that any judgment in a difficult case is in essence a political-ideological choice between these two poles, disguised as a neutral logical deduction from the existing legal texts.

## Critique of hierarchy in legal education

In his article on legal education, Kennedy argues that elite law schools do not merely impart neutral technical skills to students. Through the Socratic method of teaching, grade hierarchies, and professional competitions, they train students to accept a particular social and professional hierarchy as natural and not open to questioning. This thesis draws on critical social theory to analyze the function of the educational institution in reproducing the existing class structure through the apparently objective discourse of merit.

## Impact and criticism

Kennedy has had a broad influence on generations of critical legal scholars in the United States and Europe, and his analytic tools have extended into gender studies in law and postcolonial legal studies. His critics, both liberal and positivist, argue, however, that the thesis of 'fundamental contradiction' overstates the indeterminacy of all law, overlooking the wide areas in which cases are resolved easily by applying clear rules without any real ideological hesitation.

## The three-phase theory of legal thought

In his historical writing, including *The Rise and Fall of Classical Legal Thought* (written in the 1970s and published later in book form) and the essay "Three Globalizations of Law and Legal Thought: 1850-2000", Kennedy offers a history of Western legal thought in three phases: **classical legal thought** (from the mid-nineteenth century), based on strict formal concepts such as 'absolute ownership' and 'freedom of contract'; then **the social** (from around 1900), associated with sociological jurisprudence and legal realism, which attacked those concepts by exposing their hidden policy content; and then **policy jurisprudence** after World War II, which treats rules as instruments for balancing competing policies. Kennedy treats critical legal studies as heir to the realist attack on classical formalism, using more radical theoretical tools than the early American realists.
