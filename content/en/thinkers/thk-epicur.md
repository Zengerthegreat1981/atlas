# Epicurus of Samos

Founder of the Epicurean school and philosopher of rational pleasure and psychological tranquility (341–270 BCE). He founded the school of the "Garden" in Athens. He shaped a therapeutic philosophy that aims to free human beings from their greatest existential fears (fear of death and fear of the wrath of the gods) by means of atomic physics, and he defined true happiness as "ataraxia" (tranquility and freedom of the soul from anxiety and disturbance) and "aponia" (the absence of bodily pain).

## Life and Formation

Epicurus was born on the island of Samos in 341 BCE to an Athenian family that had settled on the island. According to Diogenes Laertius he took an interest in philosophy at an early age and was taught by Platonists and by the Democritean Nausiphanes, who introduced him to atomism. He spent a short time in Athens, then taught in Mytilene and Lampsacus in Asia Minor, and returned to Athens around 306 BCE, where he bought a house and garden that became the seat of his school. For this reason the community was called the "Garden" (Kēpos), and it admitted women and slaves to membership, among them Leontion and Themista, which was rare among the Athenian philosophical schools. Epicurus died in 270 BCE of kidney stones, and according to a letter attributed to him, written to Idomeneus before his death, the joy of remembering philosophical conversations eased his bodily pain.

## What He Contributed

He offered the "fourfold remedy" (Tetrapharmakos) as a philosophical-psychological prescription: 1. Do not fear the gods; 2. Do not worry about death; 3. The good is easy to obtain; 4. The evil is easy to endure. He stressed the value of philosophical friendship and of freeing oneself from false political and material ambitions.

## Physics and Knowledge

Epicurus's physics rests on the atomism of Democritus with a modification: the world is composed of atoms and void, and the atoms move in the void. He added that they swerve very slightly from their course (an idea that Lucretius later presents under the name "swerve", clinamen), in order to explain human freedom. As for knowledge, its source is sensations, preconceptions (prolēpseis) and feelings (pleasure and pain); these are the "criteria of truth", so what we say may not contradict clear sensation. The gods exist in his view, but they are blessed in their happiness and do not intervene in human affairs, so their punishment is not to be feared. As for death, it is nothing to us, because it is the absence of sensation, and the soul dissolves with the dissolution of the body: "When we are, death is not; and when death is, we are not."

## The Ethics of Pleasure

Epicurus makes pleasure the end of life, but he does not mean the pleasures of indulgence. He distinguishes natural and necessary desires (food, shelter, friendship), natural but unnecessary desires, and vain desires (glory and wealth). He holds that practical wisdom (phronēsis) is the greatest of the virtues because it calculates consequences, avoiding the pleasure that is followed by greater pain and enduring the pain that is followed by greater pleasure. The highest pleasure for him is the absence of pain and disturbance. He called for "live unnoticed" (lathe biōsas), that is, withdrawal from political life. He made friendship the greatest thing that wisdom provides toward happiness.

## Major Works

- *Letter to Menoeceus* (on ethics, desire, and happiness).
- *Principal Doctrines (Kyriai Doxai)*: forty concise maxims on ethics and tranquility preserved by Diogenes Laërtius.
- *Letter to Herodotus* (on atomistic physics and epistemology).
- *Letter to Pythocles* (on cosmology and meteorological phenomena).
- *On Nature (Peri Physeos)*: foundational treatise in thirty-seven books, of which fragments survive among the Herculaneum papyri.
- *Vatican Sayings*: collection of Epicurean aphorisms discovered in a 14th-century Vatican manuscript.

## Influence and Reception

Epicureanism spread in the Roman world through Lucretius and Philodemus, and receded after the triumph of Christianity, being stigmatized as atheism. Interest in it returned in the Renaissance after the discovery of Lucretius's poem in 1417, Gassendi revived it in the seventeenth century, Thomas Jefferson declared himself an adherent, and some scholars see a link between it and Bentham's utilitarianism. In popular usage "epicurean" has come to mean one who indulges in pleasures, the opposite of Epicurus's intent, since he called for moderation in desires. Scholars study him today as a philosophical therapist of anxiety, and Nussbaum compares him with the philosophical therapy of Hellenistic philosophy in *The Therapy of Desire* (1994).

## Limits and Critique

Cicero in *On Ends* and Plutarch criticized the idea that pleasure is the highest good, holding that it neglects virtue and public duty. Contemporary critics hold that "live unnoticed" calls for withdrawal from civic responsibility. Studies point out that the precision of his atomic system rested on superficial observation rather than experiment, and that the idea of the atomic swerve appeared to fill a gap in the system rather than from observation. Knowledge of his philosophy also depends for the most part on indirect sources such as Diogenes Laertius, Lucretius and Cicero, some of them hostile to him.
