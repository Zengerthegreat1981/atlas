# Daron Acemoglu

**Daron Acemoglu** is a Turkish-American economist of Armenian descent, and a professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. He is one of the leading contemporary researchers on the relationship between political and economic institutions and long-run development. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2024, shared with **Simon Johnson** and **James Robinson**, for their study of how institutions form and how they affect whether nations prosper or fail.

## Academic path

Acemoglu was born in Istanbul to a Turkish Armenian family. He studied economics at the University of York in Britain, took a master's degree at the London School of Economics, and obtained his doctorate from the London School of Economics in 1992. He joined the faculty of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1993, where he became one of the most productive and most cited economists of his generation. He collaborated with researchers from diverse backgrounds in the economics of labour, growth and political institutions.

## The colonial origins of comparative development (2001)

In a central joint study with Simon Johnson and James Robinson in 2001, Acemoglu developed an innovative statistical strategy to separate the causal effect of institutions from geographical and climatic factors in explaining current differences in income levels between countries. The researchers used the mortality rates of early European settlers in different colonies as an **instrumental variable**. Where mortality rates were high (because of malaria and yellow fever), European colonists established harsh **extractive institutions** designed to plunder resources, with minimal settlement. Where mortality was low, they established **inclusive institutions** that protected property rights and limited the power of rulers. The study showed that this early institutional difference continues to explain a large part of today's global differences in income, independently of purely geographical factors.

## Why nations fail (2012)

Acemoglu and Robinson expanded this thesis into a comprehensive book for a general readership, *Why Nations Fail* (2012). In it they distinguished between two types of institutions: **inclusive institutions**, which distribute political and economic power widely, protect property rights, and encourage innovation and competition, and **extractive institutions**, which concentrate power and wealth in the hands of a narrow elite at the expense of the rest of society. They argued that the difference between the prosperity and failure of nations goes back fundamentally to this institutional distinction, rather than to culture, geography or ignorance of correct economic policies, as competing explanations assume. They supported this with many comparative historical examples, from North and South Korea to the Spanish and English colonies in the Americas.

## Labour economics and technological change

Alongside institutional economics, Acemoglu contributed prolifically to the economics of labour and technological change. In later research, including work with Pascual Restrepo, he argued that automation and artificial intelligence may not distribute their productivity gains equally. They may displace middle-class jobs without creating alternative employment fast enough. In his view, this calls for proactive public policies to manage technological transformation, rather than the assumption that the market will solve the problem automatically.

## Critical reception

The thesis of "inclusive versus extractive institutions" has been criticized by researchers who argue that it may oversimplify the complexity of actual historical development paths. They point out that some developmentally successful cases, such as contemporary China, achieved rapid economic growth despite political institutions that are not inclusive in the democratic sense the framework assumes. This raises questions about whether institutional inclusiveness is a strictly necessary condition for growth or one contributing factor among many.

## Nobel Prize

Acemoglu was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2024, shared with Johnson and Robinson "for studies of how institutions are formed and affect prosperity". The award recognised two decades of joint work on the causal relationship between institutional structure and long-run economic development.

## Prolific research and broad influence

Acemoglu is among the most published and most cited economists of his generation. His research output is large and covers diverse topics, from democracy and political transition to economic networks and the diffusion of information. He is also the author of an authoritative graduate textbook on economic growth, *Introduction to Modern Economic Growth*, which is taught in doctoral programmes around the world. Across his career he has combined the mathematical rigour of theoretical modelling with a deep interest in the great institutional and historical questions about why wealth and power are distributed unequally across the world.

## His place in the field of institutional economics

Acemoglu's work builds on an older tradition in institutional economics developed by **Douglass North** and **Ronald Coase**, which emphasized the importance of property rights and transaction costs in explaining economic performance. He added a more explicit political dimension, linking the institutional distribution to conflict over political power between elites and the masses. This yields a more dynamic theory that explains how and why institutions change over time in response to shifting balances of power. It goes beyond a static description of the economic outcomes of existing institutions.
