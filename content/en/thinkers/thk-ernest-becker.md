# Ernest Becker

Ernest Becker (1924–1974) was an American cultural anthropologist who wrote across the boundaries of anthropology, psychiatry, psychology, and philosophy. He sought a general "science of man" that would explain human motivation and evil, and he is best known for arguing that the awareness of death shapes character and culture.

## Life and career

Born in Springfield, Massachusetts, to a Jewish immigrant family, Becker served in the United States Army during the Second World War, including at the liberation of a Nazi camp. After a period in the American foreign service in Paris, he completed a doctorate in cultural anthropology at Syracuse University in 1960. His early academic work brought him into contact with the psychiatrist Thomas Szasz. He taught at several institutions, including the University of California, Berkeley, and San Francisco State, where political disputes and his interdisciplinary manner made his position insecure. He moved to Simon Fraser University in British Columbia in 1969 and remained there until his death from cancer.

## Self-esteem and social performance

*The Birth and Death of Meaning* (1962; revised 1971) presents the human self as formed through language and social interaction. People need to regard themselves as objects of value in a world of meaning, and cultures supply roles and ceremonies through which this esteem is earned and protected. Becker drew on Freud, George Herbert Mead, Erving Goffman, and anthropology. *The Revolution in Psychiatry* (1964) offered a social account of mental disorder as a failure or restriction of meaningful action, in place of a narrowly medical one.

## The denial of death

*The Denial of Death* (1973), which received the Pulitzer Prize for general non-fiction in 1974, after his death, draws especially on Otto Rank, Søren Kierkegaard, and Norman O. Brown. Human beings are animals who know that they will die and who also live in a symbolic world. To manage the terror this produces, they pursue what Becker calls heroism: the sense of being a person of lasting worth. A culture is a "hero system" that offers routes to such significance through religion, family, achievement, nation, or other causes. Character itself functions partly as a defence that narrows experience.

## Evil

*Escape from Evil* (1975), published posthumously, applies the argument to history. Efforts to secure symbolic immortality can lead people to scapegoat others, wage war, and accept inequality, since rival hero systems threaten one's own claim to significance. The attempt to conquer evil may itself generate it.

## Assessment

Becker's ideas inspired terror management theory, developed by the social psychologists Jeff Greenberg, Sheldon Solomon, and Tom Pyszczynski, who tested effects of reminders of mortality experimentally. The results and their interpretation remain debated, including in replication research, and such experiments do not establish the whole philosophical argument. Critics find that Becker makes one motive explain too much and relies heavily on psychoanalytic theory. His comparative claims are broad and rest less on fieldwork than on synthesis. Supporters value the connection he drew between private anxiety and public violence.
