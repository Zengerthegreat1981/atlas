# Albert Bandura

A Canadian-American psychologist (Mundare, Alberta 1925 – Stanford 2021), professor at Stanford University for many decades, and founder of "Social Learning Theory," which he later developed into "Social Cognitive Theory" (1986); he was among the most cited psychologists of the twentieth century.

## Historical Context

Bandura took his first degree at the University of British Columbia and his doctorate at the University of Iowa, then a stronghold of American experimental behaviorism, and moved to Stanford in 1953, where he spent his entire academic career. His work came at a time when radical behaviorism (Skinner) dominated American psychology, assuming that learning occurs only through direct reinforcement of individual responses. Bandura noticed that this model cannot explain how children acquire complex new behaviors (such as aggressive patterns or social skills) without themselves going through an experience of direct reinforcement, which led him to develop an alternative model giving a central role to observation, imitation, and internal cognitive processes, at a historical stage immediately preceding the "cognitive revolution" that changed the face of American psychology.

## Main Ideas or Method

Bandura formulated the concept of "Observational Learning" to describe how individuals acquire new behaviors by watching a model perform them, without needing direct reinforcement for themselves personally. He demonstrated this experimentally in the famous series of "Bobo doll" experiments (1961, 1963, and extensions up to 1965), in which children watched an adult violently strike a Bobo doll, and many later imitated the same aggressive behavior even without any direct reinforcement to them; the experiments also showed that watching the model be rewarded or punished for the act affects the likelihood of the child's imitating the behavior (vicarious learning). He later developed the concept of "Reciprocal Determinism" (1978), which holds that behavior, personal factors (cognitive and emotional), and the environment each influence the others in a circular, reciprocal relationship rather than a one-directional linear one. His most important later contribution is the concept of "Self-Efficacy" (1977), that is, an individual's belief in his personal ability to organize and execute the actions required to achieve a given performance, which many later studies have found to predict performance and persistence beyond what measured skill alone would predict.

## Major Works and Representatives

Bandura influenced generations of researchers in social and cognitive learning. His ideas sit alongside those of contemporaries working in a related tradition, among them Walter Mischel, who developed cognitive-social accounts of personality and studied delay of gratification, and Julian Rotter, whose social learning theory gave rise to the concept of "locus of control" (1966). The influence of his ideas also extended to important public debates about the effect of watching media violence and video games on aggressive behavior in children, relying directly on the logic of the original Bobo doll experiments.

## Influence and Criticism

Bandura is regarded today as the clearest bridge between classical behaviorism and the cognitive revolution in American psychology: he retained the terminology of behavioral reinforcement while granting internal cognitive processes (expectation, self-evaluation, self-efficacy) a central explanatory role that radical behaviorism did not accept. The Bobo doll experiments were later subjected to methodological criticism concerning how far their results generalize to real long-term aggression outside the laboratory, but the concept of self-efficacy proved to have wide predictive power and firm practical applications in many fields, from education to mental health and sport; in recognition of this Bandura received the Grawemeyer Award in Psychology (2008) and the U.S. National Medal of Science (awarded for 2015, conferred 2016), among numerous other honors before his death in 2021.
