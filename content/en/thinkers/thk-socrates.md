# Socrates

An Athenian philosopher (470–399 BCE) who wrote nothing himself and who is remembered as the figure who turned Greek thought toward ethics and the examined life. He questioned his fellow citizens about courage, piety, justice and virtue, professed that he knew nothing of great importance, and was tried and executed by Athens in 399 BCE. Because everything we know of him comes from others, the "historical Socrates" is itself a scholarly problem.

## Life and Context

Socrates was born in Athens, the son of Sophroniscus, a stonemason, and Phaenarete, who is said to have been a midwife. He lived through the rise of Athenian power, the age of Pericles, the Peloponnesian War and the city's defeat in 404 BCE. He served as a hoplite in several campaigns, and his contemporaries remembered him as unusually hardy and indifferent to wealth. He ran no school and took no fee, which distinguished him from the professional teachers known as sophists, though comic poets such as Aristophanes lumped him together with them.

In 399 BCE he was charged by Meletus, Anytus and Lycon with not recognizing the gods of the city, introducing new divinities, and corrupting the young. A jury of several hundred citizens convicted him by a narrow margin and sentenced him to death; he drank hemlock in prison. Many readers connect the trial with the political bitterness left by the oppressive regime of the Thirty and by the part some of his associates, such as Critias and Alcibiades, had played in Athens' troubles.

## Main Ideas

The central Socratic themes are reported consistently, though not identically, across the sources. He held that the most important task for a human being is the care of the soul, and that virtue is a kind of knowledge. If virtue is knowledge, then no one does wrong willingly; wrongdoing results from ignorance of what is truly good. For this reason he is associated with the thesis that the virtues are in some sense unified, and with the claim that doing injustice is worse for the doer than suffering it.

He also made a distinctive claim about his own wisdom: that it consists in recognizing the limits of what he knows. According to Plato's *Apology*, a report from the oracle at Delphi led him to test people with reputations for wisdom, and he found that they lacked the knowledge they claimed. His philosophical mission was thus bound to a public practice of cross-examination.

## Sources of Our Knowledge

Socrates left no writings, so the evidence is indirect: Plato's dialogues (the early ones are often thought to be closest to the historical man), Xenophon's *Memorabilia*, *Apology* and *Symposium*, the caricature in Aristophanes' *Clouds*, and scattered remarks in Aristotle. These accounts differ in their aims and sometimes in their content, and Plato's later dialogues use "Socrates" as a spokesman for Plato's own developing theories. The problem of separating the historical figure from the literary one is usually called the Socratic problem, and no consensus solution exists.

## Method

His characteristic procedure, the *elenchus*, begins when an interlocutor offers a definition or claim, for example about what courage is. Socrates asks further questions, gains agreement to other beliefs, and then shows that these beliefs conflict with the original claim. Many early dialogues end in perplexity (*aporia*) rather than a positive answer. The method aims at the definition of a virtue, and it assumes that one must know what something is before one can know whether it is teachable or beneficial. In Plato's *Theaetetus* Socrates compares his work to midwifery, helping others bring their own ideas to birth; scholars disagree about how far this image reflects the historical man.

## Influence and Reception

Socrates' influence on later philosophy was immediate. Plato was his student, and other followers, including Antisthenes, Aristippus and Euclides of Megara, founded or inspired schools of their own: the Cynics, the Cyrenaics and the Megarians. The Stoics regarded him as a model of the sage. In the Arabic and Islamic tradition he was known as Suqrat, a wise ascetic. In modern thought Kierkegaard wrote on Socratic irony, Hegel treated his trial as a tragic collision of two ethical principles, and Nietzsche was both fascinated by him and hostile to him. His death became a lasting symbol of the conflict between individual conscience and the state.

## Criticisms and Debates

Aristophanes' *Clouds* shows that some Athenians viewed him as a dangerous eccentric. Aristotle credits him in the *Metaphysics* with inductive arguments and universal definitions, but in the *Nicomachean Ethics* criticizes the identification of virtue with knowledge for neglecting weakness of will (*akrasia*). Later critics, including Nietzsche, accused him of a rationalism that undervalues instinct and tragic experience. Another persistent debate is whether he had any positive doctrine or was only a questioner, and whether his "daimonion," the inner divine sign he said restrained him from certain acts, was a religious experience or a figure of speech. The justice of the verdict itself remains debated, as does the question of why he chose not to escape from prison.
