# Wilhelm Wundt

A German scientist and philosopher, founder of the first formal laboratory of experimental psychology at the University of Leipzig (1879), and a professor of philosophy both before and after founding the laboratory. This is a rare middle position between establishing a rigorous experimental psychology and systematic philosophical work.

## Life and career

Wundt was born on 16 August 1832 in Neckarau near Mannheim. He studied medicine at Tübingen and Heidelberg and earned his medical degree at Heidelberg in 1855. He worked as assistant to Hermann von Helmholtz in Heidelberg (1858-1864), and wrote *Contributions to the Theory of Sensory Perception* (*Beiträge zur Theorie der Sinneswahrnehmung*, 1862), in which he announced a project for an experimental psychology. He was appointed professor of inductive philosophy at Zurich (1874), and then at Leipzig (1875), where he remained until his retirement in 1917. He died on 31 August 1920 in Großbothen near Leipzig.

## What he gave

He laid the foundation stone for psychology as an independent science based on systematic experiment, through *Principles of Physiological Psychology* (*Grundzüge der physiologischen Psychologie*, 1873-1874), the work that drew the boundaries between physiological psychology and the philosophy of mind. In Leipzig in 1879 he opened a laboratory that began with private experiments, then became official in the early 1880s, and in 1881 he founded the journal *Philosophical Studies* (*Philosophische Studien*) to publish its results. The "Leipzig school" produced Titchener (structuralism), Külpe (the Würzburg school), Münsterberg, James McKeen Cattell, Emil Kraepelin, Ernst Meumann, Lightner Witmer, and Charles Spearman. In his later writings he developed a complete philosophical system in *Logik* (1880-83), *Ethik* (1886), and *System der Philosophie* (1889), which makes it impossible to classify him as "only a psychologist" or "only a philosopher."

## His core psychological ideas

Wundt adopted **experimental introspection** (controlled self-observation under laboratory conditions), not free philosophical introspection, and he measured **reaction time** to distinguish psychological processes. In his theory of feelings he identified three dimensions: pleasure and displeasure, excitement and calm, and tension and relaxation. He asserted that conscious experience is not composed of elements mechanically assembled, but of **creative synthesis** (*kreative Synthese*) that produces new properties, and that voluntary attention (apperception) is an organized force. Scholars call this **voluntarism**. He accepted **psychophysical parallelism**: every mental event has a parallel physical event, with no causal influence between the two chains. In his early *Contributions* (1862) he spoke of "unconscious inference" (*unbewusster Schluss*), an idea shared with Helmholtz's work on visual perception, and he later abandoned it. Preceding it in the Arab heritage are the observations of Ibn al-Haytham on judgment and inference in vision (*Kitab al-Manazir*), as presented by Abd al-Hamid Sabra in *The Optics of Ibn al-Haytham* (Warburg Institute, 1989); this is a descriptive precedent with no historical connection to Wundt.

## Folk psychology

Wundt believed that higher processes, such as language, myth, customs, religion, and thought, cannot be studied experimentally in the laboratory, so he established for them a historical-comparative branch called **folk psychology** (*Völkerpsychologie*), in which he wrote ten volumes (1900-1920). From this work George Herbert Mead took the concept of the **gesture** and framed it within his theory of the self; Mead attended Wundt's lectures in Leipzig (1888-1889). In 1907 Wundt criticized the experiments of the Würzburg school on thinking as "interrogation experiments" rather than true experiments, and a dispute arose between those who held that thinking can be subjected to experiment (Külpe) and those who held that it cannot.

## His position in the current

He represents the formal point of separation between psychology and philosophy in the German university, and at the same time a methodological bridge between physiological psychology and philosophy. Edmund Husserl attended Wundt's lectures during his study in Leipzig (1876-1878), but his fundamental formation came under Brentano and Stumpf, and Husserl later criticized the psychologism that Wundt and others represented, so Husserl's relation to Wundt is one of opposition more than discipleship.

## The qualification

The historical debate about "father of psychology" deserves clarification: Wundt founded **experimental psychology**, but he himself distinguished it from "formal philosophy," which he regarded as more comprehensive and methodologically deeper, which places experimental psychology, in his own view, in the position of a **branch** of philosophy rather than its opposite. Arthur Blumenthal, in "A Reappraisal of Wilhelm Wundt" (*American Psychologist*, 1975), showed that the image of Wundt in American textbooks (as racist and introspectionist) is the image of Titchener, not his own. Kurt Danziger, in *Constructing the Subject* (Cambridge University Press, 1990), adds that Wundt's laboratory produced data under particular social conditions (the experimenter and the subject) that were not neutral. After his death his influence receded before behaviorism and Gestalt psychology.

## His major works

*Principles of Physiological Psychology* (*Grundzüge der physiologischen Psychologie*, 1873-1874) · *Logic* (*Logik*, 1880-83) · *Ethics* (*Ethik*, 1886) · *System of Philosophy* (*System der Philosophie*, 1889) · *Outline of Psychology* (*Grundriss der Psychologie*, 1896) · *Folk Psychology* (*Völkerpsychologie*, 1900-1920, ten volumes).
