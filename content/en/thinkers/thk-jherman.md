# Judith L. Herman

An American psychiatrist and professor at Harvard University, one of the most important theorists in the field of trauma and abuse.

## What She Contributed

- **"Trauma and Recovery"** (1992): a landmark book that defines a model of "recovery from trauma" in 3 stages (safety, remembrance and mourning, reconnection).
- **"Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder"** (Complex PTSD): the concept of a new disorder describing the effects of prolonged, repeated trauma, such as captivity or long-term abuse.
- **"Father-Daughter Incest"** (1981): pioneering studies in the field of sexual trauma.

## Her Place in the Current

Herman set out the concept of "complex post-traumatic stress disorder" in the early 1990s, and this classification was adopted in the **ICD-11** (World Health Organization) but **not included in the DSM-5** as an independent diagnostic entity. Her work complemented that of Bessel van der Kolk in research on post-traumatic stress disorder.

## Her Most Important Works

- *Trauma and Recovery* (1992).
- *Father-Daughter Incest* (1981).
- *Truth and Repair: How Trauma Survivors Envision Justice* (2023).

## Brief Biography and the Context of the Books

Judith Lewis Herman was born in 1942 and worked as a clinical professor of psychiatry at Harvard Medical School, and for years she led the "Victims of Violence" program at Cambridge Hospital in Massachusetts, a clinical and research program for survivors of domestic and sexual violence. Her first book, *Father-Daughter Incest* (1981, with Lisa Hirschman, Harvard University Press), was published when open discussion of incest was nearly taboo in the medical profession, and it moved the cases of women from the margins into the clinical literature. As for *Trauma and Recovery* (1992, Basic Books), it built its historical argument on the view that the study of trauma flourishes and is then forgotten in cycles, each tied to a political movement: hysteria in nineteenth-century France with the republican anticlerical movement, war neurosis after the First World War, and the women's movement of the 1970s, which made rape and domestic violence a public subject. As Herman formulated the foundational principle of recovery (1992):

> "The core experiences of psychological trauma are disempowerment and disconnection from others. Recovery, therefore, is based upon the empowerment of the survivor and the creation of new connections."

## Complex Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder

In 1992 Herman wrote in the *Journal of Traumatic Stress* a paper on the syndrome of survivors of prolonged and repeated trauma, and held that the diagnosis of post-traumatic stress disorder in its original form does not encompass those who endured years of captivity or abuse, because their effects concern identity, relationships and emotional regulation, not only the symptoms of intrusion and avoidance. This was preceded by the study of Herman, Perry and van der Kolk (*American Journal of Psychiatry*, 1989) on childhood trauma in patients with borderline personality, which found that most of the patients had histories of abuse, and which reopened the debate over what is called a personality disorder. In 2005 van der Kolk and colleagues analyzed data from the DSM-IV field trial on the empirical structure of this concept. The concept became an independent diagnosis in the ICD-11 classification.

## Limits and Criticisms

Some researchers criticized the sequence of the three stages (safety, remembrance and mourning, reconnection) as delaying trauma-focused treatment for those who can tolerate it, which De Jongh and colleagues discussed in an analytical article in *Depression and Anxiety* (2016) on the treatment guidelines for complex PTSD, although Herman herself says that the stages are not strictly linear. In the 1990s the book bore a share of the controversy over recovered memory, as skeptics accused it of relying on accounts that are hard to verify.
