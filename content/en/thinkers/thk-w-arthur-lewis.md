# W. Arthur Lewis

**Sir W. Arthur Lewis** was an economist born on the Caribbean island of Saint Lucia. He is regarded as one of the founding fathers of modern development economics, and was the first Black person to win the Nobel Prize in Economics (1979, shared with Theodore Schultz), for his work on the theory of economic development in poor countries.

## From the Caribbean to the London School of Economics

Lewis moved to Britain to study at the London School of Economics in the 1930s, at a time when it was extremely rare for a Black student from a British colony to be admitted to a prestigious British academic institution of that standing. He took his doctorate there in 1940, taught there, and from 1948 held the Stanley Jevons chair of political economy at the University of Manchester. He also served as an economic adviser to governments and international bodies in Africa, Asia and the Caribbean during the wave of independence after the Second World War. This gave him direct practical experience of development problems, not merely abstract theoretical knowledge.

## The dual-sector model: his central contribution

In 1954 Lewis published his best-known article, "Economic Development with Unlimited Supplies of Labour," in which he presented a simple but influential analytical model. Many developing economies have a dual structure: a large traditional agricultural sector with a surplus of labour of low marginal productivity (which may approach zero in some extreme cases), and a small modern industrial sector. This modern sector can grow rapidly by drawing surplus labour out of agriculture without needing to raise real wages much, because the supply of agricultural labour remains elastic until the agricultural surplus is fully exhausted. This point later came to be called the "Lewis turning point" in the development literature. The concept has been used in later debates about whether fast-growing Asian economies, China in particular, are reaching the point where rural labour surpluses are exhausted and industrial wages begin to rise faster.

## Implications for industrialization strategy

Lewis's model was used as a theoretical justification for strategies of rapid industrialization in many developing countries during the 1950s and 1960s. He suggested that the surplus generated by this transfer from agriculture to industry (the difference between the productivity of the industrial worker and the relatively low wage) could finance capital investment internally, without full reliance on foreign capital. This inspired directed industrialization policies in several developing economies.

## Later criticism of the model

Lewis's model later faced criticism from economists who observed that the assumption of near-zero marginal productivity in traditional agriculture has not been empirically confirmed to the extent that the original model assumed in many contexts. They also noted that moving labour from countryside to city may create urban unemployment rather than full absorption into the industrial sector if it is not accompanied by sufficient industrial growth. Models of rural-urban migration such as that of Harris and Todaro (1970) formalised this concern.

## Other contributions to trade and growth

Lewis also made contributions to analysing the relationship between economic growth and international trade, examining how growth in advanced industrial economies is transmitted to countries exporting primary commodities, including the role of relative wages and productivity in agriculture in shaping the terms of trade. This partly overlaps with Raúl Prebisch's arguments on the terms of trade, although the two differed in their analysis and policy conclusions.

## Institutional legacy

After independence, Lewis served as economic adviser to the government of Ghana under Kwame Nkrumah (1957–58), was the first vice-chancellor of the University of the West Indies, and was later the first president of the Caribbean Development Bank. In this way he combined academic theorizing with direct policy practice in newly independent states. This made him a reference figure for a whole generation of economists in the Global South who sought to build national development policies after the end of direct colonialism.

## His place in the founding generation of development economics

Lewis belonged to the same generation of economists who founded the field of development economics, alongside Albert Hirschman and Gunnar Myrdal, although each approached it from a different angle. Lewis focused on the mechanisms of labour transfer between the two sectors, Hirschman was concerned with strategies of unbalanced investment, and Myrdal focused on the cumulative causal loops that perpetuate underdevelopment. This diversity within the founding generation itself reflects that development economics was never a single rigid theoretical framework, but rather a set of approaches that were sometimes complementary and sometimes competing around the same central question.

## Teaching and enduring academic influence

Lewis taught at Princeton University from 1963 until his retirement in 1983. His dual-sector model continued to be tested, extended and criticised in later work on structural change and labour markets in developing economies, well beyond his death in 1991.
