# Philippe Bourgois

Philippe Bourgois (born 1956) is an American anthropologist whose immersive ethnographies of urban poverty, drugs, and violence have made him one of the most influential figures in contemporary urban and medical anthropology. Living for years among crack dealers in East Harlem and homeless heroin users in San Francisco, he showed how the violence of the street is connected to the violence of structures — deindustrialisation, racism, and the war on drugs — and gave the concept of structural violence a concrete, ethnographic form. His work combines the intimacy of participant observation with a Marxist analysis of political economy, and it is now a standard text in anthropology, sociology, and public health.

## Life and Career

Bourgois was born in 1956 and educated at Harvard College, where he took his BA in social studies in 1978, and at Stanford University, where he earned his PhD in anthropology in 1985. His early fieldwork was in Central America, and his first book, *Ethnicity at Work* (1989), studied labour and ethnic division on banana plantations in Costa Rica and Panama. In the mid-1980s he moved with his family into an apartment in East Harlem, New York, and spent five years living alongside Puerto Rican crack dealers, an experience that produced *In Search of Respect* (1995). He has taught at Washington University in St. Louis, San Francisco State University, at the University of California, San Francisco (in its department of anthropology, history, and social medicine), and at the University of Pennsylvania, before moving to the University of California, Los Angeles, where he is a professor and a leading figure in its Center for Social Medicine and Humanities. Influenced by Pierre Bourdieu, Michel Foucault, and the political-economy tradition in anthropology, he is a leading proponent of critical medical anthropology and of what he calls public anthropology: ethnography written to intervene in public debate.

## Key Works

*Ethnicity at Work: Divided Labor on a Central American Banana Plantation* (1989) examines how ethnicity was produced and mobilised in the labour process on banana plantations. *In Search of Respect: Selling Crack in El Barrio* (1995; second edition 2003) is his best-known book: an ethnography of crack dealers in East Harlem that won the C. Wright Mills Award in 1996 and the Margaret Mead Award in 1997. *Righteous Dopefiend* (2009), written with the photographer Jeff Schonberg, applies the same immersive method to homeless heroin injectors in San Francisco, combining text with documentary photography over more than a decade of fieldwork. He co-edited *Violence in War and Peace: An Anthology* (2004) with Nancy Scheper-Hughes, a widely used collection on the anthropology of violence, and *Violence at the Urban Margins* (2015) with Javier Auyero and Scheper-Hughes. His influential article "In Search of Horatio Alger: Culture and Ideology in the Crack Economy" appeared in *Contemporary Drug Problems* in 1989.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Bourgois's central idea is that the street economy of drugs and violence cannot be understood as a failure of individual morality or culture, but only as a response to structural violence: the historical processes — factory closures in New York, racial discrimination in hiring, the collapse of manufacturing employment — that left young Puerto Rican men with almost no route to dignified work in the formal economy. Within that narrow range of options, selling crack was, in his account, a form of alternative entrepreneurship, a pursuit of "respect" and masculine dignity on the only terms available. With Jeff Schonberg he later used the term "intimate apartheid" for the racialised segregation among homeless drug users, and he drew on Bourdieu's notions of symbolic violence and social suffering to show how domination is internalised as self-blame. Methodologically, his contribution is the demonstration that long-term, immersive ethnography — living in the neighbourhood, witnessing arrests, overdoses, and funerals — can be combined with structural analysis without sacrificing either intimacy or critique. In *Righteous Dopefiend* he extended this approach to addiction, showing how homeless heroin users create fragile networks of solidarity and care even as the institutions around them — hospitals, shelters, police — reproduce their suffering.

## Influence and Critique

Bourgois's influence extends across anthropology, sociology, and public health. *In Search of Respect* became a standard text in courses on urban sociology, medical anthropology, and the anthropology of drugs, and the concept of structural violence is now a basic tool in the study of poverty, addiction, and health inequality. His work helped to establish critical medical anthropology as a field and supplied public-health researchers with an ethnographic language for the social determinants of health. His frank discussion of the ethical dilemmas of his fieldwork — witnessing crimes, being present at violence, deciding what to report — has shaped debates on ethnographic ethics. Critics have raised several objections: that his focus on men's street culture marginalises women's experience of the same neighbourhoods; that his sympathetic portrait of dealers risks romanticising violence; and that the immersive method raises unresolved ethical questions about consent, complicity, and the researcher's responsibility when witnessing harm. Others argue that structural explanations, however powerful, can underplay the agency of the people he studies. Bourgois has answered these criticisms in print, insisting that the point of his work is neither to celebrate nor to condemn, but to show how historical forces narrow the choices of real people — and to hold the society that does the narrowing to account.
