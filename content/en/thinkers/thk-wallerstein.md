# Immanuel Wallerstein

Immanuel Wallerstein (1930-2019) was an American sociologist and historical social scientist who founded world-systems analysis, a framework that treats the modern capitalist world-economy, rather than the nation-state, as the basic unit of analysis.

## Life and Context

Wallerstein was born in New York City and studied at Columbia University, where he later earned his doctorate. His early work concerned African decolonization and politics in Ghana and Cote d'Ivoire. He taught at Columbia and McGill University, then at Binghamton University, where he directed the Fernand Braudel Center for the Study of Economies, Historical Systems, and Civilizations. He served as president of the International Sociological Association from 1994 to 1998.

## Main Ideas

Wallerstein argued that a European capitalist world-economy emerged in the long sixteenth century and gradually expanded to cover the globe. It is organized as a division of labor among core, semiperipheral and peripheral zones, with surplus flowing unequally toward the core. States compete within an interstate system, and hegemonic powers rise and decline in cycles. He drew on Fernand Braudel's historical approach, on Marxism, and on Latin American dependency theory, and he used concepts such as Kondratieff cycles, geoculture and antisystemic movements. He also argued that capitalism is a historical system with a beginning and an end, and that it faces a structural crisis. Later he urged the restructuring of the social sciences, criticizing the separation of economics, politics and sociology.

## Applications and Cross-References

World-systems analysis has been used to study long cycles of hegemony (the Dutch, British and United States cases), the incorporation of regions such as Africa and Latin America into the world-economy, and the position of countries as core, semiperipheral or peripheral over time. It is closely related to dependency theory (Andre Gunder Frank, Samir Amin) and to the work of Giovanni Arrighi and Christopher Chase-Dunn. It is often compared with the nation-state-centred comparative history of Theda Skocpol and with Fernand Braudel's *longue duree*.

## Method

Wallerstein worked through long-term, large-scale historical analysis over centuries, rejecting the isolation of societies as units and calling for a unidisciplinary historical social science.

## Influence and Criticism

His approach generated a major research program in comparative and global sociology. Theda Skocpol and Robert Brenner criticized his account in the 1970s, arguing that it underestimates the role of class relations and state structures within countries and relies too heavily on circulation and trade. Others have criticized it as Eurocentric, economically reductionist and difficult to test empirically.
