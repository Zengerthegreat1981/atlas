# David Kupfer

An American psychiatrist (born 14 February 1941) and Thomas Detre Professor of Psychiatry at the University of Pittsburgh. He earned his B.A. and M.D. at Yale, trained at Yale–New Haven Hospital and the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH), and joined the Yale faculty before moving to Pittsburgh in 1973 as associate professor and director of research and research training at the Western Psychiatric Institute and Clinic (WPIC). He chaired the Department of Psychiatry from 1983 to 2009. During those years WPIC became one of the largest university psychiatric research centers in the United States, measured by federal research funding and publication. He was elected to the Institute of Medicine (now the National Academy of Medicine) in 1990 and was founding president of the International Society for Bipolar Disorders.

## Contributions

### Biological Rhythms of Sleep in Mood Disorders

Kupfer is known for pioneering research on the relationship between **sleep disturbance** (particularly rapid eye movement, or REM, sleep) and **major depression**. In 1972, with Foster, he published a study in *The Lancet* on the interval between sleep onset and the first REM period, known as REM latency, as a possible biological marker of depression. He used polysomnographic sleep recording to document this latency and changes in sleep architecture. This line of research influenced the understanding of the neurobiological basis of mood disorders. His work in this area is part of a broader current in chronobiological psychiatry that links depression and bipolar disorder with disturbance of the daily rhythm.

### Maintenance Treatment for Recurrent Depression and Social Rhythm Therapy

Together with Ellen Frank, Kupfer led long-term studies on maintenance treatment for recurrent depression. Their main results were published in 1990 in the *Archives of General Psychiatry*. They showed that continuing the same dose of imipramine that had brought the patient out of an episode reduced the likelihood of recurrence over three years, while monthly interpersonal psychotherapy alone also lengthened the period of stability, to a lesser degree. The same work was extended to **social rhythm therapy and interpersonal therapy** for patients with bipolar disorder, which aims to stabilize the timing of sleep, eating, and activity as anchors for the biological rhythm.

### Chairing the DSM-5 Task Force

Kupfer was appointed chair of the task force for the **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5)** published by the American Psychiatric Association. It appeared in 2013, after more than a decade of revision. Darrel Regier served as vice chair. The process began with a series of conferences that produced *A Research Agenda for DSM-V* in 2002, which called for introducing neurobiological dimensions and data into diagnosis. Kupfer oversaw a broad restructuring. It included abandoning the multiaxial system used since DSM-III, unifying the autism spectrum disorders, adding binge eating disorder and disruptive mood dysregulation disorder, and revising the criteria for diagnosing mood disorders, psychosis, and other conditions.

### The Controversy Over DSM-5

The process provoked wide scientific and public debate about the risk of "diagnostic inflation." Kupfer faced criticism from prominent colleagues, most notably Allen Frances, chair of the previous DSM-IV task force, who warned against widening the boundaries between normal and pathological. He also faced criticism from Thomas Insel, director of the NIMH, who announced in 2013 that the institute would direct its research toward research domain criteria rather than the manual's categories, because of their weak validity. Kupfer defended the rigor of the scientific methodology and the field trials used to shape the new manual. However, the field trial results showed only modest agreement between raters on some common diagnoses, with kappa values of about 0.28 for major depressive disorder and about 0.20 for generalized anxiety disorder.

### Academic Recognition

He is a member of the National Academy of Medicine (formerly the Institute of Medicine), in recognition of his research on mood and sleep disorders. He has published hundreds of peer-reviewed papers on drug maintenance treatment for recurrent depression and bipolar disorder.

## Legacy

Kupfer represents a link between biological research in sleep and mood psychiatry and institutional work on developing the international diagnostic systems. His name is directly associated with the history of DSM-5 and with the scientific debate over the limits of contemporary psychiatric classification. Some of the manual's innovations, such as dimensional severity measures and the removal of the multiaxial system, remain in uneven use in clinical practice.

## Limits and Critique

Critics argue that the DSM-5 project promised a scientific shift based on biological markers and then did not deliver it, so the manual ended up closer to a continuation of its predecessor with adjustments to the boundaries. Others argue that the markers Kupfer studied, including REM latency, are sensitive but not specific: they appear in other disorders and cannot serve as a diagnosis on their own. The question of separating his role as a researcher from his role as chair of an institutional task force also remains debated. Task forces were subject to questions about conflicts of interest with pharmaceutical companies, and the American Psychiatric Association adopted disclosure requirements for task-force members.
