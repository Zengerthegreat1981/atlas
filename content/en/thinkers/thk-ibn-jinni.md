# Abū al-Fatḥ ʿUthmān ibn Jinnī

An Arab grammarian and linguist of Byzantine (Roman) origin, born in Mosul, who spent most of his scholarly life in Baghdad. He is the best-known student of Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī, whose lessons he attended for many years, and a major theorist of analogy (*qiyās*) and attested usage (*samāʿ*) in Arabic grammar. In *al-Khaṣāʾiṣ* he also set out a sustained discussion of the origin of language within the Arabic tradition.

## Biography

Ibn Jinnī was born in Mosul around 322 AH (the exact year is not firmly documented) and grew up there before moving to Baghdad, where he attended the lessons of Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī, the leading grammarian of his age, for many years until his teacher's death in 377 AH, and inherited his scholarly standing. He also knew the poet Abū al-Ṭayyib al-Mutanabbī, whose *Dīwān* he explained in a commentary known as *al-Fasr*. Ibn Jinnī lived in the cultural environment of Baghdad in the 4th century AH, among the men of letters and grammarians of Basra and Kufa whose two schools met in the Abbasid capital, and he died in Baghdad in 392 AH.

Ibn Jinnī is described as being of Byzantine (Roman) origin: his father "Jinnī" had been an enslaved man belonging to one of the notable figures of Mosul. His foreign ancestry was well known in his time, and it did not prevent him from reaching a high rank among the grammarians of Arabic, as was the case with several leading scholars of the Arabic language in the Abbasid period who were not of Arab descent. He taught in Baghdad, and a generation of later grammarians studied under him, although none of his immediate students matched the standing or fame of his teacher Abū ʿAlī al-Fārisī.

## What he contributed

### *al-Khaṣāʾiṣ*: a theory of the foundations of language and grammar

The book *al-Khaṣāʾiṣ* is not an applied grammar but a theory of the **foundations** of grammar itself: why do grammarians reason as they do? What is the argument for giving priority to analogy over hearing, or the reverse? In it Ibn Jinnī discusses the question of the **origin of language**: is it "tawqīf" (divine revelation) or "iṣṭilāḥ" (human convention)? He inclined toward the possibility of convention without giving a decisive resolution. He also proposed the idea of the **greater derivation** (*al-ishtiqāq al-akbar*): that the three root letters of a word share a single semantic "resemblance" across their six different permutations (for example q-w-l / q-l-w / w-q-l, and so on). This idea presupposes a systematic relation between sound and meaning that goes beyond the single fixed root order.

### *Sirr Ṣināʿat al-Iʿrāb*

In *Sirr Ṣināʿat al-Iʿrāb* Ibn Jinnī worked at the phonetic and morphological level: the points of articulation and the qualities of the letters, and the relation between the phonetic structure of a word and its inflectional function. He continued and developed the investigations of Khalīl ibn Aḥmad and Sībawayh on this subject, in a more theoretically abstract manner.

### The relation between analogy, hearing and cause

Ibn Jinnī discussed at length the threefold distinction on which the method of Arabic grammar rests: **hearing** (*samāʿ*, what was reported from eloquent Arabs), **analogy** (*qiyās*, generalizing a rule to what was not heard, by a cause), and the **grammatical cause** (*ʿilla*, explaining the logical or functional reason for the rule), a topic later developed in the Arabic theory of grammatical causes.

### The relation between sound and meaning

One of Ibn Jinnī's most debated ideas is his view that there is a non-arbitrary relation between the sounds of some words and their meanings. That is, the Arabs' choice of particular letters in forming the root of a word is not purely random convention, but sometimes carries a sound-meaning "appropriateness" (*munāsaba*): for example, the heaviness of certain letters in words denoting heavy or rough meanings, and the lightness of other letters in words denoting light meanings. He presented this idea in a chapter of *al-Khaṣāʾiṣ* titled "Chapter on the Touching of Words to the Likeness of Meanings" (*bāb imsās al-alfāẓ ashbāh al-maʿānī*). This thesis came close to what modern linguistics calls "sound symbolism," although in Ibn Jinnī it remains scattered observations rather than a fully developed theory.

## Legacy

Ibn Jinnī is one of the medieval Arabic authors who moved from describing grammatical facts to asking about their foundations, in something close to a philosophy of language. This is why modern historians of linguistics (such as Kees Versteegh, Georges Bohas and Djamel Eddine Kouloughli, and Yasir Suleiman) read *al-Khaṣāʾiṣ* as a foundational text in the theory of medieval Arabic language, and as a recurring point of comparison with modern linguistic questions (the relation between sound and meaning, and whether language is natural or conventional). His commentary on the *Dīwān* of al-Mutanabbī remained an essential reference in literary study, and his phonetic investigations in *Sirr Ṣināʿat al-Iʿrāb* remained a standard reference for scholars of Arabic morphology and inflection.

Finally, Ibn Jinnī's reliance, like almost all grammarians of his time, on the verses of pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry as "eloquent" evidence places his linguistic material within a literary register of selected eloquent usage. It does not represent the actual variety of language use in Arab societies at that time. This limitation is shared by all traditional Arabic grammar and is not unique to Ibn Jinnī, but it remains relevant when assessing how far his claims about the "nature" of the Arabic language can be generalized.

## Qualifications

It is worth distinguishing Ibn Jinnī the literary commentator, who explained poets such as al-Mutanabbī, from Ibn Jinnī the theorist, who sometimes tried to apply general philosophical conceptions of language to partial grammatical facts without always maintaining a strict distinction between the two levels. This overlap between a refined literary sensibility and abstract linguistic theorizing is what gives *al-Khaṣāʾiṣ* its richness, but it also makes it sometimes difficult to derive one coherent theoretical system from it apart from its scattered partial contexts across its chapters.

The thesis of the "greater derivation" remained more intuitive than systematically shown. Ibn Jinnī does not present it as a rule without exceptions, and later grammarians treated it as a selective induction open to counterexamples. Likewise, the term "origin of language" for him remained closer to an inherited theological question (divine revelation or convention) than to an empirical investigation, given the absence of any means of historical verification available to him. He based most of his linguistic material on the texts of pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry as "eloquent" without systematically addressing the actual dialectal variation among the Arab tribes.

### Al-Munṣif and the morphological heritage

In *al-Munṣif*, Ibn Jinnī explained the *Kitāb al-Taṣrīf* of Abū ʿUthmān al-Māzinī, one of the oldest independent works on Arabic morphology, and added detailed analyses of *iʿlāl* (vowel-weakening alterations), *ibdāl* (substitution) and metathesis. In this he continued part of his project of linking the phonetic structure of the word (treated in *Sirr Ṣināʿat al-Iʿrāb*) with its systematic morphological transformations. This triad of major works (*al-Khaṣāʾiṣ* on the theoretical foundations, *Sirr Ṣināʿat al-Iʿrāb* on sounds, and *al-Munṣif* on morphology) gives a picture of a relatively integrated linguistic project covering the different levels of linguistic analysis as they were understood in his age.

## His principal works

- **al-Khaṣāʾiṣ**: the theory of the foundations of grammar, the origin of language and the greater derivation.
- **Sirr Ṣināʿat al-Iʿrāb**: the science of sounds and its relation to inflection and morphology.
- **al-Fasr (Commentary on the Dīwān of al-Mutanabbī)**: a literary commentary on poetry.
- **al-Munṣif**: a commentary on *al-Taṣrīf* by Abū ʿUthmān al-Māzinī.
