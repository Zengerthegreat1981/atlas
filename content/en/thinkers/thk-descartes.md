# René Descartes

The French philosopher, founder of modern **Continental Rationalism**, who formulated the metaphysical dualism between **mind** (Res Cogitans) and **matter** (Res Extensa), and the principle "I think, therefore I am" (Cogito, ergo sum), which became the **founding point** of all modern philosophy.

## What He Contributed

In the **"Discourse on Method"** (Discours de la Méthode, 1637), Descartes set out four rules of method and, in Part IV, a first account of doubt: **rejecting everything that can be doubted**, and then rebuilding knowledge on a **certain, unshakeable foundation**. The foundation he found is **the thinking subject itself**: one who doubts **cannot doubt that he is thinking**, so the Cogito is a certain truth. The doubt is developed more fully in the *Meditations*.

In the **"Meditations on First Philosophy"** (Meditationes de Prima Philosophia, 1641), he set out six meditations that explore: doubt, the nature of the mind, the existence of God, truth and error, the essence of material things, and the distinction between mind and body. He then proved **God** (the efficient cause, the guarantor of truth), and finally the **material world** (geometrical matter).

## Cartesian Dualism

- **Res Cogitans** (mind/thinking): the immaterial substance, without extension.
- **Res Extensa** (matter): the material substance, without thought, purely mechanical.
- **Will** (Voluntas): a faculty of the mind, free and capable of error.

The relation between the two substances is a double problem that cannot be solved within Descartes' own framework: **where do they meet?** The pineal gland (Glandula Pinealis) in the brain, which Descartes tried to make the **seat of the soul** (Sedes Animae) — a proposal that did not win wide acceptance even among his contemporaries.

## Direct Influence

- **Occasionalism**: Malebranche solved the problem of interaction by making God the cause on every occasion; while **Leibniz** proposed "pre-established harmony" as an alternative to it.
- **Spinozism**: rejected dualism in favour of the unity of substance.
- **All the philosophy of modernity**, from Kant to Heidegger to Richard Rorty, deals with Cartesianism either by acceptance or by criticism.

## His Most Important Works

- **Discours de la Méthode** (1637): Discourse on Method.
- **Meditationes de Prima Philosophia** (1641): Meditations on First Philosophy.
- **Principia Philosophiae** (1644): Principles of Philosophy.
- **Passiones Animae** (1649): The Passions of the Soul.
- **Le Monde** (published posthumously in 1664): The World (which he did not publish in his lifetime).

## Biography

René Descartes was born in La Haye en Touraine in the province of Touraine on 31 March 1596 to a family of nobles of the robe, and was educated at the Jesuit college of La Flèche from about 1607 to 1614, and then studied law at Poitiers. He joined military service in the Netherlands in 1618, where he met Isaac Beeckman, who directed him toward mathematical physics, and his biography relates that in November 1619 he had decisive dreams that directed his project toward a unified method of knowledge. He settled in the Netherlands from 1628 until 1649, moving among its cities in search of solitude. He wrote the "Rules for the Direction of the Mind" around 1628, which was not published in his lifetime, and prepared "The World" and then refrained from publishing it in 1633 when he learned of the condemnation of Galileo. He corresponded with Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia from 1643 on the relation between soul and body, and from this arose his book "The Passions of the Soul" (1649). He accepted Queen Christina's invitation to Stockholm, and died there of pneumonia on 11 February 1650.

## Method and the Cogito

The "Discourse on Method" appeared in 1637 as an introduction to three scientific essays on optics, meteorology, and geometry, and in it he set out four rules: not to accept anything as true unless he knew it with self-evidence, to divide each problem into parts, to arrange his thoughts from the simplest to the most complex, and to review comprehensively. In the "Meditations" (1641) he moved from methodical doubt to the Cogito and then to the proof of God's existence, and made the guarantee of a truthful God the foundation of reason's confidence in its clear and distinct perceptions. He appended to the Meditations "Objections" collected by Mersenne from Hobbes, Arnauld, Gassendi, and others, together with his replies to them, so that the book became a broad philosophical dialogue. Among the most famous objections is what has been called the "Cartesian circle": that he proves God by clear perception and then guarantees clear perception by God.

## Physics and Metaphysics

In the "Principles of Philosophy" (1644) Descartes formulated a mechanical physics that reduces nature to extension and motion, with no final causes or substantial forms as in scholastic philosophy. This had a great effect in founding modern science, though the physics of vortices he advanced was replaced by Newton's physics. He considered the animal a machine, while the human being combines the bodily machine with the thinking soul. This distinction is the source of the question that occupied Elisabeth of Bohemia: how can what has no extension move what is extended?

## Influence and Reception

Descartes influenced Malebranche, Spinoza, and Leibniz, the empiricists who discussed his argument, and Kant, who reformulated the question of the subject. His reading was renewed in the twentieth century in Husserl's phenomenology, which saw in the Cartesian Meditations the beginning of its project, and in Heidegger's critique of the modern subject. In the Arab sphere his basic works were translated and studied in the context of comparing modern rationalism with Islamic philosophy, particularly the comparison of the Cogito with Ibn Sina's "Flying Man" experience, a comparison often made in Arabic studies.

## Limits and Criticism

Gilbert Ryle, in "The Concept of Mind" (1949), criticized Cartesian dualism as a "ghost in the machine" and a category mistake. Antonio Damasio wrote "Descartes' Error" (1994) to show that reason is not separable from emotion and the body in the brain. Heidegger, and then feminist and environmental philosophy, criticized the separation of the thinking subject from the world and the body. Critics from within the method held that the Cogito argument does not establish a thinking substance but merely the existence of some thought. His empiricist opponents faulted him for relying on innate ideas. And it remained that the question of interaction between soul and body found in his texts no satisfying solution, as he himself admitted in his correspondence with Elisabeth.
