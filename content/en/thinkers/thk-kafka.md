# Franz Kafka

Franz Kafka (1883–1924) was a German-speaking Jewish novelist from Prague who worked as an insurance official and published little in his lifetime. His works are read as a direct literary embodiment of the experience of alienation and of a bureaucratic absurdity that can be neither understood nor justified.

## Life and Context

Kafka was born in Prague on 3 July 1883 into a German-speaking Jewish family. His father Hermann, a domineering merchant, left a decisive mark on his son's inner life. Kafka studied law and took his doctorate at the German university in Prague in 1906. From 1908 he worked at the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute for the Kingdom of Bohemia, where daily case files gave him a close view of modern administration. He wrote mostly at night; he composed "The Judgment" in a single night in September 1912. He twice became engaged to Felice Bauer and broke it off, corresponded at length with Milena Jesenská, and spent his last months in Berlin with Dora Diamant. Tuberculosis forced his retirement in 1922, and he died in a sanatorium at Kierling near Vienna on 3 June 1924, aged forty.

## Why He Is Mentioned Here

Camus devoted an appendix of *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942) to Kafka, treating his fictional world as one of the purest embodiments of the absurd: a world without final explanation, in which the self confronts an authority whose logic it cannot grasp. Yet Camus also objected that Kafka's endings leave a door open to hope, a "leap" that departs from the absurd as Camus understood it.

Kafka's value in this context is that he describes from the inside a structure philosophy usually describes only from outside: being accused without knowing the charge, being asked to prove innocence of an accusation never communicated, and dealing with an apparatus in which every official is courteous and excusable yet no one is responsible. This is how the adjective "Kafkaesque" entered the language of sociology, law and institutional psychiatry.

## Style and Themes

His prose is plain, precise and administrative. Strange events are told in the tone of a report, so Gregor Samsa's transformation into an insect in *The Metamorphosis* (1915) is recorded as a fact around which the family must organise itself. Many of his shorter texts are built on enigmatic parables that resist final interpretation, such as "Before the Law", which was published separately and later incorporated into *The Trial*. During his life he published collections including *Contemplation* (1912), *The Judgment* (1913), *A Country Doctor* (1919) and *A Hunger Artist* (1924), while the three great novels, *The Trial*, *The Castle* and *Amerika*, remained unfinished manuscripts.

## Reception

Psychoanalytic readings have been abundant, especially of the unsent *Letter to His Father* (1919), which describes a relationship with a crushing father in terms that today evoke shame and diminishment. But reducing his fiction to his biography is something many of his interpreters have warned against. Walter Benjamin wrote a well-known essay on him (1934), Deleuze and Guattari read him as a writer of a "minor literature" (1975), and Elias Canetti wrote about his letters to Felice in *Kafka's Other Trial* (1969). Religious, political and psychological readings compete, and none exhausts the text.

## Limits

His inclusion is as an influential literary voice in the existential vocabulary, not as a member of an organised philosophical school. Kafka himself did not intend what is attributed to him: he asked that his unpublished works be burned, and his friend Max Brod disobeyed and published them, so the corpus on which this interpretation rests exists against its author's wishes. Accounts also say he laughed while reading aloud passages of *The Trial* to friends, which sits uneasily with the sombre readings that dominate.

## Related Concepts

Alienation, the absurd, bureaucracy, guilt and shame, authority.

## Major Works

The Metamorphosis (Die Verwandlung, 1915) · The Trial (Der Process, written 1914–1915, published posthumously 1925) · The Castle (Das Schloss, written 1922, published posthumously 1926)
