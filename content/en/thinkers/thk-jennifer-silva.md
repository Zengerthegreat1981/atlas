# Jennifer M. Silva

A contemporary American sociologist who has held faculty positions including at Indiana University, known for her in-depth, methodologically careful ethnographic study of how American working-class young people deal with the complexities of the transition to adulthood in an age marked by the vanishing of stable jobs, rising education costs and the breakdown of family support networks.

## The Central Idea

In her pivotal book *Coming Up Short: Working-Class Adulthood in an Age of Uncertainty* (Oxford University Press, 2013), based on in-depth interviews with about one hundred working-class young men and women, both Black and white, in Richmond, Virginia, and Lowell, Massachusetts, Silva analyzed how these young people negotiate the meaning of "adulthood" in the absence of the traditional stable paths toward it (a steady job, marriage, an owned home). She found that many of them regard education as the key to adulthood but feel deep disappointment when it fails to translate into actually stable career paths, which leaves debt and frustration, coupled with deep distrust of government institutions and a contradictory sense of full personal responsibility for their economic and social struggles. She described a "therapeutic narrative of the self" and a "mood economy." In the absence of the traditional objective markers of adulthood (a job, marriage), these young people define adulthood in subjective psychological-therapeutic terms ("overcoming childhood trauma," "managing anger") rather than in terms of traditional objective social achievements, as the only way open to them of attaining a sense of personal accomplishment given the lack of objective economic opportunities.

## The Ethnographic Method

Silva used the method of semi-structured in-depth interviews with about one hundred young men and women who were in their twenties and early thirties, in two American industrial cities that suffered sharp economic decline after the contraction of their traditional manufacturing industries (Richmond in Virginia and Lowell in Massachusetts). She focused specifically on those without four-year college degrees, a large share of American young adults who are underrepresented in the academic literature that traditionally tends to focus on the experience of elite university graduates. This method allowed her to reach intimate personal details about debts, intermittent romantic relationships, and struggles with addiction or psychological disorders. She thus offered a picture rich in ethnographic detail of an inner world seldom documented by quantitative statistical studies alone, documenting a lived experience that is often reduced, in political and media literature, to abstract statistical figures on unemployment and poverty.

## The Therapeutic Narrative and Individualization

Silva argued that the therapeutic narrative of the self is not merely a random individual coping strategy but a direct structural product of the decline of traditional collective support institutions (labor unions, churches, locally strong political parties). These had formerly provided collective frameworks for understanding economic difficulties as a shared problem calling for collective action. The individual is left alone facing a dominant cultural discourse that holds him alone responsible for his economic failures, and pushes him, instead of collective political demand, toward inner "self-therapy" as the only solution psychologically available to him. She saw in this a pattern of coerced "individualization" of structural economic risks, in which structural problems (the decline of real wages, the breakdown of the stable labor market, the rising costs and debts of higher education) are reduced to purely personal psychological matters, the burden of solving which each individual bears alone, with no alternative collective political horizon that many of these young people even imagine as a possible option.

## Impact and Critique

The book updated the analysis of the transition to adulthood by focusing on the working class, which had been relatively marginalized in earlier literature, and it complements Frank Furstenberg's broader structural analysis with an ethnographic account of the cultural and psychological dimensions of economic uncertainty. Silva later published *We're Still Here: Pain and Politics in the Heart of America* (Oxford University Press, 2019), based on fieldwork in a post-industrial town in Pennsylvania's coal region. Some critics, however, noted that reliance on a geographically limited sample of only two cities may limit the full generalizability of her conclusions to the diversity of American working-class experiences across regions, ethnicities and economic contexts fundamentally different from the declining industrial Northeast that she studied specifically.
