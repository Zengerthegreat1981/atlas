# Gilles Deleuze

A prominent French philosopher, one of the giants of continental philosophy and post-structuralism in the twentieth century; he developed an ontological philosophy based on the concepts of difference, becoming and multiplicity, and with the psychoanalyst Félix Guattari co-founded schizoanalysis.

## What He Contributed

- **An ontology of difference and becoming**: in *Difference and Repetition* (1968) he presented a radical philosophy that places difference and immanence before identity and sameness, going beyond Hegelian dialectic and the Platonic model of original and copy.
- **Schizoanalysis and the critique of Oedipalism (with Guattari)**: in *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) and *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980) they presented a scathing critique of classical psychoanalysis for reducing human desire to the family triangle (father-mother-child), and showed that "desire" is a productive, social and historical energy that is not reducible to a lack or an Oedipus complex.
- **The concept of the rhizome**: he formulated the rhizome model of thinking against the hierarchical arborescent model, founding a horizontal, decentered network of knowledge open to connection and multiplicity.
- **Becoming and desiring-machines**: he put forward the concepts of "desiring-machines" and "becoming" (Devenir) to explain the flows of psychic and bodily energies outside rigid identitarian frameworks.
- **The body without organs (Corps sans organes)**: a term Deleuze originally borrowed from the poet and dramatist Antonin Artaud, first used in his solo writing *The Logic of Sense* (1969), and then developed with Guattari as a critical tool against the organic/functional organization of the body and desire in *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) and *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980).

## His Place in the Current

Deleuze holds a vanguard position in the philosophy of immanence and post-structuralism; his works with Guattari formed an essential bridge between radical philosophy and psychological and political critique, inspiring anti-psychiatry movements, theories of critical social psychology and body studies.

## Limitation

Deleuze's language is marked by intensive terminological innovation and complex philosophical concepts; he is classed in the philosophy section, with close overlap with psychoanalysis and institutional critique.

## Biography

Gilles Deleuze was born in Paris on 18 January 1925, studied at the Sorbonne, and passed the agrégation in philosophy in 1948. He taught in several lycées, then at the Sorbonne (1957–1960) and at the University of Lyon (1964–1969), then at the University of Paris VIII at Vincennes from 1969 until his retirement in 1987. He defended his two doctoral theses in 1969, *Difference and Repetition* and *Spinoza and the Problem of Expression*. He met Félix Guattari in 1969 and their collaboration began. He suffered from chronic lung disease in his last years, and died in Paris on 4 November 1995 by suicide.

## The History-of-Philosophy Phase

Deleuze began with books in the history of philosophy that he made creative readings rather than neutral exposition, and he defined this method as akin to fathering a "monstrous child" on the author. He wrote on Hume in *Empiricism and Subjectivity* (1953), on Nietzsche in *Nietzsche and Philosophy* (1962), in which he highlighted the concept of active and reactive will and the orientation of philosophy against Hegelian dialectic, on Kant in *Kant's Critical Philosophy* (1963), on Bergson in *Bergsonism* (1966), and on Spinoza in *Spinoza: Practical Philosophy*, and then produced *The Fold: Leibniz and the Baroque* (1988). These books are considered the foundation of his own philosophy of immanence and temporal figures.

## Philosophy, Cinema and Art

Deleuze published two books on cinema, *Cinema 1: The Movement-Image* (1983) and *Cinema 2: The Time-Image* (1985), in which he applied Bergson's philosophy to the work of directors, distinguishing between the cinema of movement, which organizes time through movement, and the cinema of time, which makes time appear in itself after the Second World War. He wrote on Proust in *Proust and Signs* (1964), on Francis Bacon in *Francis Bacon: The Logic of Sensation* (1981), and on Foucault (1986), and his last book was *Essays Critical and Clinical* (1993). With Guattari he wrote *What Is Philosophy?* (1991), which defined philosophy as the creation of concepts, distinguishing it from science, which offers functives, and from art, which offers blocs of sensation.

## Influence and Reception

Deleuze's influence spread in literature, art, cinema, architecture, feminist studies, the theory of social movements, geography and political science, and the concepts of the "rhizome" and the "line of flight" took a wide place in cultural criticism. Michael Hardt read him within the heritage of immanent ontological thinking, and Alain Badiou placed him at the center of the debate over the one and the many.

## Limits and Critique

Sokal and Bricmont in *Impostures intellectuelles* (1997; English *Fashionable Nonsense*, 1998) held against Deleuze and Guattari that they borrowed scientific and mathematical concepts without control and without justifying their significance. Others held against his style an obscurity that cultivates coined terminology, and a language that may hide the argument. Badiou held that his ontology hides a monism of being behind an appearance of multiplicity. Marxist theorists criticized their claim of productive desire because it does not offer an adequate explanation of relations of exploitation, while psychoanalysts accused them of misreading Freud and Lacan. Their concepts, such as the body without organs and deterritorialization, remain resistant to strict methodical application and have often been used in loose senses.
