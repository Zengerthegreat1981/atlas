# Anthony Giddens

Anthony Giddens (born 1938) is a British sociologist best known for structuration theory, his analysis of modernity as a reflexive and globalizing order, and his role as an adviser to the "Third Way" politics of the late 1990s. He is one of the most widely read social theorists of the late twentieth century.

## Life and Context

Giddens was born in London in 1938 and studied at Hull, the London School of Economics and Cambridge, where he taught for many years. He was Director of the London School of Economics from 1997 to 2003 and was made a life peer in 2004. He also co-founded the publisher Polity Press. His early work reinterpreted the classical sociology of Marx, Durkheim and Weber, and his later work moved to general social theory and then to diagnosing contemporary society.

## Main Ideas

- Structuration: social structures are both the medium and the outcome of the practices they organize, which Giddens called the duality of structure. Agents are knowledgeable and reflexive but act within rules and resources.
- Double hermeneutic: social science interprets a world that is already interpreted by its participants, and its concepts feed back into that world.
- Modernity: characterized by time-space distanciation, disembedding mechanisms such as money and expert systems, and institutional reflexivity.
- Self-identity and intimacy: in late modernity individuals build biographical narratives, seek ontological security and negotiate relationships more openly (the "pure relationship").
- Third Way: a centre-left politics that tried to move beyond both traditional social democracy and neoliberalism.

## Method

Giddens works mainly at the level of theoretical synthesis, drawing on hermeneutics, phenomenology, ethnomethodology and the classical tradition. He is less associated with original empirical research than with conceptual frameworks that others apply.

## Case Studies

Stephen Barley's study of the introduction of CT scanners (1986) is a well-known empirical use of structuration ideas. Observing radiology departments in two hospitals, he found that identical technology was followed by different changes in the working relations between radiologists and technologists. Established roles and differences in expertise shaped how people interacted around the new machines, and repeated interactions in turn altered or reaffirmed the departments' structure. Technology was thus an occasion for structuring rather than a cause with a fixed effect. To make such change observable over time, Barley combined Giddens's account of duality with other work on negotiated order. Wanda Orlikowski later examined the use of the same collaborative software in different organisational settings (2000), showing that users enacted different practices: some barely used it, some applied it to improve existing work, and others changed how they worked. These are researchers' adaptations of the theory, not studies by Giddens himself, and each adds methods that the general framework does not supply.

## Contemporary Empirical Examples

Giddens's account of intimacy has been confronted with evidence. Lynn Jamieson (1999) reviewed research on couples and argued that the pure relationship, sustained by mutual disclosure for as long as it satisfies both partners, understates persistent gender inequality, material dependence, children, and practical forms of care that hold relationships together. Neil Gross and Solon Simmons (2002), using a national survey of Americans in midlife, tested expected consequences of pure relationships. They found support for the expected benefits but little of the expected harm, and survey data from a single period could not establish the broad historical change that the theory describes. In information systems research, Matthew Jones and Helena Karsten (2008) reviewed the many uses of structuration theory and found that studies often adopted selected concepts, sometimes in ways departing from Giddens's own position. These examples show that particular propositions can be examined, while the framework as a whole remains difficult to test.

## Related Concepts

Related concepts include structure and agency, practice, reflexivity, modernity, globalisation, risk, trust, and self-identity. Useful comparisons are Pierre Bourdieu's habitus, Margaret Archer's morphogenetic approach, and Ulrich Beck's theory of risk society and reflexive modernisation; Giddens, Beck, and Scott Lash discussed the last theme together in *Reflexive Modernization* (1994). Structuration also bears on routine, power, and unintended consequences: ordinary repeated conduct reproduces institutions, resources give agents differing capacities to act, and actions often have results that nobody sought. Disembedding and trust in expert systems connect the theory of modernity with research on money, professions, and technology.

## Influence and Criticism

Structuration theory has been used in organization studies, geography and information systems. Margaret Archer argued that it conflates structure and agency and so cannot analyze their interplay over time. Critics of the Third Way saw it as accommodating market liberalism, and others find his work too abstract and general to test empirically.
