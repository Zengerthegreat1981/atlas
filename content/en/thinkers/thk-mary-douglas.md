# Mary Douglas: Purity, Danger, and Categorical Thought

Dame Mary Douglas (1921–2007) was a British social anthropologist best known for her analysis of classification, purity, and risk. Her central argument in *Purity and Danger* (1966) is that ideas of pollution are not primarily about hygiene but about order: they express how a society draws boundaries between categories and treats what falls between them. Her insistence that modern Western culture operates through the same kinds of classificatory logic as "traditional" societies challenged evolutionary assumptions about "primitive" thought.

## Biographical and Intellectual Context

Mary Tew was born in San Remo, Italy, in 1921, into an English family and was raised a Roman Catholic and educated at the Sacred Heart convent school at Roehampton. She studied philosophy, politics, and economics at Oxford University and then turned to social anthropology, working under **E. E. Evans-Pritchard** and taking her doctorate in 1951. She married the economist James Douglas in 1951.

Her fieldwork among the **Lele** of the Kasai region of the Belgian Congo (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo) was carried out in two periods, in 1949–50 and 1953. From 1951 she taught at University College London, where she remained until 1977. She then worked in the United States, first at the Russell Sage Foundation in New York and from 1981 as professor of humanities at Northwestern University, later holding appointments at Princeton and the University of Virginia, before returning to Britain. She was made a Dame in 1992 and died in London in 2007. Her interests expanded beyond ethnography to consumption, risk, institutions, and the Hebrew Bible.

## Purity, Pollution, and Order

Douglas's foundational insight is that **purity and pollution concern order and disorder, not hygiene**. She defined dirt as "matter out of place": what counts as dirty depends on the system of classification in which it is located. Pollution beliefs mark the boundaries between categories that structure a society's cosmology, and the things that fall between categories or belong to none are treated as dangerous or, in some contexts, as powerful.

Her best-known example is the dietary law of Leviticus. Rather than reading the prohibitions as ancient hygiene, she argued that they reflect a classification of creatures by their proper sphere: land animals that chew the cud and part the hoof are acceptable, and animals that fail to conform to the type, such as the pig, are abominable. In her early reading, these rules express a cosmology in which each creature should fit its category. Among the Lele she analyzed reverence for the pangolin, a scaled, tree-climbing animal that does not fit ordinary categories, as an anomaly handled in ritual by initiates, which she took to show that anomaly can inspire both avoidance and veneration.

Douglas also argued that bodily boundaries (skin, orifices, bodily substances) serve as a symbol of the social body, so that anxieties about bodily purity map anxieties about the integrity of the group. She stressed that every society has pollution concerns, so that modern industrial societies are not "beyond" such ideas.

## Later Theoretical Work

In *Natural Symbols* (1970) Douglas proposed a "grid and group" scheme for comparing cosmologies, relating the strength of social classification (grid) and of group boundaries (group) to beliefs and the symbolic use of the body. With the political scientist Aaron Wildavsky she applied this approach to risk in *Risk and Culture* (1982), arguing that perceptions of danger are shaped by the form of social organization to which people belong, a position that became the basis of "Cultural Theory" in risk research. With Baron Isherwood she wrote *The World of Goods* (1979), treating consumption as a system of communication. In *How Institutions Think* (1986) she argued that institutions shape classification and memory. In her late work on the Hebrew Bible, including *In the Wilderness* (1993) and *Leviticus as Literature* (1999), she revised her earlier reading of the dietary laws, interpreting them analogically and through the literary structure of the text.

## Ethnographic Focus: The Lele

The Lele were a Bantu-speaking people of the Kasai region who combined farming, hunting, and raffia-cloth production. Douglas described a matrilineal society with distinctive institutions of marriage and age that gave older men considerable authority, and she examined hunting, animal symbolism, and ritual. Her Lele ethnography, including the pangolin cult, supplied one of the key cases for her later comparative argument about anomalous categories.

## Influence and Critique

Douglas transformed the anthropology of ritual, taboo, and religion, and her work influenced sociology, religious studies, and biblical scholarship, food studies, consumption studies, and the sociology of risk.

Critics have argued that her framework risks circularity, since any purity concern can be read as an expression of social order; that it relies on binary oppositions and a strong assumption of societies as orderly wholes; that universal claims about classification may neglect history and power, including the colonial context in which her fieldwork took place; and that her reading of Leviticus was too neat, a point she accepted in her later work. Feminist scholars have also noted that her analysis of bodily purity gives limited weight to the ways in which pollution beliefs can constrain women.
