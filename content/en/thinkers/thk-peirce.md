# Charles Sanders Peirce

An American philosopher, logician and mathematician, originator of pragmatism, which William James later popularized, and author of the Pragmatic Maxim, the theory of signs (semiotics) and the doctrine of continuity (synechism).

## Life and context

Peirce was born in Cambridge, Massachusetts, in 1839, and his father Benjamin Peirce was professor of mathematics at Harvard, so he grew up in a scientific environment that opened to him the doors of discussion with the scientists of his time. He graduated from Harvard in chemistry, and worked for about thirty years (1859–1891) in the United States Coast Survey, so he experienced at close quarters the problems of measurement and experimental error, which gave his philosophy the spirit of the laboratory. He lectured between 1879 and 1884 at Johns Hopkins University, then lost his post for personal and academic reasons, and spent the rest of his life in the town of Milford in Pennsylvania in financial hardship, writing thousands of pages that were not published until after his death in 1914. In the early 1870s he took part in the "Metaphysical Club" in Cambridge with James, Wright and Holmes, and there the first ideas of pragmatism took shape.

## What he contributed

In his essay "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), published in Popular Science Monthly, Peirce formulated the pragmatic maxim: in his words, "Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object."

Peirce also laid the foundations of the theory of signs, regarding the sign as a triadic structure: the Representamen, the thing that performs the function of the sign; the Object to which it refers; and the Interpretant, the effect it produces in the mind. He divided signs into three kinds: the Icon, the Index, and the Symbol.

In the doctrine of continuity (synechism), he held that every apparent discontinuity in facts results from neglect, and that reality is continuous, not discrete. Despite his association with pragmatism, he remained a realist on the question of universals, influenced by scholasticism in the form of Duns Scotus, and holds that generals are real and not merely names.

In logic, Peirce developed the logic of relations in his essay "Description of a Notation for the Logic of Relatives" (1870), a work that later influenced Schröder and, through him, the development of modern logic in Peano and Russell. He also developed the system of Existential Graphs, a diagrammatic notation for logical reasoning.

## Central ideas

- **The three kinds of inference**: he distinguishes deduction, which draws necessary conclusions from premises, induction, which tests hypotheses by experiment, and abduction, which proposes the explanatory hypothesis. He holds that scientific inquiry passes through the three in a continuous circle.
- **The three categories**: Firstness, the quality of feeling; Secondness, collision and resistance; and Thirdness, law and mediation. He counts the sign as belonging to the Thirdness of being.
- **Fallibilism**: all our knowledge is open to correction, and truth is what the scientific community settles on at the end of an unending inquiry. He therefore tied truth to the community and method, not to the individual.
- **Chance and evolutionary love**: in his late stage he argued that the laws of nature arise and evolve, that chance (tychism) is part of the universe, and that "love" (agapism) is the law of moral and cosmic evolution.

## Influence

William James and John Dewey took up Peirce's pragmatism and developed it in broader directions, and in 1905 Peirce renamed his own doctrine "pragmaticism" to distinguish it from other interpretations of pragmatism. In the twentieth century Rorty reread him from a postmodern perspective, while Umberto Eco and Thomas Sebeok drew on his theory of signs. His theory of signs has found its way into Arabic studies in semiotics and interpretation, among them Said Benkrad's book on Peirce's semiotics.

## Limits

Peirce's writings are scattered and incomplete, and most were published after his death in an order set by the editors, so readings of his doctrine have varied greatly. His language is criticized for relying on coined and changing terms, and his classifications of signs swelled until they became ten and then sixty-six kinds without settling. His metaphysics, especially chance and evolutionary love, has also remained doubtful for many later pragmatists.

## Major works

On a New List of Categories (1868) · Description of a Notation for the Logic of Relatives (1870) · The Fixation of Belief (1877) · How to Make Our Ideas Clear (1878) · The Logic of Mathematics in Relation to Education (1898) · The Harvard Lectures on Pragmatism (1903) · Collected Papers of Charles Sanders Peirce, published posthumously (1931-1958, eight volumes, edited by Hartshorne and Weiss).
