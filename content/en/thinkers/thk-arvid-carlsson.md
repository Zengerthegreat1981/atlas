# Arvid Carlsson

Swedish pharmacologist (1923–2018), a central figure in modern biological neuroscience for showing that **dopamine** functions as a neurotransmitter in the brain in its own right, rather than merely as a chemical precursor of noradrenaline as had previously been believed. In 2000 he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, shared with Paul Greengard and Eric Kandel, in recognition of their discoveries concerning signal transduction in the nervous system.

## Context: The Spark and Soup War

Carlsson was born in Uppsala in 1923 and grew up in an academic environment; his father was a professor of history at Lund University. In the 1940s and 1950s neuroscientists were divided in what was called the "spark and soup war": one camp held that signal transmission between neurons is electrical, and another held that it is chemical. The advocates of chemical transmission in the brain prevailed, through work that included Carlsson's, which helped show that monoamines act as neurotransmitters in the brain and that drugs alter their function. Proof required that these substances be measured precisely.

## Scientific Career

Carlsson earned his medical doctorate from Lund University in 1951 and worked there until 1959, when he became professor of pharmacology at the University of Gothenburg, where he spent most of his research career. The reserpine and L-DOPA experiments that established dopamine's role were carried out at Lund with colleagues including Margit Lindqvist and Tore Magnusson. They developed a sensitive method for estimating dopamine in brain tissue and found that its concentration peaks in the basal ganglia, which control voluntary movement, rather than being distributed as would be expected if it were only a transient intermediate in noradrenaline synthesis. After the move to Gothenburg he collaborated with Swedish histochemists, including Nils-Åke Hillarp and Bengt Falck, whose fluorescence method made monoamine-containing neurons visible, and helped map pathways of dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin.

## Discovering the Link Between Dopamine and Parkinson's Disease

In animal experiments Carlsson showed that depleting monoamines with the drug reserpine causes symptoms resembling Parkinson's disease (slowness of movement and rigidity), and that giving the animals **L-DOPA**, the direct chemical precursor of dopamine, restores movement. He published these results in 1957, with Lindqvist and Magnusson, in *Nature*. Oleh Hornykiewicz then showed, with Herbert Ehringer, that dopamine is depleted in the brains of people who had died with Parkinson's disease. Clinical use of L-DOPA followed: Walther Birkmayer and Hornykiewicz reported intravenous treatment in Vienna in 1961, and George Cotzias in the United States later established a high-dose oral regimen. L-DOPA remains a cornerstone of treatment.

## Mechanism of Antipsychotic Drugs

In 1963 Carlsson, with Margit Lindqvist, published the first evidence that the older antipsychotic drugs (neuroleptics), such as chlorpromazine and haloperidol, act by **blocking dopamine receptors**. They observed that levels of dopamine metabolites rose when these drugs were given, as if the brain were compensating for the blockade by increasing activity. This finding contributed to the **dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia**, which linked overactivity of dopamine pathways to positive psychotic symptoms. In the mid-1970s Philip Seeman, Solomon Snyder, and others measured drug binding directly and found that clinical potency of many antipsychotics tracked affinity for dopamine D2 receptors. That supported the receptor-blockade account of the drugs. It did not by itself prove that excess dopamine causes schizophrenia. The hypothesis nonetheless guided the development of later antipsychotics.

## From Dopamine to Serotonin and Glutamate

Carlsson's research also extended to understanding antidepressants, through his clarification of the role of monoamine neurotransmitters in regulating mood and movement. In the late 1960s and early 1970s he worked with colleagues at the company Astra on zimelidine, a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor often described as the first SSRI to reach the market (1982). It was withdrawn in 1983 after cases of Guillain-Barré syndrome. Later SSRIs, including fluoxetine, used the same pharmacological principle. In the 1990s Carlsson and Maria Carlsson proposed an imbalance between glutamate and dopamine in schizophrenia, and called for dopamine stabilizers that reduce excessive activity without fully blocking receptors. This idea influenced the rationale for partial-agonist antipsychotics such as aripiprazole.

## Impact and Limits

His methodology for measuring neurotransmitters with high precision was a fundamental methodological contribution. It enabled neuroscience to shift from anatomical description to a quantitatively measurable biochemical science. However, subsequent decades showed that the dopamine hypothesis alone does not explain schizophrenia: negative and cognitive symptoms do not respond well to receptor blockade, and about a third of patients remain resistant to treatment. The simplified account that attributes depression to a serotonin deficiency also finds no strong support in recent reviews, observations that led Carlsson himself to attend to glutamate. His legacy remains an example of how tracing the mechanism of action of a drug leads to an understanding of the disease, although the relationship between the two is not linear.
