# Paul Bohannan

Paul Bohannan (1920–2007) was an American anthropologist best known for his fieldwork among the Tiv of central Nigeria and for his theory of the multicentric economy — the idea that a single society can contain several distinct spheres of exchange, each with its own goods and its own rules, and that money disrupts a society precisely by collapsing those spheres into one. His work became a classic of economic anthropology and a standard text for teaching how colonial money reshaped African systems of value.

## Life and Career

Bohannan was born on 5 March 1920 in the United States. After military service in the Second World War he went to Oxford University, where he studied under E. E. Evans-Pritchard and absorbed the British structural-functionalist approach to kinship, law, and stateless societies. In the late 1940s and early 1950s he and his first wife, the anthropologist Laura Bohannan, carried out joint fieldwork among the Tiv of what is now Nigeria's Middle Belt, then under British colonial rule. The two worked as a team: Paul concentrated on economy and law, Laura on kinship, religion, and the daily life of women.

Bohannan later taught at Northwestern University and at the University of Southern California. He edited several influential collections in economic and legal anthropology, and he remained active as a writer and teacher into old age. He died in 2007, at the age of eighty-seven.

## Key Works

His first major book, *Justice and Judgment among the Tiv* (1957), analysed the Tiv system of dispute settlement — the moots (*jir*) in which elders and disputants debated cases — and its relation to the formal courts introduced under British colonial rule. The book remains a classic of legal anthropology.

His best-known article, "The Impact of Money on an African Subsistence Economy" (1959), introduced the concept of the multicentric economy to a wide audience. The fullest statement of his Tiv research came in *Tiv Economy* (1968), written with Laura Bohannan, which analysed the Tiv system of exchange in detail. He also wrote the textbook *Social Anthropology* (1963) and edited *Law and Warfare: Studies in the Anthropology of Conflict* (1967).

## Main Ideas: Spheres of Exchange

Bohannan's central observation was that the traditional Tiv economy was divided into three separate spheres of exchange, and that goods could not move freely between them. Subsistence goods — yams, household tools, everyday produce — circulated in the lowest sphere. Prestige goods — slaves, cattle, and brass rods, together with ritual office and medicine — circulated in a higher sphere. And a third sphere, the highest of all, concerned rights in women and dependants, exchanged through marriage. Wealth could not simply be converted from one sphere to another: a man could not buy a wife with yams, nor purchase prestige goods with ordinary produce. Movement between spheres required specific social transactions, such as marriage or ritual gifts, that carried their own moral weight.

This model challenged the assumption that all economies work like markets, in which everything has a price and anything can be exchanged for anything else. In a multicentric economy, value is not a single scale but a set of ranked, morally charged domains, and the boundaries between them are part of the social order itself. When British colonial money entered Tiv society, it acted as a universal equivalent that dissolved those boundaries: suddenly everything could be bought with cash, and the moral distinctions that had organised Tiv life began to collapse. Bohannan showed, in concrete ethnographic detail, how the introduction of a single currency could produce social disruption far beyond the economic sphere — a finding that became a model for studying the effects of monetisation across Africa.

His work on Tiv law was equally influential. He argued that Tiv dispute settlement was a coherent system of reasoning about rights, wrongs, and restitution, and he stressed the difficulty of translating its categories into the terms of Western and colonial law.

## Influence and Critique

The concept of the multicentric economy became a staple of economic anthropology, used to analyse societies from West Africa to Melanesia, and Bohannan's account of money's disruptive effects shaped the study of colonial economies. His Tiv work, carried out jointly with Laura Bohannan, also became an early model of collaborative fieldwork by a married couple, each studying the same society from a different angle.

Later scholars, notably Jane Guyer in her work on West African economies, argued that Bohannan overstated the rigidity of the boundaries between spheres, showing that in practice exchange across spheres was more flexible and historically variable than his model suggested. The critique refined rather than overturned his model: the idea of separate, morally ranked spheres of exchange remains a standard analytical tool, even if the boundaries are now understood as negotiated and contested rather than fixed.
