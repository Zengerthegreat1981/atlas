# Ernst Troeltsch

A German theologian, philosopher of religion and historian (1865–1923). He was one of the founding members of the History-of-Religions School (Religionsgeschichtliche Schule) in Göttingen, alongside Hermann Gunkel and Wilhelm Bousset, and the most prominent developer of its general philosophical thesis: that Christianity, like any other religious phenomenon, is subject to the laws of history and cultural relativity, and may not be studied in isolation from them on the pretext of an absolute divine uniqueness that exempts it from the tools of comparative historical research.

## Biography and Context

Troeltsch was born in Haunstetten, near Augsburg, in 1865 and studied theology at Erlangen, Berlin and Göttingen, where he was influenced by Albrecht Ritschl and then by his colleagues of the circle known as the "little Göttingen faculty" (die kleine Göttinger Fakultät). He worked as professor of systematic theology at Heidelberg (1894–1915). He was a friend of Max Weber and at one point lived in the same house as him. He then moved to the University of Berlin as professor of philosophy and cultural history, and died in 1923, shortly before a trip to lecture in England. His writings combine theology, sociology and philosophy of history.

## The Principle of Historical Relativity

In *The Absoluteness of Christianity and the History of Religions* (*Die Absolutheit des Christentums und die Religionsgeschichte*, 1902), Troeltsch argued that the researcher of the history of religions has no epistemic basis for treating any religion, including Christianity to which he belongs as a Protestant theologian, as the absolute truth by which other religions are measured according to how close or far they are from it. Every religion is a historical phenomenon that arose and developed within a specific cultural context, and it cannot be extracted from history to be set up as a standard outside it. Troeltsch took this position at a critical distance from conservative theologian colleagues, who regarded such an approach as a threat to the particularity of Christian revelation. Nevertheless, he was not an absolute relativist. He held that Christianity is the "highest" of the religions for us in Europe, meaning its truth is relative to our culture, not an absolute truth transcending cultures. In *Der Historismus und seine Probleme* (1922), he continued to analyze the problem of "historicism" (*Historismus*) and the possibility of building normative values on a historical basis.

## His Major Work: The Social Teachings of the Churches

In *The Social Teaching of the Christian Churches and Groups* (*Die Soziallehren der christlichen Kirchen und Gruppen*, 1912), Troeltsch formulated his famous threefold classification of patterns of organized Christian religiosity: the church, the sect and mysticism. The church is an all-encompassing institution that accepts the world, adapts to society and gains its members by birth. The sect is a small voluntary community that is strict in its ethics and renounces the world. Mysticism is an individual, inward pattern that pays no attention to institutional structures. Through a historical survey extending from the apostolic age to the nineteenth century, he offered a study of how these patterns developed and interacted with the state, the economy and the family.

## The Dialogue with Max Weber

Troeltsch was linked to Max Weber by a close intellectual friendship, during which they exchanged ideas on the relation of religious ethics to social structures. Weber had distinguished "church" and "sect" as types of religious organisation, and Troeltsch developed and historicised the distinction, adding mysticism as a third type, in parallel with Weber's thesis on Protestant ethics and the spirit of capitalism. For a time Troeltsch lived in Weber's house in Heidelberg. However, Troeltsch remained a theologian and philosopher of religion, closer to normative questions (can a modified Christian absoluteness be defended despite historical relativity?), while Weber, by virtue of his method, held a sociological position that was value-neutral.

## Reading Troeltsch Through Ibn Khaldun

Troeltsch's classification invites a comparison with the Arab-Islamic heritage, especially with Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406 CE). In the *Muqaddima* (1377 CE), Ibn Khaldun linked religious propagation with tribal solidarity (*'asabiyya*). He argued that a religious call cannot succeed without group solidarity, and that religion can strengthen a dynasty's solidarity and so help found a state, a strength that fades as solidarity weakens. This is close to Troeltsch's interest in the transformation of small religious groups, the "sects," into large institutions, the "churches," as they expand and adapt to the world. The logic of church, sect and mysticism has been applied to Islam: the broad Sunni community is closer to the "church," the Khawarij and some reform movements to the "sect," and the Sufi orders to the third type. However, this is an analytic projection by researchers, not by Troeltsch himself, and it carries the risk of projecting the Christian "church/state" duality onto a civilization that never knew an ecclesiastical institution in the Christian sense.

## Impact and Critique

Conservative theologians accused Troeltsch of emptying Christianity of its claim to absolute truth through comprehensive historical relativity, and Karl Barth criticized him because for Troeltsch theology follows history. On the other hand, his work was regarded as a methodological foundation for every later attempt to study the major monotheistic religions with historical-comparative tools, without the dogmatic presumption of the superiority of any one of them. This is the principle on which the whole History-of-Religions School was based. It is objected to him that his threefold classification arose from the European Christian experience and does not easily fit other traditions, and that his confidence in the superiority of European civilization remained evident.
