# Jeremy Bentham

An English philosopher and **legal reformer**, the **founder of modern utilitarianism** (Utilitarianism), who established the **greatest happiness principle** (Greatest Happiness Principle) as a rule for legal, moral and political projects. His **preserved body** (the Auto-Icon) is still on display at **University College London** (UCL).

## What he contributed

### *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (printed 1780, published 1789)

The foundational text. The "greatest happiness principle": the right action **is** the action that **increases happiness for all** (the greatest happiness of the greatest number). Bentham **rejects** two rival alternatives to the principle of utility, which he calls the "principle of asceticism" and the "principle of sympathy and antipathy" — that is, judging an action merely by one's own approval or disapproval without an objective standard — and adopts in their place the **hedonic (felicific) calculus** of pleasure and pain: the **intensity** of the pleasure, its **duration**, its **certainty**, its **propinquity**, its **fecundity**, its **purity**, and its **extent**.

### *A Fragment on Government* (1776)

Bentham's first major published work (anonymous). A critique of **William Blackstone's theory** in the introduction to his *Commentaries on the Laws of England*, on three axes: (1) Blackstone's confusion of the role of "expositor" of existing law with that of "censor" of what it ought to be, and his adoption of **natural law** instead of the principle of utility as the standard; (2) the inadequacy of the social contract theory as a justification of the obligation to obey government; (3) a critique of Blackstone's treatment of sovereignty and of his praise of the English constitution. **Legislation** for Bentham is the "art" of connecting law with general happiness.

### *Panopticon* (written 1786-1787 and published 1791)

A design for a **prison** that allows the observer to see all prisoners **without being seen** — a model of **soft control**. Michel Foucault, in his book *Discipline and Punish*, which appeared after Bentham's death (1975), reread this model as a **metaphor of modern power**.

### *Anarchical Fallacies* (written about 1795; published in French in 1816 and in English in Bowring's edition in 1843)

A critique of the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (Déclaration des droits de l'homme et du citoyen) of 1789. His most famous phrase on natural rights: "simple nonsense, rhetorical nonsense, nonsense upon stilts" — for he holds that rights are not innate or natural, but a product of positive law, and must be demonstrated by utility.

## Reform projects

- *Pannomial Fragments* (published posthumously in Bowring's edition): a framework for a **comprehensive legal code**.
- **Electoral reform**: Bentham was never a member of Parliament, but he defended in writing the extension of the suffrage (annual voting, the secret ballot) in works such as *Plan of Parliamentary Reform* (1817), and influenced, through his disciples (among them James Mill), the later debates on parliamentary reform.
- **Prisons**: the **Panopticon**.
- **Schools** (Chrestomathia, 1816–1817): educational reform.

## Influence

- **John Stuart Mill**: revised utilitarianism in *Utilitarianism* (serialized in 1861, as a book in 1863), after Bentham's death — he revised the principle of pleasure (higher and lower pleasures).
- **Reform of the Poor Law** in England: the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 reflected Benthamite ideas, and his former secretary Edwin Chadwick played a leading part in it.
- **Welfare economics and legal reform**: the idea that institutions should be judged by their consequences for welfare shaped later economic and legal thought.

## Major works

- **Fragment on Government** (1776): *A Fragment on Government*.
- **An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation** (1789): an introduction to morals and legislation.
- **Panopticon** (1791): complete inspection.
- **Traités de législation civile et pénale** (1802, edited and published in French by Étienne Dumont; later in English as *The Theory of Legislation*).
- **The Book of Fallacies** (1824): the book of fallacies.
- **Deontology** (published posthumously in 1834, edited by John Bowring): the science of duty.
