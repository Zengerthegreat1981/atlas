# Jean-Paul Sartre

Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) is the one figure of existential philosophy who embraced the name "existentialist" with enthusiasm, and the most influential — and most contested — of them all in practice. Philosopher, novelist, playwright, and critic, he wrote on phenomenology, ontology, psychoanalysis, politics, and literature, refused the Nobel Prize for Literature in 1964, and became the voice of the committed intellectual in mid-twentieth-century France. His books and plays have become part of world culture.

## Life and Career

Sartre was born in Paris on 21 June 1905. His father, a naval officer, died the following year, and he grew up in the house of his maternal grandfather, Charles Schweitzer, reading the great books in his library from childhood. He entered the École Normale Supérieure in 1924 and passed the agrégation in 1929, ranked first; Simone de Beauvoir came second, beginning an intellectual and personal partnership that lasted until his death. He taught in Le Havre, spent a year in Berlin (1933–1934) studying Husserl, and was mobilised in the Second World War, captured in 1940 and released in 1941. After the Liberation he founded the journal *Les Temps Modernes* (1945), campaigned against colonialism, supported the Algerian revolution, and wrote the preface to Frantz Fanon's *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961). He visited Egypt with Beauvoir in the spring of 1967, shortly before the June War. He died in Paris on 15 April 1980, and tens of thousands followed his funeral procession.

## Existence Precedes Essence

Existence precedes essence: there is no pre-given human nature. A human being exists first, then makes what they are by what they do. Sartre set this out in his lecture *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1945, published 1946), and worked it through in *Being and Nothingness* (1943), distinguishing the "being-in-itself" (*en-soi*) — the dense, complete being of things — from the "being-for-itself" (*pour-soi*) — consciousness, which is negation and distance from itself and carries the responsibility for its choices. Hence his formula: "man is condemned to be free." Freedom cannot be escaped, and nothing — not God, nature, or history — can excuse us from choosing.

## Bad Faith

**Bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*) is his alternative to the unconscious. Nothing is hidden from me; when I say "I cannot" while I can, I am avoiding knowing that I know. His famous examples are the waiter who plays the role of waiter with exaggerated care until he becomes a thing, and the woman who leaves her hand in her companion's without deciding. In this way he criticises Freud's split between conscious and unconscious, seeing bad faith as a lie to oneself carried out within a single consciousness. For the clinic this is a powerful idea — the refusal of a diagnosis as an identity, and the confrontation with the evasions by which a person disowns their own agency — but it is also one that must be handled with care, since not every "I cannot" is an evasion.

## The Gaze of the Other

When the Other looks at me, I become an object in their world. Sartre describes a man peering through a keyhole who suddenly hears footsteps behind him and is transformed, in an instant, from seer to seen. Shame is the awareness of being as the Other sees me, not as I live myself. From this comes the line in his play *No Exit* (1944): "hell is other people." Unlike Heidegger, Sartre also denies death any meaning-giving role: death is not my own possibility but an external absurdity that cancels my possibilities.

## From Existentialism to Commitment

After the war Sartre moved from an ontology of individual freedom to an analysis of historical conditions. In *What Is Literature?* (1948) he argued for committed literature, and in *Search for a Method* (1957) and the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960) he tried to reconcile existentialism with Marxism: individual freedom moves within scarcity and social structures. In this phase he made political action the measure of philosophy, and supported liberation movements, though his positions on the Soviet Union and communism drew sharp criticism. His friendship with Albert Camus broke in 1952 over the question of violence and communism, and Heidegger answered his existentialism in the *Letter on Humanism* (1947).

## Influence and Critique

Sartre's influence in the Arab world was enormous: in the 1950s and 1960s the Beirut journal *Al-Adab*, founded by Suhayl Idris in 1953, made "commitment" its slogan, drawn from Sartre, and Abd al-Rahman Badawi introduced existential philosophy to Arabic readers and translated *Being and Nothingness*. Writers and thinkers from Naguib Mahfouz to Mahmoud Amin al-Alim and Georges Tarabishi were marked by it, while others attacked it as an individualism unsuited to the causes of liberation.

The criticisms run deep. Merleau-Ponty argued that his freedom ignores the weight of the body and history; the structuralists, Lévi-Strauss among them, charged that the centrality of the conscious self ignores structures; and his formula of freedom is close to absolute, so that carrying it into the clinic without the cautions of Beauvoir can produce blame rather than liberation. Ronald Laing, however, was influenced by him in reading psychosis as a response to a relational situation rather than a mere symptom. What survives is a vocabulary — bad faith, the gaze, being condemned to be free — that still shapes how people, in and out of therapy, talk about freedom, shame, and responsibility.
