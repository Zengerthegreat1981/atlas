# Baruch Spinoza

A Dutch philosopher of Portuguese Jewish origin (1632–1677), expelled from the Jewish community of Amsterdam in 1656, who lived as a lens grinder on the outskirts of The Hague. In the *Ethics* (Ethica, ordine geometrico demonstrata) he presented a rigorous philosophical system treating God, nature and the human being as one substance, and in the *Theological-Political Treatise* he laid the foundation for freedom of thought and for a critical, historical reading of the Bible.

## Spinoza in the Arabic Tradition

Spinoza was connected with the Islamic philosophical tradition and with the Hebrew tradition written in Arabic in more than one respect. In his formation he studied the books of Maimonides, who wrote the *Guide of the Perplexed* in Judeo-Arabic, and he then discussed his views in direct criticism in the *Theological-Political Treatise*, especially in the chapter on prophecy and on the question of interpreting texts. His great question, the relation between philosophy and religious law and the limits of interpretation, intersected with what Ibn Rushd raised in the *Decisive Treatise*, as researchers in medieval philosophy have shown. Some scholars have compared his statement "God, that is, Nature" with the concept of the unity of being in Ibn Arabi, though the origin and method differ greatly. In the modern era Hassan Hanafi translated the *Theological-Political Treatise* into Arabic, and his translation had an effect on the Arab debate over interpretation, freedom of thought and the relation between religion and politics.

## Biography

Baruch (Benedictus) Spinoza was born on 24 November 1632 in Amsterdam to a family of Portuguese Jews who had taken refuge in the Netherlands, and he was educated at the community's "Talmud Torah" school. After his father's death he worked in the family business before turning to philosophy; he learned Latin under Franciscus van den Enden and was influenced by the books of Descartes. On 27 July 1656 the community issued against him the decree of "herem", its most severe punishment, for "wrong opinions and acts", and he did not retract. He then moved to Rijnsburg, Voorburg and then The Hague, and earned his living by grinding optical lenses. He corresponded with scholars and thinkers, among them Henry Oldenburg and Leibniz, who visited him in 1676. He refused an offer to teach at the University of Heidelberg in 1673 in order to preserve his independence. He died in The Hague on 21 February 1677, and the *Ethics* and the rest of his works were published after his death in the same year.

## What He Contributed

- **The unity of substance**: he holds that there is only one substance, "God or Nature" (Deus sive Natura), with infinite attributes, the most prominent of which are thought and extension. What appears as plurality in the world consists of modes of this substance. The human soul is not an independent substance but the idea of the body, so there is no duality between mind and matter as in Descartes, but a parallelism between the two orders.
- **The geometrical method**: the *Ethics* was composed on the model of Euclid, with definitions, axioms, propositions, proofs and scholia, in the desire that speech about God, the soul and the affects be of mathematical certainty.
- **Determinism and the critique of teleology**: everything in nature proceeds by the necessity of causes, and what is called "freedom of the will" is an illusion produced by our ignorance of causes. Divine purpose is a human fancy that projects its needs onto nature.
- **The theory of the affects**: he counted joy, sadness and desire as the primary affects, and formed the remaining affects from their combination. He made the "striving to persevere" (conatus) the essence of every existing thing, and explained bondage as the human being's subjection to affects he does not understand, and freedom as knowledge of their causes.
- **Freedom as knowledge of necessity**: the human being reaches the highest degrees of perfection through the third kind of knowledge, which links him to God rationally and yields the "intellectual love of God", a happiness not tied to reward after death.
- **Historical criticism of the Bible**: in the *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670) he held that the Bible is a historical and moral text, to be read as any text is read, according to its language and circumstances, that Moses did not write the whole Torah, and that miracles do not violate the order of nature. From here he is counted among the forerunners of higher criticism. He defended freedom of philosophy as a condition of the health of the state.
- **Political thought**: in the *Political Treatise* he holds that democracy is the form of government closest to nature, that the right of the state rests on its power, and that intellectual freedom may not be suppressed.

## The *Ethics* and the *Treatise*

The two books have different aims. The *Ethics* sets out the metaphysical and psychological foundations of the system and the path to blessedness through understanding, while the *Theological-Political Treatise* is a more public work that separates the aims of philosophy (truth) and of theology (obedience and piety), and argues that the state should secure freedom of thought. The *Treatise* applies the naturalism and the view of religion implicit in the *Ethics*, without presenting its geometrical demonstrations.

## His Place in the Current

Spinoza, with Descartes and Leibniz, is counted as one of the pillars of Continental Rationalism in the 17th century. After his death his name was close to an insult, and then the controversy over "Spinozism" erupted in Germany (the pantheism controversy between Jacobi and Mendelssohn, 1785), and Lessing, Goethe, Herder, Hegel and Schelling turned to him; Hegel said that every philosopher begins with Spinozism. Nietzsche wrote in 1881 that he had found in Spinoza a precursor. His reading is renewed today in Deleuze, Antonio Negri and, in neuroscience, Antonio Damasio.

## Limits and Criticism

- His opponents criticize his strict determinism as abolishing moral responsibility and denying purpose.
- Leibniz held that the unity of substance abolishes the real individuality of creatures.
- He was accused of atheism and materialism, although his system makes God the source of everything, and the accusation is disputed among scholars.
- His geometrical form requires axioms that do not seem self-evident to today's reader, so the method is criticized as hiding assumptions under the garb of proof.
- His argument is not based on knowledge of Islamic texts or their languages, but rests on what reached him of the Hebrew and philosophical heritage.

## His Major Works

- Principles of Cartesian Philosophy (Principia Philosophiae Cartesianae, 1663).
- Theological-Political Treatise (Tractatus Theologico-Politicus, 1670).
- Ethics (Ethica, published 1677).
- Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect (published 1677).
- Political Treatise (Tractatus Politicus, published 1677).
