# Fernando Henrique Cardoso

A Brazilian sociologist, one of the founders of Latin American sociological "dependency theory," which explained Latin America's underdevelopment by its structurally dependent relationship with the advanced capitalist centers, and not by an internal lack of modernization.

## Introduction

Fernando Henrique Cardoso (born 1931) is a Brazilian sociologist and one of the most prominent founders of "Dependency Theory" in Latin American sociology, before he later turned to politics and was elected President of Brazil for two consecutive terms (1995–2003).

## Historical Context

Cardoso developed his ideas while in exile in Chile, where he went after the Brazilian military coup of 1964 forced him to leave his country, and later in Paris. This took place in the wider context of the dominance of American modernization theory (as in Talcott Parsons and Walt Rostow) over the explanation of development. That theory explained the "backwardness" of Third World countries by purely internal factors that could be overcome through gradual stages of modernization imitating the Western path. Cardoso worked in Santiago at the Latin American Institute for Economic and Social Planning (ILPES), linked to the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America (CEPAL), which since the 1950s (thanks to Raúl Prebisch) had already begun to question the hypothesis of equal trade exchange between center and periphery.

## Main Ideas or Method

With Enzo Faletto, in *Dependency and Development in Latin America* (Dependencia y desarrollo en América Latina, written in the late 1960s and published 1969), Cardoso put forward a critique of the prevailing modernization theory, which explained Latin America's "backwardness" by internal factors (lack of capital, an obstructive traditional culture) that could be overcome by gradual internal modernization. They argued instead that Latin American economies are structurally linked by a relationship of "dependency" (Dependencia) to the advanced capitalist centers (Europe and North America). They export cheap raw materials and import expensive manufactured goods, and depend on foreign investment and capital that direct the development surplus abroad instead of toward local accumulation, while their local classes (especially the national bourgeoisie) are formed through a complex relationship of dependency and alliance with foreign capital at the same time. But Cardoso, unlike the more radical and deterministic formulations of dependency (such as that of André Gunder Frank, who saw dependency as an entirely closed road making any substantial development impossible), put forward a more flexible model, often described as "associated-dependent development": the possibility of limited, real industrial growth even within the relationship of dependency itself, through an alliance between local and foreign capital and the national state. This helped explain the actual industrialization experienced by countries such as Brazil and Mexico despite their continuing structural dependency. In his analysis Cardoso relied on a methodology of "interpretation of historical situations" (the Historical-Structural Method), which combines theoretical abstraction with concrete analysis of the history of each country individually, rejecting both an abstract economistic generalization that does not distinguish between different national cases, and descriptive historiography devoid of a comparative theoretical framework.

## Major Works or Representatives

Besides Cardoso and Faletto, the Latin American dependency school included other researchers who developed more radical formulations, most notably André Gunder Frank (author of *Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America*, 1967), and Theotônio dos Santos, whose 1970 article "The Structure of Dependence" gave a Marxist formulation of the concept.

## Impact and Critique

Dependency theory became an influential framework, an alternative to modernization theory, in explaining unequal development globally, and it deeply influenced Latin American development policies and Immanuel Wallerstein's later analysis of the "world-system," which extended the "center-periphery" logic into a comprehensive theory of historical world capitalism. Cardoso himself later, after taking office as President of Brazil in 1995, turned to adopting more liberal economic policies and openness to the world market (the "Real" stabilization plan of 1994, launched while he was finance minister), a turn that raised wide debate about how consistent it was with his original academic theory. Cardoso defended it by saying that his original model had never excluded the possibility of development that is dependent and associated with world capitalism and not a rupture from it. It was closer to an analytical description of the possible and the constrained together than to a categorical political program of the necessity of complete economic isolation from the world capitalist market, as called for by the more radical formulations of dependency. His joint book with Faletto remains one of the most widely read Latin American sociological texts.
