# Karl Jaspers

Karl Jaspers (1883–1969) was a German psychiatrist and philosopher who introduced a phenomenological approach into descriptive psychopathology and became a leading thinker of modern existential philosophy (*Existenzphilosophie*), though he rejected ideological labels and distanced himself from popular existentialism.

## Life and Academic Career

Jaspers was born in Oldenburg on 23 February 1883. He studied law briefly before turning to medicine, receiving his medical degree in 1908. From 1909 to 1915 he worked at the psychiatric clinic of Heidelberg University under the direction of Franz Nissl. In 1913 he completed his habilitation in psychology, became a professor of psychology at Heidelberg in 1916, and transitioned to the chair of philosophy in 1921. 

His marriage to Gertrud Mayer, who was Jewish, placed him in grave danger under the National Socialist regime. Stripped of his professorship in 1937 and placed under a total publication ban in 1938, the couple lived under constant threat of deportation until Heidelberg was liberated in 1945. Jaspers was reinstated to his chair and helped rebuild the university, before accepting a professorship at the University of Basel in Switzerland in 1948, where he taught until his retirement in 1961. He died in Basel on 26 February 1969.

## Methodological Contribution to Psychopathology

In his seminal treatise *General Psychopathology* (*Allgemeine Psychopathologie*, 1913), Jaspers reshaped psychiatric method by applying Edmund Husserl's early phenomenological method and Wilhelm Dilthey's distinction between explanatory sciences (*Naturwissenschaften*) and interpretive sciences (*Geisteswissenschaften*). 

He distinguished sharply between **explaining** (*Erklären*), which traces psychological symptoms to external somatic or neurobiological causes, and **understanding** (*Verstehen*), which grasps psychological phenomena from within through empathetic comprehension of subjective meaning. In clinical diagnosis, Jaspers drew a distinction between understandable reactive developments from the "un-understandable" (*unverständlich*) radical psychic alterations characteristic of schizophrenic psychosis, alongside his classic classification of primary delusions (*Wahnwahrnehmungen*) versus delusion-like ideas (*wahnhafte Ideen*).

## Limit Situations and the Philosophy of Existenz

In *Psychology of Worldviews* (*Psychologie der Weltanschauungen*, 1919) and his three-volume masterwork *Philosophy* (*Philosophie*, 1932), Jaspers elaborated his philosophy of human existence (*Existenz*). Central to this thought is the concept of "limit situations" (*Grenzsituationen*): inescapable existential conditions such as death, suffering, struggle, guilt, and chance. Everyday consciousness attempts to evade or manage these realities through routine, but when confronted honestly, limit situations shatter conventional certainties and awaken the individual to authentic freedom and selfhood.

For Jaspers, *Existenz* cannot become an object of direct empirical science. It relates dialectically to the "Encompassing" (*das Umgreifende*)—the ultimate horizon of being that transcends subject-object duality—and encounters transcendence through symbolic "ciphers" (*Chiffren*). Authenticity is realized through "existential communication" (*existenzielle Kommunikation*), an open, loving contest (*liebender Kampf*) between free persons in pursuit of shared truth without dogmatism.

## Postwar Philosophy and Cultural Critique

Following World War II, Jaspers became a moral conscience in Germany. In *The Question of German Guilt* (*Die Schuldfrage*, 1946), he articulated a rigorous fourfold typology of guilt—criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical—insisting on Germany's collective political responsibility. In *The Origin and Goal of History* (*Vom Ursprung und Ziel der Geschichte*, 1949), he introduced the influential concept of the "Axial Age" (*Achsenzeit*, c. 800–200 BCE), arguing that parallel spiritual and philosophical breakthroughs in ancient China, India, Persia, Greece, and the Levant laid the universal foundations of human self-understanding.

## Influence and Legacy

Jaspers maintained a complex intellectual relationship with Martin Heidegger; their close philosophical alliance in the 1920s dissolved over Heidegger's support for National Socialism in 1933, leading to Jaspers's lifelong critical reflection on philosophy and political responsibility. His phenomenological psychopathology was an important reference point for psychiatrists of the phenomenological-anthropological tradition such as Ludwig Binswanger.
