# Michel Foucault

Michel Foucault (1926-1984) was a French philosopher and historian of ideas whose studies of madness, medicine, prisons, and sexuality reshaped sociology, especially the analysis of power, knowledge, and discipline.

## Life and Context

Foucault was born in Poitiers in 1926 and studied philosophy and psychology at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris. He taught in Sweden, Poland, and West Germany, then at the University of Clermont-Ferrand, in Tunisia, and at the experimental university of Vincennes. He was elected to the Collège de France in 1969 and held its chair in the history of systems of thought from 1970 until his death in Paris in 1984. He was politically active, notably in prison reform through the Groupe d'information sur les prisons, which sought to make prisoners' own accounts public.

## Main Ideas

- **Power/knowledge:** power is not only repressive but productive, and it is bound up with the production of knowledge. Knowledge systems and forms of power shape each other. It works through many local relations, and resistance arises inside those relations.
- **Discipline and surveillance:** in modern institutions, power operates through observation, timetables, ranking, normalization, and examination, which produce docile bodies. The Panopticon, drawn from Jeremy Bentham, serves as a model.
- **Discourse and episteme:** discourses define what can be said and known in a period, and he analyzed shifting historical frameworks of knowledge. Historically specific rules shape what can count as a meaningful or true statement.
- **Genealogy and archaeology:** methods for tracing the contingent historical formation of concepts and institutions.
- **Biopower and governmentality:** from the eighteenth century, states manage populations through health, birth, and life, as well as acting on individual bodies; later lectures developed governmentality as the guidance of conduct, including the ways people govern themselves.
- **Subjectivation:** individuals become subjects through practices of power and of self-formation. Categories such as the delinquent and the homosexual became ways of knowing and acting upon persons. Volumes 2 and 3 of *The History of Sexuality* turn toward ethics and practices of the self.

## Case Study

*Discipline and Punish* opens by setting a public execution of 1757 beside a later timetable for young prisoners, then follows disciplinary techniques through prisons, armies, schools and workshops, using Bentham's Panopticon as a diagram of surveillance.

## Method

Foucault combined historical research in archives with philosophical analysis. His archaeological work described the rules of discourse in a period, while genealogy, indebted to Nietzsche, analyzed the emergence of practices and the power relations behind them. Both approaches refuse a story of history as steady progress.

## Influence and Criticism

His work is central to critical theory, gender and queer studies, postcolonial studies, criminology, and the sociology of medicine, and it informs research on psychiatry, punishment, education, organizations and digital surveillance. Critics have questioned the selectivity and accuracy of some of his historical claims, his ambiguity about normative grounds for critique (a charge made by Jürgen Habermas and Nancy Fraser), and his treatment of resistance. Others argue that the account leaves little room for agency and underplays gender and colonialism. He rejected labels such as structuralist.

## Applications and Cross-References

Researchers use his concepts to study prisons, schools, clinics, and surveillance technologies. Compare Weber on rationalization, discipline and bureaucracy, Goffman on total institutions, Marx on power and ideology, and Butler on subject formation.
