# Carl Gustav Jung

Swiss psychiatrist, first president of the International Psychoanalytical Association (1910–1914), and founder of **analytical psychology** after his break with Freud. His influence is wider outside psychology than within it: in the study of religion, literature, anthropology and art more than in the clinic or the laboratory.

## What He Contributed

- **The word association experiment** (1904–1907, at the Burghölzli hospital): his first **experimental** contribution, which is not usually mentioned. He would read a list of words to the subject and measure in seconds the time of response to each. He found that delayed responses, stammering, forgetting, and changes in breathing and heart rate all cluster around particular words — and that these words share a single theme unknown to the subject. He called this cluster the **"complex"**: a group of emotionally charged ideas that operate independently of the will of consciousness. This term — which Jung popularized in this work, and not Freud — is what led Freud to correspond with him, as Freud saw in it **laboratory evidence for the unconscious**. Later work on physiological lie detection drew on similar ideas.
- **The collective unconscious and the archetypes**: beneath the personal unconscious lies an inherited layer shared among human beings, which carries not ready-made images but **dispositions to form images** — a fundamental distinction that is often neglected: for him the archetype is a "form without content", like a structure that each culture fills with its own materials. The best known are the **shadow** (what a person rejects in himself and attributes to others), the **anima/animus** (the contrasexual image in the psyche), the **persona** (a person's social face), and the **Self** (the center of the whole, not the center of consciousness).
- **Psychological types** (1921): extraversion and introversion, and the four functions (thinking, feeling, sensation and intuition). The origin of the classification for him was an attempt to explain **why he disagreed with Freud and Adler**: he saw their theories as the expression of two different ways of looking, not of two incompatible truths.
- **Individuation**: the aim of the second half of life is not adaptation to society but **becoming what one is**: reconciling consciousness with the unconscious, and the persona with the shadow. Hence he was among the first to make **midlife** a psychological subject in its own right, at a time when analysis was wholly preoccupied with childhood.
- **Active imagination**: his practical technique — entering into a conscious dialogue with the images of one's imagination instead of interpreting them from outside. Its source was his personal experience in the years of his crisis (1913–1930), which he recorded in the **"Red Book"**, not published until after his death, in 2009.
- **Synchronicity**: the coincidence of two events with no causal link between them that carry a shared meaning for the person. It is the most problematic of his concepts, and he developed it in correspondence with the physicist **Wolfgang Pauli**.

## His Place in the Current

Jung stands opposite Freud on one fundamental axis: Freud reads the higher through the lower (religion and art are the sublimation of a drive), and Jung reads the lower through the higher (the symptom is the psyche's attempt to say something and to grow). Hence the **unconscious** for Freud was a repository of what had been expelled, and for Jung a **creative** source that knows about the person what the person does not know about himself.

His furthest influence fell outside the psychological establishment: in the study of religion (Eliade and Campbell), in literary criticism, in the New Age movement, in the **Myers-Briggs indicator** (which was made by Katharine Briggs and her daughter, not by him, and is not endorsed by mainstream psychometric psychology), and in **Alcoholics Anonymous** — his letter to Bill Wilson about the alcoholic's need for a spiritual transformation is among the origins of the Twelve-Step program.

## Limitations
Three limitations on Jung, of varying weight:

**First — testability.** His central concepts (the collective unconscious, the archetypes and synchronicity) were formulated in such a way that it is hard to imagine what could **falsify** them. When evidence for them is sought, it usually consists of **similarities among the myths of cultures** — which are explained more simply by cultural diffusion and by humans' shared body, environment and cognitive structure, without any need for an inherited psychic legacy. Hence analytical psychology has remained outside the empirical academic mainstream, although some of its concepts have entered it in other formulations.

**Second — his statements in the 1930s.** In 1933 Jung took over the presidency of the **General Medical Society for Psychotherapy** when its German branch came under Nazi influence, edited its journal, the *Zentralblatt für Psychotherapie*, and wrote in 1934 about differences between "Germanic" and "Jewish" psychology, including remarks about an "Aryan unconscious". These are undisputed facts. Their interpretation, however, is divided: defenders say he stayed to preserve what could be preserved and to protect Jewish colleagues, that he did in fact help some of them, and that he later acknowledged having erred; opponents say that the language itself — not the administrative position — is the problem, and that it extends a tendency in his thought that links the psyche to soil and race. Both sides rely on the same documents.

**Third — his influence beyond his own safeguards.** Jung became the first authority of the literature of spiritual growth and the New Age, where he is credited with what he did not say. He himself used to warn against treating the archetypes as beings, and against replacing arduous psychological work with talk of "the spirit".

## Selected Quotation

"My life is a story of the self-realization of the unconscious. Everything in the unconscious seeks outward manifestation, and the personality too desires to evolve out of its unconscious conditions and to experience itself as a whole." — *Memories, Dreams, Reflections* (1961, Prologue)

## Major Works

Studies in Word Association (Diagnostische Assoziationsstudien, 1906) · The Psychology of Dementia Praecox (1907) · Symbols of Transformation (Wandlungen und Symbole der Libido, 1912) — the book that ended his tie with Freud · Psychological Types (Psychologische Typen, 1921) · The Relations between the Ego and the Unconscious (1928) · Psychology and Alchemy (Psychologie und Alchemie, 1944) · Aion: Researches into the Phenomenology of the Self (Aion, 1951) · Answer to Job (Antwort auf Hiob, 1952) · Memories, Dreams, Reflections (1961, with Aniela Jaffé) · The Red Book (Liber Novus, written 1913–1930 and published posthumously in 2009).
