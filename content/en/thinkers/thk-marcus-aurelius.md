# Marcus Aurelius Antoninus

A Roman emperor and Stoic philosopher, born on 26 April 121 CE in Rome. His full name was Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Augustus. He ruled from 161 to 180 CE and was the last of the "Five Good Emperors." He died on 17 March 180 CE, according to most accounts at Vindobona (present-day Vienna), during the campaigns against the Marcomanni, while another account places his death at Sirmium.

## Biography

Marcus Annius Verus was born into a family of Spanish origin (from Ucubi in Baetica) of standing in the Senate, and his grandfather held the consulship. The Emperor Antoninus Pius adopted him in 138 CE on Hadrian's orders, so he became heir, and he married Pius's daughter Faustina the Younger. He was educated by teachers of rhetoric and philosophy, among them the orator Fronto, whose correspondence with him survives, and his Stoic teacher Quintus Junius Rusticus, who introduced him to Epictetus. He became emperor in 161 CE and shared rule with his adoptive brother Lucius Verus until the latter's death in 169 CE. His reign was spent in wars on the frontiers: the Parthian war in the East (161–166), then the Marcomannic wars on the Danube from 166 to 180, and in this period the Antonine Plague struck the empire. In 175 CE the commander Avidius Cassius rebelled in the East after a rumor of Marcus's death, and it ended months later with Cassius's being killed by one of his officers; Marcus refused to punish his supporters harshly and pardoned most of them, according to ancient accounts. His son Commodus succeeded him.

## What He Gave

- **The *Meditations* (Ta eis heauton, "To Himself")**: twelve books written in Greek, the language of philosophy, in the form of personal notes, not for publication. Some books carry notes about where they were written: the first book is subscribed as written among the Quadi on the river Gran, and the second at Carnuntum, which ties them to the fields of war. They were first printed in Zurich in 1559, edited by Wilhelm Holzmann (Xylander).
- **Book One**: a list of thanks to those who taught him, from his grandfather and adoptive father and his teachers, recording what he learned from each of them.
- **Logos and the cosmos**: he holds that the cosmos is an ordered whole governed by reason (logos), that the human being is a part of it, and that harmony with nature is virtue.
- **Distinguishing what is in our power from what is not**: from Epictetus he takes the view that what a person controls is his judgment, desire, and action, while the body, reputation, and death lie outside these and are to be met with acceptance.
- **The cosmic republic**: he holds that all human beings are citizens of one city, and that duty to others originates in common reason.
- **Mental preparation**: his book repeatedly returns to the exercise of contemplating mortality, contemplating things as matter that changes, the morning examination, and viewing the earth from above.

## His Place in the Current

Marcus belongs to late Roman Stoicism with Seneca and Epictetus, a phase that turned toward practical ethics. Pierre Hadot read the *Meditations* as "spiritual exercises" and not a theoretical system, in *The Inner Citadel* (1992). Marcus did not present new logical or physical inquiry, but took the concepts of old Stoicism and repeated them in terse expressions because he was writing for himself and not for a reader, and in the *Meditations* they range between belief in providence and reflection on the possibility that the cosmos is merely atoms.

## Influence and Reception

The *Meditations* remained a text that was read in Byzantium (Arethas mentioned it in the tenth century), then spread in Europe after the 1559 edition, and Matthew Arnold wrote a famous essay on it in 1863. Today the book is circulated as a text in wisdom, self-management, and leadership. In modern political life his example is invoked as a ruler who tried to reconcile public duty with inner discipline, although this reconciliation remains open to question among historians because his reign was built on war.

## Limits

- The book does not provide an organized biography, and its terse statements are understood according to context and are a matter of disagreement in interpretation.
- The contrast is criticized between the image of the ascetic philosopher and the reality of the emperor who conducted harsh wars on the Danube, and on another side Christians were persecuted in his reign, as in the incident at Lyon in 177 CE, and he left nothing in the *Meditations* that addresses this.
- The book uses Stoic religious language about "providence" and "the gods" with a flexibility that may make some of its passages seem contradictory.

## Principal Works

- *Ta eis heauton / Meditations* (c. 170–180 CE).
- Correspondence with Fronto, preserved in a palimpsest manuscript.
