# Friedrich Carl von Savigny

**Friedrich Carl von Savigny** (1779–1861) was a German jurist and, from 1810, one of the first professors at the newly founded University of Berlin. He is regarded as the leading figure of the **historical school of law** (*Historische Rechtsschule*), which for decades was the dominant trend in German jurisprudence. It paved the way, though in an apparently paradoxical manner, for the highly systematic formulation of the German Civil Code (BGB).

## The Debate with Thibaut: Against Immediate Codification

In 1814 Anton Friedrich Justus Thibaut published a call (*Über die Nothwendigkeit eines allgemeinen bürgerlichen Rechts für Deutschland*) for a unified civil code for Germany, with the Napoleonic Code in the background. Savigny replied with his famous pamphlet *Vom Beruf unsrer Zeit für Gesetzgebung und Rechtswissenschaft* (1814), rejecting the idea that a rational legislator at a single historical moment could draft a complete law valid for an entire nation. This debate, known as the *Kodifikationsstreit* (the codification controversy), laid the intellectual foundation for his rejection of immediate comprehensive codification.

## The Popular Spirit and Organic Law

Savigny held that law, like language, customs and art, springs from the **spirit of the people** (*Volksgeist*) and grows organically through history along with the collective consciousness of the nation. It is not made by the sudden individual will of a legislator or philosopher. In its early stages law appears in unwritten popular customs and practices; then, as society becomes more complex, it develops into a technical formulation undertaken by specialized jurists, not as legislators in the voluntarist sense, but as technical representatives of the nation's organically growing legal consciousness. This organic conception led him to see the sudden French codification as a hasty attempt that cut law off from its living historical roots.

## Roman Law as Living Material: Historicism in the Service of Systematics

Despite his rejection of immediate codification, Savigny was not an advocate of random local custom. He devoted his greatest scholarly effort to the study of **Roman law** as the most mature historical material, one that holds accumulated legal experience over centuries. In his encyclopedic project *System des heutigen römischen Rechts* (8 volumes, 1840–1849), he sought to reconstruct this law into a rational system with coherent concepts (such as the theory of subjective right, the legal relationship and legal capacity) applicable to Germany in his own time. This combination of the historical method and systematic formulation produced what was later known as **conceptual jurisprudence** (*Begriffsjurisprudenz*) among his students, although Savigny himself remained closer to historical caution than to purely abstract conceptual reasoning.

His *System* also contains, in its eighth volume, his theory of conflict of laws: each legal relationship has its "seat" (*Sitz*) in a particular legal territory, and should be governed by the law of that seat. This approach became a foundation of modern private international law.

## The Theory of Possession and Ownership

Among his important early works is *Das Recht des Besitzes* (1803), on the theory of possession. In it he re-read the Roman sources with a rigorous philological method, analysing possession (its factual element of physical control and the intention to hold) and its protection by possessory remedies, as distinct from ownership. The work made his reputation and strongly influenced later European treatments of possession.

## Legacy in the German Civil Code and the European Civil Law Tradition

Despite his original opposition to immediate codification, Savigny's historical school, through his students and their successors in conceptual jurisprudence, provided the precise conceptual foundation on which the German Civil Code (BGB) was built when it was promulgated in 1896 and entered into force in 1900, with its precise concepts such as the legal transaction (*Rechtsgeschäft*), legal personality, and subjective right. Thus historicism, which began by rejecting codification, became a theoretical foundation for one of the most systematically precise European civil codes. 

## Teaching Method and the Founding of the Historical School of Law at Berlin

Alongside his scholarly output, Savigny established at the University of Berlin a demanding teaching circle that combined classical philology with precise legal analysis. He trained a generation of jurists (such as Georg Friedrich Puchta and others) in his method of reading Roman texts critically and historically, separating the successive layers of the text across the ages. This teaching approach, known for its philological precision, helped make the Berlin law faculty a leading European centre for the study of Roman law in the nineteenth century.

## Savigny as Minister: The Tension between the Jurist and the Prussian State

Savigny was appointed in 1842 as Minister for the Revision of Legislation in the Prussian government. This office placed him in an apparently contradictory position with respect to his early thesis rejecting direct legislative intervention in the organic development of law. He found himself effectively responsible for overseeing Prussian legislative projects, which led him to adopt practical positions more flexible than his original theoretical formulation. He confined his opposition to immediate **comprehensive** codification (a single unified civil code for all of Germany) without opposing limited partial legislative reforms that addressed specific issues without disturbing the structure of the existing legal system as a whole.

## The Comparison with Thibaut: A Disagreement over Timing, Not Principle

It is important to note that Savigny's disagreement with Thibaut was not a disagreement about the impossibility of a German legislative unification in principle, but about its **timing**. Thibaut held that the political moment after the Napoleonic wars was favourable to an immediate legislative unification that would strengthen the emerging German national unity. Savigny held that haste would produce a fragile law lacking sufficient jurisprudential depth, and that the desired political unity would be achieved more securely through the accumulation of a shared legal science among the German states before any formal legislative unification. Subsequent developments (the unification of German private law only some eight decades after his pamphlet appeared) showed a certain historical coherence in this view.

## Historicism and systematic formulation among his students

The tension between historical caution and systematic construction was worked out as a revision inside the school more than as an open quarrel. Georg Friedrich Puchta set out in *Das Gewohnheitsrecht* (1828) to develop Savigny's account of how law arises "on its several sides." Where Savigny in 1814 had still called jurists' law "customary law," in contrast to legislation, Puchta held that the received common Roman law was *Juristenrecht*, not custom. From 1826 he used the term *Volksgeist* for the pre-state workshop in which law is formed, borrowing and recasting a Hegelian expression. For Puchta, what validated jurists' law was not external marks such as usage, court practice or a common opinion of doctors, but the jurist's share in practical national needs together with an inner test of scientific truth. Later study of their letters and of Puchta's doctrine of interpretation finds real departures, and a further shift from jurists' law toward a "law of science," still bounded by divine commands, good morals and the principles of existing law. Their correspondence in the 1830s stayed courteous, and recent work doubts that their accounts of how law arises stood as far apart as some readings claim. The sharper attack on a closed conceptual system came after Puchta's death, when Rudolf von Jhering blamed him for an overly tight hold on Roman concepts.
