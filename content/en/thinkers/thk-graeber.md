# David Graeber

An American anthropologist, philosopher, and political activist (1961–2020), known for his work on anarchism, value, debt, and the state. He was notable for combining fieldwork, theoretical writing, and direct political activism.

## Biography

Born in New York in 1961, he took his undergraduate degree at SUNY Purchase and received his PhD from the **University of Chicago** in 1996 under the supervision of **Marshall Sahlins**. Between 1989 and 1991 he carried out ethnographic fieldwork in Betafo, a rural community in the central highlands of Madagascar, among Malagasy people descended from both slave-owning and enslaved families, and later published his findings in *Lost People* (2007). He asked not only which goods were exchanged but what people considered valuable and why, documenting how the legacy of slavery, memory, and relations with ancestors shape local ideas of power, obligation, and magic. He taught at **Yale University** from 1998, then at **Goldsmiths** in London (2007 to 2013), and from 2013 until his death he was professor of anthropology at the **London School of Economics**. He took part in the early organizing of Occupy Wall Street (2011), and is often credited, though the attribution is contested, with helping to popularize the slogan "We are the 99%." He died in Venice in September 2020, at the age of 59.

## What He Gave

- **Debt predates barter and money.** In *Debt: The First 5000 Years* (2011), he challenged the story with which economics textbooks begin: that people first bartered goods, invented money to make barter easier, and debt arose afterward. Drawing on anthropological and historical evidence, Sumerian temple records, Islamic philosophy, and colonial records, he argued that **credit systems** (mutual obligations tracked over time) are the oldest economic form, and that the "barter economy" assumed by theory has not been documented in any society. Even after commodity money and market exchange emerged, the logic of credit persisted alongside them and often dominated them.
- **Debt is a moral phenomenon.** His second insight is that debt and credit are essentially moral phenomena. Owing someone creates not only an economic obligation but a moral relationship, and when a creditor demands repayment through violence or coercion, the moral economy is violated. From this he argued that the force of the statement "debt must be repaid" rests on a confusion between a moral obligation and a quantitative contract. He also argued that debt crises and debt cancellation (the jubilee) are recurring features of history, and that taxes, the state's demand that payment be made in the currency it issues, are what create demand for money, rather than the efficiency of money as a medium of barter.
- **A theory of value.** In *Toward an Anthropological Theory of Value* (2001), he built on Marcel Mauss's *The Gift* (1925) to make value a matter of social relations and local moral judgments, drawing on examples such as Iroquois wampum and Kwakwaka'wakw and Maori exchange. Value is thus neither labor time in the Marxist sense, nor individual preferences, nor universal principles of utility and scarcity.
- **Critique of bureaucracy and "bullshit jobs."** In *The Utopia of Rules* (2015), he analyzed how bureaucratic systems appear to rationalize social life while in fact producing irrationality and suffering, and argued that technology carries a bureaucratic logic rather than being neutral. In *Bullshit Jobs: A Theory* (2018), he identified five categories of jobs whose holders see them as meaningless (flunkies, goons, duct-tapers, box-tickers, and taskmasters), and asked why capitalism, which is supposed to hate waste, produces such a quantity of work that produces nothing.
- **Anarchist anthropology.** In *Fragments of an Anarchist Anthropology* (2004), he argued that anthropology is qualified to imagine and evaluate radical alternatives to the organizational forms of the state and the market.

## Position Within the Field

He was among the best-known anarchist thinkers of his era, alongside Noam Chomsky and **Murray Bookchin**, and a prominent voice in debates on radical and direct democracy through his involvement in Occupy. His grounding in fieldwork, especially in Madagascar, gave his theses an empirical basis unusual in political philosophy, and distinguished him from contemporary critics of the economy who rely on secondary sources. His explicit political commitment to opposing imperialism and defending the sovereignty of Indigenous peoples also marked his approach. He criticized anthropology's historical complicity with colonialism, and argued that the discipline should reorient itself toward alliance with Indigenous peoples' movements and anti-capitalist struggles.

## Impact

*Debt* became a touchstone for critics of neoliberalism and debt-based capitalism. Activists cite it in calls for debt cancellation and alternative economic systems, and it became required reading for activists, development researchers, and economists skeptical of prevailing finance. The concept of "bullshit jobs" spread widely and resonated with workers who experience meaninglessness and instability, and it has been applied to critiques of automation and the future of work. His theoretical reformulation, which placed debt and morality at the center of economic anthropology, redirected research toward questions of justice, ethics, and obligation, not only structural analysis. His death in 2020 was widely mourned in activist and academic circles.

## The Most Famous Controversy

In May 2005, Yale University decided not to renew his contract, denying him promotion to tenure. A petition gathering thousands of signatures was organized, and many supporters believed the reason was his declared anarchism and his support for the graduate students' union. Yale did not publicly state its reasons, and senior anthropologists, Marshall Sahlins among them, defended the quality of his scholarship. He left Yale and his academic career moved to Britain.

## Limitations and Criticisms

- **Criticism of *Debt*.** Reviewers, including economists, argued that the book is persuasive in challenging the "myth of barter" but weaker as an account of money and political economy, and some pointed to factual slips, which Graeber acknowledged in replies. Economic historians, particularly specialists in cuneiform writing, challenged some of his interpretations of the Sumerian data. They argued that he overstated the existence of debt-based systems in early states and retrospectively applied modern concepts to ancient systems.
- **Criticism of *Bullshit Jobs*.** The book's material consists of self-reports from people who see their work as meaningless. In an empirical test published in *Work, Employment and Society* (online 2021), Magdalena Soffia, Alex J. Wood, and Brendan Burchell took the book's hypotheses to representative European survey data. They found that the sense that one's work is useless is indeed associated with poorer psychological well-being. However, the proportion of those who describe their work as useless is low and declining, and its distribution across occupations contradicts Graeber's predictions.
- **Criticism of *The Dawn of Everything* (co-written with David Wengrow, not by Graeber alone).** Published after his death, the book drew both acclaim and sharp criticism. Critics such as the historian Walter Scheidel and several archaeologists and anthropologists argued that it overstates the evidence for early egalitarian political experiments and sets up a caricatured opponent in mainstream evolutionary accounts of social complexity.
- **Broader methodological limitations.** Economists question whether systems of credit could operate on a large scale without money, and whether his account sufficiently explains the practical advantages of commodity money. Some anthropologists criticize his glorification of non-capitalist and non-state systems, which they describe as a "romantic primitivism" that obscures the real violence, coercion, and inequality in those contexts. Feminist researchers note that his treatment of gender in systems of debt and obligation is underdeveloped.
