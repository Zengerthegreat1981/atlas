# Manetho

Manetho of Sebennytos (fl. early 3rd century BCE) was an Egyptian high priest, chronicler, and historian who composed the *Aegyptiaca* (*History of Egypt*) in Greek during the reigns of the first two Ptolemaic rulers, Ptolemy I Soter and Ptolemy II Philadelphus. 

Occupying a pivotal cultural intersection between Pharaonic priestly learning and Hellenistic intellectual culture, Manetho was an Egyptian priest who presented his nation's annals, king lists, and religious traditions to Greek readers in the Greek language. His foundational division of Egyptian rulers into thirty dynasties established the chronological framework that continues to structure modern Egyptology.

## Historical and Cultural Context

A native of Sebennytos (ancient Tjebnetjer) in the central Nile Delta, Manetho was a high priest, likely associated with the cult of Ra at Heliopolis or Sebennytos itself. According to Plutarch (*De Iside et Osiride* 28), Manetho served as a trusted religious adviser to Ptolemy I Soter, collaborating with the Athenian priest Timotheus the Eumolpid to organize the official syncretic cult of Serapis, which united Greek and Egyptian subjects under a shared divine patron.

Manetho's literary activity occurred alongside that of his contemporary Berossus, the Babylonian priest who dedicated his *Babyloniaca* to Antiochus I Soter. Both indigenous scholars sought to assert the antiquity, wisdom, and institutional priority of their respective civilizations within the newly established Hellenistic successor kingdoms, correcting Greek misconceptions and establishing authoritative native chronologies for Greek readers.

## The *Aegyptiaca* and the Dynastic Framework

Comprising three books written in Greek, the *Aegyptiaca* organized Egyptian history from the mythical reign of the gods, demigods, and spirits down to the flight of Nectanebo II, the last native pharaoh of the Thirtieth Dynasty, before the Persian reconquest and Alexander the Great (343–332 BCE):
- **Book 1**: The divine dynasties, the legendary unifier Menes (Narmer), and Dynasties 1 to 11 (the Archaic Period and Old Kingdom).
- **Book 2**: Dynasties 12 to 19 (the Middle Kingdom, Second Intermediate Period, Hyksos rule, and the great pharaohs of the New Kingdom).
- **Book 3**: Dynasties 20 to 30 (the Ramesside decline, Third Intermediate Period, Nubian and Saite dynasties, Persian conquest, and the final native pharaohs).

Manetho's dynastic division was an organizational breakthrough. Rather than presenting an unbroken list of hundreds of individual pharaohs, he grouped rulers according to royal houses, geographical origins, and dynastic capitals (such as Memphis, Thebes, Elephantine, Tanis, and Sais). Modern Egyptologists adopted this scheme, organizing Pharaonic history into the Old, Middle, and New Kingdoms separated by Intermediate Periods.

## Sources and Historiographical Method

As a temple priest, Manetho had direct access to authentic hieroglyphic and hieratic temple archives, monumental inscriptions, and official king lists—traditions of the kind preserved in surviving documents such as the Palermo Stone (Fifth Dynasty), the Turin Royal Canon (Nineteenth Dynasty), and the Abydos King List. 

By translating these native documents into Greek, Manetho provided a critical corrective to Greek historians like Herodotus and Hecataeus of Abdera. Herodotus's sequence of Egyptian kings is badly out of order by comparison with the monuments—he places the Fourth Dynasty pyramid builders (Cheops and Chephren) after figures of later periods. Manetho's scheme places them in their proper early setting and explained the meaning of Pharaonic royal names and epithets. He also preserved the earliest connected historical narrative of the Hyksos ("Shepherd Kings" or *heqa khasut*, "rulers of foreign lands"), describing their invasion, fortified capital at Avaris, and eventual expulsion by Ahmose I.

## Transmission and Reception

Manetho's complete text did not survive intact into the modern era. Instead, his work is preserved through substantial excerpts, summaries, and conflicting chronographic tables cited by later Jewish, Christian, and pagan polemicists:
- **Flavius Josephus**: Quoted extensive verbatim narrative passages in *Against Apion* (c. 94 CE) to argue for the antiquity of the Jewish people and discuss the Hyksos.
- **Christian Chronographers**: Sextus Julius Africanus (3rd century CE) and Eusebius of Caesarea (4th century CE) compiled Manetho's king lists and regnal years in their world chronicles, which were subsequently preserved and synthesized by the 9th-century Byzantine monk George Syncellus.

Despite distortions introduced by successive copyists, 19th- and 20th-century decipherment of hieroglyphic inscriptions broadly corroborated the usefulness of Manetho's dynastic framework, securing his status as an important bridge between ancient Pharaonic memory and classical historiography.
