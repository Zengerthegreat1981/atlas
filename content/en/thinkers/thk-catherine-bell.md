# Catherine Bell

American scholar of religious studies (1953–2008). She earned her PhD at the University of Chicago and taught at Santa Clara University, a Jesuit university in California, where she held a named chair in religious studies, until her early death. She is regarded as one of the leading theorists of ritual in the contemporary generation, following the foundational current represented by William Robertson Smith, Émile Durkheim, Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss. She is known for her radical methodological critique of the concept of "ritual" itself as an analytical category that is assumed before examination rather than built out of it.

## Historical Context of the Field

Since the late nineteenth century, the dominant theories of ritual (those of Robertson Smith and then Durkheim) tended to treat "ritual" as a cross-cultural category with predetermined features, and then to seek its social function or symbolic meaning. Bell emerged in the 1980s and 1990s to subject this foundational assumption itself to scrutiny, influenced by the shift in the social sciences towards "practice theory" (especially in Pierre Bourdieu and Michel de Certeau), which moves the focus from fixed structures to the action and everyday strategies of the agents themselves.

## Critique of the Thought–Action Dichotomy

In her pivotal book *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (1992), Bell argued that most earlier theories of ritual, from Durkheim to Geertz, implicitly assume a dichotomy between "thought" (belief, meaning) and "action" (ritual, performance), and then ask about the relationship between them (does ritual express a prior belief, or does it generate one?). Bell argues that this very question is misleading, because it treats "ritual" as an objective entity existing in its own right that can be isolated from the rest of social practice.

## Ritualization Instead of Ritual

As an alternative, Bell proposed the concept of **ritualization**: there are no actions that are "rituals" by their nature. Rather, there are cultural strategies that distinguish some practices from their everyday context (through strict repetition, the invocation of traditional authority, or a special spatial and temporal organization), thereby giving them a different and more important character in the eyes of their practitioners. This shift, from "What is ritual?" to "How do communities ritualize their actions?", redirected the field of ritual studies towards the analysis of power, the body and everyday practice, drawing on the practice theory of Bourdieu.

## Impact

Her work helped reposition the study of ritual within comparative religious studies, away from rigid functional classifications (rites of passage, rites of renewal, etc.) and towards an analysis more sensitive to the historical and power context of each practice.

## Major Works

Among her most important works are *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (1992), her pivotal book that founded her thesis; *Ritual: Perspectives and Dimensions* (1997), a comprehensive critical survey of the history of ritual theories since the nineteenth century that is widely used as an academic introduction to the field; and numerous articles, including studies of Chinese religion and ritual.

## Conclusion

Bell remains a central figure in reshaping ritual theory within comparative religious studies. She freed the field from a heavy theoretical legacy inherited from classical functionalism, and pushed it towards an analysis more sensitive to the historical and power context of each ritual practice. Her reference books are widely assigned in university courses on ritual theory, and her concepts continue to be applied to contexts she did not directly address. Her critique of the concept of "ritual" remains one of the most important theoretical turns in the history of the field,.

## Her Position Relative to the Foundational Current

Bell explicitly criticizes the foundational current represented by William Robertson Smith, Émile Durkheim, Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss, who all assumed that "ritual" is a cross-cultural category that can be isolated from belief and studied apart from it. Bell explains that this assumption is itself a historical product of European Protestant classifications (distinguishing true inner faith from empty external "ritualism," as in the Protestant–Catholic controversy), rather than a neutral objective datum valid for all religious traditions.

## Limitations and Criticism

Her deconstructive critique of the concept of "ritual" itself has led some researchers to accuse her of emptying the field of a practical analytical tool without adequate replacement. If "ritualization" is merely a changing contextual strategy, it is sometimes difficult to identify what distinguishes ritual from any other organized social action. The debate over whether "ritualization" can serve as a practical alternative to the traditional concept of "ritual" remains open in the literature of the field, although her work remains an indispensable reference in any contemporary university course on ritual theory.
