# Carlos Castaneda

Carlos Castaneda (1925–1998) was a Peruvian-born American anthropologist and writer who became one of the most famous and most disputed figures of the counterculture. His series of books about his apprenticeship to a Yaqui "man of knowledge" called Don Juan Matus — beginning with *The Teachings of Don Juan: A Yaqui Way of Knowledge* (1968) — sold millions of copies, made him a cultural phenomenon, and helped shape the New Age movement. At the same time, academic anthropologists came to regard the books as fiction rather than ethnography, and Castaneda himself remains an enigma: even the basic facts of his birth are uncertain, with immigration records giving 25 December 1925 in Cajamarca, Peru, while he himself at different times claimed to have been born in São Paulo, Brazil, in 1931 or 1935.

## Life and Career

Castaneda was born Carlos César Salvador Arana Castañeda, most probably in Cajamarca, Peru, on 25 December 1925, according to United States immigration records. He immigrated to the United States in 1951 and studied anthropology at the University of California, Los Angeles, where he was a student of Harold Garfinkel, the founder of ethnomethodology. *The Teachings of Don Juan*, describing his encounters with Don Juan and written during his studies at UCLA, was published in 1968 and became an immediate bestseller. He received his PhD from UCLA in 1973, on the basis of *Journey to Ixtlan*, and in March of that year he appeared on the cover of *Time* magazine.

Castaneda rarely gave interviews, refused to be photographed, and cultivated secrecy about his life, a stance he presented as part of the warrior's discipline of "erasing personal history." In his later years he gathered a circle of followers in Los Angeles and, with three women known as the "witches" — Taisha Abelar, Florinda Donner-Grau, and Carol Tiggs — founded Cleargreen, an organisation teaching "magical passes" or Tensegrity, a system of movements said to redistribute energy in the body. He died of liver cancer in Los Angeles on 27 April 1998; his death was not publicly announced for nearly two months, and several women of his inner circle disappeared shortly afterwards, fuelling press speculation.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

The Don Juan books describe a "path of knowledge" in which the apprentice learns to "stop the world" — to suspend ordinary interpretation of reality — and to "see" the world as flows of energy, organised by the Eagle and its emanations. Don Juan's teachings centre on the figure of the warrior: a person who lives with death as an adviser, acts impeccably without attachment to outcome, and treats the world as a place of power rather than as a collection of objects. The books introduced a generation of readers to ideas drawn, however loosely, from Mesoamerican and Yaqui traditions: the use of the peyote cactus and datura as plants of power, the distinction between the tonal (the ordinary, describable world) and the nagual (the indescribable), and the practice of dreaming as a discipline.

Castaneda's significance lies less in the accuracy of his ethnography than in his cultural impact. His books offered a spiritual vocabulary — "stopping the world," "seeing," "impeccability," "the assemblage point" — that became part of the language of the New Age movement and of the wider counterculture, and they influenced a range of writers and thinkers, among them the philosopher Gilles Deleuze, who discussed Castaneda in *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980, with Félix Guattari). For many readers, the books functioned as a critique of Western rationality, suggesting that other ways of knowing might be as real as the scientific one.

## Influence and Critique

The academic critique of Castaneda was devastating and, by now, is close to a consensus. Richard de Mille, in *Castaneda's Journey: The Power and the Allegory* (1976) and *The Don Juan Papers* (1980), assembled internal evidence — chronological and geographical contradictions between the books — to argue that Castaneda had fabricated much of what he described. Jay Courtney Fikes, in *Carlos Castaneda, Academic Opportunism and the Psychedelic Sixties* (1993), showed that the rituals and concepts attributed to the Yaqui have no counterpart in the ethnographic literature on Yaqui people, and that no Yaqui figure corresponding to Don Juan has been identified. The books are now generally read as literary works, not as anthropology.

Yet the critique has not erased his influence. Millions of readers were deeply affected by the books, and concepts such as the "Toltec" wisdom tradition — a loose blend of Toltec, Maya, Aztec, and Yaqui materials — were taken up by popular spiritual writers such as Don Miguel Ruiz, whose *The Four Agreements* (1997) draws on a similar popular "Toltec" vocabulary. After his death, former followers, among them Amy Wallace in *Sorcerer's Apprentice* (2003), described life inside his circle as authoritarian and cult-like, a charge that has coloured his reputation further. Castaneda is thus read today in two inseparable ways: as a literary writer who gave a generation a powerful mythology of the warrior's path, and as a cautionary case in the history of anthropology — a demonstration of how easily the authority of ethnography can be borrowed, and how hard it is to give back.
