# John Dewey

An American philosopher, psychologist and educator, the greatest representative of **applied pragmatism** (Pragmatism Applied) in the twentieth century. He formulated "learning by doing", "democracy as a way of life" and "experience and knowledge".

## What He Contributed

### *Democracy and Education* (1916)

The foundational text in the **philosophy of education**. **Education** is not **preparation** for a future but **present life**. **The school** must be a **small community** (an embryonic community): children **learn** by taking part in **real projects** (occupations), not by memorizing information.

### *Experience and Nature* (1925)

A project of an **"empirical (naturalistic) metaphysics"**: experience is not a veil over a deeper reality but the interaction of a living organism with its environment. The living organism interacts with its environment, and nature and experience are continuous, not separate.

### *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938)

Thinking is not a **property** of the abstract mind but a **method** applied to solve scientific and practical problems. **Inquiry** = **practical investigation**.

### *Freedom and Culture* (1939)

A critique of **fascism** and of **corrupt liberalism**: **freedom** is not only individual **protection** but **participation** in **public life**. **Culture** is a result of **democracy** and its condition.

## Influence

- **Philosophy of education**: a leading figure of **progressive education** in America.
- **Neo-pragmatism**: Richard Rorty, in his book *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (published after Dewey's death, in 1979), revived Dewey's critique of the conception of knowledge as a "mirror" matching reality, and Cornel West read him critically within the same tradition.

## Biography

John Dewey was born in Burlington, Vermont, on 20 October 1859, studied at the University of Vermont (1879) and then received his doctorate from Johns Hopkins University in 1884. He taught at the University of Michigan (1884–1894), then at the University of Chicago (1894–1904), where in 1896 he established the laboratory school in which he tried out his ideas on education, and then at Columbia University in New York (1904–1930). He was elected president of the American Psychological Association in 1899 and of the American Philosophical Association in 1905. He visited Japan and China between 1919 and 1921, prepared a report on education in Turkey in 1924, and in 1937 chaired the commission of inquiry into the charges of the Moscow trials against Trotsky. He died in New York on 1 June 1952.

## Instrumentalism and the Theory of Inquiry

Dewey holds that thought is a tool that the living organism uses to resolve problematic situations that obstruct its activity, not a mirror that transmits an image of the world. He therefore called his philosophy "instrumentalism", and preferred to speak of "warranted assertibility" rather than absolute truth. The process of inquiry begins with a disturbed situation that turns into a definite problem, then proposes hypotheses that are tested, and the problem is resolved or the matter is taken up again. In the essay "The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology" (1896) he criticized the division of behavior into a separate stimulus and response, and said that activity is a continuous organic unity, and this essay is counted among the starting points of functionalism in American psychology.

## Education and Democracy

Dewey links education with democracy: a democratic society rests on the exchange of experiences and free communication between groups, and a good school prepares the child for this life by making learning a social activity that springs from his interests and gradually widens. In *Experience and Education* (1938) he replied to what had been wrongly understood from his school as pampering the child, and affirmed that freedom does not mean the absence of organization and that the teacher is responsible for choosing the experiences that foster growth. In *The Public and Its Problems* (1927) he replied to Walter Lippmann, who held that the public is a phantom and that decisions belong to experts, and defended the necessity of improving the means of communication and public discussion so that the public can form and govern.

## Influence and Reception

Dewey influenced modern education systems in America and beyond, from Turkey to China to Mexico. Richard Rorty rehabilitated Dewey in *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) as one of three philosophers, with Wittgenstein and Heidegger, who undermined the conception of knowledge as a mirror of reality. Readings of him widened in political and aesthetic philosophy, especially in *Art as Experience* (1934), and in the discussion of natural religion in *A Common Faith* (1934).

## Limits and Critique

Lippmann objected to Dewey's optimism about the public's capacity for rational government. Educational critics said that applying his ideas in schools in a simplified form led to weakness in cognitive content, although Dewey himself criticized these applications. Analytic philosophers, among them Russell, held against him that his theory of truth confuses utility with truth. Some critics pointed out that his language is obscure and his style cumulative, and that his political positions, such as his initial support for America's entry into the First World War, provoked opposition from his students and colleagues, among them Randolph Bourne.
