# Gordon W. Allport

An American psychologist who established the study of personality (Personality Psychology) as an independent academic field in psychology, and the author of one of the most influential books on the study of prejudice.

## What He Contributed

- **Personality: A Psychological Interpretation** (1937): a comprehensive theory of personality as a dynamic organization of traits.
- **Becoming** (1955): the mature personality is a continuous realization of being, not a fixed state.
- **Functional Autonomy** (1937): motives may become independent of their past origins and become ends in themselves, which is his direct critique of Freud's reduction of every present motive to an infantile root.
- **The lexical study of traits** (1936, with Henry Odbert): they extracted from the English dictionary about 18 thousand words describing personality and then classified them. This study is the direct origin of all the factor models of traits that followed, up to the "Big Five."
- **The Nature of Prejudice** (1954): his most influential work outside personality psychology. In it he formulated the **Contact Hypothesis**: that contact between groups reduces prejudice only under four conditions: equal status, a common goal, cooperation rather than competition, and support from authority. The hypothesis remains one of the most tested and confirmed propositions in social psychology.

## His Place in the Current

He established the study of personality as an independent field, influenced by William Stern's "personalistic psychology" and by the German tradition of "understanding psychology" (Verstehen). Among his distinctive positions was his insistence on the **idiographic method**: that studying the single individual in his uniqueness is legitimate science and not merely a prelude to generalization, a position on which he conducted a long controversy with the dominant measurement-based psychology.

## Limitation
His trait theory drew two criticisms: that the "trait" in his work is a description rather than an explanation, naming a regularity without accounting for its cause; and that his insistence on the uniqueness of every personality makes the theory resistant to comparative measurement, which pushed later generations (Cattell, Eysenck) toward the factor-analytic path that he rejected. The "contact hypothesis" has held up empirically, but its four conditions are rarely all present together in reality, which explains the modest effect of "encounter" programs in the field.

## Note: Not to Be Confused with His Brother
He had an older brother, **Floyd Henry Allport** (1890–1978, who died after Gordon's death), also a prominent American psychologist and a pioneer of experimental social psychology (author of *Social Psychology*, 1924). The two are entirely different in their main field of specialization despite the kinship and shared professional collegiality.

## Major Works

Personality: A Psychological Interpretation (1937) · The Nature of Prejudice (1954) · Becoming (1955) · Pattern and Growth in Personality (1961).

## Biography and Career

Allport was born in Indiana in 1897 and studied at Harvard, where he received his bachelor's degree in 1919 and his doctorate in psychology in 1922. Between the two degrees he spent a year teaching at Robert College in Istanbul, and then visited Vienna and met Freud, an incident he recounted in his autobiography: he told Freud that he had seen a child afraid of dirt, and Freud asked whether that child was himself, so Allport left convinced that psychoanalysis sometimes dives deeper than necessary and that manifest motives deserve attention before the hidden ones are sought. He returned to Harvard as a professor from 1930 and remained there until his death in 1967, and prominent names studied under him, among them Jerome Bruner, Stanley Milgram and Thomas Pettigrew.

## Traits and Individual Classification

Allport distinguished between common traits, which are compared across individuals, and personal dispositions, which belong only to a particular individual, and divided dispositions into cardinal ones that dominate the life of their owner, central ones (about five to ten) that describe his general character, and secondary ones of narrower effect. With Vernon he produced the Study of Values (1931), based on Spranger's types, and with Postman he wrote *The Psychology of Rumor* (1947), which analyzed how circulated news shrinks and changes. In *The Individual and His Religion* (1950) he studied religion as a mature or immature personal phenomenon.

## Prejudice and Its Degrees

In *The Nature of Prejudice* he did not content himself with the contact hypothesis but described an escalating scale of prejudiced behavior: antilocution (hostile talk), avoidance, discrimination, physical attack, and extermination. As Allport defined prejudice in the opening chapter (1954, p. 9):

> "Ethnic prejudice is an antipathy based upon a faulty and inflexible generalization. It may be felt or expressed. It may be directed toward a group as a whole, or toward an individual because he is a member of that group."

He affirmed that the mental categorization of people into groups is a natural cognitive process, but that prejudice appears when the category turns into a rigid judgment that does not change with evidence. In his study with Ross (1967) he found that "extrinsic," instrumental religiosity is associated with prejudice more than "intrinsic" religiosity, a result that opened wide research in the psychology of religion.

## Further Limits and Critique

A major meta-analytic review by Pettigrew and Tropp (2006) examined about five hundred studies and found that contact generally reduces prejudice, but that the four conditions Allport set are not necessary for the effect to occur, though they enhance it; the hypothesis was right in its direction more than in its conditions. Personality psychology after him moved toward factor-analytic measurement, which Allport did not trust, and his idiographic approach became marginal in practice, although it remained present in case studies and biographies.
