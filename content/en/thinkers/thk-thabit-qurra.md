# Thabit ibn Qurra

Abu al-Hasan Thabit ibn Qurra ibn Marwan al-Harrani al-Sabi' (d. Baghdad, 288 AH / 901 CE) was a mathematician, astronomer, physician and translator in ninth-century Baghdad. He revised and composed scientific Arabic, and he also wrote original work in geometry and astronomy. On his birth, later biographical sources give 221 AH / 836 CE. Another report gives 211 AH / 826 CE. The death date is the stable one.

## Origins and Formation

He was born in Harran in northern Upper Mesopotamia, one of the last centres of the ancient astral paganism known in Arabic sources as the Sabians. It preserved a Hermetic and Greek heritage and the Syriac language. Biographical dictionaries, among them Ibn Abi Usaybi'a's *Uyun al-Anba'* and Ibn al-Qifti's *Ta'rikh al-Hukama'*, say that he was a money-changer in Harran and that Muhammad ibn Musa ibn Shakir, one of the Banu Musa, brought him to Baghdad. He worked in that circle, alongside the translators of Hunayn ibn Ishaq's generation, and later stood close to the caliph al-Mu'tadid (r. 279–289 AH / 892–902 CE), for whom he wrote on astronomy and on the checking of observations. He wrote in Syriac and in Arabic. Ibn al-Nadim and later bibliographers list many titles. Only a part survives, some of it only in Latin or Hebrew.

## Translation and Revision

He was an editor of other men's translations as well as a translator. He revised Ishaq ibn Hunayn's Arabic of Euclid's *Elements*, and he revised Arabic versions of works of Archimedes and of Ptolemy's *Almagest*. He is credited with revising the first four books of Apollonius's *Conics* and with translating books five to seven, which do not survive in Greek. The work shows translation as a correction and a reorganization of proofs. Ibn al-Haytham and al-Biruni later used this mathematical Arabic.

## Original Contributions

In mathematics he gave a rule for amicable numbers. If b = 3·2^n − 1, c = 3·2^(n−1) − 1 and d = 9·2^(2n−1) − 1 are all prime, then 2^n·b·c and 2^n·d are amicable. For n = 2 the pair is 220 and 284. Later mathematicians in the Arabic tradition, among them Kamal al-Din al-Farisi, used the rule and produced further pairs. In the seventeenth century Fermat and Descartes arrived at the same rule in Europe. Thabit also stated a generalization of the Pythagorean theorem for triangles in which certain angles are equal, and he found the area of a parabolic segment by a method in Archimedes' tradition of exhaustion. That quadrature is not a theory of infinitesimals. A treatise on magic squares has been listed under his name in a later bibliography. It is not in al-Nadim's list, and no text survives. He attempted a proof of Euclid's parallel postulate by an argument from motion. Ibn al-Haytham, Umar al-Khayyam and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi later offered proofs of their own. They were trying to secure the postulate, not to build a non-Euclidean geometry.

In astronomy, a theory of the trepidation of the equinoxes, an advance and recession (*iqbal wa-idbar*) rather than a steady precession, was ascribed to him. The Latin treatise *De motu octavae sphaerae* circulated under his name; whether that particular text is his is disputed. He wrote on the length of the solar year and on sundials. In mechanics, his book on the *qarastun*, the balance with unequal arms, sets out the lever in geometric form and passed into Latin as *Liber karastonis*. Works on logic, the soul and the Sabians of Harran are attributed to him. Several of the philosophical and medical titles are disputed.

## Family and Legacy

Thabit was the head of a scientific family. His son Sinan ibn Thabit was a physician and astronomer who headed the physicians of Baghdad. His grandson Ibrahim ibn Sinan (d. 335 AH / 946 CE) gave a shorter proof of the quadrature of the parabola, a problem Thabit had treated, and another grandson, Thabit ibn Sinan, wrote a history. Thus the Sabian family carried Greek science across several generations in Baghdad, and its retention of its religion played a role in this, since it came from a Harranian astronomical tradition.

## Caveats

Much of the detail of his life is transmitted by Ibn Abi Usaybi'a and Ibn al-Qifti, who wrote more than three centuries later, and with this comes a risk of legendary embellishment. The attribution of some treatises to him is disputed, and some survive only in a Latin or Hebrew translation. He should also not be overstated as a forerunner of integral calculus or of non-Euclidean geometry: he did not intend this, and he was dealing with problems of Archimedes and Euclid within the framework of his age.
