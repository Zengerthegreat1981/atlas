# Thabit ibn Qurra

Abu al-Hasan Thabit ibn Qurra ibn Marwan al-Harrani al-Sabi' (died in Baghdad in 288 AH/901 CE) was a mathematician, astronomer, physician and translator, and one of the leading scholars of ninth-century Baghdad. He was one of the few who combined precise translation with original research in geometry and astronomy. Sources differ on his year of birth: most give 211 AH/826 CE, while others give 221 AH/836 CE, so his birth is placed approximately between 826 and 836 CE.

## Origins and Formation

He was born in Harran in northern Upper Mesopotamia, one of the last centres of the ancient astral paganism known in Arabic sources as the Sabians. It preserved a Hermetic and Greek heritage and the Syriac language. Biographical dictionaries such as Ibn Abi Usaybi'a's *'Uyun al-Anba'* and Ibn al-Qifti's *Ta'rikh al-Hukama'* state that he was a money-changer, and that Muhammad ibn Musa ibn Shakir, one of the Banu Musa, met him at Kafartutha, admired his learning and language, took him to Baghdad and admitted him into his circle. Baghdad at that time was the home of translation. Thabit worked in the circle of the Banu Musa and alongside the translators of Hunayn ibn Ishaq's generation, became one of the circle of astronomers, and then one of those close to the Caliph al-Mu'tadid (r. 892–902 CE), for whom he wrote on astronomy and on the verification of observations. He wrote in Syriac and Arabic. Many dozens of works are attributed to him in the catalogue of Ibn al-Nadim and other sources, of which only a part survives.

## Translation and Revision

Thabit was not only a translator but also an editor of the translations of his predecessors. He revised Ishaq ibn Hunayn's translation of Euclid's *Elements*, revised translations of works by Archimedes and Ptolemy, and revised the translation of the first four books of Apollonius's *Conics* while translating books five to seven, whose Greek original is lost and which survive only in Arabic. These works show that translation was not a literal transfer but a scientific activity that corrected, added to and reorganized proofs. This explains the depth of his school's influence on later scholars such as Ibn al-Haytham and al-Biruni.

## Original Contributions

In mathematics, Thabit formulated a rule for generating amicable numbers: if b = 3·2^n − 1, c = 3·2^(n−1) − 1 and d = 9·2^(2n−1) − 1 are all prime, then the two numbers 2^n·b·c and 2^n·d are amicable. When n = 2 the rule yields the pair 220 and 284. The rule was rediscovered after him by al-Farisi, Ibn al-Banna', Fermat and Descartes. He generalized the theorem of Pythagoras to all triangles. He linked the calculation of the area of the parabolic segment, and the volumes of the solids generated from it, to Archimedes' method of exhaustion, using partial sums that foreshadow integration, a work that historians have regarded as a step toward the calculation of infinitesimals. He also wrote on magic squares and on the postulate of parallels, attempting to prove it through the motion of lines, an attempt that Ibn al-Haytham and 'Umar al-Khayyam would repeat.

In astronomy he adopted the theory of trepidation (*al-ihtizaz*, or the advance and recession, *al-iqbal wa-l-idbar*) to explain the change in the position of the equinoxes, holding that the sphere of the fixed stars moves with a double motion. This theory lived on in Islamic astronomy and then in Latin Europe for centuries. He determined the length of the sidereal year with an accuracy close to that reached by modern scientists, and he wrote on sundials. In mechanics he wrote a book on the *qarastun*, the balance with two arms of unequal length, in which he applied the principle of moments to the lever in geometric form. In philosophy and medicine he wrote on logic, on the soul and on the Sabians, and he translated and criticized.

## Family and Legacy

Thabit was the head of a scientific family. His son Sinan ibn Thabit was a physician and astronomer who headed the physicians of Baghdad. His grandson Ibrahim ibn Sinan (d. 946 CE) was a mathematician who completed his grandfather's research on the quadrature of the parabola with a simpler proof, and another grandson, Thabit ibn Sinan, wrote a history. Thus the Sabian family carried Greek science across several generations in Baghdad, and its retention of its religion played a role in this, since it came from a Harranian astronomical tradition.

## Caveats

Much of the detail of his life is transmitted by Ibn Abi Usaybi'a and Ibn al-Qifti, who wrote more than three centuries later, and with this comes a risk of legendary embellishment. The attribution of some treatises to him is disputed, and some survive only in a Latin or Hebrew translation. He should also not be overstated as a forerunner of integral calculus or of non-Euclidean geometry: he did not intend this, and he was dealing with problems of Archimedes and Euclid within the framework of his age.
