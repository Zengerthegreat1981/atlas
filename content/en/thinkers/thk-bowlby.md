# John Bowlby

A British psychiatrist, the pioneer of attachment theory, and one of the most important theorists in developmental psychology in the twentieth century. His work is regarded as the bridge between psychoanalysis (Freud), developmental psychology (Piaget), and evolutionary/adaptive psychology (Darwin, Lorenz).

## What He Gave

### First Phase: From Psychoanalysis to Evolutionary Psychology (the 1930s to the 1950s)

Bowlby began his career as a classical psychoanalyst. He was influenced by Melanie Klein, who supervised his analytic training in the 1930s. But his work at a London child guidance clinic, his study of juvenile thieves, and his observation of children separated from their mothers in the Second World War led him to doubt the classical analytic emphasis on fantasy over real experience as the source of childhood anxiety.

In 1951 the World Health Organization published his report *Maternal Care and Mental Health*, prepared at its request on the mental health of homeless children. Bowlby concluded that **prolonged deprivation of maternal care in early childhood** can cause lasting psychological harm, and that the young child needs a **warm, intimate and continuous relationship** with a mother figure.

### Second Phase: Attachment Theory (1958-69)

In his foundational work **"The Nature of the Child's Tie to His Mother" (1958)**, Bowlby formulated his theory of attachment:
- Attachment is a **biological drive** to seek proximity to a caregiver.
- Its goal is **survival**, not sexual gratification as Freud said.
- Attachment behaviours (such as crying, clinging and following) form a behavioral system that is activated by threat and works in balance with exploration.

In the trilogy **"Attachment and Loss" (1969, 1973, 1980)**, he developed:
- **The phases of the young child's response to separation** (Protest, Despair, Detachment), and in the third volume his account of mourning in adults and children.
- **The Internal Working Model**: a mental representation of relationships that settles in the early years and affects relationships in adulthood.
- **The Secure Base**: the caregiver from whom the child sets out to explore the world and to whom the child returns in danger.

### Third Phase: The Evolutionary Basis and Late Clinical Work (the 1980s)

Bowlby expanded to integrate his theory in a deeper evolutionary framework, and gathered its therapeutic applications:
- **Monotropy**: a term he used from his 1958 paper to describe the child's tendency toward one principal attachment to a single person, usually the mother, followed by secondary attachments; he later acknowledged that children form several attachments, though often with one principal figure.
- **The environment of evolutionary adaptedness**: he linked the function of attachment to protection from predators in the conditions of early humans, so the attachment system became for him a behavioral control system like those described by cybernetic research.
- **Loss versus deprivation**: loss (death) has a different effect from deprivation (the absence of a stable caregiver).
- **Therapeutic application**: in *A Secure Base* (1988) he presented the therapist's function as being a secure base from which the patient explores and revises his internal working models.

## Biography and Formation

Bowlby was born in London in 1907 to an upper-class family, studied at Cambridge and then medicine at University College Hospital in London, and qualified in psychoanalysis at the British Psychoanalytical Society (1937); his analyst was Joan Riviere and his supervisor Melanie Klein. At the London Child Guidance Clinic he noticed that the children he studied in his paper on juvenile thieves (published in 1944 as *Forty-four Juvenile Thieves*) often had a history of early, repeated separation from the mother. After the war he joined the Tavistock Clinic, where he set up a research unit on the effects of separation, and collaborated with James Robertson, who filmed *A Two-Year-Old Goes to Hospital* (1952). Bowlby died on Skye, Scotland, in 1990.

## Impact and Criticism

The 1951 report and the book *Child Care and the Growth of Love* (1953) helped change the practices of hospitals and orphanages and allowed visits by parents. The first point of criticism was Michael Rutter's review *Maternal Deprivation Reassessed* (1972), which distinguished between maternal deprivation, lack of attachment and family disruption, and showed that the effect of separation depends on context. Feminist critics then charged that the theory had been used to justify calls for mothers to stay at home, a use that went beyond what the evidence showed. Cross-cultural studies (van IJzendoorn and Sagi-Schwartz, 2008) showed that the concept of secure attachment is common but the distribution of patterns varies.

## His Place in the Movement

- **Versus Freud**: Bowlby challenged the classical psychoanalytic account of childhood anxiety. He said that the child's anxiety is about separation from the attachment figure, not about sexual repression. The relationship is with a real mother, not with an internal object.
- **Versus Klein**: he differed with Klein over the focus on the child's "inner world." Bowlby focused on the "outer world" (real relationships).
- **Versus Harlow (Harry Harlow)**: in monkey research, Harlow (1958) showed that monkeys prefer the "cloth mother" (warmth) to the "wire mother" (food). Bowlby cited Harlow often as empirical evidence.
- **Versus Ainsworth**: Bowlby set out the theory, and Ainsworth, who worked with him at the Tavistock in the early 1950s, tested it empirically by devising the "Strange Situation" and classified patterns of attachment in infants.

## His Most Important Works

- **"Maternal Care and Mental Health" (WHO 1951)**: his influential report for the World Health Organization, which changed the concept of care.
- **"The Nature of the Child's Tie to His Mother" (1958)**: presented his theory of attachment.
- **"Attachment" (1969)**: the first volume of his trilogy.
- **"Separation: Anxiety and Anger" (1973)**: the second volume.
- **"Loss: Sadness and Depression" (1980)**: the third volume.
- **"A Secure Base" (1988)**: his late clinical research.
