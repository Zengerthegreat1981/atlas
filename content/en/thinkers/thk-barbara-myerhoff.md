# Barbara Myerhoff: Ritual, Aging, and Self-Creation

Barbara Myerhoff (1935–1985) was an American anthropologist known for her work on ritual, storytelling, aging, and Jewish identity. Her best-known book, *Number Our Days* (1978), is an ethnography of elderly Jewish immigrants at a senior centre in Venice, California. She argued that ritual is not only a means of reproducing social order but also a practice through which people actively make and remake their identities, and she developed the concept of the "definitional ceremony." She died of cancer at the age of 49.

## Biographical and Intellectual Context

Myerhoff was born in Cleveland, Ohio, in 1935 and grew up in Los Angeles. She studied at the University of California, Los Angeles, where she completed her doctorate in anthropology in 1968, after a master's degree in human development at the University of Chicago. She taught anthropology at the University of Southern California from the late 1960s until her death, and chaired the department from 1976 to 1980. She worked with filmmakers and folklorists and wrote for readers outside the university. Her early fieldwork, in the 1960s, was among the Huichol (Wixárika) of Mexico, and was published as *Peyote Hunt* (1974). From the mid-1970s she turned to an urban, domestic setting, working with elderly, mostly Yiddish-speaking Jewish immigrants in Venice, California. She was influenced by the symbolic anthropology of Victor Turner and by Clifford Geertz-style interpretive approaches, and she collaborated with Sally Falk Moore on the volumes *Symbol and Politics in Communal Ideology* (1975) and *Secular Ritual* (1977). She was diagnosed with cancer in 1984 and died in January 1985.

## Core Ideas

### Ritual as self-creation

Myerhoff shifted attention from what ritual expresses to what it does for participants. Among the elderly people she studied, ritual practices, storytelling, and communal gatherings were ways of constructing a Jewish identity in the present rather than preserving a fixed inheritance. She treated this as an instance of a wider human capacity for reflexive self-definition.

### Definitional ceremonies

Myerhoff introduced the notion of the definitional ceremony: a collective performance through which a group that is marginal or poorly recognised tells itself and others who it is. Such ceremonies depend on being witnessed, since recognition by an audience helps stabilise the identity they assert. She described occasions at the senior centre, including festivals and the community's own celebrations, as ways in which the elderly asserted their dignity and continuity in a society that often overlooked them. One example is a public march organised after a member was fatally injured. She treated re-membering as a purposeful gathering of the people and episodes that belong to a life, rather than as passive recollection. Narrative therapists, notably Michael White, later took up both ideas.

### Aging, memory, and liminality

Her work on aging drew on Turner's idea of liminality but suggested that old age in a youth-oriented society can resemble a prolonged margin rather than a passage with a clear outcome. She was also interested in how life stories are told and retold to give coherence to the past. She spoke of "remembering" as an act of re-membering, a gathering together of the self and community, and of collective storytelling as a form of ritual work.

### Reflexive ethnography

Myerhoff was explicit about her own presence in the field: in *Number Our Days* she described the relationship between herself, as a younger woman and anthropologist, and the elderly informants, and she reflected on how her own mortality and family history shaped the research. Her work is often cited as an early example of reflexive ethnography and of attention to performance and narrative.

## Major Works

- **Peyote Hunt: The Sacred Journey of the Huichol Indians** (1974): an ethnography of the Huichol peyote pilgrimage, interpreted as a symbolic return to an ancestral place and a reversal of everyday order. Working with Peter Furst and the ritual specialist Ramón Medina Silva, she took part in a pilgrimage to Wirikuta, where peyote is gathered. The book analyses the linked symbols of deer, maize, and peyote. Later scholars have asked how far one closely involved group and its leading narrator can stand for Huichol practice.
- **Number Our Days** (1978): her study of the elderly Jewish community at a Venice, California senior centre, drawing on field research and life-history interviews. Many members of the Israel Levin Senior Adult Center had emigrated from Eastern Europe and had outlived the world of their childhood, including relatives killed in the Holocaust. Poverty, distance from children, and public inattention marked their old age. The book includes classes, disputes, and celebrations, and treats quarrels as part of social life. Some conversations are reconstructed, including an imagined continuing dialogue with a member who had died. A short documentary film also called *Number Our Days*, directed by Lynne Littman and inspired by her work, won an Academy Award in 1977.
- **Secular Ritual** (1977, edited with Sally Falk Moore): a volume arguing that ritual form can give an appearance of order and continuity to matters that are uncertain, and that it need not involve religion.
- **A Crack in the Mirror: Reflexive Perspectives in Anthropology** (1982, edited by Jay Ruby): Myerhoff and Ruby distinguish deliberate attention to how knowledge is made from mere self-absorption.
- **Remembered Lives: The Work of Ritual, Storytelling, and Growing Older** (1992, posthumous): a collection of essays edited by Marc Kaminsky.
- **In Her Own Time** (1985), a film made with Lynne Littman, records late research on Orthodox Jewish life in the Fairfax neighbourhood of Los Angeles, undertaken while she was ill, and her search for support.

## Influence and Debates

Myerhoff helped establish the study of aging as a subject for anthropology and was an early advocate of anthropology at home, in contemporary American life. Her concept of definitional ceremonies continues to be used in narrative studies, performance studies, and therapy and social work practice. Critics have noted that her emphasis on creative self-making may underplay the constraints, hierarchies, and conflicts within ritual communities, and that a study of one small community may not generalise widely. Readers have also asked how editing and reconstruction shape voices presented with great immediacy, and whether creative self-definition is given more weight than material hardship. Her accounts of the Huichol, like other ethnography of that period, have also been read in the light of later debates about representation and authority.
