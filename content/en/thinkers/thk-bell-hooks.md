# bell hooks

bell hooks was the pen name of Gloria Jean Watkins (1952-2021), an American feminist theorist, cultural critic and teacher. Her writing examined how race, gender and class operate together, and it is widely read in sociology, education, and gender and cultural studies.

## Life and Context

hooks was born in Hopkinsville, Kentucky, and attended segregated and then integrated schools. She took her first degree at Stanford University, a master's degree at the University of Wisconsin-Madison and a doctorate in literature at the University of California, Santa Cruz, with research on Toni Morrison. She taught at institutions including Yale University, Oberlin College and the City College of New York, and returned to Kentucky in the 2000s to join Berea College. Her pen name honours a great-grandmother, and she wrote it in lower case to direct attention to the work. She died in Berea in 2021.

## Main Ideas

- **Race, sex and class together.** *Ain't I a Woman* (1981) traced the position of Black women from slavery onward and criticized both a women's movement centred on white experience and Black politics centred on men. She often named the joint system "imperialist white-supremacist capitalist patriarchy."
- **Feminism as a movement against sexist oppression.** In *Feminist Theory: From Margin to Center* (1984) she rejected a definition of feminism as equality with men, since men differ greatly in status, and defined it as a struggle to end sexism, sexual exploitation and oppression.
- **The margin.** Marginal positions can provide a critical view of both margin and centre and a space of resistance.
- **Representation and the gaze.** *Black Looks* (1992) analyzed images of Blackness and proposed an "oppositional gaze" for critical spectatorship, particularly by Black women.
- **Education as freedom.** *Teaching to Transgress* (1994), influenced by Paulo Freire and Thich Nhat Hanh, presents the classroom as a participatory community engaging the whole person.
- **Class and love.** Later books include *Where We Stand: Class Matters* (2000) and *All About Love* (2000), which treats love as a practice with ethical and political consequences.

## Method

She combined autobiography, close reading of films, books and popular culture, and social theory, deliberately using plain language and often omitting footnotes so as to reach readers beyond universities.

## Case Studies

Two examples show how hooks worked from concrete material. The essay on the oppositional gaze, in *Black Looks*, begins from histories of prohibited looking: under slavery and racial domination, Black people could be punished for how they looked at those in power. hooks then considered Black women as film spectators. Mainstream cinema either left them out or showed them through demeaning types, and she argued that many responded by looking critically, refusing to identify with the images offered. Drawing partly on conversations with Black women, she also questioned feminist film theory that analysed sexual difference while neglecting race, and she discussed independent films by Black women as places where other ways of seeing were being built. In *Teaching to Transgress*, her own schooling supplies a case. In segregated schools, Black teachers treated learning as part of resistance to racism and took an interest in pupils' whole lives; after desegregation, she encountered classrooms where obedience mattered more and Black children's eagerness could be read as a threat. The contrast informs her argument for engaged teaching. It rests on recollection, so it illustrates an experience rather than measuring the effects of desegregation.

## Influence and Criticism

Her work is a reference point for Black feminist thought and critical pedagogy and is frequently discussed in relation to intersectional analysis. Critics have objected that her arguments rely heavily on personal observation, that documentation is thin and that some judgments on popular figures are polemical. A related objection is that broad terms such as patriarchy are not always precise enough for systematic or empirical use. Admirers regard the accessible style as part of her political method.

## Related Concepts

Intersectionality, standpoint, critical pedagogy; compare Patricia Hill Collins, Kimberlé Crenshaw and Paulo Freire.

## Contemporary Empirical Examples

Researchers have begun to treat engaged pedagogy as something that can be observed in classrooms, not only argued from memoir. Hilary E. Hughes, Rachel Ranschaert and Kelsey L. Benson report a qualitative case study in *Middle Grades Review* (2023) based on eight months of data from a Title I middle school in the southeastern United States during the COVID-19 period. They use hooks's 1994 account of engaged pedagogy to name what justice-oriented teachers were already doing. In their reading, that pedagogy means an interactive relationship in which teachers and students learn together; ongoing self-actualization, including work to identify and unlearn harmful dominant narratives about race and ethnicity, social class, disability and gender; and attention to the teacher's own well-being so that academic and social-emotional care for students remains possible. The authors present the study as illustrations of practice rather than a blueprint they claim will generalise. It is one school and one stretch of pandemic schooling, so it shows how the concept can organise field observation without settling how widely such teaching occurs.
