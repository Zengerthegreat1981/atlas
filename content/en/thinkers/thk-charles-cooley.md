# Charles Cooley

Charles Horton Cooley (1864-1929) was an American sociologist who helped found the study of the self as a social product. He is remembered for the concept of the "looking-glass self" and for his analysis of primary groups, and he is regarded as a forerunner of symbolic interactionism.

## Life and Context

Cooley was born in Ann Arbor, Michigan, and spent nearly his whole career at the University of Michigan, where he studied and then taught. He trained first in engineering and economics, and his doctoral work dealt with the theory of transportation. He turned to sociology and developed a psychological and organic view of society. He served as president of the American Sociological Society, later renamed the American Sociological Association, in 1918.

## Main Ideas

- Looking-glass self: our sense of self arises from imagining how others perceive us, how they judge that appearance, and the feeling that results, such as pride or shame.
- Primary groups: small, face-to-face groups such as family, play groups and neighbourhoods, where the "we-feeling" forms and individuals acquire basic social ideals.
- Organic unity of individual and society: person and society are aspects of one process, not opposed things.
- Sympathetic introspection: understanding social life requires imaginative insight into other people's thoughts and feelings.
- Communication as the basis of social organization.

## Method

Cooley favoured qualitative, interpretive methods, including observation of children and reflection on his own experience, in contrast to statistical approaches. He stressed understanding meaning over measuring behaviour.

## Case Studies

Cooley's closest observations concerned his own children. He noted how a young daughter came to use words such as *I*, *my*, and *mine*, and argued that their meaning could not be learned simply by imitation, since the same word refers to different people according to who speaks. The child connected these words with a feeling of appropriation and self-assertion, expressed for instance in claiming objects against others, and with attention to how others responded. He published a study of the child's early use of self-words in 1908. His observations also described children learning to influence adults by watching the effects of their behaviour. The family and the play group serve as his other principal cases: in face-to-face cooperation and rivalry, children encounter loyalty, fairness, and concern for others, which Cooley regarded as roots of wider social ideals. These materials are rich but narrow. They come from a particular family and milieu, were interpreted by a parent, and cannot by themselves show that development follows the same course everywhere.

## Contemporary Empirical Examples

King-To Yeung and John Levi Martin (2003) tested the looking-glass self using data on members of communal living groups in the United States. Because members rated themselves and one another, and were observed over time, the researchers could compare self-perceptions with others' perceptions. They found evidence consistent with people internalising how others saw them, and showed that the process depended on relations of authority and recognition within the group, rather than everyone's opinion counting equally. This is useful for separating three things that are easily confused: what others actually think, what a person believes they think, and how the person sees themselves. A match among them does not alone establish the direction of influence, since people may persuade others to accept their own self-image. The particular groups studied also limit generalisation to other settings.

## Related Concepts

Related concepts include the self, socialisation, primary and secondary groups, reflected appraisal, identity, role taking, sympathy, and interpretive understanding. Cooley is usefully compared with William James on the social self, George Herbert Mead on taking the role of the other, and Erving Goffman on impression management. James had already observed that a person has different social selves in relation to different groups; Mead gave a fuller account of communication and role taking and did not rest it on introspection; Goffman examined how impressions are staged in encounters. The primary group also connects Cooley with later research on families, peers, solidarity, and informal social control, and with the contrast between close personal ties and impersonal organisations.

## Influence and Criticism

His ideas strongly shaped George Herbert Mead, Herbert Blumer and later work on the self, socialization and identity. Critics point to the reliance on introspection, the vagueness of concepts like "mind" and "larger mind", and a harmonious picture of society that gives little attention to conflict and power.
