# Hafiz of Shiraz

Shams al-Din Muhammad Hafiz of Shiraz (c. 1320–1390 CE; the birth year is uncertain) is the greatest poet of the love lyric (ghazal) in Persian literature. His divan, in the standard Qazvini–Ghani edition, contains roughly five hundred ghazals, and it has become the most widely present of all Persian divans in homes and gatherings. Hafiz is not an Arab thinker, so this text does not belong to a direct Arabic tradition. Nevertheless, his poetry is built on the Quranic lexicon and the Sufi heritage that the Arabs first wrote, and it is read in the Islamic world as a link in the chain of spiritual love poetry that began with Rabi'a al-'Adawiyya, Ibn al-Farid and Ibn 'Arabi.

## Life and Political Context

Hafiz was born in Shiraz to a family of middling status, and he died there. He lived in a period when the Ilkhanid state was collapsing and local dynasties were competing for control of Fars. Shiraz was ruled by Abu Ishaq Inju (1343–1353), whom the poet praised, and his rule is remembered as relatively open. It was then ruled by Mubariz al-Din Muhammad al-Muzaffari (1353–1358), who closed the taverns and tightened religious supervision, and Hafiz criticized this severity with skill. It was later ruled by Shah Shuja' (1358–1384), whose relationship with the poet was volatile. In 1387 Timur entered Shiraz. A well-known anecdote tells that Timur summoned Hafiz to reproach him for a verse offering Samarqand and Bukhara in exchange for the mole of a Turkish beloved of Shiraz; scholars doubt the authenticity of this story. His honorific (laqab) indicates that he memorized the Quran with its recognized readings (qira'at), and he had a knowledge of Arabic and of exegesis that shows in the quotations he embedded in his verse.

## The Ghazal and Ambiguity

Muhammad Gulandam collected his divan after his death. Hafiz consolidated the form of the Persian ghazal, based on a set of independent couplets (bayt) held together by a single tone and rhyme. A ghazal usually runs from about five to fifteen couplets. In the last couplet the poet mentions his pen-name (takhallus), that is, the name Hafiz, so the final couplet becomes a signature and a commentary. He built his language on a set of symbols: wine, the cupbearer, the tavern, the magians (maghan), the rend (a free-spirited drinker), the censor (muhtasib) and the ascetic (zahid). All of these admit both a Sufi and a worldly reading, and this ambiguity is what kept the divan open to contradictory interpretations. A prominent feature of his work is his criticism of hypocritical ascetics and jurists, a criticism that recalls the Malamatiyya's stance against the display of piety, although it does not express a clear doctrinal position. His art is called *lisan al-ghayb* (the tongue of the unseen), and from it comes the custom of opening the divan for omens (fal), a custom that is still alive in Iran.

## Relation to the Arabic and Sufi Tradition

Hafiz's poetry draws on a lexicon shared by Persian and Arabic. Words such as 'ishq (love), rend, khirqa (the Sufi cloak), sama' (spiritual audition) and fana' (annihilation) and baqa' (subsistence) are of Sufi origin, and they were set out in the works of al-Qushayri, al-Hujwiri and al-Ghazali, and later systematised in the school of Ibn 'Arabi. In Hafiz, the idea of pure divine love is connected to an old line stretching back to Rabi'a al-'Adawiyya, although his language is more playful and ironic. The direct Arabic presence is slight: he sometimes embeds Arabic phrases and verses, but he did not compose in Arabic. Claims of a direct Arab influence or an early Arab circulation of his work should therefore not be made.

## Interpretation and Reception

Interpreters have disagreed about Hafiz continuously. Ottoman and Indian commentators, such as the Bosnian-born Ottoman commentator Sudi, read him as a gnostic poet ('arif). Other modern readers see him as a worldly poet who uses the Sufi symbol as a veil. The essays in *Hafiz and the Religion of Love in Classical Persian Poetry* (2010), edited by Leonard Lewisohn, argue that the Persian ghazal is woven into a religious and poetic tradition in which the sensual and the spiritual cannot be separated. In Europe, Joseph von Hammer-Purgstall translated him into German in 1812–1813, and this inspired Goethe's *West-östlicher Divan* (1819). Then Wilberforce Clarke published an English translation in 1891, followed by Arberry and Avery. These translations became part of his modern image, along with their simplification of the symbols.

## Caveats

Scholars do not possess a complete divan documented by a definitive critical edition. The manuscripts of the divan vary in the number and order of their ghazals. Muhammad Qazvini and Qasim Ghani edited a widely used version in the 1940s, but disagreement persisted over the couplets attributed to him. The poet's biography rests on references in his poetry and on later sources such as Dowlatshah's *Tazkirat al-Shu'ara*. His tomb in the Musalla of Shiraz, which has been rebuilt several times, most recently in the twentieth century, remains a popular destination where visitors read his couplets and open the divan for omens. One should also be cautious about treating every couplet as having a Sufi meaning, since some of the ghazals are genuine praise of princes and of worldly love.
