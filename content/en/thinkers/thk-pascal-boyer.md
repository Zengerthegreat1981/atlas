# Pascal Boyer

A French cognitive anthropologist and a leading figure in the cognitive science of religion. He began his training as a field anthropologist, doing fieldwork in Cameroon among the Fang. In the 1990s he shifted to the cognitive-evolutionary framework, attempting to explain why religious beliefs resemble one another across cultures that are geographically and historically distant, with no contact between them. He is a professor at Washington University in St. Louis, and his career has combined cognitive science with evolutionary anthropology.

## The Central Thesis: Belief as a Natural Cognitive Product

In his key work *Religion Explained* (2001), Boyer rejects traditional explanations of religion (a psychological need for comfort, the explanation of natural phenomena, and social control) as insufficient in explanatory terms. He proposes instead that religious beliefs spread because they are easy to remember and transmit cognitively. Religious beings are typically "minimally counterintuitive" concepts. They break only one expectation from their innate ontological category, for example a person who has a mind but passes through walls, while retaining the other properties of the category. They thus become attention-grabbing and easy to memorize and transmit orally, without being so random that they are hard to remember.

## Boyer and Ramble (2001)

Boyer tested this hypothesis experimentally together with Charles Ramble, through recall experiments with participants in France, Nepal and Gabon. Participants were asked to recall concepts of varying strangeness. The authors reported that concepts with a small number of counterintuitive features were recalled better than wholly ordinary ones, which they interpreted as cross-cultural support for the thesis.

## An Explanatory, Not Normative, Method

Boyer insists that his project is explanatory. It does not aim to refute or confirm the truth of religion, but to explain why it is widespread and persistent across cultures as a natural human phenomenon, drawing on evolutionary psychology and cognitive neuroscience. He shares this position with colleagues in the field, such as Justin Barrett and Scott Atran, though he differs from them in the details of the presumed cognitive mechanism. While Barrett focuses on agency detection systems, Boyer focuses on the structure of the innate ontological categories themselves, some of whose expectations are violated in the religious concept.

## His Relation to His Colleagues in the Cognitive Science of Religion

Together with Justin Barrett, Scott Atran and Harvey Whitehouse, Boyer is counted among the central figures of the cognitive science of religion as an academic field distinct from the traditional anthropology of religion and classical psychology of religion.

## His Critique of Traditional Functional Explanations of Religion

Boyer devotes a large part of his argument to dismantling the classical functional explanations of religion that dominated the social sciences throughout the twentieth century. Among these are Durkheim's account, which links religion to social cohesion, and Marx's account, which sees it as an ideological tool of class sedation. He argues that these explanations, though they contain partial truths, fail to explain the more precise question: why do religious beliefs take specific forms (supernatural beings with particular attributes) rather than the other forms that are theoretically unlimited?

## His Early Field Work in Cameroon

Before turning to cognitive theory, Boyer spent years in anthropological fieldwork among the Fang people of Cameroon, studying their oral traditions and local religious practice in a classical ethnographic manner, notably in *Tradition as Truth and Communication* (1990). This fieldwork gave him an empirical background for his later cognitive theory.

## Influence

Boyer's work helped advance the cognitive-explanatory approach as an alternative to functional and interpretive approaches in the study of religion.

## Writing for a General Audience

Boyer wrote *Religion Explained* in a clear style aimed at an educated general audience, not only at specialists. The book reached a wide readership and brought these ideas into public debates about the origins of religion.
