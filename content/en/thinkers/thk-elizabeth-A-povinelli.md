# Elizabeth A. Povinelli

Elizabeth A. Povinelli (born 1962) is an American anthropologist, critical theorist, and filmmaker, and Franz Boas Professor of Anthropology and Gender Studies at Columbia University. Her work, grounded in long-term collaboration with Aboriginal communities in Australia's Northern Territory, is a sustained attempt to think anthropology, critical theory, and Indigenous politics together. From *The Cunning of Recognition* (2002) to *Geontologies: A Requiem to Late Liberalism* (2016), she has argued that liberal multiculturalism, for all its promises of recognition, continues the work of settler colonialism, and she has developed an "anthropology of the otherwise" — a way of thinking about worlds that refuse liberalism's terms.

## Life and Career

Povinelli was born in 1962 in the United States. She took her PhD in anthropology from Yale University in 1991. She began working with the Belyuen community in the Northern Territory of Australia early in her career, a relationship that has lasted for decades and that underpins all her major work. She is a founding member of the Karrabing Film Collective, an Indigenous media group based in the Northern Territory whose films explore the conditions of inequality faced by Aboriginal people, and she has directed the Institute for Research on Women, Gender, and Sexuality at Columbia and co-directed the Centre for the Study of Law and Culture. She has also been an editor of the journal *Public Culture*.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

*The Cunning of Recognition* (2002) is Povinelli's most influential book. She argued that Australian multiculturalism, which promised to recognise Indigenous difference, in fact demanded that Aboriginal people perform a particular kind of authentic alterity — that they be recognisably "traditional" — in order to be granted rights. The state, she showed, recognised Indigenous people only insofar as they conformed to a liberal fantasy of what an Indigenous person should be, and it withdrew recognition when they did not. The "cunning" of recognition was that it presented itself as generosity while functioning as a mechanism of control, and the book became a central text in the study of settler colonialism and liberal governmentality.

*Economies of Abandonment* (2011) extended the argument to the politics of endurance. Drawing on her work with the Belyuen community, Povinelli examined how liberal societies decide which lives are worth sustaining and which can be abandoned — left to die slowly, through neglect, poverty, and environmental harm. She contrasted the liberal language of crisis, in which some events demand urgent action, with the slow, ordinary suffering of those who live in the "durative present" of late liberalism, a present that never quite becomes a crisis and therefore never quite demands a response.

In *Geontologies* (2016) she turned to the distinction between Life and Nonlife — between the biological and the geological — that underpins liberal governance. She argued that late liberalism governs by deciding what counts as alive and therefore as having rights, and what counts as mere matter, available for extraction. Against this "geontology," she proposed an attention to the forms of existence — ancestral, geological, climatic — that liberalism cannot recognise, and to the Indigenous philosophies in which rocks, rivers, and ancestors are not objects but relations. *Between Gaia and Ground* (2021) continued this work, arguing that the climate catastrophe is not a future event but an ancestral one, already lived by Indigenous peoples whose worlds were destroyed by colonisation.

## Influence and Critique

Povinelli's influence extends across anthropology, Indigenous studies, critical theory, and the arts. Her concept of the "cunning of recognition" has been taken up far beyond Australia, in studies of multiculturalism in Canada, the United States, and Europe, and her work on geontologies has shaped debates in the environmental humanities and in the anthropology of the Anthropocene. The Karrabing Film Collective, of which she is a member, has brought her ideas to an international audience through films shown at film festivals and in galleries and museums internationally.

Critics have raised two main questions. Some anthropologists have asked whether her theoretical vocabulary — late liberalism, geontologies, the otherwise — risks obscuring the ethnographic detail on which it rests, and whether the people she writes about would recognise themselves in it. Others have questioned the politics of her position as a non-Indigenous scholar theorising Indigenous worlds, and whether the "anthropology of the otherwise" can avoid reproducing the very appropriation it criticises. Her work stresses its own collaborative character — the Karrabing films are made collectively, not by her alone — and treats theory as a matter of thinking from within shared relations and obligations.
