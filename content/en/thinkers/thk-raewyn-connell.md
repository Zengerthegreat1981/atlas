# Raewyn Connell

Raewyn Connell (born 1944) is an Australian sociologist known for her work on gender, masculinities, class, education and the global geography of social science. She is the originator of the concept of hegemonic masculinity.

## Life and Context

Connell was born in Sydney and was known as Robert William (Bob) Connell until she transitioned in the 2000s. She worked for many years at the University of Sydney, after earlier posts at other Australian universities, and has held visiting positions elsewhere. Her early research dealt with class, schooling and politics in Australia, and she was active in left-wing and educational movements.

## Main Ideas

- **Gender as social structure.** In *Gender and Power* (1987), she analysed gender as a structure involving power relations, the division of labour and emotional relations (cathexis).
- **Masculinities and hegemonic masculinity.** In *Masculinities* (1995), she argued that there are multiple masculinities in a hierarchy, with a dominant, culturally honoured form that supports men's overall dominance over women, while other forms are subordinated or marginalised.
- **Southern theory.** In *Southern Theory* (2007), she argued that mainstream sociology draws its concepts from the experience of the global North and ignores intellectual work from colonised and post-colonial regions, such as Africa, Latin America, South Asia and Indigenous Australia.
- **Social justice in education.** She studied how schools reproduce inequality and argued for curricula built from the standpoint of the least advantaged.

## Method

Connell has used life-history interviews, institutional analysis and historical sociology, and has argued for a reflexive approach to how knowledge is produced.

## Influence and Criticism

Hegemonic masculinity became one of the most used concepts in gender studies. In "Hegemonic Masculinity: Rethinking the Concept" (2005, with James Messerschmidt), the authors replied to critics and revised the idea. Critics argued that the concept was too static, risked treating types as fixed character traits, and was used loosely. Southern Theory has been praised for stimulating debate on decolonising social science, though some have asked whether it risks treating the "South" as uniform.
