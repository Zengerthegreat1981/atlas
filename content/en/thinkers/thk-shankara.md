# Shankara (Advaita Vedanta)

An Indian philosopher and teacher, the best-known representative of "Advaita Vedanta" (non-dualism), and one of the most influential figures in the history of Indian thought. Hindu tradition attributes to him the founding of four major monastic seats ("mathas") at the edges of India, and the commentary on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras and the Bhagavad Gita, by which he made of them a single philosophical structure revolving around the idea that ultimate reality is one. He is a central reference point in classical Indian philosophy, for the followers of his school and its opponents alike.

## His life and history

Tradition places his birth in 788 and his death in 820 CE, in the village of Kaladi in the state of Kerala in southern India, but modern studies consider it more likely that he lived in the eighth century CE, about 700–750 CE. Traditional biographical writings such as the "Shankara Digvijaya", attributed to Madhava (Vidyaranya) and Anandagiri, are centuries later (roughly the fourteenth century, and their attributions are themselves doubtful), and so are not relied on as a trustworthy historical source, and many of the reports of his journeys and debates are legendary. Traditions mention that he studied under Govinda Bhagavatpada, a student of Gaudapada, author of the "Mandukya Karika", which influenced his formulation. It is told that he debated a thinker of the Mimamsa school, Mandana Mishra, and convinced him, and that he argued with Buddhists and other followers of the sects.

## What he contributed

**Brahman alone is the truth.** Ultimate reality is "Brahman", the absolute indivisible principle, and the "self" (atman) in the depths of every being is Brahman itself. The slogan "Brahman is real, the world is false" is a later summary of the school and is traditionally ascribed to him through works whose authorship is uncertain; it should not be treated as a quotation from his commentaries. The world for him is not nothingness but is what appears because of "maya", that is, primal ignorance (avidya), which confuses the self with the non-self.

**Superimposition (adhyasa).** In the introduction to his commentary on the "Brahma Sutra" he defines ignorance as "superimposition" of the attributes of one thing on another, as nacre is mistaken for silver (the rope-and-snake example belongs to the later tradition). This superimposition is not removed by good works or by ritual but by direct knowledge that realizes the unity of the self and the Absolute.

**Two levels of reality.** Shankara distinguishes between the perspective of "absolute truth" (paramarthika), which is Brahman, and the perspective of "practical life" (vyavaharika), in which the world, experience, ethics and ritual are valid. Later followers added a third level, the "apparent illusory" (pratibhasika) of dreams and individual illusions, and for this reason the full threefold division should not be attributed to Shankara himself.

**Knowledge is the path of liberation.** Direct knowledge of the self (jnana) alone liberates (moksha), not action or ritual. The Upanishadic "great sayings" such as "That thou art" (tat tvam asi) are the means of teaching, received by the student from a teacher and meditated upon until they are disclosed.

## His writings

The most authentic of what is attributed to him are his commentaries (bhashya) on the "Brahma Sutra" and on the major Upanishads (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Isha, Kena, Katha, Mundaka and Prashna) and on the "Mandukya Karika", and his commentary on the Bhagavad Gita. The "Upadeshasahasri" (A Thousand Teachings) is also counted among his most authentic independent works according to the modern textual study of Paul Hacker and Sengaku Mayeda. As for the "Vivekachudamani" (Crest-Jewel of Discrimination), its attribution to him is not certain, and likewise the "Atma Bodha" (Knowledge of the Self) is disputed, and both books may have been written by his followers.

## His position in the tradition

He is considered in Hindu tradition the "renewer" who unified the Upanishadic texts into a coherent system, who built a series of monastic seats that still preserve his teachings, and who set a pattern for Indian philosophical debate against Buddhism and Mimamsa. Five centuries later Madhva (Dvaita, thirteenth century) came in opposition to him, defending a dualism of God, soul and world. The presence of Upanishadic thought in the Islamic world has its own history: Abu al-Rayhan al-Biruni in his book on India (about 1030 CE) compared the Indian conception of the union of the soul with the Absolute with the conceptions of the Sufis, and the Mughal prince Dara Shikoh translated fifty Upanishads into Persian in the "Sirr-i Akbar" (1657), from which Anquetil-Duperron derived his Latin translation, in which Schopenhauer read the Upanishads.

## Limits and critique

The precise dates of his life are disputed, and in the tradition they are built on later biographies. A large part of the works attributed to him was perhaps written by his students. Ramanuja (eleventh century) criticized the theory of maya, saying that it abolishes the connection between God and beings and makes ignorance reside in Brahman itself, and Madhva criticized it as denying the reality of the world and the truth of differentiation, while some followers of other sects accused him of being a "crypto-Buddhist" for holding to an apparent world with no reality. Scholars note that the source of knowledge for him is the Upanishadic text heard from the teacher, so he does not make reason alone a path to certainty. His works have not been transmitted into Arabic in a unified translation to date.

## Major works

- *Brahma Sutra Bhashya* (commentary on the Brahma Sutra).
- *Bhashya on Principal Upanishads* (commentaries on the principal Upanishads).
- *Bhashya on Bhagavad Gita* (commentary on the Gita).
- *Upadesha Sahasri* (A Thousand Teachings).
- *Vivekachudamani* (Crest-Jewel of Discrimination, attributed to him) and *Atma Bodha* (Knowledge of the Self, attributed to him).
