# Terence Turner

Terence Turner (1935–2015) was an American anthropologist best known for his decades of ethnographic work among the Kayapo — who call themselves the Mebêngôkre — of the Brazilian Amazon, and for his transformation of that ethnography into a sustained campaign for Indigenous rights. He was a theorist of the social construction of the body, a critic of structuralism, and one of the first anthropologists to put video cameras in the hands of the people he studied, turning documentation into a political weapon. His career joined scholarship and activism more closely than almost any other in his generation.

## Life and Career

Turner was born in 1935 in the United States. He studied at Harvard and took his doctorate in social anthropology there, working with David Maybury-Lewis and the Harvard Central Brazil project. His thesis, *Social Structure and Political Organization among the Northern Cayapo* (1966), was based on fieldwork among the Kayapo in the early 1960s.

He taught at the University of Chicago and at Cornell University, where he became professor emeritus. He died on 7 November 2015, at the age of seventy-nine.

## Key Works

Turner's early work was a study of Kayapo social organisation: how villages were structured, how chiefship worked, and how kinship and ritual produced political authority. His essay "The Social Skin" (1980) argued that the body is not a natural given but a social surface — painted, ornamented, and shaped — through which persons are made and social relations displayed. Among the Kayapo, body painting, lip plugs, and ear discs were not decoration but the visible language of social identity.

In "Ethno-Ethnohistory: Myth and History in Native South American Representations of Contact with Western Society" (1988), he examined how the Kayapo narrated their own history of contact with Brazilian society, treating myth and history not as opposed genres but as different ways of making sense of the past. "Representing, Resisting, Rethinking: Historical Transformations of Kayapo Culture and Anthropological Consciousness" (1991) traced how Kayapo culture changed through contact and how anthropological understanding of that change had to change with it. His essay "Social Body and Embodied Subject: Bodiliness, Subjectivity, and Sociality among the Kayapo" (1995) developed his theory of the body into a full account of personhood, arguing that the Kayapo self is formed through bodily practice and social interaction rather than given in advance.

## Main Ideas: The Body, History, and Rights

Turner's anthropology rested on a single conviction: that social life is made, not given, and that it is made through the body, through history, and through political struggle. His theory of the body rejected the idea that the person is a fixed inner essence wearing a cultural costume; instead, the body itself — its adornments, its movements, its disciplines — is the medium through which social persons are produced. This work influenced the anthropology of embodiment and the study of personhood across the discipline.

His historical work insisted that Indigenous peoples are not timeless survivals of a pre-modern past but historical actors who interpret, resist, and reshape their encounters with the state. The Kayapo, in his account, were not passive victims of Brazilian expansion but strategists who used myth, ritual, and political organisation to defend their territory. This view shaped a generation of Amazonian anthropology, which moved away from static structural models toward the study of history, agency, and inter-ethnic relations.

His activism followed from his ethnography. From the 1970s onward Turner acted as a spokesman for Kayapo rights, helping to organise their political mobilisation against dams, mining, and deforestation. From the late 1980s he supported the Kayapo Video Project, which trained Kayapo people to document illegal logging, land grabbing, and other threats to their territory, and to use the footage in national and international campaigns. The project became a model for Indigenous media activism worldwide.

## Influence and Critique

Turner's influence runs in two directions. As a theorist, his work on the social body, on the construction of personhood, and on the historicity of Indigenous societies shaped Amazonian anthropology and the anthropology of the body, and his critique of structuralism helped push the discipline toward the study of practice, history, and agency. As an activist, he helped establish the model of the anthropologist as advocate — a role now widely accepted but controversial when he adopted it.

Critics raised questions that still attend engaged anthropology. Some argued that his political commitments compromised his ethnographic objectivity, and that his portrait of Kayapo society emphasised resistance and coherence at the expense of internal division. Others questioned whether the Kayapo Video Project, for all its power, placed the anthropologist in the role of broker between Indigenous communities and global audiences in ways that reproduced older dependencies. Turner himself answered that neutrality in the face of dispossession was itself a political choice, and that the anthropologist's responsibility to the people studied outweighed the discipline's claim to detachment.

His engagement with the Kayapo, extending over decades, made his one of the longest continuous ethnographic relationships in the history of the discipline.
