# Simone de Beauvoir

A French philosopher, novelist and essayist (1908–1986), long presented as a companion of Sartre but now widely recognised as an original thinker in her own right. Her ethics of ambiguity and her analysis of woman's situation in *The Second Sex* are influential contributions to existentialist thought, and they offer a significant reply to the common charge that the school is individualist and blind to power.

## Life and Context

Born in Paris in 1908, she studied philosophy at the Sorbonne and in 1929 placed second to Jean-Paul Sartre in the agrégation in philosophy, the youngest candidate to have passed it at that time. She taught in lycées in Marseille, Rouen and Paris until 1943, when she was dismissed, and thereafter lived by writing. With Sartre and others she helped found the journal *Les Temps modernes* in 1945. Her novel *Les Mandarins* won the Prix Goncourt in 1954. She died in Paris in April 1986.

## What She Gave

**The ethics of ambiguity.** Human beings are both free consciousnesses and embodied, finite beings in a world with others. Beauvoir argued that freedom wills itself only by willing the freedom of others: my freedom is not realised except through the liberation of those around me. This answers a purely individualistic reading of existentialism and rejects both the quest for a fixed ethical system and nihilism.

**The point of the situation.** The lived situation (body, gender, class and history) constrains freedom in fact, not merely in theory. Saying "you are always free" is therefore inadequate even within existentialism, and oppression is understood as the denial of the possibility of transcendence to others.

**Woman as the Other.** In *The Second Sex* (1949) she analysed how women have been defined in relation to men as the second sex, the Other, through myths, institutions and upbringing, and she denied that this position follows from biology or from an "eternal feminine." Her formula that one is not born, but becomes, a woman expresses this constructed character of femininity. The book became a foundational text for twentieth-century feminism.

## A Note on Writing History

Her presence in presentations of existentialism has long been smaller than her actual influence. Scholars such as Margaret Simons and Toril Moi have argued that she was often read as a disciple of Sartre, whereas her own philosophical development, including her early *Pyrrhus et Cinéas* (1944), deserves independent study. This is a pattern worth recording, not merely correcting.

## Her Major Works

*She Came to Stay* (L'Invitée, 1943) · *Pyrrhus et Cinéas* (1944) · *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (Pour une morale de l'ambiguïté, 1947) · *The Second Sex* (Le Deuxième Sexe, 1949) · *The Mandarins* (Les Mandarins, 1954) · *Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter* (Mémoires d'une jeune fille rangée, 1958) · *Old Age* (La Vieillesse, 1970)

## Selected Quotations

"One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." — *The Second Sex*, 1949 (Vol. 2, Book 1)

## Criticisms and Debates

Critics have asked how far her account of freedom and of the body remains tied to Sartrean assumptions, and some feminists have contested her suspicion of maternity and the female body. Others, such as Judith Butler, have drawn on her distinction between sex and gender in ways that go beyond her own text. The 1953 English translation of *The Second Sex* by H. M. Parshley omitted much material and obscured philosophical vocabulary, which was a reason for the new translation of 2009.
