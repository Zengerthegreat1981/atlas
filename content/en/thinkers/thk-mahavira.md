# Mahavira (Founder of Jainism)

The twenty-fourth spiritual teacher (*tirthankara*) in the Jain tradition, whose name before renunciation was Vardhamana. He is credited with reorganizing the ancient Jain path and establishing its core doctrines of rigorous non-violence (*ahimsa*), ascetic discipline, and the multifaceted nature of truth. Tradition places his life in the sixth century BCE in what is now Bihar, while modern critical scholarship generally places his death in the fifth century BCE (c. 490–410 BCE), identifying him as an older contemporary of Siddhartha Gautama (the Buddha).

## Biography

According to canonical tradition, Vardhamana was born at Kundagrama near Vaishali to King Siddhartha and Queen Trishala of the aristocratic Jnatrika clan. Around the age of thirty, he renounced his princely life and possessions to pursue rigorous asceticism. For twelve years he engaged in intense contemplation, fasting, and self-denial, wandering without a permanent dwelling. At the end of this period, on the banks of the Rijupalika River, he achieved *kevala jnana* (omniscience or supreme realization). He was thereupon honored with the epithets *Mahavira* ("Great Hero") and *Jina* ("Conqueror" of passions and karmic bondage), from which the term Jainism derives. Mahavira spent the remaining thirty years of his life teaching across northern India, establishing a four-fold community of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen. He passed away (*nirvana*) at Pavapuri, traditionally dated to 527 BCE. In early Buddhist Pali texts, he is referred to as *Nigantha Nataputta* (Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta).

## What He Contributed

- **Non-violence (*Ahimsa*)**: The absolute ethical injunction not to harm any living being (*jiva*), encompassing humans, animals, plants, and elemental or microscopic life forms. This principle shapes monastic practices, including filtering water, wearing mouth cloths (*mukhavastrika* in certain sects), and sweeping paths to avoid crushing small creatures.
- **The Five Great Vows (*Mahavratas*)**: The ethical foundation of Jain monasticism: non-violence (*ahimsa*), truthfulness (*satya*), non-stealing (*asteya*), celibacy (*brahmacharya*), and non-possession/non-attachment (*aparigraha*). Jain tradition holds that while the twenty-third Tirthankara, Parshvanatha, taught four vows, Mahavira made celibacy an explicit fifth vow.
- **The Many-Sidedness of Reality (*Anekantavada*)**: The metaphysical doctrine that reality is infinitely complex and multi-faceted; no single human perspective can capture the absolute totality of truth.
- **Conditional Predication (*Syadvada*)**: Later systematized by Jain logicians, this epistemological method prefixes assertions with *syat* ("in some respect"), articulating seven modes of qualified predication (*saptabhangi*) to avoid dogmatic absolutism.
- **Karma as Subtle Matter**: In Jain metaphysics, karma is not merely an abstract moral principle, but subtle material particles (*pudgala*) that cling to the soul through passionate or volitional action, obscuring its inherent purity and omniscience. Liberation (*moksha*) requires purifying accumulated karma through austerities and preventing the influx of new karma.

## The Jain Community and Practice

Mahavira established a four-fold community (*sangha*) comprising monks (*sadhus*), nuns (*sadhvis*), laymen (*shravakas*), and laywomen (*shravikas*). While monastics observe the strict five great vows (*mahavratas*), lay adherents observe modified, practical versions known as *anuvratas*. Because traditional agriculture inevitably injures soil-dwelling organisms, Jain lay practitioners historically gravitated toward commerce, banking, and scholarly professions.

## Sectarian Division and Later Scholasticism

Jainism is one of the *shramana* traditions of ancient India that rejected the authority of the Vedic scriptures. Following Mahavira, the tradition divided into two major branches:
- **Digambara** ("sky-clad"): Monks practice complete nudity as an expression of total non-possession, and the sect holds that the original canonical texts were lost, relying instead on secondary classical commentaries.
- **Svetambara** ("white-clad"): Monks and nuns wear simple white garments, accept the authority of the surviving canonical *Agamas*, and affirm that women can attain spiritual liberation.

Later philosophers—most notably Umasvati (author of the *Tattvartha Sutra*), Siddhasena Divakara, and Haribhadra Suri—formalized Jain epistemology, metaphysics, and logic in relation to other Indian philosophical schools.

## Textual Transmission and Translation

Mahavira did not compose written treatises; his teachings were preserved through oral transmission by his direct disciples (*ganadharas*), headed by Indrabhuti Gautama. Svetambara tradition records that the canonical texts were formally edited and written down at the Council of Valabhi in the fifth century CE. The *Ācārāṅga Sūtra*, the oldest among the canonical *Angas*, contains early monastic rules and poetic descriptions of Mahavira's life. In Western scholarship, the canonical texts were translated by Hermann Jacobi in Max Müller's *Sacred Books of the East* series (1884). No complete direct translation of the canonical *Agamas* into Arabic exists; Arabic scholarly studies address Jain thought within broader comparative religion works and encyclopedic surveys of Eastern philosophy.

## Limits

- Because Mahavira's teachings were orally transmitted for several centuries prior to the Valabhi council, historical-critical scholars distinguish between the historical teacher and the layered accretions in canonical literature.
- The Digambara tradition rejects the authenticity of the Svetambara *Agamas*, maintaining that the original teachings were lost.
- Chronological debates continue regarding the precise historical dates of Mahavira's life and his temporal overlap with the Buddha.

## Major Canonical Texts (Preserving His Teachings)

- *Ācārāṅga Sūtra* (Monastic conduct and early biography)
- *Sūtrakṛtāṅga Sūtra* (Doctrinal defenses against rival schools)
- *Bhagavatī Sūtra* (*Vyākhyāprajñapti*, extensive dialogues on cosmology and ethics)
- *Uttarādhyayana Sūtra* (Final instructions and pedagogical parables)
