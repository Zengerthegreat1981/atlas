# Mahavira (Founder of Jainism)

The twenty-fourth spiritual teacher (tirthankara) in the Jain tradition, whose name before renunciation was Vardhamana. He is credited with reorganizing Jainism and with its call to absolute non-violence and the many-sidedness of truth. Tradition places him in the sixth century BCE in what is now the state of Bihar, while many modern scholars place his death later, in the fifth century BCE; he was an older contemporary of the Buddha, or at least lived in the same region and period.

## Biography

Vardhamana was born at Kundagrama near Vaishali, of the royal Jnatrika clan, to his father Siddhartha and his mother Trishala. At about the age of thirty he left his family and kingship and took the path of asceticism; he spent twelve years in austerity and contemplation without taking a fixed shelter, and then attained "kevala" (complete knowledge) on the bank of the river Rijupalika, and was given the titles "Mahavira" (the great hero) and "Jina" (the conqueror of the passions), from which the name Jainism derives. He taught for thirty years, founded a community of monks, nuns and laypeople, and died at Pavapuri around 527 BCE according to tradition. Early Buddhist (Pali) texts call him "Nigantha Nataputta" (Nigaṇṭha Nātaputta) and count him among the rival teachers of the Buddha's day.

## What He Contributed

- **Non-violence (ahimsa)**: one must not harm any living being, whether human, animal, plant or microscopic creature, because the soul (jiva) exists in everything alive. This is reflected in practices such as filtering water, sweeping the ground before sitting, and avoiding travel at night.
- **The five great vows**: monastic life rests on five pledges: non-harm, truthfulness, non-stealing, chastity and non-possession (aparigraha). Traditions state that his predecessor Parshva (Pārśva) taught four, and that Mahavira added chastity as a separate vow.
- **The many-sidedness of truth (anekantavada)**: truth has endless aspects and cannot be encompassed by a single judgment, and therefore no position possesses the whole truth.
- **"Perhaps" and the seven formulations (syadvada)**: Jain theorists after him, not Mahavira himself, formulated a method that attaches to every judgment the word "syat" (in some respect) and enumerates seven formulations for judging a thing: it exists in one respect; it does not exist in another; it both exists and does not exist; it is indescribable; and the three further combinations of "indescribable" with each of the first three.
- **Karma as matter**: Jainism conceives karma as subtle matter that adheres to the soul through the passions and weighs it down, and liberation is the soul's release from it through asceticism, knowledge and right conduct.

## The Jain Community

Mahavira established a community of four pillars: monks, nuns, lay devotees and laywomen, and defined for each its share of the vows: monks observe the five vows fully (maha-vrata), while laypeople observe them in a lightened form (anu-vrata) compatible with family and professional life. For this reason the occupations of Jain laypeople were historically confined to trade and banking more than agriculture, because plowing destroys microscopic creatures. This structure has remained in place in India to this day.

## His Place in the Tradition

Jainism is among the schools that do not recognize the authority of the Vedas (nastika in Indian terminology), and after Mahavira it divided into two great sects: the "Svetambara" (white-clad), who accept the surviving Agamas as sacred texts and hold that women can attain liberation, and the "Digambara" (sky-clad), who hold that monks go naked and deny that the original texts survived, and so rely on later scholastic works. In the following centuries thinkers such as Umasvati, Siddhasena Divakara and Hemachandra added a philosophical and logical structure.

## Influence and Reception

The Jain principle of non-violence remained influential in modern Indian social and political movements, and many scholars hold that Gandhi was influenced by Jainism in his formulation of "ahimsa", through his Jain friend Shrimad Rajchandra. As for "anekantavada", it is used today in interfaith dialogue and conflict management. His teaching remains present in the daily rites of the Jains, including abstaining from meat and caring for what crawls on the ground, and in the institutions of mercy for animals run by their communities in India.

## Limits

- Mahavira wrote nothing down, and the recording of the Agamas came late: Svetambara sources agree that the texts were fixed at the council of Valabhi in the fifth century CE, after centuries of oral transmission, which makes the attribution of specific teachings to him a matter of debate.
- Of the twelve Agamas (angas), only eleven survive for the Svetambaras, while the twelfth was lost, and the Digambaras do not recognize the existing texts.
- The dating of his life is disputed: the traditional dates are 599–527 BCE, and many modern scholars put his death roughly a century later or more.

## His Most Important Works

There is no work attributed to him with certainty. The most important texts preserving his teachings for the Svetambaras are:

- *Ācārāṅga Sūtra*, the oldest of the angas, containing the rules of monastic conduct.
- *Sūtrakṛtāṅga Sūtra*, containing debate with the other doctrines.
- *Bhagavatī Sūtra*, the largest of the texts.
