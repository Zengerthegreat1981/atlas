# Plato

A major Greek philosopher and the founder of the Academy of Athens (an early institution of philosophical and scientific research and teaching in the West); a student of Socrates and the teacher of Aristotle; he developed the metaphysical theory of the "Forms" and presented in his dialogues (such as the Republic and the Phaedrus) an influential division of the human soul into three parts: the rational, the spirited and the appetitive.

## Presence in the Arabic heritage

The Arab-Islamic heritage knew Plato widely, though partially. Translators of the ninth century, among them Hunayn ibn Ishaq, rendered Galen's summaries of Plato's dialogues, Ibn al-Nadim in the "Fihrist" listed the works of Plato known in Arabic, and the "Timaeus", the "Republic" and the "Laws" were known largely through summaries and paraphrases. Al-Farabi wrote "The Philosophy of Plato and Its Parts" and "Summary of Plato's Laws", and took the "Republic" and the "Laws" as the basis of his theory of the virtuous city and the philosopher-ruler. Ibn Rushd wrote the "Commentary on Plato's Republic" (Jawami' siyasat Aflatun), which was preserved in Hebrew, in which he read the "Republic" in a reading applicable to Islamic societies, and discussed the position of women and the rule of philosophers. The Neoplatonism that came in the "Theology of Aristotle" occupied a place in the thought of al-Kindi, Ibn Sina and the Sufis. Thus Plato became in the Islamic world the "divine sage" who is paired with Socrates and viewed in the light of Neoplatonism, and from him branched a current that came to see philosophy as a path to happiness and the virtuous city.

## Life and context

Plato was born in Athens in 427 BC to an aristocratic family, and was preparing for a political life when the trial and execution of Socrates in 399 BC caused a shock that turned his course toward philosophy. Reports state that he then traveled abroad (later sources mention Egypt), visited Italy and Sicily, made contact with the Pythagoreans and with the court of Syracuse, and about 387 BC founded the Academy in Athens, where he continued to direct research until his death in 347 BC. He lived in the time of the war between Athens and Sparta, the collapse of the Athenian empire and the defeats of democracy, so the political question was the companion of the philosophical question in his writings.

## What he contributed

- **The tripartite division of the soul**: in the "Republic" (Book IV) Plato presented an influential structural model of the soul, arguing for it by the principle of **non-contradiction**: since a person may thirst and refrain from drinking at the same time, there must be in him more than one principle of action. He therefore divided it into:
  - **the rational** (logistikon), which calculates, balances and considers what is best in the long run.
  - **the spirited** (thymoeides), the seat of anger, zeal, dignity and shame, the natural ally of reason if education is good.
  - **the appetitive** (epithymetikon): hunger, thirst, sex and greed.
  He then compared it in the "Phaedrus" to the winged chariot: a driver (reason) and two horses, one white and noble, one black and unruly. This tripartite model (a rational power that rules, an intermediate power that is enlisted, and an appetitive power that is restrained) is often compared with Freud's triad (id/ego/superego); Freud himself acknowledged the affinity between psychoanalytic Eros and Plato's Eros in the *Symposium*, though the structural comparison is a modern one.
- **Justice as psychic health**: the most important shift in the "Republic" is that justice is not external behavior but a **state of inner order**: that each power performs its function without encroaching on another. Conversely, injustice is "disorder and rebellion" among the parts of the soul. Thus Plato framed virtue and vice explicitly in the language of **psychic health and sickness**, a comparison that has recurred in therapeutic discourse since.
- **The theory of Forms and recollection (anamnesis)**: knowledge for him is not acquisition from outside but **recovery** of what the soul knew before its embodiment, as in the experiment with the boy in the "Meno", from whom a geometrical proof is drawn by questioning alone. The questioning procedure of the *Meno* is a model of the "Socratic method", often cited as a distant ancestor of therapeutic practices that assume the answer lies in the counselee and not in the counselor.
- **Eros as an ascending energy**: in the "Symposium" he made desire a single force that rises by degrees from love of one body to love of bodies, then of souls, then of laws and sciences, then of beauty itself. The idea that appetitive energy can be **directed to higher ends** without being abolished is what psychoanalysis later called "sublimation".
- **Dream and repressed desire**: in Book IX of the "Republic" there is a striking observation that in every person, even the moderate, there is a kind of "wild and lawless" desires that appear only in sleep when the rational part slumbers. This text is an early observation of the idea that dreams may reveal desires that the waking person restrains.

## His position in the tradition

Plato is the starting point from which everything after him is measured. Aristotle, his student, replied to him by bringing the form down from the world of Forms into the interior of things; Middle and then Neoplatonism (Plotinus, Porphyry, Proclus) developed his metaphysical side into a system of emanation; and through this path he entered **Islamic philosophy** (al-Farabi in "Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City") and **Christian theology** (Augustine). In the twentieth century he remained present in psychology as the author of an early and influential model of inner conflict between the parts of the soul.

## Limits

Aristotle objected to the theory of Forms with the "third man" argument and on the ground that it doubles the world needlessly, and said that the form does not exist separately from things. Karl Popper attacked him in "The Open Society and Its Enemies" (1945) because he sees in the "Republic" a totalitarian conception that places the state before the individual. Scholars note that Plato wrote dialogues and not a fixed doctrine, and that in most of them Socrates is the principal speaker, so it is hard to be certain what is his opinion and what is a dramatic role; moreover his last books, such as the "Laws", modify some of what he said in the "Republic". Jacques Derrida in "Plato's Pharmacy" criticized his preference for living speech over writing. As for comparing the tripartite model of the soul with Freud's conception, it is an approximation and not an identity: Plato speaks of parts connected with moral and cognitive functions, not of an unconscious structure.

## Major works

- *Republic*: the division of the soul, justice as inner order, the cave of knowledge.
- *Phaedrus*: the chariot of the winged soul, eros, the critique of writing.
- *Symposium*: the ascending ladder of eros.
- *Phaedo*: the immortality of the soul.
- *Meno*: recollection and the Socratic method.
- *Apology*

## Dialogue Chronology and Transmission

Scholars conventionally group the dialogues by style and content into early (for example *Apology*, *Euthyphro*, *Crito*, *Laches*), middle (*Phaedo*, *Symposium*, *Republic*, *Phaedrus*) and late works (*Theaetetus*, *Parmenides*, *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Timaeus*, *Laws*), although the exact order is debated. In antiquity the works were arranged in nine tetralogies by Thrasyllus (first century CE). Among the oldest surviving medieval manuscripts is the Clarke codex (Bodleian Library, copied in 895 CE).

## A Famous Line

"The unexamined life is not worth living" is spoken by Socrates in Plato's *Apology* (38a). In the *Republic* (Book 7, 536e–537a) Socrates also recommends that early education proceed through play rather than compulsion, so that each child's natural bent may show.
