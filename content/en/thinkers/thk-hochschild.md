# Arlie Russell Hochschild

Arlie Russell Hochschild (born 1940) is an American sociologist, professor emerita at the University of California, Berkeley, known for her work on the sociology of emotion, work and family life, and, more recently, the emotional foundations of American politics.

## Life and Context

Hochschild studied at Swarthmore College and earned her doctorate at the University of California, Berkeley, where she later became a professor. Her early studies examined social life among older people and the sociology of emotions, and she became a leading figure in feminist sociology and the study of work and family. In later decades she turned to ethnographic research on political polarization in the United States.

She was born Arlie Russell in Boston on 15 January 1940. She graduated from Swarthmore in 1962, received an M.A. (1965) and a Ph.D. (1969) in sociology at Berkeley, taught at the University of California, Santa Cruz from 1969 to 1971, and then joined the Berkeley faculty. Her work draws on Erving Goffman, C. Wright Mills, Freud, Marx, and feminism from the late 1960s.

## Main Ideas

In *The Managed Heart*, based on research with flight attendants and bill collectors, Hochschild introduced the concept of emotional labor: the management of feeling to create a publicly observable facial and bodily display as part of paid work. She distinguished surface acting from deep acting and described feeling rules, the social norms that guide what people feel is appropriate. She had set out feeling rules and emotion work, the management of feeling in private life, in a 1979 article in the *American Journal of Sociology*. Flight attendants in the later study were trained to produce warmth; bill collectors were expected to project suspicion or severity. Workers may become estranged from feelings they manage for an employer. In *The Second Shift*, written with Anne Machung, she documented how employed women did the bulk of domestic and childcare work after returning home from paid jobs. She called this lag a stalled revolution: women entered paid work faster than workplaces and men's share of housework adjusted. The study used interviews and observation in the homes of two-earner couples. *The Time Bind* examined how workplaces that promised family-friendly policies could still draw parents toward work, as home became a place of stress. With Barbara Ehrenreich she edited *Global Woman*, which explored global care chains in which migrant women provide care labor. In *Strangers in Their Own Land*, she introduced the idea of a deep story, a feels-as-if narrative that underlies political views, and the empathy wall that separates people across political divides.

## Case Studies

*The Managed Heart* drew on research with Delta Air Lines flight attendants and with bill collectors. *The Time Bind* was based on fieldwork at a large manufacturing company that the book calls Amerco. *The Unexpected Community* studied a San Francisco-area apartment complex for older residents. *Strangers in Their Own Land* grew out of several years of interviews with Tea Party-aligned residents of the Lake Charles area of Louisiana, a region marked by heavy industrial pollution. *Stolen Pride* (2024) continues that inquiry in Pike County, Kentucky, treating loss and shame as part of the appeal of the political right.

## Method

Hochschild uses in-depth interviews and long-term ethnography, often immersed in a field site, to interpret how structural conditions are felt and narrated by participants.

## Related Concepts

Hochschild's concepts sit within a wider network. Emotional labor extends the study of alienation, which Marx developed for industrial work, to the sale of managed feeling, and *The Managed Heart* also draws on Erving Goffman's account of the presentation of self. Goffman concentrates on outward performance, whereas Hochschild's deep acting concerns efforts to change what one actually feels. Feeling rules are a special kind of social norm and are acquired through socialization. *The Second Shift* connects with the gender division of labour, social reproduction and unpaid care work, and with her notion of a gender ideology that shapes how couples justify their arrangements. Global care chains link care to international migration and global inequality. The deep story relates to framing and narrative in the study of social movements, and to political polarization. Emotion work, her term for managing feeling in private life, should be distinguished from emotional labor, which is performed for a wage.

## Influence and Criticism

Her concept of emotional labor became widely used in sociology, management studies and public discussion, though some scholars argue that its popular usage has stretched beyond her original meaning. Others note that emotional labor can also be satisfying or under the worker's control, and that a sharp line between authentic feeling and managed feeling is hard to sustain. Critics of *Strangers in Their Own Land* have asked how far findings from one region of Louisiana can be generalized, and whether the deep story gives enough weight to racial attitudes and structural factors.
