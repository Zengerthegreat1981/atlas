# Abu Nasr Muhammad ibn Muhammad al-Farabi

A philosopher of Turkic or Persian origin (the sources disagree), traditionally titled "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle, as the thinker who built one of the first integrated philosophical systems in Islam, political and metaphysical, combining Aristotelian logic, Plotinian metaphysics and Platonic political philosophy. He studied in Baghdad under Yuhanna ibn Haylan in logic, and, according to some reports, under Abu Bishr Matta ibn Yunus, and moved late in life to the court of Sayf al-Dawla al-Hamdani in Aleppo.

## Biography

The early biographical sources (Ibn Abi Usaybi'a, Ibn Khallikan, Ibn al-Qifti) conflict over the details of al-Farabi's upbringing, but agree that he was born in the region of Farab (or Utrar) in Transoxiana about 257 AH / 870 CE, and that he came to Baghdad to seek knowledge, where he studied logic under the Christian Yuhanna ibn Haylan and, according to some reports, with Abu Bishr Matta ibn Yunus, one of the great transmitters of Aristotelian logic into Arabic. His birth year is uncertain (estimates range from 870 to 872 CE), and the accounts of his journeys rest on later reports and are not contemporaneously documented. He lived in Baghdad for many years, then moved to Damascus and Egypt before settling in the last years of his life at the court of Sayf al-Dawla al-Hamdani in Aleppo, who was known for his patronage of scholars and men of letters. Al-Farabi died in Damascus in 339 AH / 950 CE.

## What he contributed

### Political philosophy: the virtuous city

Al-Farabi's most famous work, "The Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City", formulates a coherent political-metaphysical theory: the virtuous city needs a leader who combines prophecy and philosophy, because human perfection is achieved only in an organized civic association aiming at true happiness. Al-Farabi contrasted the "virtuous city" with kinds of "ignorant", "immoral", "transformed" and "errant" cities, in a classification parallel to Plato's classification of regimes in the "Republic" but in an Islamic framework that links prophecy to philosophy through the theory of the Active Intellect and revelation as an emanation upon the imaginative faculty.

### The theory of emanation and the order of being

Al-Farabi, influenced by Plotinus through the "Theology of Aristotle", built a cosmic system in which being proceeds from the "First One" through an order of ten intellects (each intellect giving rise to a subsequent intellect and a sphere), and this sequence ends with the Active Intellect, responsible for bestowing forms on the terrestrial world and for intellectual contact with man. This "emanationist" system became the basic framework on which Ibn Sina later built his metaphysics, though he developed and modified it profoundly.

### Logic and the sciences

In the "Enumeration of the Sciences" al-Farabi classified the sciences methodically (linguistics, logic, mathematics, natural science, metaphysics, politics, jurisprudence, theology), a text that, translated into Latin, influenced the scholastic classification of the sciences in Europe. He also wrote detailed commentaries on Aristotle's logical books ("On Interpretation", "Prior Analytics") and independent treatises such as "al-Tawti'a" as an introduction to logic, and so earned the title "the Second Teacher".

### Music

In the "Great Book of Music" al-Farabi presented a theoretical and practical treatment of music combining mathematics (the theory of melody and intervals), physics (the nature of sound) and philosophy (the effect of music on the soul); it is counted among the most important texts in medieval Arabic music theory.

### The theory of intellect and contact

In his treatise "On the Intellect" al-Farabi distinguished several ranks of the human intellect (the potential or material, the actual, the acquired), up to the separate "Active Intellect" that pours intelligibles upon prepared souls, a theory on which he later built his conception of how the perfect philosopher and the prophet make contact with the upper world. This distinction became a reference framework for all the philosophers of the East and al-Andalus who came after him, though they differed over its details (such as the problem of the unity of the Active Intellect that Ibn Rushd later raised).

## Influence

Al-Farabi was the bridge across which late Greek philosophy (Plotinian and commented Aristotelian) passed to Ibn Sina, who stated in his autobiography that he did not understand Aristotle's "Metaphysics" until he came upon al-Farabi's treatise "On the Purposes of the Metaphysics". His influence extended to Ibn Bajja and Ibn Rushd in al-Andalus, and to Moses Maimonides, who advised his students to read al-Farabi on logic. In Latin Europe he was known as Alpharabius and some of his works were translated (including the "Enumeration of the Sciences" in the translation of Gerard of Cremona), influencing the classification of the sciences among the men of the Latin schools.

### Aristotle and Platonism: "The Harmonization of the Opinions of the Two Sages"

In a treatise attributed to him entitled "The Harmonization of the Opinions of the Two Sages", al-Farabi tries to reconcile Plato and Aristotle on apparently disputed questions (such as the question of the Platonic Forms and the eternity of the world), relying in part on Plotinian texts that were wrongly attributed to Aristotle (the "Theology of Aristotle"), which explains an aspect of the "Plotinian syncretic" character of his reading of Aristotle, in contrast to Ibn Rushd's later reading, more committed to the pure Aristotelian text.

## Limits

It is held against al-Farabi's project that he built his conception of prophecy and revelation on a psychological theory (emanation upon the imaginative faculty) that subjects the religious phenomenon to a purely philosophical rational explanation, which provoked the objection of theologians and Sufis who saw in it a bending of prophecy to philosophical categories foreign to Islam. Moreover his political idealism in the "Virtuous City" (a philosopher-prophet leader who combines wisdom and law) had no real counterpart contemporary with him, which led some scholars to describe it as utopian in an explicitly Platonic sense rather than a practical political program. The attribution to him of some works, such as *Fusus al-hikam* (Bezels of Wisdom), is doubted by scholars, as is, by some, the authenticity of the *Harmonization of the Two Sages*. Another problem remains, al-Farabi's reconciliation between the eternity of the world (an Aristotelian position on which he built his emanationist system) and the Islamic doctrine of creation, the very question al-Ghazali would later attack in "The Incoherence of the Philosophers", directing his critique specifically at al-Farabi and Ibn Sina.

## His key terms

The concept of the "virtuous city" and its leader who combines prophecy and philosophy, the theory of the ten-fold "emanation" of the intellects, and his distinction between the demonstrative, dialectical and rhetorical modes of persuasion are all terms al-Farabi formulated with a systematic precision that became a reference for all later Islamic political philosophy and metaphysics, even among those who differed with him over their details.

## Major works

- "The Opinions of the People of the Virtuous City" (Mabadi' Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila)
- "Enumeration of the Sciences" (Ihsa' al-'Ulum)
- "Civil Politics" (al-Siyasa al-Madaniyya)
- "The Great Book of Music" (Kitab al-Musiqa al-Kabir)
- "On the Purposes of the Metaphysics" (Fi Aghrad ma Ba'd al-Tabi'a)
- "al-Tawti'a" and treatises on logic
