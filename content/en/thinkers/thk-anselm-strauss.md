# Anselm L. Strauss

Anselm L. Strauss (1916-1996) was an American sociologist and a central figure in the symbolic interactionist tradition. He is best known as the co-founder, with Barney Glaser, of grounded theory, and for his studies of medical settings, illness and dying, and for developing the idea of the negotiated order.

## Life and Context

Strauss was born in New York City in 1916 and trained at the University of Chicago, where he was shaped by the Chicago school, by Herbert Blumer's interactionism, and by pragmatist philosophy (Mead, Dewey). He taught at Indiana University and the University of Chicago before moving in 1960 to the University of California, San Francisco, where he worked in the School of Nursing and built a long research program on health care and chronic illness. He died in 1996.

His full name was Anselm Leonard Strauss; he was born on 18 December 1916. He earned a biology degree at the University of Virginia in 1939, then an M.A. (1942) and a Ph.D. (1945) in sociology at Chicago, studying interactionism with Blumer and completing the dissertation under Ernest Burgess. He taught at Lawrence College from 1944 to 1947 and at Indiana University, where he and Alfred Lindesmith published *Social Psychology* (1949). He returned to Chicago in 1952, directed research at Michael Reese Hospital, and in 1960 joined the UCSF School of Nursing, where he founded the Department of Social and Behavioral Sciences and chaired it until he became emeritus in 1987. He died in San Francisco on 5 September 1996.

## Main Ideas

- **Identity as process:** in *Mirrors and Masks* (1959) he treated identity as formed and revised through language, interaction, and membership in groups across the life course.
- **Negotiated order:** Strauss argued that organizations such as hospitals are not run only by fixed rules and hierarchy. Order is continually produced through negotiation among staff, patients, and others, who bargain over tasks, meanings, and rules.
- **Grounded theory:** theory should be developed from systematic analysis of data rather than only tested against pre-existing grand theories. This involves constant comparison, coding, and theoretical sampling.
- **Trajectory and illness work:** with Juliet Corbin he described chronic illness as a trajectory shaped by the work done by patients, families, and staff.
- **Awareness contexts:** in studies of dying he showed how what the dying person, staff, and family know about approaching death structures their interaction. *Awareness of Dying* distinguished closed awareness, suspected awareness, mutual pretense, and open awareness.
- **Social worlds and arenas:** he analyzed how groups organized around shared activities and commitments negotiate within wider arenas.

## Method

Strauss favored fieldwork, interviews, and close observation in natural settings, combined with an explicit analytic procedure. Grounded theory sought to give qualitative research systematic rigor and a route to theory building. After the split with Glaser, Strauss and Corbin elaborated a more structured coding approach, while Glaser defended a more emergent version. Glaser treated the later coding scheme as forcing data into preset categories.

## Case Studies

*Boys in White* (1961), written with Howard Becker, Blanche Geer, and Everett Hughes, was a field study of students at the University of Kansas medical school. It showed how students built shared perspectives for coping with an unmanageable workload and with faculty demands.

## Influence and Criticism

Grounded theory became one of the most widely used approaches in qualitative research, especially in nursing, health studies, education, and organizational research. Critics have questioned whether researchers can truly approach data without prior theory, and the Glaser-Strauss disagreement about procedure is well known. A further criticism is that a focus on negotiation understates durable power and structural constraint. Later constructivist versions, notably by Kathy Charmaz, revised the approach.

## Applications and Cross-References

The negotiated order approach has been applied to hospitals, schools, and workplaces. It connects to Blumer's interactionism and to Erving Goffman's studies of institutions, and contrasts with structural-functional accounts of organizations.
