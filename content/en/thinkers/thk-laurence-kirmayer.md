# Laurence Kirmayer

A Canadian psychiatrist (born 23 October 1952 in Montreal) and Distinguished James McGill Professor of Psychiatry. He directs the Division of Social and Transcultural Psychiatry at McGill University and the Culture and Mental Health Research Unit at the Jewish General Hospital. He long edited the journal *Transcultural Psychiatry* and is now its editor-in-chief emeritus. His work joins clinical practice, medical anthropology, and philosophy of psychiatry in studying how psychological suffering is shaped, expressed, and treated in cultural context. He studied psychology and medicine at McGill, completed psychiatry residency at the University of California, Davis, and in 1991 founded the annual McGill Summer Program in Social and Cultural Psychiatry.

## Central Idea: Culture as Part of the Structure, Not a Layer on Top

Kirmayer starts from the premise that culture is not a dependent variable added to a ready-made biological model. Rather, it is a framework that produces meaning, experience and the body together. He therefore criticizes the model that treats symptoms as universal content that people wear in a cultural garment, and proposes instead a view in which the interaction of brain, body, meaning and social context forms a single loop. He developed this view in "What Kind of Science for Psychiatry?" (with Daina Crafa, *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience*, 2014), a critique of the National Institute of Mental Health Research Domain Criteria as a complete model of mental disorder. The article argues for several kinds of evidence, including social process, rather than neural explanation alone.

## Somatization and Idioms of Distress

Kirmayer began in the 1980s to study **somatization**, the expression of psychological distress through physical complaints. In his 1984 study "Culture, Affect and Somatization" he argued that treating somatization as a mask or a denial of emotions is a Western interpretation, and that many cultures understand distress through the body without this reflecting a deficit of emotional awareness. In his 1998 article with Allan Young on culture and somatization, he distinguished several forms of it, including physical symptoms that the patient presents as the first complaint of an underlying psychiatric disorder, and symptoms associated with fear of illness. He showed that the distinction between the psychological and the physical is not universal. This connects with the concept of **"idioms of distress"** formulated in medical anthropology: expressions such as "tightness of the chest" or "heaviness of the heart," found in many languages, carry both a psychological and a bodily meaning together, and should not be translated into symptoms of depression or anxiety without understanding their context. A physician who asks only about "sadness" may therefore miss what the patient is conveying in their own language.

## Culture, Diagnosis and Cultural Consultation

In 2001, in a supplement of the *Journal of Clinical Psychiatry*, Kirmayer wrote about the variation in the clinical presentation of depression and anxiety across cultures and its effect on diagnosis and treatment. With his team in Montreal he helped develop the **cultural consultation** service, in which physicians request the help of a cultural mediator, an interpreter and an expert in assessing the condition of a patient from a different background. He published the experience of this service in the book *Cultural Consultation: Encountering the Other in Mental Health Care* (2014), which he co-edited with Guzder and Rousseau. This line of work is related to the **Cultural Formulation Interview**, which was included in DSM-5 in 2013 and which Kirmayer helped to develop.

## Trauma, Global Mental Health and Indigenous Peoples

Kirmayer worked with research teams studying the psychological impact of colonial history on Indigenous peoples in Canada, and co-edited *Healing Traditions: The Mental Health of Aboriginal Peoples in Canada* (2009). In a 2014 article with Gone and Moses he critiqued the concept of **"historical trauma,"** which is used to explain the problems of these peoples. He argued that the concept blurs historical events with individual response, and may turn colonialism into a psychiatric pathology. He called for linking suffering to the continuing present-day reality of discrimination, poverty and loss of land. In 2007 he co-edited *Understanding Trauma: Integrating Biological, Clinical, and Cultural Perspectives*. In global mental health, he and Pedersen wrote in 2014 about a "new architecture" that warns against transferring ready-made Western models to other countries without listening to local knowledge and healing traditions.

## Impact

Kirmayer's work helped bring attention to culture to the core of clinical assessment rather than its margins. It also helped establish the idea that a physician needs both cultural competence and epistemic humility. His work is used as a framework for understanding why the expression of psychological pain, particularly among migrant and minority patients, may differ from the expectations of the clinician.

## Limits and Criticism

Critics from the biological-neural current argue that emphasizing culture may obscure what humans share in depression and psychosis. Others argue that the concept of "idioms of distress" could turn into a general classification that tempts physicians to attribute somatic complaints to culture instead of searching for an organic illness. The Cultural Formulation Interview has limited evidence in everyday practice and requires time that is rarely available in crowded clinics. This entry is educational and not clinical advice. Moreover, thinking based on critique of Western models itself needs experimental research to show that cultural approaches actually improve clinical outcomes, a question that has not yet been settled.
