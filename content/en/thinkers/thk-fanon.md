# Frantz Fanon

Frantz Fanon (1925-1961) was a Martinican-born psychiatrist, philosopher and revolutionary whose writings on racism, colonialism and decolonization became foundational for postcolonial thought and anti-colonial movements.

## Life and Context

Fanon was born in Martinique, then a French colony, and served with the Free French forces in the Second World War. He studied medicine and psychiatry in Lyon. In 1953 he took a post at the Blida-Joinville psychiatric hospital in Algeria, and as the Algerian war of independence began he came to support the National Liberation Front (FLN). He was expelled from Algeria in 1957, worked in Tunis and as an ambassador of the Algerian provisional government, and died of leukemia in the United States in December 1961.

## Main Ideas

In *Black Skin, White Masks*, Fanon analyzed how racism shapes the psychology of the colonized, describing the internalization of a sense of inferiority and the experience of being fixed by the white gaze. In *The Wretched of the Earth*, he described the colonial world as divided in a Manichean way between colonizer and colonized, argued that decolonization is a profound and often violent process of historical change, and warned that postcolonial elites could reproduce colonial structures. He emphasized the need to build a genuine national culture and consciousness and to move beyond narrow nationalism toward social and human liberation. His clinical work informed his analysis of the psychological effects of colonial violence and torture.

## Major Works

- *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952)
- *A Dying Colonialism* (1959)
- *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961, with a preface by Jean-Paul Sartre)
- *Toward the African Revolution* (collected essays, published 1964)

## Method

Fanon blended clinical psychiatry, phenomenology, Marxism and political writing. He grounded his argument in lived experience as well as structural analysis.

## Influence and Criticism

His writings strongly influenced anti-colonial movements, the Black Power movement and postcolonial studies, including scholars such as Edward Said and Homi Bhabha. Critics, notably Hannah Arendt in *On Violence* (1970), challenged his treatment of violence. Others have questioned his generalizations about colonial societies and his account of gender, while defenders argue that his claims on violence are often read out of context.
