# Frantz Fanon

Frantz Fanon (1925-1961) was a Martinican-born psychiatrist, philosopher and revolutionary whose writings on racism, colonialism and decolonization became foundational for postcolonial thought and anti-colonial movements.

## Life and Context

Fanon was born in Fort-de-France, Martinique, then a French colony, and attended the Lycée Schoelcher, where Aimé Césaire taught. He served with the Free French forces in the Second World War. He studied medicine and psychiatry in Lyon and trained with François Tosquelles at Saint-Alban, a center of institutional psychotherapy. In 1953 he took a post at the Blida-Joinville psychiatric hospital in Algeria, and as the Algerian war of independence began he came to support the National Liberation Front (FLN). He resigned in 1956, was expelled from Algeria in 1957, worked in Tunis, wrote for the FLN newspaper *El Moudjahid*, and served as an ambassador of the Algerian provisional government, including in Ghana in 1960. He died of leukemia in Bethesda, Maryland, in December 1961, shortly after *The Wretched of the Earth* appeared.

## Main Ideas

In *Black Skin, White Masks*, Fanon analyzed how racism shapes the psychology of the colonized, describing the internalization of a sense of inferiority and the experience of being fixed by the white gaze. He traced effects on language, desire and self-perception, and argued that this suffering is sociogenic: its causes are social and cannot be reduced to the individual. In *The Wretched of the Earth*, he described the colonial world as divided in a Manichean way between colonizer and colonized, a division maintained by force. He argued that colonial rule rests on violence and that decolonization is a profound and often violent process of historical change through which the colonized may recover agency. He warned that postcolonial elites could reproduce colonial structures, and he called for political education alongside a genuine national culture and consciousness, moving beyond narrow nationalism toward social and human liberation. His clinical work informed his analysis of the psychological effects of colonial violence and torture.

## Major Works

- *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952)
- *A Dying Colonialism* (1959)
- *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961, with a preface by Jean-Paul Sartre)
- *Toward the African Revolution* (collected essays, published 1964)

## Case Studies

In *A Dying Colonialism* Fanon examined how the Algerian revolution changed everyday practices, including the changing meaning of the veil, the role of the radio (the Voice of Algeria), family relations, and relations between Algerian patients and French medicine. The chapter "Colonial War and Mental Disorders" in *The Wretched of the Earth* presents clinical cases of Algerian and French patients affected by the violence of the war, drawn from his psychiatric practice.

## Related Concepts

Fanon's work connects with the concepts of colonialism, racism, alienation, recognition, decolonisation, violence, national consciousness, and the psychology of oppression. In *Black Skin, White Masks* he reworks Hegel's account of recognition between master and bondsman for a racial order in which recognition is distorted, and draws on phenomenology and psychoanalysis. His critique of the national bourgeoisie relates to dependency and neocolonialism: formal independence may leave economic relations and institutions of domination largely in place. Useful comparisons include Aimé Césaire's *Discourse on Colonialism* and negritude, which Fanon engaged both sympathetically and critically; Albert Memmi's portrait of colonised and coloniser; and W. E. B. Du Bois's double consciousness, a related but distinct account of seeing oneself through a racially dominant gaze. Later connections include Edward Said's analysis of colonial representation, Homi Bhabha's reading of ambivalence and identity, and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's question of whether the subaltern can speak. Ali Shariati helped introduce Fanon's anticolonial ideas to Iranian readers, while giving religion a larger positive role in political renewal.

## Method

Fanon blended clinical psychiatry, phenomenology, Marxism and political writing. He grounded his argument in lived experience as well as structural analysis.

## Influence and Criticism

His writings strongly influenced anti-colonial movements, the Black Power movement and postcolonial studies, including scholars such as Edward Said and Homi Bhabha. Critics, notably Hannah Arendt in *On Violence* (1970), challenged his treatment of violence, especially the celebration she associated with Sartre and with Fanon's admirers. Other readers stress that Fanon also documented the psychological damage of that violence. Others have questioned his generalizations about colonial societies and his account of gender, including his discussion of Mayotte Capécia and of the veil, while defenders argue that his claims on violence are often read out of context.
