# Henri Bergson

A French philosopher, author of the most important modern philosophical distinction between time as the clock measures it and time as it is actually lived, a distinction that informed later phenomenological work on lived time in psychiatry.

## What He Gave

In the "Essay on the Immediate Data of Consciousness" (Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience, 1889, known in English as Time and Free Will), Bergson distinguished between quantitative time, which can be measured and divided (the time of the clock and of physics), and "duration" (durée), qualitative time as consciousness actually lives it: a continuous flow that cannot be divided, not discrete moments laid side by side but a living extension that carries the past within the present. This distinction is the direct philosophical basis on which Minkowski later built his concept of "lived time" in his phenomenological analysis of schizophrenia and depression.

Later, in a different book, "Creative Evolution" (L'Évolution créatrice, 1907), Bergson put forward his second most famous concept: the "vital impetus" (élan vital), a creative force or momentum that pervades living beings and drives biological evolution, neither as a blind mechanism (as in mechanistic Darwinism) nor as a predetermined finality, but as continuous invention that cannot be fully predicted. The two concepts, duration and élan vital, are linked for Bergson (both resist reducing time/life to measurable spatiality), but they belong to two different books eighteen years apart, and they must not be confused or one attributed to the title of the other.

## His Relation to William James

James and Bergson developed their concepts, James's "stream of consciousness" and Bergson's "duration," in a nearly parallel fashion, with documented mutual correspondence and familiarity between the two men; James was among the first to promote Bergson's philosophy in American academic circles.

## Limits

He is not an existential thinker in the direct sense, nor a psychotherapist: his contribution here is purely philosophical, in the theory of time, which later passed through Minkowski and psychopathological phenomenology into the heart of existential psychiatry.

## Major Works

Essay on the Immediate Data of Consciousness (Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience, 1889) · Matter and Memory (Matière et mémoire, 1896) · Creative Evolution (L'Évolution créatrice, 1907) · The Two Sources of Morality and Religion (Les Deux Sources de la morale et de la religion, 1932)

## Selected Quotations

"Time is invention, or it is nothing at all." (Creative Evolution, 1907)

## Biography and Career

Henri Bergson was born in Paris on 18 October 1859 to a Jewish father of Polish origin, a musician, and an English mother. He entered the École Normale Supérieure and obtained the agrégation in philosophy in 1881. He taught at the lycées of Angers and Clermont-Ferrand, then in Paris, and received his doctorate in 1889 with his thesis "Essay on the Immediate Data of Consciousness" and a Latin thesis on Aristotle's concept of place. In 1900 he took the chair of Greek and Latin philosophy at the Collège de France, then, from 1904, the chair of modern philosophy, and remained there until 1921. He was elected to the Académie française in 1914 and received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1927. His lectures at the Collège de France were a Parisian cultural event attended by a wide public. He died in Paris on 4 January 1941 under the German occupation, and historical records confirm that he renounced the honorary exemptions offered to him by the Vichy authorities, queuing in December 1940 to register as a Jew under the anti-Semitic statute in solidarity with fellow Jews, shortly before his death from bronchitis.

## Memory and the Body

In "Matter and Memory" (1896) Bergson addressed the problem of the relation between body and mind, and distinguished between two memories: habit memory, which is realized through repetition and stored in the body, and pure memory, which preserves the whole of the past with its details and remains in potentiality, the present calling up from it what is needed for action. The brain, in his view, plays the role of a selecting organ rather than a store: it determines what appears to consciousness according to the needs of action, and does not generate memories. In this he objected to the purely cerebral explanation of consciousness, though it remains a matter of debate among specialists in neuroscience and philosophy.

## Intuition and Method

Bergson makes intuition a philosophical method: the analytical intellect produces fixed concepts that serve practical action, but it is unable to grasp movement and becoming from within, and intuition is the ability to sympathize with duration from the inside. He explained this in "Introduction to Metaphysics" (1903) and in "The Creative Mind" (La Pensée et le mouvant, 1934). In "Laughter" (1900) he presented a theory that laughter is a social corrective to everything mechanical and rigid in human behavior.

## Religion and Morality

In his last major book, "The Two Sources of Morality and Religion" (1932), he distinguished between a closed morality based on social pressure and habit and an open morality carried by mystics and moral heroes out of love, and between a static religion that protects the group and a dynamic, mystical religion. This distinction influenced Karl Popper in "The Open Society and Its Enemies."

## Influence and Reception

Bergson influenced Marcel Proust, Charles Péguy, William James, and Whitehead, and Merleau-Ponty, Sartre, and Gilles Deleuze, who revived him in "Bergsonism" (1966). He influenced the Muslim thinker Muhammad Iqbal, who drew on the idea of pure time in "The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam" (1930), and a number of Arab writers and philosophers in the first half of the twentieth century who read him in French. In April 1922 he debated Einstein at the Société française de philosophie on relativity, then published "Duration and Simultaneity" (1922), criticizing what he saw as a confusion between physical time and lived time; this debate was later regarded as a defeat for Bergson from the physical standpoint, and his standing declined after the First World War.

## Critique and Limitations

Bertrand Russell objected to Bergson in "The Philosophy of Bergson" (1912) that his argument relies on metaphors instead of evidence, and that his distinction between quantitative and qualitative time confuses perception with physical reality. The vital impetus was criticized as not being a testable scientific explanation, and intuition as leaving knowledge without controls. The Catholic Church placed his books on the Index of Forbidden Books in 1914. Critics from phenomenology, among them Heidegger in "Being and Time," held that his lived time remains influenced by a conception of time as a flow of moments.
