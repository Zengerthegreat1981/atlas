# Kirk Schneider

Kirk J. Schneider is a contemporary American existential-humanistic psychologist, often counted among the third generation of the American humanistic-existential current. He has long been a faculty member at Saybrook University and is the author of an integrative approach that tries to move the current out of the dichotomy of technique versus philosophy.

## Context

Schneider writes in the line that runs from Rollo May and James Bugental to the present. He edited the *Journal of Humanistic Psychology* for a number of years and has argued for the continued scientific and cultural standing of the humanistic tradition after its decline in the face of cognitive-behavioural therapies, and for repairing its relation to empirical research. With James Pierson and James Bugental he co-edited *The Handbook of Humanistic Psychology* (first edition 2001, second edition 2015), a reference work on the history and applications of the movement.

## Main Ideas

**The paradoxical self.** In *The Paradoxical Self* (1990) he proposes that the human self is by nature paradoxical: it joins light and dark, individuality and belonging, life and death. The paradox is not a defect but the basic structure of the self.

**Constriction and expansion; the fluid center.** Schneider describes a polarity between constriction and expansion. Under threat people tend to constrict, clinging to the familiar and avoiding the new; when they rush into freedom without grounding they expand into chaos. Psychological health lies not in settling at one pole but in flexible movement between them, which he calls the "fluid center" of life.

**Awe.** In *Rediscovery of Awe* (2004) and *Awakening to Awe* (2009) he argues that awe, a blend of wonder and fear before what exceeds understanding, is a central dimension of human existence alongside anxiety. Confronting the human condition produces not fear alone but also amazement at mystery. His earlier *Horror and the Holy* (1993) read monster tales as expressions of both fear of losing control and attraction to it. He suggests that the loss of awe in technological culture feeds anxiety and the pull toward extremes.

**The polarized mind.** *The Polarized Mind* (2013) applies the same framework to political and psychological polarization, reading extremism as a response to existential anxiety that has constricted into rigid, absolutist thinking.

## In Practice

His integrative approach moves between levels of intervention, from attention to symptoms to attention to meaning, according to what the client can bear, instead of committing to a single level. It is set out in *Existential-Integrative Psychotherapy* (2008) and, with Orah Krug, in *Existential-Humanistic Therapy* (2010), a concise introduction to the roots of the approach in May, Bugental and Yalom and to its therapeutic stance of presence, attention to the client's immediate experience, and openness to the client's whole range of experience.

## Position and Criticism

His project can be read as an answer to the charge that the American current is either technical or literary, with no third option. His position is debated within the current: some regard him as a rescuer of the tradition; others consider his political writing on polarization to have drifted from the core of clinical practice. His work is more a synthesis and organization of a tradition than a new technique, so it offers few protocols that can be tested as cognitive therapies can, and its influence shows in the training of therapists and in the language of the field more than in outcome trials.
