# José Ortega y Gasset

José Ortega y Gasset (1883–1955) was a Spanish philosopher, essayist and public intellectual, the author of the famous formula "I am myself and my circumstance" (*Yo soy yo y mi circunstancia*). He is often mentioned alongside Unamuno as a Spanish voice parallel to German and French existentialism, though he is more accurately described as a philosopher of life whose thought developed in dialogue with phenomenology.

## Life and Career

Ortega was born in Madrid on 9 May 1883 into a family of journalists and publishers. He studied in Madrid and spent periods in Germany, notably in Marburg, where he absorbed Neo-Kantianism through the school of Hermann Cohen and Paul Natorp. In 1910 he became professor of metaphysics at the University of Madrid, and he founded the influential journal *Revista de Occidente* in 1923, which introduced Husserl, Heidegger and other European thinkers to the Spanish-speaking world. He left Spain during the Civil War (1936), lived in exile in Argentina and Portugal, returned to Spain in the 1940s, and founded the Institute of Humanities in Madrid in 1948 with his pupil Julián Marías. He died in Madrid on 18 October 1955.

## What He Gave

His first book, *Meditations on Quixote* (1914), contains the formula "I am myself and my circumstance, and if I do not save it, I do not save myself." The self is never understood in isolation from the historical and material circumstance surrounding it: the circumstance is not an external constraint on freedom but a structural part of the self. This idea is often compared with Heidegger's concept of thrownness, though the two developed largely independently.

From this starting point he developed **ratiovitalism** (*razón vital*): reason is not a detached faculty but a function of life, and life, in his phrase, is the "radical reality" in which every other reality appears. Related to it is his **perspectivism**: each person sees reality from a particular vital standpoint, so that truth is reached by combining perspectives rather than by abandoning them. He also stressed **historical reason**, the view that human beings have no fixed nature but a history.

## Social and Political Thought

*The Revolt of the Masses* (1929–30) is his best-known book. It describes the rise of the "mass man," who feels no obligation to standards beyond himself and demands rights without accepting responsibilities, and it argues that European civilisation depends on creative minorities. In *Invertebrate Spain* (1921) he analysed Spain's political fragmentation. Readers have taken his work as a conservative-liberal critique of mass society, though his elitist vocabulary has drawn criticism as undemocratic.

## Place in the Tradition

He did not call his philosophy existentialism, and his classification here rests on an objective convergence (the self and circumstance), not on a declared affiliation. In the literature he is classified sometimes within existentialism and sometimes as an independent philosopher of life (*Lebensphilosophie*) influenced by Dilthey, Nietzsche and Husserl. Among those he influenced are Julián Marías, José Gaos and María Zambrano.

## Limits

Much of his writing consists of essays, lectures and newspaper articles rather than a systematic treatise, and some of his central works (such as *Historical Reason* and *Man and People*) were published posthumously from lecture material. His elitism and his remarks on mass society remain contested.
