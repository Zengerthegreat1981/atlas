# Eleanor Leacock

Eleanor Leacock (1922–1987) was an American anthropologist and an influential figure in the development of feminist anthropology. Working in the Marxist tradition, she argued that women's subordination was not a universal of human nature but a historical product of class society, private property, and the state, and she showed, through ethnography of the Montagnais-Naskapi (Innu) people of Labrador, that egalitarian hunter-gatherer societies had known a very different organisation of gender. Her book *Myths of Male Dominance* (1981) and her edition of Friedrich Engels's *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1972) made her a prominent Marxist-feminist thinker of the twentieth century.

## Life and Career

Leacock was born in 1922. She studied at Barnard College and at Columbia University, where she took her doctorate in anthropology in the early 1950s. Her dissertation research took her to Labrador, where she studied the Montagnais-Naskapi, an Innu people whose lives had been transformed by the fur trade. She taught at the City College of New York (CUNY), where she was a professor of anthropology, and she was active in the political movements of her time — a committed Marxist and a supporter of anti-colonial struggles. She died in 1987.

Her career was shaped by a double commitment: to ethnography and to political engagement. She insisted that the discipline must study its own society's inequalities as well as distant cultures, and she was a prominent figure in the anthropology of women. Her studies of schooling in New York City, *Teaching and Learning in City Schools* (1969), reflected that commitment.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Leacock's central argument was that the subordination of women is a historical phenomenon, not a biological one. In *The Montagnais "Hunting Territory" and the Fur Trade* (1954), she argued that family hunting territories were not an aboriginal institution but developed with the fur trade. In later essays, drawing also on seventeenth-century Jesuit records, she argued that Montagnais-Naskapi society had been egalitarian, with consensual decision-making and considerable female autonomy, and that trade and missionary colonization undermined these relations. This work became an important contribution to the anthropology of colonialism, suggesting that "traditional" gender roles could be products of colonial contact.

In her introduction to Engels's *The Origin of the Family* (1972), Leacock revived and revised the Marxist account of women's oppression, arguing that the "world-historical defeat of the female sex" was tied to the emergence of private property and the state, and that it could therefore be undone. *Myths of Male Dominance* (1981) collected her essays on women cross-culturally, attacking the assumption — common in anthropology at the time — that male dominance was universal, and showing that in many egalitarian societies women enjoyed autonomy, authority, and respect.

Leacock's Marxist approach also shaped her view of anthropology itself. She argued that anthropologists must attend to history, to political economy, and to the material conditions of life, and that a purely cultural account of gender — one that treated women's roles as a matter of symbols and meanings — missed the point. Gender inequality, for Leacock, was a matter of who controls resources, whose labour is valued, and who makes decisions, and these are questions of power, not of culture alone.

## Influence and Critique

Leacock's influence on feminist anthropology was foundational. She provided the historical and materialist framework within which a generation of feminist anthropologists worked, and her insistence that gender relations must be studied in their historical and economic context shaped the field's development. Her work on the Montagnais-Naskapi remains a classic of the anthropology of colonialism, and her edition of Engels was widely used by students of Marxist feminism.

Her work has also been criticised. Some feminist anthropologists argued that her Marxist framework underestimated the autonomy of gender as a system of meaning, and that not all forms of male dominance can be reduced to class. Others questioned whether the egalitarianism she described among the Montagnais-Naskapi was as complete as she suggested, and whether her account romanticised hunter-gatherer life. Still others noted that her focus on class and colonialism left less room for the analysis of race and sexuality that later feminist anthropology would develop. These criticisms have not diminished her standing: Leacock's argument that women's subordination has a history — and therefore a future, in which it might end — remains one of the most powerful ideas in feminist anthropology, and her work continues to be read as a model of politically engaged ethnography.
