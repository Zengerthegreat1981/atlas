# Rudolf Carnap

A German-American philosopher, one of the most prominent members of the Vienna Circle, and the most important theorist of logical positivism in its methodical formulation. He was born in 1891 in Ronsdorf, Germany, and died in 1970 in Santa Monica, California. He emigrated to the United States in 1935 with the rise of Nazism, and worked as a professor at the University of Chicago from 1936 to 1952, then at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton (1952–54), then at the University of California, Los Angeles, from 1954 until his retirement.

## Formation and Career

Carnap studied physics, mathematics, and philosophy at the University of Jena, and attended Gottlob Frege's lectures on symbolic logic between 1910 and 1914, which shaped his entire philosophical direction, and received his doctorate in 1921 with a dissertation on the concept of space. In 1926 he joined the Vienna Circle at the invitation of Moritz Schlick and became one of its most active members, and then taught at the German University in Prague from 1931 until his emigration. With Hans Reichenbach he co-edited the journal "Erkenntnis," which became the platform of logical positivism.

## Basic Ideas

Carnap's ambition was that philosophy be transformed from conflicting doctrines into a logical analysis of the language of science. In "The Logical Structure of the World" he tried to construct all the concepts of science by graduated logical definition starting from a single relation among elementary experiences, namely recollection of similarity, and thereby wanted to show that all scientific sentences are reducible to the contents of experience. In his article "The Elimination of Metaphysics Through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932) he held that the sentences of metaphysics are devoid of cognitive meaning because they are not verifiable and are not analytic propositions, and he exemplified this with Heidegger's phrase "the Nothing nothings."

He then shifted, in "The Logical Syntax of Language" (1934), from an attempt to build a single justified language to the principle of tolerance: each person has the freedom to build his own language and logical rules as he wishes, and the philosopher's work is limited to stating the conditions and studying the consequences of each framework. This idea moved the debate from "which language is correct?" to "which language is more useful for a given purpose?" In "Meaning and Necessity" (1947) he developed a semantics based on intension and extension to analyse sentences containing modal forms.

In the 1950s he turned to inductive logic, proposing the degree of confirmation that evidence gives a hypothesis as a logical relation between two sentences, and formulated from it a calculus of how probability changes. In "Philosophical Foundations of Physics" (1966) he treated the structure of physical concepts, measurement, and causality in an exposition still used in teaching philosophy of science.

## Reception in the Arab World

The Arab reader came to know logical positivism in the 1950s through Zaki Naguib Mahmoud, who presented its ideas in "Positivist Logic" (1951) and "The Myth of Metaphysics" (1953), adopting the criterion of meaning that Carnap and his colleagues discussed, and then later modified his position toward a reading of the heritage. This tendency stirred debate between those who saw in it a liberation of thought from vagueness and those who saw in it the exclusion of values and religion from the sphere of meaning.

## Limits of the Project and Its Critique

Carnap's programme faced criticism from within the analytic tradition itself. Quine, in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), attacked the analytic-synthetic distinction on which Carnap's division between internal and external questions rests, and held that the sensory reduction Carnap sought is an illusion. Thomas Kuhn and other researchers showed that science changes through revolutions in frameworks, which Carnap's conception of a fixed language does not easily accommodate. The verification criterion itself remained exposed to unresolved difficulties, among them that general laws are never finally verified, and that the criterion does not satisfy its own condition. As for Carnap, he gradually softened the criterion to confirmability.

## What He Contributed

- **"The Logical Structure of the World" (Aufbau, 1928)**: a programme for reducing all cognitive concepts to basic sensory data (*das Eigenpsychische*).
- **"Logical Syntax" (Logische Syntax, 1934)**: the Principle of Tolerance in constructing languages, and the rejection of justifying a single logical language as though it were correct by nature.
- **"Logical Foundations of Probability" (1950)**: a theory of logical probability as the axis for analysing induction.
- **"Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950)**: in it he distinguished internal questions posed within a linguistic framework from external questions about the choice of the framework itself.

## His Most Important Works

- *Der logische Aufbau der Welt* (1928).
- *Logische Syntax der Sprache* (1934).
- *Meaning and Necessity* (1947).
- *Logical Foundations of Probability* (1950).
- *Philosophical Foundations of Physics* (1966).

## Influence

Carnap is considered the most important bridge between European logical positivism and American Quinean naturalism. Quine was his friend and foremost interlocutor; he met him in Prague in 1933, and a philosophical dialogue began between them that lasted decades.
