# Jonas R. Rappeport

An American psychiatrist (died 8 September 2020, aged 95) who organized forensic psychiatry as a professional field in the United States. He was born in Baltimore on 16 October 1924, raised there, and graduated from the University of Maryland School of Medicine in 1952. Early in his career he worked with **Manfred Guttmacher**, who directed the medical office of the Supreme Bench of Baltimore. Rappeport later became chief medical officer for the Circuit Court for Baltimore City. He retired from forensic practice in 1999 and continued to teach in the Maryland fellowship.

## Background: Criminal Responsibility in Islamic Tradition and Anglo-American Law

Rappeport's work addresses an old question: when is a mentally disturbed person held accountable for their act? In Islamic jurisprudence the principle is stated that "the pen is lifted from three: from the sleeper until he wakes, from the child until he reaches puberty, and from the insane person until he regains reason." Jurists built on it the principle that legal capacity and responsibility revolve around reason, and distinguished between permanent, continuous insanity and intermittent insanity, in which the person is held accountable during lucid periods. Jurists also discussed the guardianship of an insane person over his property and the role of his guardian. Michael Dols's study of the madman in medieval Islamic society and Rudolph Peters's study of crime and punishment in Islamic law address these topics. In English law the M'Naghten rules (1843) were the reference point, later adopted widely in the United States. It is a cognitive standard that asks whether the defendant understood the nature of the act and that it was wrong. In the United States, the *Durham v. United States* decision (1954) then added the "product of mental disease" standard, which courts later abandoned. The American Law Institute's Model Penal Code standard (1962) combines a cognitive and a volitional element. Forensic psychiatry grew as a profession needed by the courts in this climate.

## Contributions

### Establishing the American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law (AAPL)

Rappeport was a principal founder of the **American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law** (AAPL, established in 1969), and served as its first president and, for many years, as its **medical director**. In that role he helped build the main American professional society in this field, which contributed to forensic psychiatry's later recognition as a medical subspecialty.

### Writing on Risk Assessment

Rappeport edited the book *The Clinical Evaluation of the Dangerousness of the Mentally Ill*, published in 1967 by Charles C. Thomas. It is one of the early works that brought together the experience of the Baltimore forensic clinic in estimating risk. The subject remained a focus of discussion afterward, including the studies of Henry Steadman and colleagues on risk assessment.

### An Early Specialized Fellowship

In 1968 Rappeport obtained funding for a forensic fellow at the University of Maryland. The program is one of the oldest specialized forensic psychiatry fellowships in the United States. By his retirement he had trained several dozen fellows, and he continued to teach after 1999.

### Notable Forensic Consultations

He was a consultant in several politically prominent cases, including **Arthur Bremer**, who shot Alabama governor George Wallace in 1972, **Sara Jane Moore**, who attempted to assassinate President Gerald Ford in 1975, and the case of **John Hinckley Jr.**, who shot President Ronald Reagan in 1981 and was acquitted by reason of insanity in 1982. The Hinckley verdict produced widespread anger and the federal Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984, which placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant and narrowed the standard. Many states made similar changes. Henry Steadman and colleagues later studied those statutory changes.

## Impact

- The academy, the Maryland fellowship, and the examination work of court clinics were part of the process by which forensic psychiatry later gained recognition as a psychiatric subspecialty in the United States. Rappeport was a principal organizer of that infrastructure. The American Psychiatric Association gave him the Isaac Ray Award in 1984. AAPL named a resident fellowship after him in 1985.
- The Maryland fellowship was one of the early models for forensic training programs at other American universities.

## Limits and Criticism

Unlike some of his contemporaries, Rappeport did not produce an independent theoretical framework or classification model under his name in forensic psychiatry. His main contribution was **institutional, training-based, and applied**, namely building the professional society, training programs, and documentation of reference cases, rather than theory. For this reason he remains less present in the theoretical literature than in his actual influence on shaping the specialty as an academic and professional institution. The specialty he helped build faces enduring ethical questions, most notably the "dual agency" problem of divided loyalty between patient and court discussed by Alan Stone and Paul Appelbaum, and the limited predictive accuracy of clinical risk prediction shown by later studies.
