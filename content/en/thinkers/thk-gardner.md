# Howard Gardner

An American developmental psychologist, author of the **theory of multiple intelligences** (1983), which shifted the educational debate from the question "How intelligent is this child?" to "How is this child intelligent?" It is at the same time one of the most widely adopted psychological theories in education and one with limited empirical support.

## What he contributed

- **The theory of multiple intelligences (MI)**: In *Frames of Mind* (1983) he rejected the idea that intelligence is a single general ability measured by one number, and proposed instead **relatively independent intelligences**: linguistic, logical-mathematical, spatial, musical, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, and intrapersonal. He later added the **naturalist**, and left the "existential" as an open question.
- **Eight criteria for what counts as an intelligence**: The most important feature of the method: he did not derive his list from factor analysis but from **declared criteria**, including: the possibility of isolating it through specific brain damage, the existence of exceptional individuals (prodigies and savants), a distinct developmental history, an evolutionary history, and a specific symbolic coding system. The main source of his theory is therefore **neuropsychology and neurological cases**, not psychological tests.
- **Critique of the intelligence test**: His strongest practical impact. He argued that an intelligence test measures two or three domains (the linguistic, the logical, and some of the spatial) and then generalizes to "intelligence" as a whole, and that this generalization is a cultural decision, not an empirical result.
- **Project Zero and assessment of understanding**: From within the Harvard Graduate School of Education he and his colleagues developed approaches to **performance assessment** and "teaching for understanding", that is, measuring what learners do with their knowledge rather than what they remember of it.
- **The Five Minds and GoodWork**: At a later stage he moved to professional ethics: *Five Minds for the Future* (2006), and the **GoodWork** project on the meaning of excellent and responsible work.

## His place in the field

Gardner was influenced by **Jerome Bruner** and **Piaget**, and his work is an extension of cognitive developmentalism, not of the psychometric lineage. His position in this debate is **exactly opposite to Spearman's**: Spearman established the general factor (g), and Gardner denied that it has any educational meaning. In this he stands alongside **Thurstone** (primary mental abilities) and **Sternberg** (the triarchic theory), and he preceded the formulations of **emotional intelligence** by Salovey, Mayer and Goleman, which draw on his interpersonal and intrapersonal intelligences.

## Major works

- *Frames of Mind: The Theory of Multiple Intelligences* (1983): the founding text.
- *The Mind's New Science* (1985): a history of the cognitive sciences.
- *Intelligence Reframed* (1999): a revision of the theory and the addition of naturalist intelligence.
- *Five Minds for the Future* (2006).

## The limitation

**The theory of multiple intelligences is not an empirically proven theory, and this is a point of agreement in mainstream psychology, not a fringe opinion.** The core objections are three: (1) no valid measurement instrument has been produced for the eight intelligences, so the theory is practically untestable; (2) the abilities it calls independent intelligences show positive intercorrelations in the data, which is precisely what the **general factor** (g), which it denies, explains; (3) there is no evidence that teaching material in "multiple modes" actually improves learning. The theory was received widely in education because of its **moral appeal** (every child is intelligent in his or her own way) more than its evidential support. Gardner himself has distinguished his theory from the notion of "learning styles," arguing in a 2013 newspaper article that multiple intelligences are not learning styles. This entry offers no educational guidance and no evaluation of individuals.

## Biography and context

Gardner was born in 1943 in Scranton, Pennsylvania, to a Jewish family that had emigrated from Nazi Germany. He studied at Harvard, where he was influenced by Erik Erikson and Jerome Bruner, and carried out his research in neurocognitive work with Norman Geschwind at the Boston Veterans Administration Hospital, where he saw how stroke patients lose one ability while retaining another. He combined these two worlds: the study of children's artistic development with Project Zero, which he joined early on, and the study of injured brains. From this combination came his thesis that the mind is a set of distinct modules, not a single machine.

## After multiple intelligences

Gardner revised his theory in *Intelligence Reframed* (1999), defining intelligence as a biopsychological potential to process information of a particular kind within cultural systems, and he rejected translating the intelligences into curricular templates. He expanded on it in *Creating Minds* (1993), a study of seven creative figures, each with a dominant intelligence: Freud, Einstein, Picasso, Stravinsky, Eliot, Graham and Gandhi. He also wrote *Truth, Beauty, and Goodness Reframed* (2011), on rethinking the virtues in an age of digital learning and cultural relativism.

## Further limits and critique

Willingham (2004), Waterhouse (2006), and others suggested that the eight intelligences are closer to distinct talents and abilities than to independent cognitive systems. Gardner replied that the name "intelligence" is a conceptual decision about respecting diversity, and that he does not claim a testable system on the model of the general factor. The educational impact remains tangible in curricula, but it rests on moral appreciation, not on a controlled experiment demonstrating superiority.
