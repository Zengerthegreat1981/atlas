# Bertrand Russell

A British philosopher and a leading founder of analytic philosophy. He co-authored "Principia Mathematica" (1910–1913) with Whitehead, and developed logical atomism in "Our Knowledge of the External World" (1914) and the lectures on "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism" (1918).

## What He Offered

- **The theory of descriptions** (1905) in his essay "On Denoting": an analysis of definite descriptions (such as "the present King of France") into their logical components, to solve the paradoxes of existence.
- **The theory of types** (1908) to prevent paradoxes in set theory.
- **Logical atomism** (1911–1918): the world is composed of simple facts, and the ideal language is analyzed into simple propositions.
- **Neutral monism** (1918–1924): reducing both the material and the mental to a primary neutral stuff that is neither mind nor matter.

## Most Important Works

- *The Principles of Mathematics* (1903).
- *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913, with Whitehead).
- *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912).
- *Our Knowledge of the External World* (1914).
- *The Analysis of Mind* (1921).
- *The Analysis of Matter* (1927).

## Impact

Russell directly influenced Wittgenstein (who came to him with a recommendation from Frege), logical positivism, and analytic philosophy of language. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950 "in recognition of his varied and significant writings in which he champions humanitarian ideals and freedom of thought." In 1955 he signed with Einstein the "Russell–Einstein Manifesto," warning of the danger of nuclear weapons, and he remained active in the campaign against nuclear armament and in public causes until the late 1960s, that is, until near his death in 1970.

## Life and Formation

Bertrand Arthur William Russell was born on 18 May 1872 in Trellech in Monmouthshire in Wales, the grandson of Lord John Russell, the British prime minister, and he lost his parents young and grew up in his grandfather's house. He studied mathematics and then moral sciences at Trinity College, Cambridge (1890–1894), and was influenced at the start by the Hegelian idealism of McTaggart and Bradley, then revolted against it with George Edward Moore around 1898, and considered this a liberation. He inherited the title and became the 3rd Earl Russell in 1931. He died on 2 February 1970 in Wales.

## Logic and the Foundations of Mathematics

In "The Principles of Mathematics" (1903) Russell tried to reduce mathematics to logic, but in 1901 he discovered a paradox in set theory (the set of sets that do not contain themselves) and communicated it to Frege in a letter in 1902, which struck at the core of Frege's project in the "Basic Laws of Arithmetic". To treat the paradox he devised the theory of types (1908), then produced with Whitehead "Principia Mathematica" in three volumes (1910–1913) from Cambridge University Press. In the essay "On Denoting" (the journal "Mind," 1905) he presented the theory of descriptions, which analyzes a phrase such as "the present King of France is bald" into three propositions: that there is a king of France, that there is only one, and that he is bald, so that the problem of speaking about something that does not exist dissolves. Ramsey considered this theory "a paradigm of philosophy."

## Epistemology and Logical Atomism

In "The Problems of Philosophy" (1912) Russell distinguished between "knowledge by acquaintance," which concerns sense-data and universals, and "knowledge by description," by which we know external things and persons. In "Our Knowledge of the External World" (1914) he tried to construct the physical world from sense-data by logical construction. He then presented in the lectures on "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism" (1918) that the world is composed of atomic facts to which atomic propositions correspond. In this phase he was influenced by his student Ludwig Wittgenstein, whom he taught at Cambridge (1911–1913) and for whose "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus" he wrote the introduction (1922). In "The Analysis of Mind" (1921) and "The Analysis of Matter" (1927) he adopted "neutral monism": neither matter nor mind, but neutral events that are organized in two ways.

## The Public Philosopher

Russell wrote works directed to the general reader on ethics, education, politics and religion, among them "Marriage and Morals" (1929), "Why I Am Not a Christian" (1927) and "A History of Western Philosophy" (1945), which Zaki Naguib Mahmoud translated into Arabic in three parts (Cairo, Committee for Authorship, Translation and Publication, 1954–1957), while Fu'ad Zakariyya translated "The Wisdom of the West" (Alam al-Ma'rifa, 1983). He opposed the First World War, and was dismissed from Trinity College in 1916, and was sentenced in 1918 to six months' imprisonment, serving about five months in Brixton Prison, where he wrote "Introduction to Mathematical Philosophy" (1919). He visited China (1920–1921) and wrote about it. In 1940 he was barred by court ruling from teaching at the College of the City of New York because of his views on sexual ethics. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1950, and signed the 1955 Russell–Einstein Manifesto against nuclear weapons, which led to the Pugwash conferences. He also initiated the International War Crimes Tribunal on the Vietnam War, known as the "Russell Tribunal" (1966–1967).

## Impact and Reception

With Frege, Moore and Wittgenstein, Russell represented the starting point of analytic philosophy. He influenced the logical positivists in Vienna and Carnap and Quine. In the Arab world he was among the widely translated Western philosophers and an influence on the generation educated in the Egyptian universities, including Zaki Naguib Mahmoud, who moved from logical positivism to the study of the heritage.

## Limits and Critique

In 1931 Gödel proved that a formal system sufficient for arithmetic cannot be both complete and consistent, thereby undermining the project of reducing mathematics to logic in its strong form; moreover, the axioms of infinity and reducibility that Russell adopted are not purely logical propositions. Strawson in "On Referring" (1950) criticized the theory of descriptions on the ground that a phrase such as "the present King of France" is neither true nor false if the referent does not exist, and that it confuses the sentence with its use. The later Wittgenstein criticized logical atomism on the ground that it presupposes an ideal language that does not exist. His later ethical position, a broadly emotivist view that ethical judgments express desires and feelings, was faulted for conflicting with his moral enthusiasm in politics.
