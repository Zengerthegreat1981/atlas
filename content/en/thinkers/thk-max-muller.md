# Friedrich Max Müller

A philologist and mythologist, German-born and resident in Britain (1823–1900). He served as professor of comparative philology at Oxford and is regarded as the academic founder of what he himself called the "Science of Religion" (*Religionswissenschaft*). It was the first systematic attempt to study religions by comparison using the tools of linguistic science, rather than the tools of theology or polemical missionary argument.

## Biography

Müller was born in Dessau, Germany, studied at Leipzig, and continued in Berlin and Paris, where Eugène Burnouf was an important teacher. He moved to England in 1846 to study the Veda manuscripts in the libraries of London and Oxford, and settled there. He held the chair of modern European languages (1854) and then the chair of comparative philology (1868) at Oxford, and died there in 1900.

## From the Philology of the Indo-European Languages to the Comparison of Religions

Müller began his career as editor of the Sanskrit Vedic texts, publishing the *Rig Veda* with the commentary of Sayanacharya in six volumes (1849–1874). From his philological background he developed his central thesis: that religious myths arise from a gradual linguistic "misunderstanding." Ancient poetic metaphors for natural phenomena (the sun, the dawn, the storm) hardened over generations into names of persons and gods. Their metaphorical origin was forgotten and they became "myths" in the literal sense. He formulated this in his essay "Comparative Mythology" (1856), named the process the "disease of language," and gave his name to what became known as the school of "Solar Mythology." He also popularised the term "henotheism" (the worship of one god at a time as if he were supreme), as a description of an early stage of Vedic religion.

## The Lectures on the Science of Religion (1870) and the Comparative Programme

In his lectures at the Royal Institution in London in 1870, published in 1873 as *Introduction to the Science of Religion* (Longmans), Müller announced his founding principle: "He who knows one, knows none," a formulation adapted from Goethe's remark about languages. That is, understanding any religion requires comparing it with other religions within a unified methodological framework, just as the grammar of a language cannot be understood except by comparing it with related languages. He called for treating religious texts in the manner of historical-linguistic documents open to critical analysis, not as an infallible sacred revelation. This made his project ecclesiastically controversial in his time, despite his personal piety. He followed these with the Hibbert Lectures (1878) and the Gifford Lectures (1888–1892).

## The *Sacred Books of the East* Project and the Qur'an

Müller supervised the series *Sacred Books of the East* (Oxford, 1879–1910, fifty volumes), the first systematic and comprehensive English translation of Indian, Chinese, Persian and Islamic religious texts. It made the Upanishads, the Avesta and the Tao Te Ching available to a wide Western academic readership for comparative study. The series included the Qur'an in the translation of Edward Henry Palmer (E. H. Palmer), in two volumes (6 and 9, 1880), and Müller wrote its introduction. Here the limits of his project appear: the Qur'an was placed among the "books of the East" as a literary text, and the framework of the "Semitic" and "Aryan" peoples that he inherited from his time was expanded. Within this framework Müller accepted the division of religions into Aryan, Semitic and Turanian, a linguistic-racial classification close to the division of Ernest Renan, who claimed that the Semitic mind is by nature monotheistic and incapable of science. Jamal al-Din al-Afghani responded to Renan in 1883 in an article published in *Journal des Débats*. He argued that religion, whether Islam or Christianity, stands at the beginnings of societies as an obstacle to science, that conditions change with changing societies, and that it is therefore not valid to confine the cause to Islam alone. Müller himself criticised, in his later years, the use of "Aryan" as a racial term, insisting that it was a linguistic concept, and that speaking of an "Aryan race" in racial terms was as absurd as speaking of a "dolichocephalic dictionary."

## Criticism and the Historical Decline of His School

His theory of "solar mythology" lost the scientific contest to a later generation of evolutionary anthropologists, above all Edward Tylor and Andrew Lang. They proposed in its place "animism" (*Animism*), holding that the origin of religion is belief in spiritual beings rather than a fossilised linguistic metaphor, an explanation that dominated anthropology until the early twentieth century. Post-colonial critics, among them Edward Said and David Chidester, argued that the "Sacred Books" project produced knowledge about the East within a European colonial framework. It selected the "ancient" texts it regarded as the standard and neglected living traditions, and in this way participated in creating the "religions of the world." Nevertheless, Müller remains the founder of the institutional and methodological framework of the science of religion as an academic field independent of theology. This framework was later inherited by the German school of the history of religions and then by the phenomenology of religion in Otto and Eliade.
