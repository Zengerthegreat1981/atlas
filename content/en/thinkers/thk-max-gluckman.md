# Max Gluckman: Ritual, Conflict, and Social Drama

Max Gluckman (1911–1975) was a South African-born British social anthropologist who founded the Manchester School of anthropology. He is best known for arguing that social order is sustained through, and not despite, conflict, and for his analysis of "rituals of rebellion," ceremonies in which normal hierarchies are ritually inverted. His ideas about conflict, law, and social process shaped the study of ritual and political anthropology, and his students, among them Victor Turner, carried them further.

## Biographical and Intellectual Context

Gluckman was born in Johannesburg in 1911, the son of Jewish immigrants of Eastern European origin, and grew up in South Africa under conditions of racial inequality. He studied at the University of the Witwatersrand, where he was influenced by the anthropologists Winifred Hoernlé and Isaac Schapera, and then went to Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar in the mid-1930s. At Oxford he worked under **E. E. Evans-Pritchard** and took his doctorate; he was also influenced by the structural-functionalism of Radcliffe-Brown and by Marxist thought.

He carried out fieldwork in Zululand (1936–1938) and among the **Lozi (Barotse)** of Northern Rhodesia (now Zambia) in 1939–1940. In 1941 he became director of the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute in Northern Rhodesia, which he made a centre of research on social change in central Africa. He moved to Manchester in 1949 as professor of social anthropology, where he remained until 1971. He died in Jerusalem in 1975.

Gluckman insisted that anthropology study African societies in their contemporary colonial context rather than reconstruct an imagined precolonial past. His essay "Analysis of a Social Situation in Modern Zululand" (published 1940 and 1942) examined the opening of a bridge in Zululand to show how Africans and Europeans formed a single social field, and it is often cited as the origin of the "situational analysis" and "extended case" approach.

## The Manchester School

At Manchester Gluckman gathered a group of researchers, the Manchester School, who emphasized detailed analysis of actual cases and conflicts, social process and change, the study of contemporary rather than traditional society, and political awareness of the colonial context. His students and colleagues included Victor Turner, J. Clyde Mitchell, Elizabeth Colson, Frederick Bailey, and Arnold Epstein. The Manchester seminar became famous for its intensity.

## Conflict and Cross-Cutting Ties

Gluckman rejected the view of societies as harmonious integrated wholes. In *Custom and Conflict in Africa* (1955), including the essay "The Peace in the Feud," he argued that conflict is structural: because people belong to multiple groups (kin, neighbors, ritual and political communities), their loyalties cut across one another, and the resulting cross-cutting ties inhibit feuds from escalating into permanent division. Conflict within these limits thus helps to bind a society together.

## Rituals of Rebellion

His best-known essay, "Rituals of Rebellion in South-East Africa" (1954; reprinted in *Order and Rebellion in Tribal Africa*, 1963), analyzed ceremonies among the Zulu and Swazi in which normal rules of authority and gender were temporarily reversed or in which subjects expressed hostility toward the king. The Swazi *Incwala*, a first-fruits kingship ceremony, was central to his discussion. He argued that such rituals expressed social tensions in a controlled way, while affirming the institution of kingship, so that rebellion within ritual forms reinforced the established order. He distinguished this from revolution, which sought to change the social order itself.

## Law and Judgment

In *The Judicial Process among the Barotse of Northern Rhodesia* (1955), Gluckman analyzed Lozi court cases to show how judges reason, use the standard of the "reasonable man," and reconcile conflicting norms in order to restore relationships. The book helped found the anthropology of law. He later debated the comparison of Lozi and Western jurisprudence with Paul Bohannan, who argued for studying local legal concepts in their own terms.

## Major Works

- *Rituals of Rebellion in South-East Africa* (1954).
- *The Judicial Process among the Barotse of Northern Rhodesia* (1955).
- *Custom and Conflict in Africa* (1955).
- *Order and Rebellion in Tribal Africa* (1963).
- *Politics, Law and Ritual in Tribal Society* (1965).

## Influence and Critique

Gluckman made case-based ethnography a method for theory, and he established political and legal anthropology as central fields. Critics have argued that his analysis of rituals of rebellion is itself functionalist, assuming that ritual serves integration, and that the "safety valve" interpretation is difficult to test. Others contend that it underestimates the possibility of real social transformation, that his framework gives little attention to gender, and that African politics and resistance require more explicit historical analysis than his approach provided. His work nevertheless shaped later research on ritual, conflict, law, and social change.
