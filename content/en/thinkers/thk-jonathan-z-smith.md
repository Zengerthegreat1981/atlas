# Jonathan Zittell Smith

An American historian of religions (1938–2017), professor at the University of Chicago (the same institution that employed Eliade), and one of the most influential critics of the methodological foundations on which the field of "comparative religion" itself was built. He was not merely a researcher within the field but a questioner of its core premises. He earned his PhD from Yale University in 1969, served as Robert O. Anderson Distinguished Service Professor, and was dean of the college of the university (The College) from 1977 to 1982.

## *Imagining Religion: From Babylon to Jonestown* (1982)

In this major work (University of Chicago Press), Smith put forward his best-known formulation: "religion" is the invention of the academic scholar; it has no independent existence outside the academic study that defines it ("Religion is solely the creation of the scholar's study"). This does not deny that the practices and beliefs we call "religious" exist; it insists that grouping them together under a single umbrella called "religion" is an analytic act performed by the researcher, not a ready-made objective fact in the world. He returned to the idea in his essay "Religion, Religions, Religious" in *Critical Terms for Religious Studies* (1998), where he traced the history of the European term *religio* and its transformation into a general category.

## "Religion" and religion: The Problem of the Term in Arabic

Smith's thesis gains clarity when set against the concept of "religion" in Arabic, since the word that translates "religion" is not an exact equivalent. The Qur'an uses *din* in the sense of recompense ("Master of the Day of Judgment", *malik yawm al-din*), of obedience and worship ("Indeed, the religion with God is Islam", Al 'Imran 3:19), and of law and community ("For you is your religion, and for me is my religion", al-Kafirun 109:6). Classical books of sects and creeds did not adopt a general category resembling the modern European "religion"; instead they had their own classifications. Al-Shahrastani, in *al-Milal wa-l-Nihal* (c. 1127–1128 CE), distinguished the peoples of the world into "people of communities" (*ahl al-milal*, those with scripture) and "people of opinions and sects" (*ahl al-ahwa' wa-l-nihal*), with scripture and prophecy as a main criterion of division, not an abstract concept of "religion". This case illustrates what Smith says: classification is a craft formed within an intellectual tradition that has its own reasons. Wilfred Cantwell Smith, in *The Meaning and End of Religion* (1962), reformulated the question by arguing that "Islam", "Christianity" and "Hinduism" are reified names for living trajectories. Talal Asad, in "The Construction of Religion as an Anthropological Category" (1993), added that the modern concept of religion is the product of a specific European Christian history.

## "In Comparison a Magic Dwells": Critique of the Phenomenological Comparative Method

In his influential essay "In Comparison a Magic Dwells" (in *Imagining Religion*), Smith directed a sharp methodological critique at the prevailing manner of religious comparison among Eliade and his peers: selecting phenomena that are formally similar from historical and cultural contexts that are very distant, such as comparing an Australian ritual with an ancient Jewish one, without any explicit methodological justification for why these two were chosen from thousands of possibilities. He described this as closer to "magic" than to a disciplined scientific method. He had already criticized the maps of comparison drawn by Frazer and Eliade in *Map Is Not Territory* (Brill, 1978), arguing that the map is not the territory. In *To Take Place: Toward Theory in Ritual* (University of Chicago Press, 1987), he reconceived ritual as the production of a meaningful "place".

## *Drudgery Divine* (1990)

In *Drudgery Divine: On the Comparison of Early Christianities and the Religions of Late Antiquity* (University of Chicago Press, 1990), Smith applied his critique to a historical case: the history of comparing early Christianity with Hellenistic mystery religions, showing that many academic comparisons were in essence a continuation of an internal Protestant-Catholic sectarian dispute dressed in neutral academic language. He also wrote the essay "Dying and Rising Gods" in the *Encyclopedia of Religion* (1987), in which he argued that Frazer's category of "dying and rising gods" is not well supported by the evidence.

## Critique of Girard: The "Domestication of the Sacrifice"

At a debate at Stanford University in 1983 (published in *Violent Origins*, Stanford, 1987, edited by Robert Hamerton-Kelly), which brought together Smith with René Girard and Walter Burkert, Smith directed a specific critique at Girard: the Girardian mechanism of the scapegoat is built on exceptional literary-mythological texts, whereas actual ritual sacrifice in the ethnographic record is a domesticated and tightly controlled process, far from the violent and random chaos assumed by Girard's scenario.

## Position in the Field and Limits of the Critique

Smith occupies a unique position. He is not reductionist: he does not see religion as a mere psychological or social illusion, as Freud or Durkheim did; and he is not non-reductionist in the Eliadean sense, since he does not assume a transcendent essence of the sacred. Rather, he is a methodological critic who questions the analytic tools themselves, the concept of "religion" and the method of comparison, among both phenomenologists and reductionists alike. His critique pushed a generation of researchers to justify their comparative choices explicitly and influenced the history of the concept, as in Tomoko Masuzawa's *The Invention of World Religions* (University of Chicago Press, 2005) and Brent Nongbri's *Before Religion* (Yale University Press, 2013). Wendy Doniger, in *The Implied Spider* (Columbia University Press, 1998), defended comparison as indispensable, in implicit contrast to Smith's sceptical stance. A limitation of the thesis is that it may leave the question unanswered: if "religion" is the scholar's craft, by what criterion is one classification preferred over another?
