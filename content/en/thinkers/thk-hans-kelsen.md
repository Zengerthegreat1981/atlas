# Hans Kelsen

An Austrian jurist and philosopher of law (1881–1973), one of the leading theorists of legal positivism in the twentieth century and author of the **Pure Theory of Law** (*Reine Rechtslehre*). He worked as a professor in Vienna and took part in drafting the Austrian constitution of 1920 and designing one of the first modern European constitutional courts. After the rise of Nazism he was forced to leave Germany, as he was of Jewish origin, and finally settled in the United States, at the University of California, Berkeley.

## The Project: Purifying the Science of Law of Everything Non-Legal

Kelsen wanted the study of law to be a **pure science** (*Reine*) in the sense that it is separated from politics, ethics, sociology, and psychology. This was not a denial of law's connection to these fields. Rather, it was an analytical isolation of the object of legal science: **what makes a legal rule valid as law**, regardless of its justice or social effectiveness.

## The Hierarchy of Norms and the "Basic Norm"

The core of his theory is that every legal norm derives its validity from a higher norm within a hierarchical system: an executive order from a statute, and a statute from the constitution. This chain cannot extend infinitely, so one must presuppose a highest norm that does not derive its validity from another norm but is methodologically assumed in order to justify the entire system. This is the **basic norm** (*Grundnorm*).

## Impact and Debate

The presuppositional nature of the concept of *Grundnorm* provoked broad debate. Is it a purely logical presupposition (what Kelsen described as a "transcendental-logical presupposition," borrowing a Kantian concept), or a social fact disguised in logical dress? This debate paved the way for later comparison with Hart's concept of the **rule of recognition**, which offered an alternative grounded in actual social practice rather than in a purely logical presupposition.

## From Vienna to Exile: A Biography Shaped by the Century

Kelsen was born in Prague in 1881 to a Jewish family and grew up in Vienna, where he studied law and was influenced by neo-Kantian philosophy. In 1919, after the fall of the empire, Chancellor Karl Renner asked him to help draft the new Austrian constitution, adopted in 1920. There he introduced a pivotal institutional innovation that is still taught in comparative constitutional law: a **specialized constitutional court** with the power of centralized review of the constitutionality of laws (the "Kelsenian" or "European" model of constitutional review, in contrast to the decentralized "American" model). He served as a judge on that court until his removal for political reasons in 1930. He then moved to Cologne (1930-33), Geneva (1933-40) and the German University in Prague (1936-38), and emigrated to the United States in 1940 to flee Nazism, teaching at Harvard and then, from 1942, at the University of California, Berkeley. He died in 1973.

## Legal Positivism as an Epistemological Project, Not a Moral One

One must distinguish Kelsen's positivism from simpler legal positivisms, such as John Austin's theory of law as the "command of the sovereign." Kelsen does not say that law is merely an order issued by an authority capable of coercion. Instead he builds a pure **normative** system that explains how a rule acquires its **validity** as a legal rule, independent of its moral content or actual social effectiveness. This distinction later became the cornerstone of every debate on the "separation of law and morality" in contemporary analytical philosophy of law.

## The Echo of the Dispute with Carl Schmitt

Among Kelsen's most notable intellectual exchanges was his dispute with Carl Schmitt over who should be the "guardian of the constitution." Schmitt argued that the Reich President, not a court, was best suited to be the guardian of the constitution. Kelsen defended the view that specialized judicial review is more consistent with the idea of the rule of law than the delegation of exceptional power to an individual leader. The dispute acquired tragic dimensions given what followed in 1933.

## His Relation to Comparisons with Islamic Jurisprudence

Kelsen's analytical tools were sometimes used in comparative studies of Islamic usul al-fiqh. A superficial structural resemblance can be noted between Kelsen's idea of normative hierarchy and the hierarchy of sources among the jurists of usul, such as the ordering of the sources of law (the Quran, the Sunna, consensus and analogy) set out in the tradition of usul al-fiqh from al-Shafi'i onward. The essential difference remains, however. The jurists' hierarchy derives its validity from a transcendent textual source (revelation), not from a purely methodological presupposition as in Kelsen. This difference makes the comparison analytically useful within its limits, but it precludes a complete identity between the two frameworks.
