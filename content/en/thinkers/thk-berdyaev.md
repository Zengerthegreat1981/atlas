# Nikolai Berdyaev

A Russian religious philosopher (1874–1948), one of the most prominent voices of Christian existentialism and personalism in the twentieth century, who made freedom prior even to being itself and defended the creative person against every form of collectivism and objectification.

## Life and Context

Born in Kyiv in 1874 into an aristocratic family, he attended the Kyiv Cadet Corps and then the University of Kyiv, where he joined Marxist circles. He was arrested in 1898 and exiled to Vologda in 1901. Moving away from Marxism toward idealism and then Christianity, he contributed to *Problems of Idealism* (1902) and to the collection *Vekhi* (1909). After the Revolution he founded in Moscow the Free Academy of Spiritual Culture (1918), but in 1922 he was expelled from Soviet Russia with other intellectuals. He lived in Berlin and, from 1924, in Paris and its suburb Clamart, where he edited the journal *Put'* and died in 1948. In 1947 Cambridge awarded him an honorary doctorate.

## What He Gave

**Freedom before being.** Drawing on the German mystic Jakob Böhme, he held that freedom is rooted in an uncreated, "meonic" ground (the *Ungrund*) that precedes being and even God's act of creation, so that God does not control freedom, and evil arises from it. This position is extreme even within religious existentialism and was criticised by many Orthodox and other theologians.

**Spirit and objectification.** He contrasted the free life of spirit with "objectification," the process by which the person is turned into a thing, a social function or a part of a system. For this reason he opposed both Marxist collectivism and the institutional authoritarianism he saw in churches and states.

**Creativity and personality.** Human beings are called to be co-creators, and creative acts, not mere obedience, are the highest form of response to God. His personalism treats the person as an irreducible spiritual centre, not a part of nature or society.

## Major Works

*The Philosophy of Freedom* (1911) · *The Meaning of the Creative Act* (1916) · *The Meaning of History* (1923) · *Freedom and the Spirit* (1927) · *The Destiny of Man* (1931) · *Solitude and Society* (1934) · *The Origin of Russian Communism* (1937) · *Slavery and Freedom* (1939) · *The Russian Idea* (1946) · *Dream and Reality* (autobiography, posthumous, 1949)

## Limitation

His influence on therapeutic practice, as distinct from philosophy, is not documented to the same degree as that of Tillich or Marcel in this Atlas. He wrote in an essayistic, non-systematic style, and his readings of Dostoevsky and of Russian history are interpretive rather than strictly scholarly.
