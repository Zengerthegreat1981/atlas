# Rudolf Otto

A German theologian and philosopher of religion (1869–1937), born in Peine in the Province of Hanover, who taught theology at Göttingen and Breslau before becoming professor at Marburg (1917 until his retirement in 1929). Otto is a principal founder of the phenomenological study of religion through his best-known book *Das Heilige* (*The Holy*, 1917), in which he put forward the concept of the "numinous" (*Numinous*, derived from the Latin *numen*) as a dimension of religious experience prior to any rational or moral classification, which he describes through the duality of the "awe-inspiring terrifying" and the "fascinating" (*mysterium tremendum et fascinans*).

## Definition

Otto distinguishes two elements in the idea of the divine: a rational element that can be expressed through moral and metaphysical concepts such as goodness, justice and perfection, and a non-rational element, which is the "numinous" itself, a primary feeling of dread before a transcendent presence that exceeds rational perception and cannot be reduced to it. Otto borrowed the term by analogy with aesthetics: just as beauty cannot be reduced to a sum of rational properties, the holy is a feeling apprehended directly before any theoretical explanation.

## Historical Context

Otto wrote in the theological atmosphere of Germany at the end of the nineteenth and the beginning of the twentieth century, when the liberal Protestant school of Friedrich Schleiermacher and Albrecht Ritschl tended to interpret religion in moral or general emotional terms. Otto was influenced by the philosophy of Immanuel Kant, especially by Jakob Friedrich Fries's reading of Kant, which allowed him to speak of an independent cognitive intuition that apprehends the holy directly without the mediation of purely rational concepts.

## Main Ideas and Method

Unlike reductive explanations of religion, such as the sociological ones of Émile Durkheim or the psychological ones of Sigmund Freud, Otto held that religious experience has a specific essence of its own that cannot be reduced to a social function or another psychological mechanism. This position, later known as non-reductionism, became one of the central poles of the methodological debate in the study of religion throughout the twentieth century. Otto also travelled to India, North Africa and the Middle East, and in his later book *Mysticism East and West* (German 1926; English 1932) compared the Christian mysticism of Meister Eckhart with the Hindu mysticism of Shankara, arguing that there is a shared experiential structure between religious traditions that are geographically and doctrinally distant.

## Major Works and Representatives

His central work is *Das Heilige* (1917), translated into English as *The Idea of the Holy* by John W. Harvey in 1923, which enjoyed wide circulation in the English-speaking world. Among his other works are *India's Religion of Grace and Christianity Compared and Contrasted* (German 1930), in which he compared Indian devotional (bhakti) religion, especially that associated with Ramanuja, with Protestant Christian piety, along with writings on the philosophy of religion in the Kantian-Friesian tradition.

## Impact and Critique

Otto had a direct influence on Mircea Eliade, who borrowed the concepts of the sacred and the profane and developed them phenomenologically in his landmarks of the history of religions; and on Ninian Smart, who included the experiential dimension among his seven dimensions of religion, although the two differed with him on details of method. Otto was criticised from two directions: by historians of religion who considered the concept of the numinous vague and not amenable to empirical verification, and by scholars of non-Western religions who saw in his comparisons between Eastern and Western mysticism a tendency to project a Protestant Christian template onto religious material different in structure and context. Nevertheless, *Das Heilige* is counted among the founding texts of the field of the science of religion (*Religionswissenschaft*) as an academic discipline independent of normative Christian theology on the one hand, and of the evolutionary anthropology of Edward Tylor and James Frazer on the other; Otto insists that the holy is a phenomenon that deserves study with its own tools, not with the borrowed tools of other sciences.

Otto was also a public figure: he sat in the Prussian parliament (1913–1918) for a liberal party and was a member of the Prussian constituent assembly after the First World War, before withdrawing from direct political activity. In 1927 he founded the *Religionskundliche Sammlung* in Marburg, a collection of ritual objects from many religions assembled as an educational resource; it still exists as part of the University of Marburg.

## The Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Otto did not directly address the Islamic tradition in his comparative works, as his Eastern comparisons were limited to Hinduism and Christianity. However, some later scholars of Islam and of Sufism have drawn on the concept of the "numinous" as a descriptive tool for experiences of awe before the divine, while stressing that Islamic doctrinal conditions differ fundamentally from the Protestant Christian context in which Otto formulated it.
