# Thomas Piketty

**Thomas Piketty** is a French economist, professor at the Paris School of Economics and the École des hautes études en sciences sociales, and one of the most prominent contemporary voices on the economics of inequality. His reputation rests on his work compiling long-run historical tax data, which returned the global debate on the concentration of wealth to the centre of contemporary political economy.

## From young mathematician to economic historian

Piketty showed early talent in mathematics and obtained a doctorate from the École des hautes études en sciences sociales and the London School of Economics at the age of twenty-two, with a dissertation in pure economic theory. He later moved, after a short period in American academia during which he grew disillusioned with the dominance of abstract mathematical modelling over the great empirical questions, to a fundamentally different research project: building long-run historical tax databases that allow the evolution of the distribution of income and wealth to be measured over a century or more in several countries.

## Capital in the Twenty-First Century: a large data project

Together with colleagues, most notably Anthony Atkinson, Piketty assembled tax data from France, Britain, the United States and other countries spanning more than a century, and published in 2013 (English edition 2014) his book *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*. In it he argued that inequality did not fall automatically through natural laws of growth, as the optimistic Kuznets hypothesis proposed. Instead, it fell temporarily in the mid-twentieth century because of exceptional shocks (the two world wars and high post-war taxes), before rising strongly again from the 1980s onward to approach the levels of the European "Belle Époque" at the end of the nineteenth century.

## The r > g law

Piketty offered a central explanatory formulation of the concentration of wealth: when the rate of return on capital (r) exceeds the growth rate of the economy as a whole (g) over a long period, which he regards as the historical norm rather than the exception, wealth tends to concentrate in the hands of those who already own capital, growing faster than general income. Over time this produces societies in which wealth is inherited more than it is earned through work, bringing him close to the concerns of nineteenth-century economists about inheritance and capitalist rent.

## Radical policy recommendations

Piketty concluded with an ambitious policy recommendation: a coordinated global progressive tax on wealth (not income alone). He argued that unilateral national taxes run into tax competition between states that drives wealth to flee to tax havens. The recommendation faced wide scepticism from other economists about its political feasibility in the absence of the broad international coordination that is difficult to achieve in practice.

## Methodological critique of his thesis

Piketty faced technical criticism from economists (for example Matthew Rognlie) who questioned the assumed elasticity of substitution between capital and labour on which the rise of the capital share depends. They argued that the relationship between the rate of return on capital and economic growth does not follow automatically from historical data alone, without additional modelling assumptions that can be seriously debated.

## Later work: on ideology and elections

In his later work, most notably *Capital and Ideology* (2019), Piketty widened his analysis to examine how different political and ideological systems throughout history justify uneven distributions of wealth. He also analysed the changing class voting patterns in Western democracies, showing how the left has historically shifted from a party of the working class to a party of the "highly educated elite" in several Western countries.

## Measuring inequality in the Arab region

Together with Facundo Alvaredo and Lydia Assouad, Piketty published in 2019 a study that provided the first systematic estimate of inequality in the Middle East between 1990 and 2016, drawing on his methodology of using tax and alternative data. It concluded that the region may be among the most unequal in the world, with an explicit reservation from the researchers about the fragility of the quality of regional data available.

## A legacy of Marx without inevitable adoption

Piketty partly revives Karl Marx's old question about the tendency of capital to concentrate, but he explicitly rejects the inevitability of capitalist collapse that some readings of Marx imply. He offers instead an empirical description of a historical distributional pattern that can be politically reversed through tax and institutional instruments, not through an inevitable revolution. This position places him closer to a democratic social reformer than to a traditional Marxist, despite borrowing some of the vocabulary of Marxist analysis of capital and rent.

## Broad media and political impact

The echo of *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* extended beyond academic circles into a wide public debate. Despite its large size and relatively technical statistical character, it topped bestseller lists and became a frequently cited reference in public debate on inequality in many countries.
