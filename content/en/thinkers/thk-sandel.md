# Michael Sandel

An American political philosopher born in Minneapolis in 1953, professor of government at Harvard University, one of the best-known critics of John Rawls in the debate that has reshaped political philosophy since the 1980s, and regarded as one of the most prominent figures associated with what has been called "Communitarianism", although he has remained reserved about the label. He combined academic rigor with the ability to address the general public, so that his lectures on justice became among the most widely viewed philosophical lectures in the world.

## His Life

Sandel studied at Brandeis University, graduating in 1975, then went to Oxford on a Rhodes scholarship, studied under Charles Taylor, and received his doctorate in 1981. He joined the Harvard faculty in 1980, held a chair in government there, and his course on justice became one of the university's best-known courses; his lectures were broadcast to the public in 2009 on television and the internet, and he gave the Reith Lectures on the BBC under the title "A New Citizenship" (2009).

## What He Contributed

In his book *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) he directed a focused critique at Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971), not with regard to its political results but with regard to the assumed picture of the self. Behind the "veil of ignorance" stands a self stripped of its ends, its attachments and its communities, which Sandel called the "Unencumbered Self", and he said that it anticipates what cannot be dispensed with: that people know themselves through constitutive bonds, such as family, nation and heritage, and that some obligations we do not choose but discover. For this reason liberalism prefers the "right" over the "good", which Sandel regards as an unjustified claim.

In *Democracy's Discontent* (1996) he sketched what he called the "procedural republic", that is, a society that limits its vision of the common good in favor of neutral rules, and so loses its immunity against market forces and the sense of powerlessness. Against it he revived a civic republican tradition that holds that freedom requires participation in self-government and the cultivation of the virtues of citizenship. In *The Case Against Perfection* (2007) he criticized the use of biotechnology to improve humans genetically and regarded it as undermining the meaning of "giftedness" and humility before what we have not made. In *Justice: What's the Right Thing to Do?* (2009) he offered the public an introduction to utilitarianism in Bentham and Mill, freedom in Kant and Nozick, and virtue in Aristotle.

He did not confine himself to academic books: in *Public Philosophy: Essays on Morality in Politics* (2005) he collected essays on religion in public life, abortion and civic debate, and in them defended the view that moral disagreement is not settled by avoiding it but by engaging it with argument and mutual respect.

He then widened his critique in *What Money Can't Buy* (2012) to the penetration of market logic into the fields of education, health, sport and the environment, and said that some things change their nature when sold and are corrupted by price. In *The Tyranny of Merit* (2020) he linked popular anger and populism to the meritocratic promise that tells the successful that they deserved their success and the failed that they deserved their failure, and showed how this generates hubris among the winners and humiliation among the losers.

## His Place in the Current

Sandel is mentioned with Charles Taylor, Alasdair MacIntyre and Michael Walzer among the communitarians who criticized Rawlsian liberalism, although their positions differ greatly. Sandel sometimes rejects the label because he holds that the argument is not to accept the community's values as they are, but that political philosophy cannot avoid judging the good. Rawls replied in *Political Liberalism* (1993) that his conception of the person is political and not metaphysical, which moved the debate onto new ground.

## Limits and Criticism

His critique of the "isolated" self is strong in diagnosing the situation, but he does not offer a clear institutional alternative, which leaves the question "which communities and which values?" hanging before feminist and ethnic critique, for feminism may see in some "constitutive bonds" forms of oppression rather than a fate to be celebrated. Amy Gutmann and Will Kymlicka wrote that liberalism can acknowledge the context of choice without abandoning neutrality. Others hold that his call for the common good may open the door for the majority against the minority. Also, his popular books are less rigorous than his first work from the philosophical point of view, although more able to influence public debate.

## His Major Works

Liberalism and the Limits of Justice (1982) · Democracy's Discontent (1996) · The Case Against Perfection (2007) · Justice: What's the Right Thing to Do? (2009) · What Money Can't Buy (2012) · The Tyranny of Merit (2020).
