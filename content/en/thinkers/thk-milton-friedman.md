# Milton Friedman

**Milton Friedman** was an American economist at the University of Chicago. He is the most prominent representative of the monetarist school and one of the most influential economists in global economic policy during the second half of the twentieth century. He was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1976 and was a leading advocate of free markets and limited state intervention in the economy.

## From New York to Chicago

Friedman grew up in New York in a modest family of Jewish immigrants from Carpathian Ruthenia (now in Ukraine). He studied mathematics and economics at Rutgers University, took a master's degree at the University of Chicago and a doctorate at Columbia University (1946), and joined the University of Chicago faculty in 1946, where he became a leading professor and the intellectual leader of what became known as the "Chicago School" of economics. That school combined rigorous empirical methodology with a principled defence of the efficiency of free markets compared with direct government regulation.

## The quantity theory of money: a modern revival

Friedman reformulated the classical quantity theory of money in a modern empirical style. He argued that inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon": in other words, growth in the money supply at a rate exceeding the growth of real output is the main cause of sustained inflation in the long run, regardless of other aggregate demand factors on which Keynesian economics focused.

## Monetary history: reinterpreting the Great Depression

Together with Anna Schwartz, in their landmark book *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963), Friedman offered a detailed empirical argument that reinterprets the Great Depression itself. They presented the contraction of 1929–1933 as the result of failures of monetary policy by the Federal Reserve, which allowed a sharp fall in the money supply, rather than as an inherent failure of the free market. This reading challenged one of the strongest historical arguments of Keynesianism.

## The Phillips curve: no permanent trade-off

In his presidential address to the American Economic Association in 1967 (published in 1968 as "The Role of Monetary Policy"), Friedman launched a decisive critique of the traditional Keynesian Phillips curve and, in parallel with Edmund Phelps, proposed the concept of the "natural rate of unemployment". Any attempt to reduce unemployment below this rate through deliberate inflation succeeds only temporarily, until individuals' expectations adjust. Unemployment then returns to its natural rate, with permanently higher inflation. The stagflation of the 1970s, which followed the oil shock of 1973, was widely seen as supporting this view and strengthened the credibility of monetarism.

## Capitalism and freedom: a philosophical defence of the market

Friedman went beyond purely technical economics to a broader philosophical defence of economic freedom in his book *Capitalism and Freedom* (1962). He linked economic freedom to political freedom and called for reducing the government's role in many areas, from education to healthcare. His arguments were similar in general spirit (though different in detail) to Friedrich Hayek's thesis in *The Road to Serfdom* about the dangers of the expansion of the state's economic power for individual liberties.

## The Nobel Prize and direct political influence

Friedman received the Nobel Prize in 1976, cited "for his achievements in the fields of consumption analysis, monetary history and theory and for his demonstration of the complexity of stabilization policy". His consumption work includes *A Theory of the Consumption Function* (1957), which set out the permanent income hypothesis. His ideas became a direct political reference point during the tenures of Margaret Thatcher in Britain and Ronald Reagan in the United States in the early 1980s. Central banks in the United States and Britain experimented with targeting the growth of the money supply to curb the high inflation of the late 1970s, alongside general policies to liberalize markets, cut taxes and reduce government regulation.

## Controversial international advisory work

Friedman provided economic advice to several governments around the world. Among them was a brief visit to Chile in 1975, during the military rule of General Augusto Pinochet, where a group of Chilean economists trained at Chicago (the "Chicago Boys") were designing radical liberalizing reforms. Friedman's role there was that of a visiting adviser who delivered lectures and wrote a letter to Pinochet. This advisory role provoked wide political and moral debate about the relationship of economists with authoritarian regimes, a debate that goes beyond the scope of a purely technical economic evaluation.

## A television programme and wide public reach

Friedman reached a broad public beyond academic circles through his documentary television series *Free to Choose* (1980), which was broadcast on American public television and accompanied by a book written with his wife Rose Friedman and turned his economic ideas into direct popular political discourse about the virtues of free markets. This distinguished him from most prominent academic economists in his rare ability to bring technical economic ideas to a wide audience outside universities and specialist circles.

## Additional positions: education and military conscription

Friedman also advocated political proposals broader than monetary policy alone. These included the proposal of an "education voucher" (school vouchers) system that allows parents to choose their children's schools with transferable government funding, and his explicit opposition to compulsory military conscription in the United States. This made him an influential figure in American political debates that extended beyond macroeconomics to wider public policy questions, drawing throughout on his general philosophy of expanding the scope of free individual choice.
