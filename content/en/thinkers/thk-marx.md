# Karl Marx

Karl Marx (1818-1883) was a German philosopher, economist, and revolutionary whose analysis of capitalism, class, and historical change became one of the foundations of sociology and the social sciences.

## Life and Context

Marx was born in Trier in 1818 and studied law and philosophy at Bonn and Berlin, receiving his doctorate in 1841. He worked as a journalist, was expelled from Germany, France, and Belgium for his radical views, and settled in London in 1849, where he lived for the rest of his life, supported by his collaborator Friedrich Engels. He took part in the International Workingmen's Association. He died in London in 1883.

His partnership with Friedrich Engels began in Paris. He edited the *Rheinische Zeitung* until censorship closed the paper, took part in the revolutions of 1848-1849, and in London continued as a journalist and researcher, often in poverty.

## Main Ideas

- **Historical materialism:** the way a society organizes production (forces and relations of production) shapes its social, political, and ideological life.
- **Class and class struggle:** history is marked by conflict between classes; under capitalism, the bourgeoisie and the proletariat.
- **Alienation:** under capitalism, workers are estranged from the product of their labor, from the labor process, from others, and from their human potential.
- **Surplus value and exploitation:** profit arises from the unpaid portion of workers' labor.
- **Commodity fetishism:** social relations between people appear as relations between things.
- **Ideology:** the ruling ideas of an age tend to be those that serve the ruling class.
- **Capitalism's contradictions:** he argued the system generates crises and the conditions for its own transformation.
- **Accumulation:** competition drives technical change, the concentration of capital, and recurring crises, while class struggle may transform the social order.

## Major Works

- *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* (written 1844, published 1932)
- *The German Ideology* (written 1845-1846, with Engels; published 1932)
- *The Communist Manifesto* (1848, with Engels)
- *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852)
- *Grundrisse* (written 1857-1858, published 1939-1941)
- *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859)
- *Capital*, volume 1 (1867); volumes 2 and 3 edited by Engels (1885, 1894)

## Case Studies

*The Eighteenth Brumaire* examines how classes, political factions, and the state shaped Louis Bonaparte's coup in France. In *Capital*, the chapters on the working day and on machinery draw on reports by British factory inspectors.

## Method

Marx combined dialectical analysis, derived from Hegel and transformed into a materialist form, with study of political economy and historical evidence. He attended to the totality of social relations and to historical specificity. He treated the categories of political economy as expressions of historically specific social relations.

## Influence and Criticism

Marx shaped conflict theory, critical theory, world-systems theory, and labor studies, and he influenced Weber, who engaged with his ideas, and many later thinkers. Critics have challenged the labor theory of value, economic determinism, predictions about proletarian revolution, and the political regimes that claimed his name. Defenders emphasize his analysis of capitalism's dynamics. Weber argued that status, political power, and religious ideas cannot be reduced to class and economy. Interpreters differ on the continuity between the early and later writings, and on how far Marx's work bears responsibility for governments that invoked it. The same body of work shaped revolutionary states and the sociology of work.

## Applications and Cross-References

His concepts are applied to studies of work, inequality, globalization, and ideology. Compare Weber on class, status, and party, Durkheim on solidarity, and Foucault on power. Compare also Gramsci on hegemony.
