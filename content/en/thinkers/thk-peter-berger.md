# Peter L. Berger

An Austrian-American sociologist, Thomas Luckmann's partner in laying the foundational work of contemporary social constructionism, and later one of the most prominent sociologists of religion of the twentieth century.

## Historical Context

Peter Berger was born in Vienna in 1929 and emigrated to the United States after the Second World War. He studied at Wagner College and took his doctorate at the New School in 1954. There he came under the influence of Alfred Schutz at the New School for Social Research in New York, which was then an academic refuge for many European thinkers who had fled Nazism and the war. From Schutz, Berger absorbed the tools of German phenomenology (derived originally from Edmund Husserl through Alfred Schutz), which he later applied to the sociology of knowledge in collaboration with Thomas Luckmann, whom he met at the New School, producing their pivotal joint work in 1966. This came amid a rising American academic interest in questions about the nature of social reality and how individuals subjectively perceive it, in parallel with the rise of symbolic interactionism and ethnomethodology as micro-sociological approaches alternative to the dominant structural functionalism.

## Main Ideas and Method

In his joint book with Luckmann, *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966), Berger developed an influential dialectical conception of how human beings produce their social institutions through a continuous, constantly repeated three-part process: "externalization" (Externalization), the individual's projection of himself and his meaning into the external world through action and speech; then "objectivation" (Objectivation), the transformation of this human product into a reality that appears objective, external and independent of its original makers (such as institutions, rules and language itself); and finally "internalization" (Internalization), the individual's reabsorption, through the process of socialization, of this originally artificial "objective" reality as a self-evident given truth rather than a historical human creation. They held that what appears to be a solid objective reality beyond questioning (such as religion, gender roles, the political order) is in essence a historical human creation whose changing human origin has been forgotten through this continuous cycle of "externalization", "objectivation" and "internalization". This is what they called the continuous "dialectic" of the construction of social reality.

## Major Works and Representatives

Besides *The Social Construction of Reality* (with Thomas Luckmann, 1966) and the influential introductory *Invitation to Sociology* (1963), his pivotal works include *A Rumor of Angels* (1969) and *The Sacred Canopy: Elements of a Sociological Theory of Religion* (1967), in which he applied his social-constructionist framework specifically to religion. He regarded religions as symbolic "sacred canopies" that human beings build historically to protect themselves from the chaos and absolute randomness of existence (Anomie), by endowing it with a stable, comprehensive cosmic meaning. In this work Berger observed that modernity, with its mounting religious and intellectual pluralism, weakens the ability of any single "sacred canopy" to impose its comprehensive singular meaning unchallenged, producing what he called the modern religious "crisis of credibility". Berger later retreated from his early thesis of inevitable secularization (according to which the importance of religion would inevitably decline gradually with modernity), acknowledging in his later works (as editor of *The Desecularization of the World*, 1999) that the contemporary world is in fact witnessing a wide religious revival that contradicts the expectations of the classical secularization theory that he himself had earlier helped formulate.

## Influence and Criticism

This work became the cornerstone of the whole "social constructionism" current in the human sciences, and Berger's influence later extended to the sociology of religion and to studies of modernization and development. His later retreat from the theory of inevitable secularization deeply influenced the reformulation of the whole contemporary field of the sociology of religion toward more complex models that take into account the actual global diversity in the relation of religion to modernity. The Berger-Luckmann social-constructionist framework was criticized for sometimes tending to portray the process of social "internalization" as almost automatic, which reduces the space for conscious individual resistance and agency toward imposed social structures. Anthony Giddens's structuration theory offers a different treatment of the relation between agency and structure.

Berger directed the Institute on Culture, Religion and World Affairs at Boston University and continued his work in the comparative sociology of religion until late in life, openly revising his earlier secularization thesis in light of new evidence. He also wrote on development and modernization, notably *Pyramids of Sacrifice: Political Ethics and Social Change* (1974), a critique of both capitalist and socialist development models, and *The Capitalist Revolution* (1986). He died in 2017.
