# Jon Kabat-Zinn

A molecular biologist by training and a meditation teacher, a professor emeritus at the University of Massachusetts Medical School (UMass Medical School), and the founder of the Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction program (MBSR) and of the Stress Reduction Clinic in 1979.

## What he contributed

He created MBSR in 1979 as an eight-week institutional group program for people with chronic pain and clinical patients who had not benefited from conventional treatments, and then defined "mindfulness" in a secular-empirical definition as paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, and without judgment — a definition that became the reference standard in the psychological literature (its short formulation in his 1994 book, and its expanded formulation in a 2003 article). His book *Wherever You Go, There You Are* (1994) introduced the general public to the practice.

## Direct influence on the movement

Kabat-Zinn was a central figure in bringing mindfulness into medicine and psychology as an institutional practice, and he founded the UMass Center for Mindfulness. MBCT (Segal, Williams and Teasdale) and MBRP (Marlatt and Witkiewitz) both draw on MBSR's basic meditation techniques: the body scan, breath meditation, mindful movement, and mindfulness in daily activities.

## His most important works

- *Full Catastrophe Living* (1990) — the founding book of MBSR.
- *Wherever You Go, There You Are* (1994).
- *Coming to Our Senses* (2005).
- *The Healing Power of Mindfulness: A New Way of Being* (2018).

## Criticisms

- **"Secularization"**: he was criticized by the "McMindfulness" movement (Purser, 2019) on the claim that he commodified mindfulness and stripped it of its Buddhist spiritual context when it was transferred to corporations.
- The evidence for MBSR's effect in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) is often described as moderate in size, not "stunning."

## Comparison with Sufi practice

Some Muslim researchers compare mindfulness with the Sufi practices of "muraqaba" (watchfulness) and "muhasaba" (self-reckoning), that is, observing the self and the heart in the present moment; this is an interpretive comparison that Kabat-Zinn himself does not offer. The aim differs: muraqaba in the tradition is directed toward God and proceeds within a devotional framework, whereas Kabat-Zinn's formulation is secular and aims at reducing stress and improving health.

## From molecular biology to the clinic

Kabat-Zinn received a doctorate in molecular biology from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1971, and studied meditation with Buddhist teachers, among them Philip Kapleau in Rochester and Thich Nhat Hanh, and at the Insight Meditation Society in Barre, Massachusetts. In his early paper in *General Hospital Psychiatry* (1982) he presented the results of his first program for chronic pain patients, and in a 1985 paper published in the *Journal of Behavioral Medicine* with Lipworth and Burney he presented the results for a larger group. Later Davidson and colleagues published in *Psychosomatic Medicine* (2003) a study comparing MBSR trainees with a waiting list in brain activity and vaccine response, and it became among the most cited in support of the program.

The definition of mindfulness took its famous form in *Wherever You Go, There You Are* (1994): paying attention in a particular way, on purpose, in the present moment, and without judgments. Then in a 2003 article in *Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice* he reformulated it as the awareness that arises from intentional, nonjudgmental attention to the unfolding of experience moment by moment. After that came Segal, Williams and Teasdale's development of MBCT (2002), which adapted the program to prevent depressive relapse.

## The Center for Mindfulness

The Center for Mindfulness in Medicine, Health Care, and Society at the University of Massachusetts was founded in 1995, after the Stress Reduction Clinic in 1979.

## Neuroimaging and Structural Brain Changes

Following early electroencephalographic (EEG) investigations of MBSR by Davidson and colleagues (2003), neuroimaging researchers began examining whether mindfulness meditation alters neural structure and function. A landmark cross-sectional study led by Sara Lazar and colleagues (2005) reported that regular meditation practice is associated with increased cortical thickness in brain regions involved in somatosensory, interoceptive, and emotional processing, notably the prefrontal cortex and right anterior insula, with differences particularly pronounced in older participants, suggesting a potential neuroprotective buffer against age-related cortical thinning.

Subsequently, a longitudinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study by Britta Hölzel, Lazar, and colleagues (2011) investigated MBSR participants before and after the eight-week intervention compared with waitlist controls. The trial documented significant increases in gray matter concentration within the left hippocampus, posterior cingulate cortex, temporo-parietal junction, and cerebellum—structures implicated in memory consolidation, perspective-taking, and emotion regulation. While these findings generated substantial clinical interest, contemporary reviews (such as Van Dam et al., 2018) emphasize that neuroimaging trials in contemplative science typically feature modest sample sizes, active-control limitations, and cross-sectional confounds, warranting cautious interpretation rather than definitive mechanistic claims.

## Additional limits and criticisms

Goyal and colleagues in *JAMA Internal Medicine* (2014) summarized a systematic review of meditation programs, and found moderate evidence of improvement in anxiety, depression and pain, and weak evidence for other outcomes, and not better than other active treatments. In the article by Van Dam and colleagues "Mind the Hype" (*Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2018), problems of definition and measurement and of small-sample trials were criticized, a critique that includes even supporters of the research. Added to Purser's critique of "McMindfulness" is a critique that secularization changed the nature of the practice, which Kabat-Zinn answers by saying that the essence of Buddhism, that is, the Dharma, does not contradict the neutral formulation.
