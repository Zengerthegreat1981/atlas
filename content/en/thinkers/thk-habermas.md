# Jürgen Habermas

A German philosopher and sociologist (born 1929), professor of philosophy at the University of Frankfurt until his retirement in 1994, a leading theorist of the second generation of critical theory, author of the theory of "communicative action" and a major theorist of deliberative democracy in political and social philosophy. He studied under Theodor Adorno and worked as his assistant at the Institute for Social Research in Frankfurt (1956–1959).

## Biography and Formation

Habermas was born in Düsseldorf in 1929 and grew up in Gummersbach; in his childhood he was part of a generation that lived through the collapse of Nazism in adolescence, an experience that shaped his sensitivity to the question of memory and German historical responsibility. He studied philosophy in Göttingen, Zurich, and Bonn, and received his doctorate in 1954 with a dissertation on Schelling. He then worked as Adorno's assistant in Frankfurt, and completed his habilitation in Marburg under Wolfgang Abendroth with his book *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere*. He taught in Heidelberg, then succeeded Horkheimer in the Frankfurt chair in 1964, directed the Max Planck Institute in Starnberg (1971–1981), and then returned to Frankfurt.

## What He Gave

In *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (Strukturwandel der Öffentlichkeit, 1962) he traced the rise of the bourgeois public sphere in the coffeehouses and the press, and its dissolution under the pressure of mass media and consumption. In *Knowledge and Human Interests* (Erkenntnis und Interesse, 1968) he distinguished three cognitive interests: technical, practical, and emancipatory. Then in *The Theory of Communicative Action* (Theorie des kommunikativen Handelns, 1981) he moved the foundation of critical theory from the philosophy of consciousness to the philosophy of language: for him rationality lies in the structure of understanding itself, that is, in the claims to truth, rightness, and sincerity that participants in dialogue implicitly raise. He distinguished between "strategic action" oriented to success and "communicative action" oriented to understanding, and between the "lifeworld" and the "system", which colonizes the lifeworld when the logic of money and power penetrates the domains of everyday life.

In *Between Facts and Norms* (Faktizität und Geltung, 1992) he established a democratic model based on "public discourse" and the "will to rational consensus", in which political decisions acquire their legitimacy not from majority vote alone but from a non-coercive public discussion subject to the conditions of the "Ideal Speech Situation": inclusiveness, equality, and the elimination of coercion. In ethics he developed "discourse ethics" (Diskursethik), which makes the moral criterion what all those concerned could accept in a practical discourse.

## The Major Debates

Habermas engaged in controversies that shaped postwar German thought: the "positivism dispute" with Karl Popper and his followers in the 1960s, his debate with Gadamer over hermeneutics and ideology critique, his debate with Niklas Luhmann over social theory, and the "historians' dispute" (Historikerstreit) in 1986 in defense of the critical memory of the Holocaust. In the 1990s a dialogue took place with John Rawls over political liberalism, and in 2004 a dialogue brought him together with Cardinal Ratzinger on the prepolitical foundations of the constitutional state. He entered a disagreement with Michel Foucault over modernity and the Enlightenment, in what became known as the debate that did not directly take place, and then his book *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985) came with a critique of the postmodernists.

## Late Works

In recent decades Habermas worked on questions of globalization and postnational citizenship ("Die postnationale Konstellation", 1998), on religion in the public sphere ("Zwischen Naturalismus und Religion", 2005), and on the future of the European Union and its constitution. In 2019 he published "Auch eine Geschichte der Philosophie" in two volumes, a tracing of the history of the relation between faith and knowledge. He received the Peace Prize of the German Book Trade in 2001 and the Kyoto Prize in 2004.

## His Place in the Current

A leading theorist of modern "deliberative democracy", and the builder of the bridge between the Frankfurt School (Adorno, Horkheimer) and contemporary analytic political philosophy. His dialogues with John Rawls, Hans-Georg Gadamer, and Michel Foucault are among the most important debates of the second half of the twentieth century. In the Arab world he is viewed as a reference in the discussion of the public sphere, modernity, and secularism; his works have been translated into Arabic in several stages, and Arab studies on modernity and communication have taken him up.

## Limits and Criticism

His stipulation of strict "ideal" conditions for discourse makes the model fragile in the face of questions of power and material and cultural inequality, as was charged against him by Iris Marion Young from the standpoint of feminist critique, that requiring abstract rational argument excludes the forms of expression used by marginalized groups (narrative, protest, and rhetoric), and by postcolonial critics. Poststructuralists, among them Laclau and Mouffe, criticized him on the ground that his desired consensus denies the permanently conflictual character of politics. Others held that his reconstruction of modernity retains a Eurocentrism, and that the concept of deliberative legitimacy is hard to realize in multicultural and unequal societies.
