# Halim Barakat

A Syrian sociologist and novelist (born in the village of Kafroun in the Syrian coastal mountains; the birth year is commonly given as 1933), among the most prominent who tried to write **a sociology of Arab society from within** and not as a test case for Western modernization theories. He was a professor at the American University of Beirut and then at the Center for Contemporary Arab Studies at Georgetown University. His project is a composite attempt: to describe Arab society as an existing structure (family, sect, class, state), and at the same time to diagnose **alienation and rebellion** as two lived experiences. He wrote the second side in the novel, not in research.

## What He Contributed

- ***Contemporary Arab Society* (1984, Center for Arab Unity Studies)**: a comprehensive sociological survey that treats the Arab world as a single unit of analysis while insisting on its internal diversity. The book's core is that **the family**, not the individual and not class alone, is the pivotal social institution: the unit of economy, loyalty, identity and control, through which politics, religion and education are understood.
- ***The Arab World: Society, Culture, and State* (1993)**: his most widely circulated English formulation, in which the analysis has three parts: a social structure of multiple affiliations, a culture in conflict between established and emerging values, and a state that monopolizes speaking in the name of society without producing citizenship.
- **The concept of the mosaic society**: the Arab world is a **mosaic** structure of ethnic, sectarian, tribal, regional and urban-rural groups, neither a homogeneous mass nor merely one nation. The concept's importance is twofold: it explains the fragility of national integration, and it also explains these groups' capacity to continue as protection networks when the state recedes. Barakat uses it with critical caution, neither as a justification for division nor as a denial of the unifying Arab belonging.
- **Alienation and rebellion as a sociological subject**: he moved alienation from a philosophical term to a description of the condition of an Arab generation after 1967: estrangement from the state, the institution and the official discourse, and a rebellion that finds no organized political form to absorb it.
- ***Lebanon in Strife* (1977)**: an empirical study of Lebanese students before the war, which showed that sectarian division was being reproduced in consciousness and aspirations before it exploded militarily, a field work of rare timing.
- **The novel as an instrument of knowledge**: *Six Days* (1961) and *The Return of the Bird to the Sea* (1969, about the defeat of 1967) are not an activity parallel to his academic work but its other face: what the statistical table cannot say about defeat and uprooting was written as narrative.

## His Place in the Current

- **Within the wave that founded a critical Arab sociology** in the 1970s and 1980s, with Hisham Sharabi (neopatriarchy) and Ali al-Wardi (duality of personality and the conflict between Bedouin and civilization), three adjacent but not identical diagnoses. Sharabi stresses the patriarchal structure modernized in form, al-Wardi the value contradiction between Bedouin life and civilization, and Barakat the family and mosaic plurality.
- **Heir to Ibn Khaldun's question, not his method**: he shares with him making group solidarity (asabiyya) and collective cohesion the key to analyzing power and the state, but he works with the tools of empirical survey and modern comparative sociology and not with the cyclical logic of the science of civilization (umran).
- **Against modernization theory**: he rejects the implicit assumption that the road is one and that the family and the sect are "traditional remnants" that will disappear with modernization. For him they are active institutions that adapt and strengthen under incomplete modernization and a state of weak legitimacy.
- **Close to Said's critique of Orientalism without being rhetorical**: he strips outside representation of its validity over Arab society, but his answer is empirical (write the society from within with verifiable tools), not textual deconstruction.
- **A bilingual presence**: he wrote in Arabic for an Arab audience and in English for an American academic audience, and this split is itself a sociological subject concerning who holds the right to define society.

## Limitations

- **Limits of the unit of analysis "Arab society"**: treating the Arab space as one unit produces generalizations that cover deep structural differences. A rentier Gulf state, an agrarian Maghrebi one and a multi-sectarian Mashriqi one are not a single case.
- **The centrality of the family as a total explanation**: the analysis's strength is also its limit. If the family explains politics, economy, religion and education, the concept becomes unfalsifiable, and the role of political economy, oil rent, foreign intervention and the structure of the labor market is lost.
- **The mosaic metaphor carries heavy costs**: it was later used in political and academic discourse that presents Arab societies as sects before they are citizens, a use contrary to Barakat's intention but attached to the metaphor itself, which calls for a caution in any use of it.
- **Description is stronger than the explanatory model**: the strength lies in description and survey, not in building a theory with testable variables and hypotheses, a recurrent internal criticism of Arab sociology of his period.
- **Data overtaken by time**: the structure he described predates three huge transformations (mass migrations to the Gulf, digital communication, then 2011 and after). The validity of the diagnosis today is an open question, not an axiom.
- **Gender is present but incomplete**: the family is a central institution for him, but the gender analysis of power within it is far less developed than the Arab feminist works contemporary with him, a gap that the work of Arab feminist writers such as Nawal El Saadawi and Fatima Mernissi helps to fill.

## His Major Works

- *Contemporary Arab Society: An Exploratory Social Study* (Center for Arab Unity Studies, Beirut, 1984).
- *The Arab World: Society, Culture, and State* (University of California Press, 1993).
- *Lebanon in Strife: Student Preludes to the Civil War* (University of Texas Press, 1977).
- *Arab Society in the Twentieth Century: A Study in Changing Conditions and Relations* (Center for Arab Unity Studies, 2000).
- *Six Days* (novel, 1961; published in English as *Days of Dust*).
- *The Return of the Bird to the Sea* (novel, 1969; published in English as *The Crane*).
