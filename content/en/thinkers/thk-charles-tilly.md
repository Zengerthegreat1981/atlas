# Charles Tilly

Charles Tilly (1929-2008) was an American sociologist, historian and political scientist who studied state formation, collective action, revolutions and inequality through large-scale historical comparison.

## Life and Context

Tilly was born in Lombard, Illinois, and earned his doctorate at Harvard University. He held posts at the University of Michigan, the New School for Social Research and Columbia University. His early research on the Vendee counterrevolution in France led him to see political conflict as embedded in long-term structural change such as urbanization, state-building and capitalism.

## Main Ideas

Tilly argued that European states emerged largely from the demands of war, famously summarized as war making and state making being mutually reinforcing. In *Coercion, Capital, and European States*, he traced how different mixes of coercion and capital produced different state forms. On collective action, he developed the concept of repertoires of contention, the limited set of routines of protest available to people at a given time and place, and contributed to the political process approach to social movements. Later he focused on inequality, arguing that durable inequality rests on categorical pairs (such as male and female or citizen and noncitizen) sustained by exploitation and opportunity hoarding. He also wrote on democratization and on the role of trust networks.

## Method

Tilly championed relational and processual analysis over reified entities, and he used historical archives, event catalogs and systematic comparison. He distinguished several comparative strategies, from individualizing to encompassing comparisons.

## Case Studies

*The Vendée* asked why parts of western France rose against the Revolution in 1793 while neighbouring districts largely did not. Rejecting explanations based simply on peasant backwardness or loyalty to king and church, Tilly compared areas by their degree of urbanisation and examined landholding, markets, local elites, clergy, and officials. Conflict was sharper where uneven economic and administrative change brought new urban-connected groups into competition with established local authority. His later study of Great Britain from 1758 to 1834 used a large catalogue of public gatherings compiled from periodicals and other records. It traced a change from local and direct forms, such as seizures of grain or attacks on particular targets, toward public meetings, petitions, demonstrations, and campaigns aimed at national authorities, especially Parliament. Tilly connected this shift in repertoire with war, the expansion of the state and of capitalism, and the growing political weight of Parliament. Event catalogues permit systematic comparison, although they depend on what contemporary sources chose to record.

## Contemporary Empirical Examples

Researchers have tested the limits of Tilly's account of war and states. Jeffrey Herbst's *States and Power in Africa* (2000) argued that low population density and the protection of inherited boundaries gave many African states different incentives from those of early modern European rulers. Miguel Angel Centeno's *Blood and Debt* (2002) found that wars in Latin America generally did not produce comparable gains in state capacity, partly because their effects depended on existing administrative organisation and on elite and class divisions. In the study of inequality, Donald Tomaskovic-Devey and Dustin Avent-Holt's *Relational Inequalities* (2019) examined workplaces, building on Tilly's ideas of categorical distinctions, exploitation, and social closure to explain how resources and respect are distributed within organisations. These works qualify and extend his mechanisms rather than confirming a single general law.

## Related Concepts

Related concepts include state formation, collective action, contentious politics, repertoires of contention, political opportunity, social mechanisms, democratisation, categorical inequality, opportunity hoarding, and social closure. Useful comparisons include Max Weber on the state, Barrington Moore on routes to modern regimes, and Theda Skocpol on revolutions. Weber's monopoly of legitimate force frames the question of how rulers gained control over coercion. Moore and Skocpol, like Tilly, compare historical paths, but Tilly's later work gave particular weight to recurring relational mechanisms that combine differently across cases. In movement studies his ideas connect with resource mobilisation and framing; in stratification they connect with closure, exploitation, and organisational boundaries.

## Influence and Criticism

His work shaped historical sociology, social movement studies and the study of the state. Critics have argued that the bellicist account of state formation fits Europe better than other regions, and that the political process framework gives limited attention to culture and emotion.
