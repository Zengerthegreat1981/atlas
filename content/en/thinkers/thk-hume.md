# David Hume

A Scottish philosopher (1711–1776), a leading representative of empiricism and skepticism in the eighteenth century. In his book *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–1740) he offered an influential critique of the principle of causality and of the relation between facts (Is) and values (Ought) in moral philosophy. He was born in Edinburgh and died there, wrote on philosophy, history, economics, and religion, and was among the most prominent figures of the Scottish Enlightenment.

## Life and Context

Hume was born on 7 May 1711 into a family of the small landowning class, and studied at the University of Edinburgh before abandoning the study of law and turning to philosophy. He completed the *Treatise of Human Nature* in France, at La Flèche, while in his twenties, and said that it "fell dead-born from the press" because of the lack of interest in it. He then tried to apply for the chair of moral philosophy at Edinburgh (1744–1745) and was rejected on the charge of atheism and skepticism, as he was likewise at Glasgow. He worked as Keeper of the Advocates' Library in Edinburgh (1752–1757), where he wrote the *History of England* in six volumes (1754–1761), which brought him fame and money. He served as secretary of the British embassy in Paris (1763–1765) and was celebrated by the salons of the French Enlightenment; he became acquainted with Rousseau and then fell out with him. He died on 25 August 1776.

## What He Gave

### Book One: Of the Understanding

In the *Treatise of Human Nature* Hume showed that what we call causation is not a necessary connection in reality but a mental habit (Custom): we observe that "A" is followed by "B," and so we expect this order to recur, but there is no evidence of a necessity linking them. This is his famous argument on the "Problem of Induction," which Kant later confronted. In the same place he presented the "bundle of perceptions" theory of the self: when one looks within oneself, one finds no fixed "I," only successive perceptions.

### Book Two: Of the Passions

Hume distinguished between the direct passions (desire and aversion, joy and grief, hope and fear), which arise from pleasure and pain immediately, and the indirect passions (pride and humility, love and hatred), which require an intermediate idea of the self or of another person, since pride consists only in my attributing something good to myself. In this book too is his most famous statement on motivation: "Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions."

### Book Three: Of Morals

Hume presented the sentimentalist position in ethics (Sentimentalism): virtue is not discovered by reason but by feeling. Here is his most famous statement on the move from "what is" (Is) to "what ought to be" (Ought); he observed that moral writers move between the two without justification: "I am surpriz'd to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not" (*Treatise* 3.1.1). This cut between facts and values (The Fact-Value Gap) became one of the central questions of analytic moral philosophy.

## Experience and Skepticism

In *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), the most mature formulation of his position, Hume set out his critique of causation: we expect that the sun will rise tomorrow, but this is a psychological expectation, not a logical inference, since reason cannot establish a logical ground for supposing that the future will resemble the past. In the chapter on miracles he held that testimony to a miracle does not reach the degree of credibility reached by the uniform experience that contradicts it. In the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (1779) he examined the argument from design and criticized it severely.

## Influence

- **Kant**: said that Hume "awakened him from his dogmatic slumber," and built his critique of pure reason as a reply to him.
- **Adam Smith**: a close friend, who built the *Theory of Moral Sentiments* on the same Humean ground of sympathy, though differing in its details; he is the one who wrote the famous letter describing Hume's calm, unafraid death, which provoked more anger than all of Hume's books did.
- **Logical Positivism**: the Vienna Circle drew on his distinction between propositions concerning relations of ideas and propositions concerning matters of fact.
- **Pragmatism**: James and Dewey were influenced by Hume's empiricism.

## Limits and Criticism

Thomas Reid and the Scottish "common sense" philosophers criticized him for drawing skeptical conclusions from false premises in the theory of ideas, and Kant answered his critique of causation by holding that it is an a priori category of the understanding. Scholars have asked about his position on causation: is he a skeptic who holds that there is no necessity in the world, or a "realist" who holds that necessity exists but we do not know it? This remains a matter of debate among interpreters. As for the theory of the self, Hume himself said in the appendix to the *Treatise* that he had found no satisfactory solution to it. His moral theory has been charged with making morality fickle with emotion, and scholars have not agreed on whether it defends a constancy of moral judgments through the idea of the "general point of view."

## Major Works

- A Treatise of Human Nature (1739–1740).
- An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748).
- An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals (1751).
- The Natural History of Religion (1757).
- Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (published posthumously in 1779).
