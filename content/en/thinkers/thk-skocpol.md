# Theda Skocpol

An American sociologist, one of the founders of the new "comparative historical sociology", which explained the great revolutions by the structure of the state and international relations rather than by ideological revolutionary intentions alone.

## The Central Idea

In *States and Social Revolutions* (1979), Skocpol presented a rigorous systematic comparative analysis of three great revolutions (the French of 1789, the Russian of 1917, the Chinese of 1949), criticizing earlier theories that explained revolutions either by the ideological will of revolutionary actors or by abstract economic deprivation alone. She argued instead for a "structural" approach focusing on two combined factors: (1) the collapse of the old state apparatus's ability to continue, often under the pressure of an international military or financial crisis that weakens central control; and (2) independent rural peasant uprisings that exploit this power vacuum. She stressed that successful revolutions were not the product of a tightly controlled prior revolutionary plan by an ideological leadership, but the result of a complex structural interaction between the crisis of the state and the popular uprising, often independent of it in its early stages.

## Historical Context

Theda Skocpol wrote her thesis in an American academic context that saw, during the 1970s, wide debate over the explanation of the great revolutions. She was influenced by the work of earlier scholars such as Barrington Moore Jr. in *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy* (1966). But she criticized the then-dominant cultural and socio-psychological approaches (such as the "relative deprivation" theories that explained revolution by the growth of subjective collective frustration), considering that they overlook the decisive role of the structure of the state and of international relations in explaining the timing and likelihood of success of the great revolutions in particular.

## Additional Ideas

In *States and Social Revolutions* and in the 1985 volume *Bringing the State Back In* (edited with Peter Evans and Dietrich Rueschemeyer), Skocpol stressed the autonomy and capacities of states, arguing that states are not merely neutral instruments executing the will of the economically dominant class, as traditional Marxist theory assumes, but relatively autonomous actors with their own interests and organizational logic (such as maintaining internal stability and external military capacity). This relative autonomy of the state is what sometimes explains policies that do not directly serve the interests of any particular social class but the interests of the state apparatus itself in survival and continuity.

## Method

Skocpol adopted the "disciplined comparative-historical method" (Comparative-Historical Method), drawing on John Stuart Mill's logic of the "method of agreement" and the "method of difference" to compare cases of successful revolution (France, Russia, China) with cases that were apparently similar but did not lead to a full social revolution (such as Prussia and Japan at the dawn of modernity). She tried to isolate the shared causal factors that distinguished the revolutionary cases from their non-revolutionary counterparts despite the similarity of their apparent initial conditions in many other respects.

## Influence

This work was highly influential in comparative historical sociology and fed the "Bringing the State Back In" current in the sociological analysis of major social change. Skocpol was criticized by scholars interested in the role of ideology, culture and conscious human agency in making revolutions (such as William Sewell, in a 1985 article on the French case), and the Iranian revolution of 1979 challenged the structural account, to which she responded in "Rentier State and Shi'a Islam in the Iranian Revolution" (1982) by acknowledging the part played by religious networks and ideology. Such critics considered her structural analysis to marginalize ideas and revolutionary leadership in favor of almost deterministic structural factors. Yet she remained one of the most influential theorists of comparative historical sociology, while her later research extended to analyzing the American social welfare state and the role of women's civic movements in its early formation. In *Protecting Soldiers and Mothers* (1992), she put forward a thesis opposing the prevailing view that the American welfare state is "backward" relative to its European counterparts, showing that the United States in fact developed a relatively early social welfare system, but in a different form, built on veterans' pensions and then on "maternalist" measures such as mothers' pensions, rather than the "paternalist" social insurance for male wage earners found in Europe, with the maternalist measures driven by the pressure of politically active civic women's organizations in that early era. This work received the American Political Science Association's Woodrow Wilson Foundation Award. Skocpol is a professor at Harvard University and was president of the American Political Science Association in 2002-03. Her later research has included American civic associations (*Diminished Democracy*, 2003) and, with Vanessa Williamson, the Tea Party (*The Tea Party and the Remaking of Republican Conservatism*, 2012).
