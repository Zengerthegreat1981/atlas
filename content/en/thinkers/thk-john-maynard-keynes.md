# John Maynard Keynes

**John Maynard Keynes** was a British economist regarded as one of the most influential economic thinkers of the twentieth century. His book *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) marked a methodological break with the prevailing classical economics and founded an entire new field later known as modern macroeconomics. He also played a direct practical role in designing the international financial system after the Second World War.

## From Cambridge to the British Treasury

Keynes studied mathematics at King's College, Cambridge, before moving to economics under the influence of his teacher **Alfred Marshall**. He worked at the British Treasury during the First World War, which gave him direct experience of practical economic policy. He first gained fame with *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919), in which he sharply criticized the heavy reparations imposed on Germany by the Treaty of Versailles, warning that they would sow the seeds of a later European economic and political crisis.

## From a critical theory to a revolution in macroeconomics

Keynes worked throughout the 1920s to develop his ideas on money and the business cycle, publishing *A Tract on Monetary Reform* (1923) and *A Treatise on Money* (1930). The Great Depression of the 1930s, however, and the failure of classical economics to explain the persistence of mass unemployment for many years, led him to formulate a radical theoretical break in the *General Theory*. In it, the economy can settle at an equilibrium with long-lasting involuntary unemployment, and no automatic market mechanism guarantees a return to full employment. This contradicted the classical doctrine of **Say's law**, according to which supply creates its own demand.

## Effective demand and liquidity preference

Keynes replaced the classical logic of supply with the concept of **effective demand**: the actual level of aggregate spending determines output and employment. He also developed the theory of **liquidity preference** to explain the interest rate as the price of giving up liquid money, rather than the price of waiting. This allows situations in which lowering the interest rate cannot stimulate sufficient investment when deep pessimism prevails among investors. He called the forces driving investment decisions under fundamental uncertainty about the future "animal spirits".

## Fiscal policy as a tool of recovery

Keynes concluded that the government can intervene with public spending to raise aggregate demand when the private sector falters, even if this produces a temporary budget deficit. He relied on the idea of the **multiplier**: each additional unit of government spending generates an increase in total income larger than its original value, through successive rounds of spending and income (the concept was first formalised by his Cambridge colleague Richard Kahn in 1931). This idea formed the theoretical basis for Western reconstruction policies after the Second World War.

## Bretton Woods: from theory to the design of the international system

Keynes represented Britain at the **Bretton Woods conference** of 1944, which redrew the international monetary system after the war. He proposed an International Clearing Union with a new international reserve currency (the "bancor") and a mechanism for addressing trade imbalances that would place part of the burden on surplus countries, not only on debtor countries. This plan was not accepted in full, in the face of the American proposals associated with **Harry Dexter White**, which anchored the system to the dollar and gold. Keynes nevertheless took part in shaping the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, and he chaired the commission on the Bank.

## Academic reception and diffusion

**John Hicks** and **Paul Samuelson** restated Keynes's ideas in simplified models (the IS–LM model and the neoclassical–Keynesian synthesis), which dominated the teaching of macroeconomics until the mid-1970s. Later critics from within the same tradition, among them **Joan Robinson** and the post-Keynesians, argued that this simplification stripped Keynes's original ideas about fundamental uncertainty of their radical edge.

## Criticism from the Austrian school

Keynes faced sharp counter-criticism from **Friedrich Hayek**, who saw in the prescription of intensive financial and monetary state intervention a structural threat to economic freedom. He feared that such intervention might gradually slide toward comprehensive central planning, which would threaten political freedoms themselves. Hayek's *The Road to Serfdom* (1944) developed this concern about planning more generally; Keynes wrote to Hayek privately that he agreed with much of the book while doubting that moderate intervention would lead down that road. The Keynes–Hayek debate became one of the best-known ideological polarizations in the history of modern economic thought.

## Early death and enduring legacy

Keynes died of a heart attack in April 1946, having long suffered from heart disease that was aggravated by his exertions in wartime and post-war financial negotiations between Britain and the United States. He left an intellectual legacy divided among several currents competing to interpret what Keynes actually meant, from the reconciling neoclassical synthesis to the radical post-Keynesian reading.

## A many-sided personal life

Keynes's activity was not confined to academic economics. He was a collector of art and a successful investor in financial markets, managing his own wealth and, as Bursar, the investments of King's College, Cambridge. He was also a prominent member of the **Bloomsbury Group**, the literary and artistic circle that included influential British writers and artists in the early twentieth century. This range of interests made him a public intellectual whose reach extended beyond narrow economic specialization into wider cultural and political life in interwar Britain.
