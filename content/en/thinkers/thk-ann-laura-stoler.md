# Ann Laura Stoler

An American anthropological historian and professor at the New School for Social Research, who developed an influential method for reading colonial archives as objects of analysis in their own right, rather than as neutral repositories of documents.

## What She Gave

- **Along the Archival Grain (Along the Archival Grain: Epistemic Anxieties and Colonial Common Sense, 2009)**: her best-known work, in which she proposes reading the archives of the Dutch colonial administration in Indonesia "along the grain" rather than only "against the grain," as Ranajit Guha and the subaltern studies school proposed. Her study of the anxieties of the colonial officials themselves (their hesitations, internal contradictions, and repeated reformulations of racial categories) reveals the structure of colonial power from within, as much as a counter-reading in favor of the colonized subject's voice does.
- **Carnal Knowledge and Imperial Power (2002)**: a study of how the Dutch colonial authorities regulated sexual relations, marriage and child-rearing between Europeans and indigenous populations, showing that controlling intimate and family life was a central instrument for producing and reproducing colonial racial distinctions, not a side effect of them.
- **The concept of "colonial common sense"**: it describes how seemingly "obvious" racial and social assumptions take shape among colonial administrators through a repeated daily bureaucratic practice (writing reports, classifying populations), rather than through a openly declared ideology.

## Position within the Current

Stoler is the leading representative of the "archival turn" in contemporary colonial history, combining the tools of anthropology with Foucauldian archaeological analysis to read the archive itself, not only its content, as an object of power and knowledge.

## Major Works

- *Capitalism and Confrontation in Sumatra's Plantation Belt* (1985).
- *Carnal Knowledge and Imperial Power: Race and the Intimate in Colonial Rule* (2002).
- *Along the Archival Grain: Epistemic Anxieties and Colonial Common Sense* (2009).
