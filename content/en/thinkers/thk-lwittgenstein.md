# Ludwig Wittgenstein

An Austrian-British philosopher, one of the most important philosophers of the twentieth century, who developed a philosophy in two radically different phases: logical atomism (1911–1921), then a later philosophy of language-games and ordinary use (1929–1951).

## What he gave

- **The Tractatus (Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 1921)**: the world is the totality of facts, not things; language is a logical mirror of reality; elementary propositions are combinations of names that picture atomic facts; complex propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions. The Tractatus ends with the famous seventh proposition: "What we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence" (Wovon man nicht sprechen kann, darüber muss man schweigen), which is a call to the limit of what can be said meaningfully, not a verdict on the divine in particular.
- **Philosophical Investigations (published after his death in 1953)**: the philosophy of "language-games" (Sprachspiele), the rejection of the ideal logical picture, the analysis of meaning by use, and the rejection of the "private meaning" (private language argument).

## Biography

Ludwig Wittgenstein was born in Vienna on 26 April 1889 into one of the wealthiest industrial families of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, and his home was a musical and cultural center. He studied engineering in Berlin and then Manchester, and turned from aeronautical engineering to the foundations of mathematics, so he went to Gottlob Frege in Jena, and Frege advised him to study with Bertrand Russell at Cambridge, where he enrolled in 1911. In 1913 he moved to Norway to write in solitude, then volunteered in the Austrian army in the First World War, and completed the Tractatus in 1918 while on the Italian front, after which the Italians captured him. The Tractatus was first published in German in 1921, then in English in 1922 in a translation by C. K. Ogden (with F. P. Ramsey) with an introduction by Russell. After deciding that he had solved the problems of philosophy, he gave up his large inheritance and worked as a teacher in rural schools in Austria (1920–1926), then returned to Cambridge in 1929, where he received the doctorate on the same work and worked as a fellow of Trinity College, then as professor of philosophy (1939–1947). He died in Cambridge on 29 April 1951.

## From the Tractatus to the Investigations

The later Wittgenstein reconsidered the foundations of the Tractatus: a proposition does not acquire its meaning from picturing atomic facts, but from its place in social "practices" which he called "language-games", and the word "game" in him indicates family resemblances and not a common essence. In the *Philosophical Investigations* (1953, edited by G. E. M. Anscombe and R. Rhees) he treats the question of rule-following: nothing in the rule itself determines its application, and what determines it is agreement in forms of life. His argument against "private language" is that the inner naming of a sensation, which no one but me can verify, has no criterion of correctness, and so does not play the role of a language. In the manuscripts of *On Certainty* (published 1969) he approaches the question of doubt: some propositions, such as that I have two hands, function as "hinges" on which inference turns and do not admit of proof.

## Arabic reception

The Tractatus was rendered into Arabic under the title "Risala mantiqiyya falsafiyya" in a translation by Azmi Islam (Cairo: Anglo-Egyptian Bookshop, 1968), and Abd al-Razzaq Banur translated the "Investigations" (Beirut: Arab Organization for Translation, 2007). These are the basis on which Arabic studies in the philosophy of language have built.

## The transitional phase

Between 1929 and 1936 Wittgenstein wrote in Cambridge a series of remarks and lectures, then in 1933–1935 dictated to his students what became known as the "Blue Book" and the "Brown Book", which were the first presentation of his new method: to consider how a word is used in simple examples before asking about its essence. In this phase he criticized his picture theory, and moved from logical analysis to a description resembling therapy, in which philosophy is an activity that removes the confusions that arise when "language goes on holiday".

## Limitation

The Tractatus is a terse, coded book open to many readings, from logical positivism to the reading known as the "resolute" reading in Cora Diamond and James Conant. As for his later philosophy, he did not bring it to a final form, so the "Investigations" remained arranged remarks and not a system. The private language argument and the reading of "rule-following" are the subject of wide interpretive dispute, including Saul Kripke's "Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language" (1982), which offered a "skeptical" reading on which the discussion is still open.

## Influence

He influenced logical positivism (the Tractatus was a central text for the Vienna Circle, although Wittgenstein did not share its program), ordinary language philosophy and later analytic philosophy of language and mind (Anscombe, Ryle, Kripke), whether in agreement or in reaction.

## Main works

- *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921).
- *Philosophical Investigations* (published after his death in 1953).
- *On Certainty* (published after his death in 1969).
- *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics* (published after his death in 1956).
