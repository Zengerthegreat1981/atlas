# Heraclitus of Ephesus

A Greek philosopher from Ephesus in Ionia (c. 535 – 475 BCE), the philosopher of becoming, perpetual change, and the harmony of opposites; author of the image of the river made famous in the formulation "You cannot step into the same river twice," which is Plato's wording and not his own. The surviving fragment reads: "Upon those who step into the same rivers, other and yet other waters flow," in which stability itself (the river) is what change (the water) produces, not what it negates. He held that living, intelligent "fire" is the dynamic substance of the cosmos, and that the world is governed by a comprehensive wisdom and a single cosmic law, the "Logos."

## Life and Context

Heraclitus was born in Ephesus, one of the largest cities of Ionia on the coast of Asia Minor, into an aristocratic family said to descend from the founders of the city. His inheritance there was the hereditary priestly office of "king," which, according to the account of Diogenes Laertius (Book IX), he relinquished to his brother. In ancient tradition he was known by the epithet "the Obscure" (ho skoteinos) for the obscurity and density of his style, and for his contempt for ordinary people and for the great poets and sages: he attacked Homer, Hesiod, Pythagoras, and Xenophanes for their abundance of learning without understanding. According to the ancient account he deposited his book in the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, and only fragments of it have reached us, 126 numbered fragments in the Diels-Kranz edition.

## What He Gave

He founded the philosophy of becoming and made the unity of opposites a principle rather than an exception: the road up and the road down are one, the sea is the purest and the foulest water, and "war is the father of all things," that is, the tension between opposites is what holds a thing in being, so that if the conflict were removed the cosmos would dissolve rather than be preserved. In the image of the bow and the lyre he saw "a harmony turning back on itself in two directions," that is, the hidden attunement is stronger than the apparent.

The "Logos" is for him the word, the proportion, and the common law on which everything proceeds; it is what a human being ought to follow, while most people live as if each had a private understanding. "Fire" is read by many scholars not as a first matter in the strict physical sense but as an emblem of the cosmos's ordered, ever-renewed life, for the cosmos "was made by neither god nor man, but ever was, is, and will be: an ever-living fire, kindling in measures and going out in measures." As to the soul, he spoke of an unfathomable depth: "You would not find the limits of the soul, even by travelling every road, so deep is its logos." Later students of psychology saw in this an early indication of the depth of the self.

## Influence and Reception

He left a lasting mark on Stoicism through the Logos and fire, and on Plato, who attributed to the "Heracliteans" the doctrine of universal flux in the *Cratylus* and the *Theaetetus*; Aristotle criticized him in the *Metaphysics* because the denial of the principle of non-contradiction follows from his view. In the modern period Hegel saw in the unity of opposites the origin of the dialectic, and said in his lectures on the history of philosophy that there is no proposition of Heraclitus that he had not adopted in his logic. Nietzsche was influenced by him in the conception of innocent becoming, and Heidegger devoted a whole seminar to him with Eugen Fink in Freiburg (1966/67). In the earliest Arabic sources on Greek philosophy his name entered with the "pre-Socratic philosophers," and Yusuf Karam introduced him in his history of Greek philosophy.

## Ethics and Knowledge

His fragments contain ethical and epistemic guidance: "A person's character is his fate," for those who read his famous phrase (ēthos anthrōpō daimōn), that is, one's character is one's destiny. He says that "waking" and sleep are images of the condition of people: most live like sleepers, unaware of what they do, while the sage is the one who listens to the Logos. Hence he counted thinking a great virtue common to humankind, and he put forward reticence and caution toward knowledge accumulated without understanding.

## Limits and Criticism

The truth is that everything we know of him is fragments transmitted by later authors, such as Plato, Aristotle, Clement of Alexandria, Hippolytus, and Sextus Empiricus, each of whom had a purpose that guided his quotation, so the ordering and interpretation of the fragments have become a matter of sharp disagreement among his students. Aristotle and the later tradition faulted him for obscurity and contradiction, and some scholars hold that the "theory of flux" attributed to him was made by Plato and his followers, and that Heraclitus himself spoke of the constancy of unity beneath change. The meaning of "fire," the "unity of opposites," and the relation of the Logos to human knowledge are among the most contested questions of his interpretation, and it is observed that the readings of him by Hegel, Nietzsche, and Heidegger project their own systems onto a fragmentary text.

## Major Works

- *On Nature* (Peri Physeōs), the only book attributed to him, which he deposited in the temple of Artemis at Ephesus; only the fragments have reached us.
