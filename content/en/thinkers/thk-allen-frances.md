# Allen Frances

An American psychiatrist (born 1942), known for chairing the task force that produced the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), published in 1994. He is also known for his later sharp criticism of the expansion of psychiatric diagnosis in contemporary American medicine, including the influence of the manual he himself oversaw.

## Career

Early in his academic career, Frances worked in academic psychiatry at Cornell University Medical College (New York), with research interests that included personality disorders and diagnostic classification, and he later became chair of the Department of Psychiatry at Duke University School of Medicine. He took part in the work on earlier diagnostic manuals. By 1988 he was chair of the DSM-IV task force, an appointment often dated to 1987. At that time he sought a "diagnostic conservatism" approach, under which no change would be made to the system without accumulated empirical evidence, having concluded that the two previous editions had opened the door to a rapid expansion of the boundaries of illness. DSM-IV was published in 1994, followed by a text revision in 2000.

## Contributions

### Supervision of DSM-IV and the Evidence-Review Method

The task force relied on systematic reviews of the literature, reanalysis of published data, and field trials before approving any change. This differed from the earlier editions, which had been shaped more by expert consensus. Frances later acknowledged, however, that the manual had unintentionally contributed to waves of overdiagnosis. For example, the introduction of Asperger's disorder in 1994 coincided with a sharp rise in the diagnosis of autism spectrum conditions. The rise in diagnoses of pediatric bipolar disorder, and of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, was linked to loose interpretation of the criteria and to the influence of the pharmaceutical industry and of marketing directed at physicians and parents.

### The Concept of Diagnostic Fads

Frances described as "fads" or "false epidemics" the sharp rise in diagnosis rates of three disorders in children: autism, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and bipolar disorder. He argued that these increases resulted from a redefinition of what counts as normal, not from an objective increase in illness. This observation is consistent with public health studies that link diagnosis rates to changes in awareness, criteria, insurance coverage, and school services.

### Critique of DSM-5 and the Argument of Saving Normal

Beginning in 2009, Frances published a series of articles in *Psychiatric Times* in which he criticized proposals of the DSM-5 task force, which was chaired by David Kupfer. Among these proposals were the adoption of "attenuated psychosis syndrome," the introduction of "disruptive mood dysregulation disorder," and the removal of the bereavement exclusion from major depression. He feared these proposals would turn ordinary sadness and childhood anger outbursts into conditions to be treated with medication. He gathered his critique in his book *Saving Normal* (2013), in which he described diagnostic inflation and the influence of the pharmaceutical industry on diagnostic policy. He called for slowing the pace of change and for protecting the boundary between the normal and the pathological. This position was consistent with the argument of Horwitz and Wakefield in *The Loss of Sadness* (2007), which holds that psychiatry confuses justified grief with depression.

### Other Writings

Frances wrote *Essentials of Psychiatric Diagnosis*, to help clinicians apply the criteria of the manual with caution. In 2013 he published an article in *Annals of Internal Medicine* on the crisis of confidence in psychiatric diagnosis. In 2017 he published *Twilight of American Sanity*, in which he examined the social psychology behind the rise of U.S. President Donald Trump and argued against assigning Trump a psychiatric diagnosis. (The American Psychiatric Association's Goldwater rule bars its members from giving professional opinions on public figures they have not examined.)

## Legacy

Frances's position is widely cited in discussions of the limits of psychiatry and of the relationship between diagnostic classification and the pharmaceutical industry. He is one of the few voices that combine internal institutional authority (having chaired an official diagnostic task force) with a later, fundamental critique of the system he helped draft. His criticism is often credited with helping to soften some controversial DSM-5 proposals (for example, attenuated psychosis syndrome was placed in the section for conditions needing further study rather than adopted as a diagnosis), although the extent of his influence is disputed. Separately, from around 2009 to 2010 the US National Institute of Mental Health developed the Research Domain Criteria project as a research alternative to diagnostic categories.

## Limitations and Criticism

Critics, including Kupfer and Regier, argue that Frances presented an exaggerated picture of the DSM-5 proposals, and that the manual's field trials and procedures were broader than he portrayed. Others contend that his warnings about overdiagnosis can be used to downplay real suffering among patients who receive inadequate treatment, since overdiagnosis and underdiagnosis coexist in practice. Some observers note that responsibility for the alleged diagnostic epidemics cannot be settled by evidence alone, because other factors operated at the same time. This is a methodological disagreement that has not been resolved.
