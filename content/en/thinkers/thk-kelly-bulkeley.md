# Kelly Bulkeley

An American scholar of religion and a prominent contemporary researcher on dreaming as a religious and cultural phenomenon subject to comparative analysis, rather than a purely clinical topic. He received his academic training at the University of Chicago Divinity School, where he combined the comparative history-of-religions method established in the field by earlier generations of the Chicago school, among them Mircea Eliade, with empirical approaches from psychology and sleep studies.

## Definition

Bulkeley treats dreaming as a borderline phenomenon in which neurobiology intersects with religious experience. On the one hand, dreaming is a physiological activity that can be studied in the laboratory (sleep stages, rapid eye movement). On the other hand, throughout religious history it has formed a major channel for revelation, prophecy and contact with the sacred in many traditions. Bulkeley seeks to study both dimensions through a systematic comparison that does not reduce either one to the other.

## Historical Context

Bulkeley has an interdisciplinary formation: a bachelor's degree from Stanford University, then a master's in theological studies from Harvard Divinity School, then a PhD in religion and psychological studies from the University of Chicago Divinity School. There he encountered the comparative "history of religions" tradition associated with Joachim Wach, Mircea Eliade and Joseph Kitagawa. This three-part formation (religion, psychology, comparative history) enabled him to approach dreaming from several angles that go beyond the narrow clinical framework of classical Freudian or Jungian analysis.

## Main Ideas and Method

Bulkeley created the Sleep and Dream Database, a large digital archive of dream reports that allows quantitative analysis. This is a tool uncommon in the history of religions, which had traditionally relied on individual textual reading. Bulkeley argues that certain types of dream (falling, flying, being chased by a frightening creature, meeting a deceased person) recur widely across cultures and eras. This suggests, in his view, a shared psychological-neurological basis, over which various religious interpretations are subsequently applied according to the cultural framework of each tradition.

## Principal Works and Representatives

In his central book *Dreaming in the World's Religions* (2008), he brought together a comparative survey of the functions of dreaming across the major religious traditions (including Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism and Buddhism, among others), showing how dreaming was used in each as a means of revelation, guidance or spiritual purification. Before this, he edited a reference anthology titled *Dreams: A Reader on the Religious, Cultural and Psychological Dimensions of Dreaming* (2001), in which he gathered a variety of religious, psychological and anthropological approaches. He has also served as president of the International Association for the Study of Dreams (IASD).

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Dream interpretation occupies a central place in the Islamic heritage, from the classical "interpretation of dreams" books, such as the works attributed to Ibn Sirin (d. 110 AH), to the status of the good dream in a hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari as "one part of forty-six parts of prophecy". Bulkeley's comparative survey treats Islam as one of the traditions with a developed theory of dreaming. Islamic tradition classifies dreams into the true vision from God, the dream from the self, and the dream from Satan, a distinction that can be compared, functionally, with modern distinctions between dreaming as a neurological process and dreaming as a source of meaning.

## Impact and Criticism

Bulkeley helped extend the study of dreaming beyond the Freudian and Jungian clinical frameworks toward a comparative religious and cultural approach, and his database offers quantitative tools to the field. A general methodological caution applies to this kind of work: reliance on dream reports, rather than direct laboratory recording of sleep stages, leaves conclusions exposed to biases of memory and narration.

Bulkeley seeks to avoid a double reduction: he neither treats religious dreaming as meaningless neural noise nor accepts every religious interpretation uncritically, but takes the dreamer's experience seriously while subjecting it to comparative and empirical analysis. He also writes for general audiences, for example in *Psychology Today*.
