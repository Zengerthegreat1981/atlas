# Stephen Levinson

British anthropological linguist (Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics), one of the leading contemporary researchers on the relationship between language and cognition. With Penelope Brown he co-authored the 1978 essay and 1987 book that set the standard face-based account of linguistic politeness.

## Absolute Spatial Orientations

Levinson led a large comparative research project on how different languages represent space. Speakers of languages that use an absolute directional system (cardinal directions, as in the Australian language Guugu Yimithirr), rather than a relative system such as right and left in English, performed differently on some non-linguistic spatial tasks. Levinson and colleagues have treated those results as evidence for a limited form of linguistic relativity. The experiments have been debated, both in their design and in how far they generalize.

## Politeness Theory

Levinson developed, together with the anthropologist Penelope Brown, one of the most influential theories of linguistic politeness (first published in 1978 and as a book in 1987). It draws on Erving Goffman's concept of "face." Speakers use graded linguistic strategies, direct or indirect and softening or intensifying, to manage the potential threat to the face of the addressee or of the speaker themselves in socially sensitive speech acts such as requests and objections.

## The Three Neo-Gricean Principles

In his book *Pragmatics* (1983) Levinson gave an influential textbook account of Grice's work, and in *Presumptive Meanings: The Theory of Generalized Conversational Implicature* (2000), building on his earlier papers, he reformulated the four conversational maxims of Paul Grice (quantity, quality, relation, manner) into a simplified system of three interacting neo-Gricean principles. The Q-principle produces implicatures from the non-use of a stronger available expression. The I-principle (informativeness) pushes the listener to enrich a brief expression to its stereotypical, more specific interpretation. The M-principle (manner) links unusual expressions to unusual meanings. Levinson argues that these "generalized conversational implicatures" are inferred by default, independently of any particular context. Particularized implicatures, by contrast, require a specific context. This distinction has deeply influenced contemporary pragmatics.

## Anthropological Linguistics and Comparative Language Study

As a director at the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics in Nijmegen, Levinson led large comparative field projects covering many little-documented languages around the world. These examined variation in areas such as the expression of time and space and speech acts. He supported the view that actual linguistic diversity is far broader than universalist formal theories, such as Chomskyan generative grammar, allow. This placed him in intellectual confrontation with hypotheses of innate linguistic universals.

## The Contemporary Debate on Linguistic Relativity

Levinson played an active role in reviving the scientific debate on the "linguistic relativity" hypothesis, associated with Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf, after decades of widespread skepticism in generative linguistics. Unlike Lera Boroditsky, who emphasizes relatively strong linguistic effects on cognition in domains such as time and color, Levinson adopts a more cautious stance, sometimes called "moderate relativity." In this view, language shapes measurable "habitual thought" in specific tasks, such as spatial orientation. It does not determine human cognition deterministically, as in the strong formulation of Whorf's original hypothesis.

## Max Planck Institute and the Language Diversity Project

Levinson headed the Max Planck Cognitive Anthropology Research Group in Nijmegen and later became a director of the Max Planck Institute for Psycholinguistics. The group, later the Language and Cognition department, ran long-term fieldwork on little-documented languages, including Australia, Rossel Island in Papua New Guinea, and Latin America, and used shared elicitation tasks so that results could be compared.

## Awards and Academic Recognition

His career runs from Cambridge linguistics and the pragmatics textbook of 1983 to the comparative work on diversity at Nijmegen.

## Collaboration with Penelope Brown

Levinson developed politeness theory in close collaboration with the anthropologist Penelope Brown, Brown's fieldwork was on Tzeltal, a Mayan language of Mexico, and Levinson's on Tamil in South India; with English data these gave their theory a cross-cultural empirical basis from the outset. Later critics argued that this basis remained limited in scope compared with the breadth of the theory's universal claims.
