# Sherry Ortner: Practice Theory and Ritual Agency

Sherry Ortner (1941–present) is the architect of **practice theory** in anthropology, a theoretical framework that attempts to bridge the seemingly irreconcilable gap between structure and agency. Her foundational question—how do human agents reproduce, transform, and resist the social structures that constrain them?—has shaped decades of anthropological research. Ortner demonstrated that ritual and cultural performance are not merely expressions of underlying structures but are *themselves* the mechanisms through which structures are enacted, negotiated, and sometimes transformed. Her work, grounded in feminist anthropology's insistence on attending to gender and power, elevated practice theory beyond abstract philosophy into a concrete methodology for understanding how people *make history*, even if not always in the conditions of their choosing.

## Biographical and Intellectual Context

Sherry Ortner was born and raised in Newark, New Jersey, and studied anthropology and philosophy at Bryn Mawr College (1959–1963). She came to anthropology during a period of intellectual ferment, when the discipline was engaging with symbolic anthropology (Turner, Geertz) while also being pushed by feminist scholars to attend to questions of gender, power, and whose perspectives anthropology had been representing.

She conducted her primary fieldwork among the **Sherpa** people of Nepal (1966–1968, 1970–1971), focusing on their religious practice, cosmology, and social organization. Her Sherpa work demonstrated how Buddhist ritual practice both reproduced Sherpa society and provided resources for individual agency and contestation—a key insight that practice theory would develop further.

Ortner held positions at University of Michigan (1970–1996) and Princeton (1996–2013), and has been deeply engaged in theorizing not just ritual and culture but anthropology's own practice as a discipline. Her development of *historical ethnography*—attending to how people experience and narrate historical processes—has been enormously influential. She also pioneered what she calls *studying up*—conducting ethnography among powerful and privileged groups, not just marginalized ones.

## Core Theoretical Framework

### The Practice Approach: Bridging Structure and Agency

Ortner's central contribution is the **practice approach**, a theoretical framework that treats human action ("practice") as the locus where structure and agency meet. She argues that previous anthropology had been stuck in a sterile dichotomy: either humans are determined by structure (structural-functionalism, structuralism) or humans are free agents whose actions are unpredictable (voluntarism, existentialism). Practice theory, by contrast, asks: how do humans, as structured beings living within constraining structures, nonetheless exercise agency and sometimes transform those structures?

The answer, she argues, lies in attending carefully to *how people act*—to the specific practices through which they live. In these practices, both structure and agency are simultaneously present: structure provides the resources and constraints within which action occurs, while agency consists of the creative, improvisational ways people navigate and sometimes challenge those structures.

### Ritual as Practice: The Case of Sherpa Buddhism

Ortner's analysis of Sherpa Buddhist ritual exemplifies the practice approach. Sherpa Buddhist rituals (particularly high-altitude rituals on sacred mountains) are not mere mechanical expressions of Buddhist doctrine or Sherpa social structure. Rather, they are *performances* through which Sherpas both enact their religious faith and negotiate their social relationships, gender roles, and relationships to power.

Crucially, these ritual performances are not uniform or determinate. Different Sherpas participate in and interpret rituals differently. Some rituals emphasize hierarchy and male religious authority; others (particularly those led by women ritual specialists) carve out spaces for female agency. The rituals are simultaneously structures (they follow established patterns, norms, and meanings) and openings (they allow creative reinterpretation, improvisation, and contestation).

This analysis shows that ritual is not a mechanism for reproducing society unchanged. Rather, rituals are one key site where structures are perpetually negotiated.

### Gender, Power, and the Politics of Cultural Production

Ortner's contribution to feminist anthropology is to show that **women are not passive victims of patriarchal structure but active agents in producing and sometimes transforming it**. In her Sherpa research, she documented how women used ritual, kinship claims, and religious authority to assert themselves within a male-dominated religious hierarchy.

More broadly, she argued that anthropology's previous erasure of women's agency reflected not the facts of human societies but anthropology's own blindness. When one attends carefully to what women actually *do*—their practices, strategies, performances—one finds agency, contestation, and power, not mere victimhood.

### Habitus and the Internalization of Constraint

Drawing on Pierre Bourdieu's concept of habitus, Ortner developed the idea that structures are not merely external constraints but are *internalized*. Through socialization, people develop dispositions (habitus) that make them want to do what the structure requires. This creates what she calls "thick constraint"—it is very difficult for people to imagine acting otherwise because the structure has become part of their sense of self and possibility.

Yet habitus is not destiny. People can reflect on their dispositions, can challenge them, can risk the discomfort of acting against internalized constraint. Ritual, play, and artistic performance are particular sites where this reflexivity and contestation become possible.

### Second-Order Consciousness and the Politics of Interpretation

Ortner argues that **anthropologists need to attend to multiple levels of interpretation**. At the first-order, people act, and their action makes sense within their cultural framework. At the second-order, anthropologists interpret those actions, sometimes discovering meanings that participants themselves might not articulate. At the third-order, the anthropologist must ask: how are my interpretations themselves structured by my position, interests, and power?

This reflexivity prevents anthropology from naively claiming to simply report "what's really happening." It insists that all anthropological interpretation is itself a form of practice, with political implications.

## Major Works

**Is Female to Male as Nature is to Culture?** (1974) — Her most famous essay, arguing that the universal subordination of women stems from a pervasive symbolic association of women with nature (and men with culture). Though later critiqued for universalizing and essentialism, this work was foundational for gender anthropology and opened new research directions.

**Sherpas through Their Rituals** (1978) — Ethnographic study of Sherpa Buddhist ritual practice. Demonstrates how ritual is a form of practice through which Sherpas enact religious meaning, navigate gender relations, and negotiate their position as a minority within Nepal.

**High Religion: A Cultural and Political History of Sherpa Buddhism** (1989) — Her most ambitious work, a sweeping history of how Sherpa Buddhism developed through its engagement with Tibetan Buddhism, Hinduism, and modernity. Demonstrates how Sherpas actively shaped their religion rather than passively receiving it.

**Making Gender: The Politics and Erotics of Culture** (1996) — Collection of essays on gender, culture, and identity. Revisits her earlier work, refining and sometimes revising her arguments in light of postcolonial and queer theory. Shows her willingness to evolve her thinking.

**Anthropology and Social Theory: Culture, Power, and the Acting Subject** (2006) — Her most theoretically systematic work, articulating the practice approach comprehensively. A crucial reference for understanding contemporary practice theory. Includes her famous provocations about anthropology's place in the academy and its political responsibilities.

**Life and Death on the Margins of the Nation: Anthropology, Ethics, and the New Jersey Turnpike** (2016) — Working-class ethnography in New Jersey, studying the lives of poor and working-class Americans. Demonstrates the continued relevance of practice theory for understanding contemporary American society.

## Ethnographic Focus: The Sherpa of Nepal

Ortner conducted extensive fieldwork among the **Sherpa**, a Tibetan-speaking Buddhist ethnic group living in the high Himalayan valleys of Nepal, particularly around Mount Everest. The Sherpa were (and are) a minority within Nepal, navigating between their own religious and cultural traditions, Hindu-majority Nepal, and the broader global economy.

### Major Ethnographic Interests:

**High-altitude Buddhist ritual**: Ortner documented sacred mountain rituals where Sherpa conducted Buddhist ceremonies at extreme altitudes. These rituals expressed Sherpa relationship to the sacred geography of the Himalayas and served to generate community among dispersed Sherpa communities.

**Gender and religious leadership**: Ortner documented how women occupied complex positions in Sherpa religious life. Though male lamas held formal authority, female ritual specialists (particularly in household and local rituals) wielded significant spiritual power. She analyzed this gendered division as neither simply patriarchal oppression nor female equality, but as a complex negotiation of power.

**Sherpa Buddhism and modernity**: Ortner tracked how Sherpa Buddhism was transforming through encounter with global Buddhism, tourism, and modernization. Rather than seeing tradition being lost, she documented how Sherpas actively reinterpreted their Buddhism in contemporary contexts.

**The Sherpa and Everest**: Ortner attended to how Sherpa engagement with Everest mountaineering—as guides and high-altitude workers—shaped Sherpa identity and economy. She analyzed the political economy of mountaineering and Sherpa labor within it.

**Class differentiation**: Ortner documented increasing economic differentiation among Sherpa, with some becoming wealthy through tourism while others remained relatively poor. She analyzed ritual and religion as sites where Sherpas negotiated these class tensions.

## Theoretical Influence and Debates

Ortner's practice theory has been enormously influential, shaping how multiple generations of anthropologists approach questions of structure, agency, gender, and cultural change.

**In her favor**: Ortner provided a philosophical and methodological framework for addressing what many felt was the central problem of social science: how to understand both structure and agency without reducing one to the other. Her framework was simultaneously theoretically sophisticated and empirically grounded.

**Major criticisms**:

**The nature/culture argument**: Her early essay arguing that female subordination stems from universal symbolic association of women with nature was critiqued for essentialism and universalism. Critics argued it didn't account for variation across cultures and risked naturalizing gender inequality as inevitable.

**The practice-theory fetish**: Some argued that practice theory, while conceptually elegant, sometimes substitutes philosophical sophistication for empirical insight. Not all problems can be solved by invoking "practice."

**The limits of agency**: Poststructuralist critics argued that Ortner's framework, while attending to agency, still ultimately privileges human intentionality and consciousness. They insisted on attending to non-human actants, affective forces, and structural violence that exceed human agency.

**Occidentalism**: Some postcolonial scholars argued that even Ortner's sophisticated anthropology ultimately imposes Western philosophical categories (structure/agency, nature/culture) onto non-Western systems. How does practice theory itself reflect Western intellectual commitments?

## Methodological Contributions

Ortner transformed how anthropologists conduct ethnography:

**Studying up**: Rather than always studying those with less power, Ortner advocated for and practiced ethnography of the powerful and privileged. She studied high-altitude mountaineering expeditions and affluent Americans, demonstrating that anthropological methods are equally valuable for understanding privilege.

**Historical ethnography**: Ortner developed methods for studying how people experience and narrate historical processes. Rather than treating history and ethnography as separate, she showed how ethnographic methods could illuminate history and historical thinking could enrich ethnography.

**Reflexive positioning**: Ortner insisted that ethnographers be explicit about their own positioning—their power, interests, and stake in interpretation. This reflexivity became standard in contemporary ethnography.

**Multi-sited ethnography**: While not originating the method, Ortner's work helped develop ethnography that follows subjects across multiple locations and times, rather than studying a single place.

## Postcolonial Legacy and Critique

Ortner's work has been engaged extensively by postcolonial scholars. Her theoretical framework, while feminist and attentive to power, emerged from Western philosophy, and postcolonial scholars have pushed her to engage with non-Western epistemologies and knowledge systems.

**On universalism**: Postcolonial scholars have questioned her earlier universalizing claims (like nature/culture) while recognizing her later work's attention to historical specificity and cultural difference.

**On the politics of representation**: Scholars have critiqued anthropology's continued claim to represent other people's lives and have questioned whether anthropology's practice, no matter how reflexive, can overcome these power asymmetries. Ortner has engaged with these critiques seriously.

**On colonialism and modernity**: Postcolonial scholars have pushed anthropology to attend more explicitly to how colonialism, capitalism, and modernity have shaped the societies anthropologists study. Ortner's work on modernity and capitalism among the Sherpa addresses this, but critics argue more remains to be done.

## Contemporary Relevance

Ortner's framework remains central to contemporary anthropology:

**Ritual and performance studies**: Her analysis of ritual as practice continues to influence how scholars analyze religious ritual, secular ritual, performance art, and cultural performance.

**Gender and sexuality studies**: Scholars of gender, sexuality, and queer theory continue to deploy her concepts, particularly her attention to how people navigate and sometimes contest structures of gender and sexuality.

**Political anthropology**: Scholars analyzing resistance, social movements, and political change use Ortner's framework to understand how people are both structured by systems of domination and capable of challenging them.

**Digital anthropology**: Scholars studying digital culture and online communities have adapted practice theory to understand how people act within digital structures and sometimes transform them.

**What has been superseded**: The strict binary of structure and agency, while useful, has been complicated by theories of affect, materiality, and non-human agency that exceed both structure and human agency. Additionally, Ortner's emphasis on human intentionality has been challenged by scholarship on structural violence and systemic injustice that doesn't reduce to human action.

**Ongoing debates**: How does practice theory apply to increasingly distributed, digitized forms of social action? Can practice theory address climate change and other challenges that exceed human agency? These remain live questions.
