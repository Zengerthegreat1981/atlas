# Fei Xiaotong

A Chinese sociologist and anthropologist, regarded as one of the principal builders of sociology and anthropology in modern China. He was among the first to apply the methods of fieldwork-based anthropology to Chinese rural society itself rather than to distant "primitive societies," thereby helping to shift the subject of anthropology from the colonial "other" to the national society.

## Biography

Fei Xiaotong was born in 1910 in Wujiang, Jiangsu Province, into a family of intellectuals. He studied sociology at Yenching University and anthropology at Tsinghua University, and then moved to the London School of Economics (LSE), where he was a student of Bronisław Malinowski, the leading proponent of long-term participant observation, and received his doctorate in 1938. Before travelling to London, Fei conducted fieldwork in the village of Kaixiangong in Jiangsu (known in his book by the name Jiangcun), which formed the material of his doctoral dissertation and then his first book. Earlier, in 1935, he had conducted fieldwork with his first wife, Wang Tonghui, among the Yao people in the mountains of Guangxi. During that research he was seriously injured, and his wife died in an accident. He then moved to Kaixiangong to recuperate, and there he gathered the material for his first book.

After returning to China, he worked as a professor and researcher and helped establish the institutions of Chinese sociology and anthropology. With the founding of the People's Republic of China in 1949, he continued academic and administrative work, but sociology and anthropology, the fields in which he worked, were abolished as independent academic disciplines in China in 1952 on the Soviet model and were denounced as "bourgeois pseudo-science." In the Anti-Rightist Campaign of 1957, Fei was labeled a "rightist" for defending the independence of sociology, and he was barred from teaching and publishing for roughly two decades. He suffered further marginalization during the Cultural Revolution. He was formally rehabilitated after 1979, with China's opening, and his scholarly and political standing was restored. He took part in rebuilding Chinese sociology and anthropology and held senior academic and political posts until his death in Beijing in 2005.

## The Campaign Years

In the relative openness of the Hundred Flowers period, Fei published an essay in 1957, usually translated as "The Early Spring Weather of the Intellectuals," which described both hope and lingering caution among scholars. He also joined efforts to restore sociology. When the Anti-Rightist Campaign began later that year, these activities became grounds for attack. He was subjected to public criticism, in which some colleagues took part, lost important positions, and was largely cut off from research and publication. He remained attached to the Central Institute for Nationalities in Beijing.

During the Cultural Revolution, which began in 1966, Fei again came under attack as a former rightist and was assigned to manual work. Around 1969 he was sent to a May Seventh Cadre School in the countryside of Hubei, where intellectuals and officials combined physical labour with political study. After returning to Beijing in the early 1970s he took part in collective translation work on world history and related subjects. Fei later spoke about these years in interviews, including a conversation with the anthropologist Burton Pasternak published in 1988. R. David Arkush's biography treats the 1957 campaign and its background in detail.

## Key Contributions

### *Peasant Life in China* (1939)

This is the report of his dissertation on the village of Kaixiangong. It is regarded today as a reference work in the history of anthropology, because it carried the method of participant observation developed by Malinowski in the Trobriand Islands over to the study of a large agricultural "civilized" society rather than an isolated island community. In doing so it brought the analysis of the rural economy and handicraft industry (silkworm rearing) into the toolkit of anthropology.

### *Earthbound China* (1945), with Chang Chih-I

A comparative study of three villages in Yunnan that differ in their economies and patterns of land use, in which Fei deepened the analysis of the relationship between economic structure and the structures of kinship and property.

### *Xiangtu Zhongguo* (1947; published in English later as *From the Soil*)

His most important theoretical work, in which he formulated the concept of the **"differential mode of association"** (差序格局, *chaxugeju*). This describes Chinese society as a network of concentric circles radiating from the individual, in the way circles radiate from a stone dropped into water. Every relationship is measured by its distance from the "self." This contrasts with the Western model, which Fei portrays as a structure of "bundles" or groups with defined boundaries (an organization of "groups" rather than "circles"). This distinction has become a reference concept in the study of social networks and relational ethics in China, and it is cited continually in contemporary Chinese sociology.

### The Theory of "Plurality and Unity" in the Structure of the Chinese Nation (1988)

In his later phase, Fei formulated the theory of the "pluralistic-unitary structure of the Chinese nation" (中华民族多元一体格局), which describes the Chinese nation (*Zhonghua minzu*) as a single whole that was historically formed through the integration of multiple ethnic groups, each of which retained its cultural distinctiveness within a unifying political framework. This theory became an influential official framework in Chinese ethnic policy.

## Impact

- He trained students and promoted a tradition of anthropology and sociology based on social surveys and rural development.
- The concept of the "differential mode of association" influenced later studies of social networks (关系, *guanxi*) in Chinese sociology and in English-language Chinese studies.
- After 1980 he worked to connect anthropology with rural development policy and small-scale rural industrialization, and he is regarded as a pioneer of what is known as "applied anthropology" in the Chinese context.

## Limitations

Fei's work has been challenged on two main grounds. The first is methodological: many of his general conclusions about "Chinese society" rest on the study of a limited number of village cases (such as Kaixiangong and the Yunnan villages), a generalization that later scholars have criticized as statistically insufficient to represent a country of such size and regional diversity. The second is political: the character of his cooperation with the state after 1979, and his formulation of the theory of "plurality and unity" within the framework of official ethnic policy, have led some Western scholars to question how independent this framework is from the official discourse of building a unified Chinese nation, particularly in its treatment of the relationship between the Han majority and ethnic minorities.
