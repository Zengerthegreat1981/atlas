# Pierre Bourdieu

Pierre Bourdieu (1930-2002) was a French sociologist and one of the most influential social theorists of the late twentieth century. He explained how social inequality is reproduced through culture, education and taste, using the concepts of habitus, field and capital.

## Life and Context

Born in Denguin in southwestern France to a family of modest means, Bourdieu studied philosophy at the Ecole Normale Superieure in Paris. During military service in Algeria in the late 1950s he turned to ethnography, studying Kabyle society. He later directed the Centre de sociologie europeenne and held a chair at the College de France from 1981. In his final years he became a public critic of neoliberalism.

## Main Ideas

Bourdieu sought to overcome the opposition between objectivism and subjectivism. Habitus refers to durable dispositions acquired through upbringing that shape perception and action. A field is a structured social arena, such as education, art or politics, in which agents compete for position. Capital takes several forms: economic, cultural, social and symbolic. Cultural capital, such as credentials and familiarity with legitimate culture, helps explain unequal school outcomes. Symbolic violence describes how domination is accepted as natural by the dominated, and doxa the taken-for-granted beliefs of a field.

## Method

Bourdieu combined ethnography, statistical techniques such as correspondence analysis, and historical analysis. He insisted on reflexivity, urging sociologists to analyze their own position within the academic field.

## Influence and Criticism

His concepts are used across the sociology of education, culture, class and stratification. Critics argue that the theory is deterministic and leaves little room for change or agency, that habitus is vaguely defined, and that his account of French taste does not transfer easily to other societies.
