# Hisham Sharabi

Hisham Sharabi (1927-2005) was a Palestinian-American scholar of Arab intellectual history and social thought. He is best known for his theory of "neopatriarchy", a critique of contemporary Arab society as a distorted hybrid of tradition and modernity, and for his institution-building work on Palestine and Arab studies in the United States, including the Journal of Palestine Studies, which he helped found.

## Life and Context

Sharabi was born in Jaffa in 1927 and educated at the American University of Beirut and the University of Chicago, where he earned his doctorate. He taught at Georgetown University as a professor of European intellectual history and was active in founding academic and public institutions concerned with Palestine and Arab affairs in Washington. His life was shaped by the loss of Palestine in 1948 and by his engagement with Arab nationalism. He died in Beirut in 2005.

## Main Ideas

- Neopatriarchy: Arab societies, he argued, had not become modern but combined the authoritarian, patriarchal structures of the past with the outward forms of modern states and technology. The family, state and school reproduce paternal authority.
- Distorted change: dependence on the West and uneven capitalism produced modernization without genuine transformation.
- Role of intellectuals: critical thinking, secular reform and education were necessary for a real break with patriarchal structures.
- Arab thought and the West: earlier Arab intellectuals responded to European influence by various strategies of reform and adaptation.

## Major Works

- Nationalism and Revolution in the Arab World (1966)
- Arab Intellectuals and the West: The Formative Years, 1875-1914 (1970)
- Muqaddimat li-dirasat al-mujtama' al-'arabi (Introductions to the Study of Arab Society, 1975)
- Neopatriarchy: A Theory of Distorted Change in Arab Society (1988)

## Method

Sharabi drew on intellectual history, critical social theory and psychoanalytic ideas about family authority, with attention to language and culture as vehicles of social structure.

## Application

In *Neopatriarchy* Sharabi applied the concept to the Arab family, the educational system, language and religion, and to the structure of Arab political regimes, arguing that in each domain paternal authority persisted behind modern institutional forms. Earlier, *Arab Intellectuals and the West* examined how thinkers of the Arab Nahda between 1875 and 1914 responded to European influence.

## Related Concepts

Neopatriarchy builds on several wider concepts. Patriarchy is the base term: the authority of the father in the family, extended by analogy to rulers, teachers and religious figures. Max Weber's patrimonialism, a form of rule treated as an extension of the ruler's household, offers a useful comparison, and political scientists have separately used neopatrimonialism for states that mix personal rule with bureaucratic forms. The two neo-terms should not be treated as identical. Sharabi's account of distorted modernization also relates to dependency theory, since he connects internal authority structures to economic and cultural dependence on the West, and it challenges modernization theory's expectation that traditional forms would steadily fade. Authoritarianism, socialization, the role of intellectuals and the Arab Nahda are further points of contact. Halim Barakat's analysis of the family in Arab society is a neighbouring approach that places more weight on social diversity and change.

## Influence and Criticism

Neopatriarchy became an influential concept in Arab social science and gender studies. Critics suggest it generalizes across very different Arab societies, underplays internal variation and agency, and draws too much on a cultural-psychological account of authority. Some also note the limited empirical base of the theory.
