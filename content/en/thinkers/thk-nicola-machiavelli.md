# Niccolò Machiavelli: The Political Historian

Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527) was a Florentine political theorist, diplomat, and historian. While best remembered for his political treatise *The Prince* (*Il Principe*), he was also an official historian whose *Florentine Histories* (*Istorie Fiorentine*) marked a significant turning point in Renaissance historiography. Machiavelli shifted the focus of historical inquiry from rhetorical moralizing and civic panegyric toward a structural, realist analysis of political power, constitutional dynamics, and social conflict.

## Historical and Political Context

From 1498 to 1512, Machiavelli served as Second Chancellor and Secretary to the Ten of War (*Dieci di Balìa*) of the restored Florentine Republic under Piero Soderini, undertaking high-level diplomatic missions across Italy, France, and Germany. The restoration of Medici rule in 1512 abruptly ended his diplomatic career. Deposed from office, imprisoned, and tortured under suspicion of conspiracy, Machiavelli was exiled to his estate at Sant'Andrea in Percussina. 

In November 1520, through the patronage of Cardinal Giulio de' Medici (later Pope Clement VII) and the *Studio Fiorentino*, Machiavelli received an official commission to write a comprehensive history of Florence. He presented the completed eight books of the *Florentine Histories* to Pope Clement VII in Rome in May 1525.

## Historiographical Method and Major Historical Works

### *Florentine Histories* (*Istorie Fiorentine*, completed 1525)
Machiavelli departed sharply from the earlier civic humanist historiographical tradition represented by Leonardo Bruni and Poggio Bracciolini. While Bruni celebrated Florence's internal unity and external republican liberty, Machiavelli identified continuous domestic factional strife as the defining engine of Florentine history:
- **Structural Conflict as the Cause of Decay**: Unlike ancient Rome, where conflict between the Senate and the Plebeians resulted in laws protecting civic freedom, Florentine conflict pitted the nobility (*grandi*) against the popular classes (*popolo*) and rival economic factions. Machiavelli observed that these divisions were conducted through private patron-client factions rather than institutionalized public dispute, fostering instability, exile, and ultimate subservience to dynastic rule.
- **Rhetorical Speeches as Political Diagnostic**: Adopting classical rhetorical forms, Machiavelli inserted attributed speeches at crucial turning points—most famously the speech of an anonymous wool worker during the Ciompi Revolt of 1378 (Book III). Rather than recording verbatim words, Machiavelli used these speeches to diagnose the socioeconomic grievances and ideological motivations of disenfranchised social groups.
- **Patronage and Prudence**: Composing an official history commissioned by the Medici, Machiavelli organized his work strategically. The first four books covered Italian and Florentine history up to 1434; the final four books analyzed the era of Medici ascendancy under Cosimo the Elder and Lorenzo the Magnificent down to 1492. Machiavelli exercised considerable narrative subtlety, acknowledging Medici statesmanship while implicitly demonstrating how their private wealth subverted the constitutional mechanisms of the republic.

### *Discourses on Livy* (*Discorsi sopra la prima deca di Tito Livio*, 1531)
In this major work of political historiography, Machiavelli utilized the first ten books of Livy's Roman history as an empirical laboratory. He developed a cyclical theory of constitutional regimes (*anacyclosis*, adapted from Polybius), asserting that historical examples provide timeless empirical evidence for evaluating human political nature and designing resilient republican institutions.
