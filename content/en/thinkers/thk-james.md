# William James

An American philosopher and psychologist, a founder of American pragmatism and originator of the concept of the "stream of consciousness" (Stream of Consciousness), and the first to offer a systematic psychological description of lived experience in English, before any European translation of phenomenology.

## Life and Context

William James was born in New York on 11 January 1842 into a cultured family; his father, Henry James Sr., was a Swedenborgian theologian, and his brother was the novelist Henry James. He studied painting and then medicine, receiving his degree from Harvard in 1869, and in his youth suffered an existential depressive crisis from which he recovered by reading Renouvier on free will. He taught at Harvard from 1873, first physiology, then psychology, then philosophy, and established there a small laboratory for psychological experiments; among his students were G. Stanley Hall, Mary Calkins, and Gertrude Stein. He died on 26 August 1910.

## What He Contributed

**The Principles of Psychology (1890)**: a two-volume work that took twelve years and was published by Henry Holt. It contains the chapter "The Stream of Thought," which described consciousness as a continuous flow rather than a chain of separate elements, with "fringes" and a "specious present." As James famously wrote (1890, Vol. 1, p. 239):

> "Consciousness, then, does not appear to itself chopped up in bits... It is nothing jointed; it flows. A 'river' or a 'stream' are the metaphors by which it is most naturally described. In talking of it hereafter, let us call it the stream of thought, of consciousness, or of subjective life."

He distinguished between the acting "I" and the known "Me," which includes the material, social, and spiritual selves. He also wrote an influential chapter on habit. This description is very close to Husserl's later description of the inner temporality of consciousness, and it is mentioned that Husserl read the *Principles* and appreciated its description, but the two developed their ideas largely independently.

**The James-Lange theory of emotion**: James published the article "What Is an Emotion?" in the journal Mind in 1884, and the Dane Carl Lange put forward a similar conception in 1885: emotion is the perception of bodily change, not its cause, so we do not tremble because we are afraid but are afraid because we tremble. Walter Cannon criticized this theory in his article in the American Journal of Psychology in 1927 (and Philip Bard developed it), and Schachter and Singer presented the two-factor model in 1962, stressing the cognitive interpretation of arousal. Yet the question remained alive in research on bodily feedback, and in Antonio Damasio's "somatic markers."

**The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902)**: the Gifford Lectures in Edinburgh, in which he treated religion as individual experience that he described and classified with no prior judgment on its truth, and then measured its value by its fruits rather than its origins. In the two chapters on mysticism he cited texts from several mystical traditions.

**Pragmatism (1907)**: lectures delivered in Boston and New York, in which he defined truth by its practical value, that is, the "cash value" of the idea in experience, and this led him to "radical empiricism," which he presented in essays published after his death. In *A Pluralistic Universe* (1909) he devoted one lecture to Fechner and another to Bergson, whom he greatly admired.

## His Place in the Current

He represents the earliest American voice that prepared the ground for receiving the European tradition (phenomenology and then existentialism) when it arrived later through the book *Existence* (May, Angel, and Ellenberger, 1958), although he preceded them by decades and never met any of the European existentialists directly. He studied extraordinary phenomena, serving as president of the Society for Psychical Research in 1894-1895 and giving the Lowell lectures on exceptional mental states in 1896, which affected the founding of the psychology of religion and abnormal psychology in America.

## Limits and Criticism

The strictly experimental school was put off by him; James himself described psychology in the *Briefer Course* (1892) as the hope of a science rather than an existing science, and he was reluctant to make it purely laboratory-based, so after the *Principles* he turned increasingly to philosophy. His pragmatism is faulted for loosening the criterion of truth so that it appears to justify comforting belief, and Russell and Moore attacked it. It is true that he used neither the term "phenomenology" nor "existentialism," and his thought developed independently of the German-French tradition; he is therefore listed here as a parallel precursor rather than a member of the school. On the other hand, his description of consciousness is based on individual introspection, a method later abandoned for good reasons.

## Major Works
