# Alfred Adler

## Definition

Alfred Adler (1870–1937) was an Austrian physician, a former president of the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society, the founder of "Individual Psychology," and a pioneer of the psychology of teleology and social interest. He was born in Rudolfsheim, a suburb of Vienna, to a Jewish family of grain merchants, studied medicine at the University of Vienna and graduated in 1895, practising first as an ophthalmologist and then as a general practitioner before turning to neurology and psychiatry.

## Historical Context

Adler joined the weekly discussion circle that Freud founded in Vienna in 1902, and became president of the Vienna Psychoanalytic Society in 1910. But accumulating theoretical disagreements over the centrality of the sexual drive in explaining neurosis led him to withdraw from the presidency of the Society in February 1911, and the break reached its peak in October of the same year, when he left definitively with a group of colleagues to found the "Society for Free Psychoanalytic Research," which was renamed in 1912 the "Austrian Society for Individual Psychology." This was the first major split in the history of the psychoanalytic movement, preceding Jung's split by two years.

## Main Ideas and Method

Adler rejected Freud's biological-sexual reductionism and put forward a teleological model in its place: a human being is not driven by the determinism of the past and the instincts, but is propelled by the "striving for superiority and perfection" (Striving for Superiority) in an attempt to overcome the "inferiority complex" that usually forms in early childhood through experiences of physical or social weakness. He formulated the concept of "style of life" (Lebensstil) to describe the unique pattern each individual builds to meet life's challenges, and the concept of "social interest" or "community feeling" (Gemeinschaftsgefühl) as the basic criterion of mental health: how far the individual can contribute to the welfare of the group. He also studied birth order among siblings (the firstborn, middle, youngest and only child) as an influential factor in the formation of style of life.

## Principal Works and Representatives

Among his most important writings are *Study of Organ Inferiority* (Studie über Minderwertigkeit von Organen, 1907), in which he laid down the concept of organic compensation; *On the Nervous Character* (Über den nervösen Charakter, 1912); *Understanding Human Nature* (Menschenkenntnis, translated as Understanding Human Nature, 1927), the most famous of his books addressed to the general public; *The Practice and Theory of Individual Psychology* (1924); and *What Life Could Mean to You* (1931). Among his most prominent students and intellectual heirs are Heinz and Rowena Ansbacher, who collected his works in the reference volume *The Individual Psychology of Alfred Adler* (1956), and Rudolf Dreikurs, who developed its educational applications.

## Impact and Criticism

Adler influenced later humanistic and existential psychology, especially through Viktor Frankl (who was associated with his circle early on) and Rollo May, and to some degree Karen Horney, who adopted his critique of Freudian biological determinism and his emphasis on meaning, choice, and social context. He is faulted because his focus on consciousness and social teleology led him away from exploring the depths of the dynamic unconscious as Freud did, and because some of his concepts (such as the inferiority complex) became, in popular usage, simplifications far from their original clinical precision. Adler died in May 1937 during a lecture tour in Aberdeen, Scotland. His school continues today through a network of institutes and societies of Individual Psychology across Europe and North America, and through wide applications in family and educational counseling, especially through the "Parent Study Groups" developed by Rudolf Dreikurs on the basis of Adler's principles on birth order and mutual respect. Adler's thought is taught today in specialized graduate programs, such as Adler University in Chicago and Vancouver, which bears his name and continues to train counselors and psychotherapists according to his teleological-social model.
Adler's works have been translated into many languages, and his legacy gave rise to an enduring network of educators and counselors who apply the analysis of the hidden goals of misbehavior and classroom encouragement strategies, tools still taught today in teacher and school-counselor training programs as practical alternatives to long-term analytic techniques. His name remains, alongside Freud and Jung, one of the three great founding names in the history of early depth psychology.
