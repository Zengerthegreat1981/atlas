# Lila Abu-Lughod

An American anthropologist of Palestinian-American background. She conducted her principal fieldwork among the Awlad 'Ali Bedouin in Egypt, and is considered one of the leading theorists of feminist anthropology in the Arab and Muslim world.

## Veiled Sentiments

In *Veiled Sentiments: Honor and Poetry in a Bedouin Society* (1986), Abu-Lughod studied how Awlad 'Ali Bedouin women express intimate feelings (love, loss, protest) through short oral poems (*ghinnawa*) recited in specific social contexts, thereby going beyond the strict honor system that governs everyday public discourse. She showed that this poetry constitutes a parallel expressive system that reveals the complexity of female agency within a patriarchal structure, rather than mere silent submission.

## Critique of the "Saving Muslim Women" Discourse

In her influential article "Do Muslim Women Really Need Saving?" (2002) and her later book *Do Muslim Women Need Saving?* (2013), Abu-Lughod criticized the dominant Western discourse that portrays Muslim women as passive victims in need of "rescue" from outside (particularly in the context of justifying the American military intervention in Afghanistan after 2001). Drawing on her extended ethnographic research, she showed the complexity of women's agency, religiosity, and choice within Islamic contexts, and rejected the Orientalist assumption that "liberation" has a single Western form valid worldwide.

## Fieldwork Context: The Awlad 'Ali Bedouin

Abu-Lughod began her fieldwork in the late 1970s among the Awlad 'Ali Bedouin tribe in the western desert of Egypt. This is an Arab tribe with tribal ties extending into Libya, living according to a strict system of values of honor and modesty that governs relations between the sexes and generations. Her father, Ibrahim Abu-Lughod, was a Palestinian political scientist and intellectual, and her mother, Janet Abu-Lughod, an American sociologist. This background gave her a dual sensitivity toward the politics of Orientalist representation of Arabs and Muslims from the beginning of her academic career.

## Oral Poetry and Connection to the Arab Heritage

The *ghinnawa* that Abu-Lughod studied are short lyric poems belonging to the wider tradition of Arab Bedouin oral poetry. Their concise verse and indirect imagery allow the expression of feelings, above all of love and loss, that the code of honor discourages in ordinary speech. By treating this poetry as a way of knowing and feeling in its own right rather than as raw ethnographic material, Abu-Lughod offered a reading that avoids Orientalist stereotypes about Bedouin society.

## Writing Women's Worlds

In her second monograph, *Writing Women's Worlds: Bedouin Stories* (1993), Abu-Lughod moved from explicit theoretical analysis to experimentation in ethnographic writing. She presented a collection of life stories narrated in the voices of Awlad 'Ali women themselves, with limited analytical commentary. Her aim was to dismantle the authority of a single analytical anthropological voice and to make room for a plurality of local female voices, in line with the debates of the "crisis of representation" in American anthropology in the 1980s.

## Impact

Abu-Lughod is an important bridge between feminist anthropology and postcolonial criticism (in this she converges with Talal Asad and Edward Said). Her work has helped reshape academic and media debates about women, Islam, and Western intervention. She is professor of anthropology and gender studies at Columbia University, and her essay "Writing Against Culture" (1991) became a much-cited critique of the culture concept. With Ahmad H. Sa'di she co-edited *Nakba: Palestine, 1948, and the Claims of Memory* (2007). Her books are widely taught in Middle East studies and feminist anthropology programs around the world. She has also remained a critical voice in public debates about Palestine, Orientalism, and American policies toward the Muslim world.

## Dramas of Nationhood: Television Politics in Egypt

In *Dramas of Nationhood: The Politics of Television in Egypt* (2005), Abu-Lughod extended her analysis to how Egyptian television serials produce a national educational discourse about family and social "modernization." She examined how rural and poor audiences actually receive these elite messages, through her fieldwork with Egyptian families while they watched television. She showed that the Egyptian state used the media as a tool to shape an imagined modern citizenship defined by specific class and educational standards, often at the expense of a condescending representation of the inhabitants of the countryside and Upper Egypt.

## Academic Training and Institutional Position

Abu-Lughod received her PhD from Harvard University, taught at Princeton and New York University, and then joined Columbia University. She has received major fellowships and awards, including a Guggenheim Fellowship.
