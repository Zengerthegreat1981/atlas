# Talcott Parsons

Talcott Parsons (1902-1979) was an American sociologist, a professor at Harvard, and the dominant figure in mid-twentieth-century structural functionalism. He built a general theory of action and of social systems, and introduced much of European classical sociology to American readers.

## Life and Context

Parsons was born in Colorado Springs in 1902. He studied at Amherst College, the London School of Economics and the University of Heidelberg, where he earned a doctorate in 1927 with a dissertation on the concept of capitalism in German thought. He joined Harvard in 1927 and later helped found its Department of Social Relations. He translated Max Weber's The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism into English (1930) and served as president of the American Sociological Association in 1949.

## Main Ideas

- Voluntaristic theory of action: action involves actors with goals and values in situations constrained by norms.
- Social system: societies are systems whose parts contribute to stability. The AGIL scheme identifies four functional problems: adaptation, goal attainment, integration and latency (pattern maintenance).
- Pattern variables: paired orientations, such as universalism versus particularism, used to describe role relations.
- Social order: shared values and their institutionalization explain how order is possible, in answer to the Hobbesian problem.
- Later work on the evolution of societies, modernity, and the sick role in medical sociology.

## Major Works

- The Structure of Social Action (1937)
- The Social System (1951)
- Toward a General Theory of Action (1951, with Edward Shils)
- Working Papers in the Theory of Action (1953, with Robert Bales and Edward Shils)
- Economy and Society (1956, with Neil Smelser)
- Societies: Evolutionary and Comparative Perspectives (1966)
- The System of Modern Societies (1971)

## Method

Parsons practised abstract, analytical theorizing, building conceptual schemes that integrate sociology, economics, psychology and anthropology rather than conducting empirical research of his own.

## Influence and Criticism

His work dominated postwar American sociology and trained major students, including Robert K. Merton and Neil Smelser. From the 1950s, critics such as David Lockwood, C. Wright Mills (The Sociological Imagination, 1959) and Alvin Gouldner (The Coming Crisis of Western Sociology, 1970) accused him of emphasizing consensus, neglecting conflict and change, and writing in impenetrable prose. Interest revived through neofunctionalism, associated with Jeffrey Alexander.
