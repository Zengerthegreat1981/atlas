# Gustav Fechner

Gustav Fechner was born on 19 April 1801 in Groß-Särchen (Saxony) and died on 18 November 1887 in Leipzig. He was a German philosopher, natural scientist, mathematician, and psychologist, regarded as the **founder of psychophysics**: the branch that studies the quantitative relationship between physical stimuli and conscious experience.

## An Arab Root in Optics

Interest in sensation and measurement in Fechner's work was preceded by an Arab tradition in the study of visual perception, most notably Ibn al-Haytham in *Kitāb al-Manāẓir* (composed c. 1011-1021 CE), in which he explained that vision is not accomplished through sensation alone but through judgment, measurement, and comparison carried out by the soul. Some scholars have argued that he anticipated Helmholtz's concept of unconscious inference. 'Abd al-Hamid Sabra published a translation and edition of the first three books in *The Optics of Ibn al-Haytham* (Warburg Institute, 1989). This is a comparative connection, not a genealogical one: there is no evidence that Fechner read Ibn al-Haytham, and Ibn al-Haytham's aim was to explain sight, not to measure sensation.

## Context: German Natural Philosophy

Fechner studied medicine at Leipzig, graduating in 1822, then worked as a lecturer before being appointed professor of physics at the same university in 1834. He gradually turned toward philosophy and psychophysics. In his youth he was influenced by German *Naturphilosophie* (notably Lorenz Oken and Schelling), but he soon developed an independent empirical position. He wrote satirical and humorous pieces under the pseudonym "Dr. Mises" and translated French works on physics and chemistry, including those of Biot and Thénard.

## Major Contributions

### 1. Elements of Psychophysics (Elemente der Psychophysik, 1860)

His best-known work. The fundamental idea is said to have occurred to him on the morning of 22 October 1850, a date that Edwin Boring, in *A History of Experimental Psychology* (1929), made a symbolic birth date for the field. In it he built on Weber's law and formulated the logarithmic law of sensation, and the later power law offered an alternative:

- **Weber's Law**: the ratio between the just-noticeable difference and the strength of the stimulus is constant.
  ΔI/I = k
- **Fechner's Law**: the strength of sensation is logarithmically proportional to the intensity of the stimulus.
  S = k · log(I/I₀)
- **Stevens' Power Law**, developed by Stanley Smith Stevens in 1957 as an alternative to Fechner's law: S = k · I^a (the power law).

### 2. The Living Universe in Zend-Avesta (1851)

In this book Fechner proposed that **the entire universe has a spiritual dimension** (panpsychism): the earth and the stars have a psychic life, and soul and body are two aspects of one reality, so that what appears from within as mind appears from without as matter. In his later book *The Daylight View versus the Night View* (1879) he called this the "daylight view." He had resigned his chair of physics in 1839 because of illness, was later pensioned by the university, and after his recovery turned to philosophy and psychophysics.

### 3. Experimental Aesthetics (Vorschule der Aesthetik, 1876)

An application of psychophysics to aesthetics: he attempted to measure **proportions of beauty** in paintings and architecture, including experimental tests of the golden ratio, and he introduced an experiment in which subjects chose their preferred rectangles, one of the earliest experiments in experimental aesthetics.

### 4. The Psychological Crisis and Personal Experience

From about 1839 to 1843 Fechner went through a severe illness (usually described as a depressive and hypochondriacal breakdown) that began after eye damage sustained during experiments on afterimages, in which he gazed at the sun through colored glass. He was unable to work or read for a long period. He recovered gradually in the mid-1840s. Later accounts describe a moment of illumination in his garden, in which he experienced nature as ensouled; he regarded this as a spiritual turning point that shaped his later panpsychic view of the world.

## Influence on Psychology and Philosophy

### On psychology

- Fechner is regarded as a **pioneer of the quantitative experimental method in psychology**, roughly two decades before Wundt's Leipzig laboratory (1879). Wundt drew on the *Elements* in his *Principles of Physiological Psychology* (1873-74), while disagreeing with him over interpretation. Historians still debate how priority in founding experimental psychology should be divided between the two.
- Freud borrowed from Fechner the principle of the tendency toward constancy in *Beyond the Pleasure Principle* (1920), and in *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900) cited Fechner's suggestion that the scene of dreams is "another scene" than that of waking ideation.
- He paved the way for measuring **sensation, perception, and memory** as quantities that can be tested, and Ebbinghaus, who acknowledged the influence of Fechner's *Elements*, applied this quantitative approach to memory (1885).
- William James wrote a chapter on Fechner in *A Pluralistic Universe* (1909), "Concerning Fechner," and admired his conception of a wider consciousness.

### On philosophy

- He offered **an empirical approach to metaphysical questions** (such as the nature of the soul).
- He remains a model of an attempt to combine rigorous science with contemplative spirituality.

## Objections and Limits

In 1882, Johannes von Kries criticized the idea of measuring sensation, arguing that sensations are not magnitudes that can be added and subtracted, and Stevens later proposed the power law as an alternative. Joel Michell, in *Measurement in Psychology* (Cambridge University Press, 1999), argued that psychophysics has never demonstrated that sensation is a quantity measurable in the physical sense, and that assuming it is a postulate. Fechner's law remains roughly useful in a middle range of intensities. His metaphysics, however, has not been accepted by empirical science.

## Fechner's Place in the Bridge

Fechner stands in the bridge because he:
- On one hand, belongs to the **tradition of German natural philosophy** (Oken, Schelling).
- On the other hand, **establishes the experimental measurement of psychological phenomena**.
- Links **metaphysics** (soul, cosmos) and **physics** (measurement, method) through psychology.
