# James George Frazer

Sir James George Frazer (1854–1941) was a Scottish classicist and social anthropologist, best known for *The Golden Bough*, a vast comparative study of magic, religion, and myth. He is one of the most influential figures of Victorian "armchair" anthropology, although his comparative method, which paid little attention to context, was later sharply criticized.

## Biography and Academic Career

Frazer was born on 1 January 1854 in Glasgow. He studied at the University of Glasgow and then at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he read classics and became a fellow in 1879. He spent most of his career at Trinity, apart from a brief tenure in 1908 as the first holder of a chair of social anthropology at the University of Liverpool. He was knighted in 1914 and died in Cambridge on 7 May 1941.

Frazer conducted no ethnographic fieldwork of his own. He relied on the reports of missionaries, colonial administrators, travelers, and others, and he circulated questionnaires to obtain information from those in the field. This "armchair" practice distinguished his generation from the fieldworkers who followed Bronislaw Malinowski.

## Intellectual Influences

Frazer was influenced by the evolutionism of Edward Burnett Tylor, and by his friend, the biblical scholar and Semitist William Robertson Smith, whose work on sacrifice and totemism in ancient Semitic religion encouraged his interest in ritual and kingship. His early work included *Totemism* (1887). He also drew on classical scholarship, notably his edition and translation of Pausanias's *Description of Greece*.

## The Golden Bough

*The Golden Bough: A Study in Magic and Religion* first appeared in two volumes in 1890, grew to three volumes in 1900, and reached twelve volumes in 1906–1915; a one-volume abridgment followed in 1922 and a supplementary volume, *Aftermath*, in 1936. The work takes as its starting point the ancient priesthood at Nemi, near Rome, where a priest-king, the *rex Nemorensis*, held office until killed by his successor. From this puzzle Frazer ranged across the world to gather myths, folk customs, and rituals concerning sacred kingship, the dying and reviving god (Adonis, Attis, Osiris), the scapegoat, and the cycles of vegetation.

## The Three-Stage Theory

Frazer proposed an evolutionary sequence of intellectual development:

1. **Magic**: an attempt to control nature through the mistaken application of the laws of association.
2. **Religion**: the recognition of personal spiritual powers that must be propitiated.
3. **Science**: a more reliable understanding of natural laws.

He treated magic as a primitive and mistaken form of science and religion as a later stage, an idea that later anthropologists criticized as unilinear and ethnocentric.

## Sympathetic Magic

Frazer classified magic into two basic types, both resting on the principle of sympathy:

- **Homeopathic (imitative) magic**: the law of similarity, by which like produces like.
- **Contagious magic**: the law of contact, by which things once in contact continue to act on one another.

## Impact and Critique

*The Golden Bough* exerted a very wide influence on literature and the arts, including on T. S. Eliot's *The Waste Land*, and on the wider study of myth and ritual, in the work of the Cambridge Ritualists. Within anthropology, Malinowski and others admired its scope but replaced its method with intensive fieldwork, and E. E. Evans-Pritchard and later Edmund Leach criticized its decontextualized comparison and evolutionism. The philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein left critical *Remarks on Frazer's Golden Bough*, arguing that Frazer misunderstood ritual as mistaken science rather than expressive action.

Frazer is today studied more in literary and intellectual history than as a source of anthropological method, but he remains a standard subject in courses on the history of anthropological theory.
