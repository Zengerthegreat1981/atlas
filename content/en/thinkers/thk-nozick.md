# Robert Nozick

An American philosopher (1938–2002), professor at Harvard University, who offered the best-known contemporary philosophical defense of the "night-watchman state" (Minimal State), the state whose function is limited to protecting lives and property and enforcing contracts, and of the principle of individual rights as side constraints on what may be done to individuals.

## Biography and formation

Robert Nozick was born in 1938 in Brooklyn, New York, studied at Columbia and then received his doctorate from Princeton in 1963, and taught at Harvard from 1969 until his death in 2002. His first book, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), came in the wake of John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971), so the two books became the two poles of Anglo-American political philosophy in the 1970s: Rawls defends distributive justice, and Nozick rights and property. His book received the National Book Award in the United States in 1975.

## What he gave

- **The minimal state**: Nozick begins with the anarchists' question: why have a state at all? He answers that a limited minimal state arises from a state of nature by an "invisible hand" mechanism, without violating rights, when competing protection agencies turn into a dominant agency; whereas all redistribution programs that go beyond it violate individuals' rights.
- **Entitlement theory**: it holds that justice in distribution rests on three principles: justice in original acquisition, justice in transfer, and the rectification of past injustice. If holdings arise in this way, then any distribution that results from them is just, whatever its patterns.
- **The Wilt Chamberlain argument**: Nozick imagines an initial distribution satisfying any patterned principle, then millions of spectators voluntarily pay a small sum to the basketball player, so the original pattern is upset without anyone committing an injustice, an argument that liberty upsets any imposed distribution.
- **The Lockean proviso**: Nozick accepts Locke's proviso that forbids appropriating a resource if it leads to others being harmed, but interprets it narrowly.
- **The experience machine**: in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* and then in *Philosophical Explanations* (1981) he poses a thought experiment in which a person is offered a connection to a machine that gives him an illusory happy life, and holds that most people's refusal of it is evidence that they want reality and not pleasure alone.
- **Utopia as a framework**: he ends the book by saying that the minimal state is the framework within which diverse communities arise, each of which an individual chooses to join.

## His place in the movement

Nozick is regarded as the most prominent theorist of philosophical libertarianism in the American academy, but in his book *The Examined Life* (1989) he moved away from some of his early positions, saying that the libertarian outlook is deficient because it does not express the human bond between individuals. His later books turned to questions of meaning, knowledge and value rather than politics alone, and include *The Nature of Rationality* (1993), *Socratic Puzzles* (1997) and *Invariances* (2001).

## Limitation and criticism

It is noticed that Nozick's argument from rights depends on the assumption that individuals have natural rights prior to politics, an assumption for which he did not provide a detailed foundation, and he stated in the preface to his book that he does not offer a theory of the basis of these rights.

Rawls criticized the minimal state from the angle of social justice, and many held that entitlement theory assumes that original acquisition is sound, whereas the history of property is full of violence, conquest and slavery, so the "rectification of injustice" that Nozick himself affirmed as a third principle cannot be ignored, without his offering a detailed theory of it. G. A. Cohen replied to him in his essay "Robert Nozick and Wilt Chamberlain" (1977) and in *Self-Ownership, Freedom, and Equality* (1995), arguing, among other things, that the Chamberlain argument overlooks how freedom may be constrained by poverty.
