# Marshall Sahlins

Marshall Sahlins (1930–2021) was an American anthropologist whose work reshaped economic anthropology, Pacific ethnography, and the anthropology of history. Over a career spanning more than six decades, he argued that economic life is never a universal calculus of scarcity and self-interest, but a cultural system: what counts as wealth, need, and rational action varies with the meanings a society gives to things. His best-known ideas — the "original affluent society," the domestic mode of production, and the interplay of structure and history — remain standard reference points in anthropology, development studies, and ecological thought.

## Life and Career

Sahlins was born in Chicago in 1930 and educated at the University of Michigan and Columbia University, where he took his doctorate in 1954. He taught at the University of Michigan from 1956 to 1973 and then at the University of Chicago, where he became the Charles F. Grey Distinguished Service Professor and remained a central figure in the anthropology department until his retirement. His first fieldwork was in Fiji in the mid-1950s, on the island of Moala, where systems of kinship, chiefship, and ceremonial exchange became the empirical ground for his economic theory (*Moala: Culture and Nature on a Fijian Island*, 1962). He later worked extensively on Hawaii, whose history — from first European contact to the Hawaiian kingdom and its incorporation into the United States — supplied the material for his most ambitious theoretical work. Sahlins was also a public intellectual and activist, notably opposing the Vietnam War. He died in Chicago in 2021, at the age of ninety.

## Key Works

*Stone Age Economics* (1972) is Sahlins's most influential book. It gathers essays on hunter-gatherer economies, the domestic mode of production, and the sociology of primitive exchange, and it contains the famous essay on the "original affluent society." *Culture and Practical Reason* (1976) is his sharpest attack on materialist and utilitarian explanations of culture, defending the autonomy of symbolic order against Marxist and ecological determinism. *Historical Metaphors and Mythical Realities* (1981) and *Islands of History* (1985) apply structural analysis to historical events, above all the encounter between Hawaiians and Captain James Cook. *Anahulu: The Anthropology of History in the Kingdom of Hawaii* (1992), written with Patrick V. Kirch, combines archaeology, ethnohistory, and ethnography in a long-term study of a Hawaiian valley. He answered Obeyesekere in *How "Natives" Think: About Captain Cook, for Example* (1995). Later books include *Apologies to Thucydides: Understanding History as Culture and Vice Versa* (2004), *The Western Illusion of Human Nature* (2008), and *What Kinship Is — and Is Not* (2013).

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Sahlins's central contribution was to the substantivist position in economic anthropology: against the "formalists," who held that the principles of neoclassical economics — scarcity, maximisation, supply and demand — apply everywhere, he argued that economies are embedded in cultural and social relations that differ fundamentally from one society to another. Behaviour that looks irrational from a capitalist standpoint, such as destroying wealth in a potlatch or growing prestige rather than profit, is perfectly rational within its own cultural logic. From this followed his concept of the domestic mode of production, the economic organisation characteristic of kinship-based societies, in which production is geared to consumption rather than accumulation for exchange, and redistribution rather than market exchange is the dominant mode of allocating resources. In such societies the household is at once an economic, social, and ritual unit.

His most famous argument is that of the "original affluent society." Drawing on Richard B. Lee's research among the Ju/'hoansi of the Kalahari, Sahlins argued that hunter-gatherers met their subsistence needs with only a modest number of hours of work a week (Lee's figures were roughly twelve to twenty), leaving abundant leisure — and were therefore "affluent" by satisfying limited wants rather than by endlessly expanding production. Poverty and affluence, he insisted, are culturally defined; modern industrial societies manufacture scarcity by expanding wants without end. In his later work he developed a theory of structure and history: cultural categories shape how people interpret historical events, but those events in turn transform the categories. His analysis of Cook's arrival in Hawaii argued that Hawaiians understood Cook through the categories of their own cosmology — above all the god Lono — and that the encounter redefined those categories in the process.

## Influence and Critique

Sahlins's substantivism was influential in economic anthropology and continues to underpin critiques of development, which he saw as imposing a capitalist logic on fundamentally different systems. The "original affluent society" became a touchstone for environmental anthropology, degrowth movements, and Indigenous sovereignty arguments. His work on structure and history shaped historical anthropology and postcolonial approaches to colonial encounters. The Cook debate with Gananath Obeyesekere — who argued in *The Apotheosis of Captain Cook* (1992) that Hawaiians saw Cook as a chief rather than a god — became one of anthropology's most famous controversies, raising enduring questions about who may interpret Indigenous thought. Critics have also questioned the empirical basis of the affluent-society claim, pointing to the short periods of observation and to the way foragers were already embedded in wider regional economies; feminist anthropologists have observed that Sahlins's analyses of gift economies sometimes marginalise women's exchanges; and some scholars find his structuralism occasionally deterministic, underplaying individual agency and the violence of colonial dispossession. His defence of cultural autonomy remains, for supporters and critics alike, one of the most consequential positions in twentieth-century anthropology.
