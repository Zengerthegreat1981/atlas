# Harriet Martineau

Harriet Martineau (1802-1876) was an English writer, journalist and social theorist, often regarded as one of the first women sociologists. She wrote on political economy, American society and the methods of social observation.

## Life and Context

Martineau was born in Norwich into a Unitarian manufacturing family. She became partly deaf in youth and educated herself widely. Her *Illustrations of Political Economy* (1832-34) made her famous by explaining economic ideas through stories. She travelled in the United States from 1834 to 1836, and later settled in Ambleside in the Lake District, where she continued to write on many subjects until her death.

## Main Ideas

- **Society as an object of observation.** She argued that the moral and social life of a nation can be studied systematically by observing institutions, customs and everyday practices.
- **Measure a society by its own principles.** In *Society in America* (1837), she compared American ideals of liberty and equality with the realities of slavery and the position of women, and criticised the gap between them.
- **Gender and inequality.** She argued that women's lack of education and legal rights contradicted professed democratic principles.
- **Positivism.** She translated and condensed Auguste Comte's *Cours de philosophie positive* as *The Positive Philosophy of Auguste Comte* (1853), which helped make his ideas known in English.

## Case Studies

In *Society in America* Martineau drew on interviews and observation during her 1834-36 journey, including contacts with abolitionists such as William Lloyd Garrison, to assess slavery, women's education and legal status, and the working of American institutions against the nation's declared principles. *Illustrations of Political Economy* presented economic arguments through a series of short narratives of ordinary working life.

## Method

*How to Observe Morals and Manners* is an early methodological text. It advises the observer to attend to a wide range of evidence, including institutions, religion and domestic arrangements, to talk with people from different ranks, to be aware of their own bias, and to place weight on what people actually do and not only on what they say.

## Related Concepts

Martineau's writing connects with concepts that sociology later named and refined. Her instruction to attend to institutions and customs relates to the study of social norms, values and culture, and her comparison of professed principles with actual practice anticipates what is often called immanent critique, the testing of a society against its own standards. Her warning about the observer's prior assumptions bears on reflexivity and ethnocentrism. The advice to look at what she called things, meaning institutions and records, alongside talk can be compared with Emile Durkheim's later rule that social facts should be treated as things, although there is no need to assume a line of influence. Her accounts of slavery and the legal position of women connect with gender inequality, citizenship and social stratification. Through her version of Comte she is also tied to positivism. These later labels are aids to comparison and were mostly not her own vocabulary.

## Influence and Criticism

For much of the twentieth century Martineau was left out of the sociological canon, partly because she wrote for a general public and was a woman. Historians such as Michael R. Hill and Susan Hoecker-Drysdale have argued for her recognition as an early sociologist. Critics note that she lacked a formal system of theory, and that some of her economic views and her interpretation of other societies reflected the limits of her time.
