# Henry J. Steadman

An American sociologist specializing in mental health services and the criminal justice system, known for co-leading the **MacArthur Violence Risk Assessment Study**, a major empirical study of psychiatric disorder and violence.

## Career

Steadman earned a B.A. and an M.A. in sociology at Boston College and a Ph.D. in sociology at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill in 1971. National library authority files record his birth year as 1943, which fits a 2017 description of him as in his early seventies, but they do not give the day. He spent about seventeen years in research at the New York State Office of Mental Health, taught at the University of North Carolina, Union College, Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute, and the University at Albany, and in 1987 founded Policy Research Associates in Delmar, New York, which he led until his retirement at the end of 2017. The work concerns violence risk, jail mental health services, mental health courts, and programs that divert people with psychiatric disorders from incarceration into care. With John Monahan he edited *Mentally Disordered Offenders* (1983) and he co-wrote *The Mentally Ill in Jail* (1989) with Dennis McCarty and Joseph Morrissey. *Before and After Hinckley: Evaluating Insanity Defense Reform* (1993, with Margaret McGreevy and other co-authors) examined how states changed the insanity defense after the Hinckley verdict.

## The MacArthur Violence Risk Assessment Study

Steadman led the multi-site MacArthur research project between 1992 and 1995 together with **John Monahan** and other researchers. The project followed a sample of patients recently discharged from acute psychiatric hospitals to measure their actual rate of involvement in community violence, through periodic interviews with the patients and with close informants (usually family members), alongside arrest records and rehospitalization records. The main findings were published in *Rethinking Risk Assessment: The MacArthur Study of Mental Disorder and Violence* (Oxford University Press, 2001), written with John Monahan, Eric Silver, Paul Appelbaum, and other members of the MacArthur network. An earlier overview of the risk-assessment field appeared as *Violence and Mental Disorder* (1994), edited with Monahan.

## Central Ideas and Contributions

- **Challenging the direct assumption linking mental illness and violence**: The study showed that the relationship between psychiatric disorder and violence is more complex than common stereotypes suggest, and that factors such as substance use and a personal history of violence play a stronger predictive role than psychiatric diagnosis alone in many cases.
- **The classification tree approach to risk assessment**: Instead of a single linear predictive model, Steadman and his team developed a classification tree tool that combines several interacting risk factors to estimate the probability of violence in a given individual. It was published in *Law and Human Behavior* in 2000.
- **Parallel studies in justice and mental health**: He also contributed to research on specialized mental health courts and on programs that divert defendants with psychiatric disorders away from prison.

## Impact

The MacArthur methodology and its findings became a basic reference for research on violence risk assessment, including the development of actuarial tools such as the Classification of Violence Risk (COVR) software. They also helped shift public policy discourse about the "dangerousness" of people with psychiatric disorders from general assumptions to predictive models based on empirically identified risk factors.

## Limits

The study drew on samples discharged from specific hospitals in particular American cities (Pittsburgh, Kansas City, and Worcester, Massachusetts), which raises questions about whether its findings can be generalized to radically different social and health contexts. The classification tree tools derived from it also require periodic updating as patterns of psychiatric care and judicial policy change. Risk-assessment instruments estimate group-level probabilities and do not predict an individual's behavior with certainty.
