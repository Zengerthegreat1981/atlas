# William Robertson Smith

A Scottish theologian and Semitist (1846–1894), an early scholar to apply the methods of comparative anthropology to the study of ancient Semitic religion, and an influential figure in the development of the modern theory of ritual in comparative religious studies.

## From Higher Criticism to Comparative Anthropology

Smith began his academic life as a biblical scholar influenced by the German method of higher criticism (the documentary hypothesis of Wellhausen). This led to his trial for heresy in the Free Church of Scotland in 1881 and his removal from his post at Aberdeen. He then moved to Cambridge (1883), where he turned his attention to the comparative anthropology of ancient Semitic religion, drawing on the ethnographic materials emerging in his period.

## The Primacy of Ritual over Belief

In his central lectures, *Lectures on the Religion of the Semites* (1889), he put forward his central thesis: ancient religions, especially Semitic ones, were defined primarily by their collective ritual practices (sacrifice, communal feasting, pilgrimage to shrines) rather than by a written doctrinal system. Belief, he argued, arose later as an explanation for the existing ritual, not the reverse. This methodological reversal, which takes practice rather than belief as the first unit of analysis, influenced the emerging sociology of religion. It shaped Émile Durkheim's later analysis of the sacred/profane duality, and the work of Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss on sacrifice.

## Totemic Sacrifice

Smith suggested that the earliest form of Semitic sacrifice was a communal feast in which the sacred totemic animal was slaughtered and eaten in common by the community and its god, thereby renewing the kinship bond between them. This specific ethnographic detail was later subjected to critical reviews, which regarded it as a projection of the then-current theory of totemism onto scattered and earlier Semitic sources. Yet the lasting methodological value of his work (the primacy of ritual, and the socially binding function of sacrifice) has remained influential regardless of the fate of the totemic detail.

## His Impact on the Study of Higher Criticism of the Bible

Before turning to comparative anthropology, Smith was among those who introduced the methods of German higher criticism, especially the documentary hypothesis formulated by Julius Wellhausen about the composition of the five books of Moses from multiple, later textual sources, into English-speaking academic circles. He did this partly through his articles in the ninth edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, which he co-edited. This intellectual transmission angered the conservative current within the Free Church of Scotland, which saw applying the tools of historical criticism to the sacred text as a threat to the doctrine of direct revelation. It referred him to a heresy trial that ended with his removal from the chair of Hebrew and Old Testament at the Free Church College in Aberdeen in 1881, although he was not expelled from membership of the Church itself.

## The Move to Cambridge and Comparative Ethnography

After his removal, Smith moved to Cambridge, where he was appointed Lord Almoner's Professor of Arabic (1883) and became a fellow of Christ's College (1885). He served as chief editor of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* from 1887 and became Sir Thomas Adams's Professor of Arabic in 1889. In this new academic environment, which was less directly tied to ecclesiastical oversight, Smith developed his growing interest in the comparative ethnographic materials emerging at the time (reports of travelers and missionaries about "primitive" peoples in Australia, Africa and North America). From these he drew a method for reading ancient Semitic texts (the Torah, pre-Islamic Arabic inscriptions, and the accounts of pre-Islamic times by later Muslim historians) as evidence of living ritual practices rather than as abstract theological texts.

## His Relation to the Study of the Arabs and the Pre-Islamic Period

One of Smith's less famous works, but closely related to this trajectory, is his *Kinship and Marriage in Early Arabia* (1885). In it he studied the systems of kinship and marriage among the Arabs of the pre-Islamic period and earlier, relying on classical Arabic sources (genealogical reports and books of literature and language). He tried to demonstrate the existence of an ancient matrilineal system that preceded the historically known patriarchal system. This work, though its central hypothesis was subjected to later critical reviews by orientalists and anthropologists, shows that Smith's interest in Semitic studies was not limited to the Old Testament. It extended to using classical Arabic sources as ethnographic evidence for the history of the earlier Semitic religions.

## His Enduring Influence on the Sociology of Religion

Smith's influence on Émile Durkheim is directly documented. Durkheim drew on Smith's lectures on Semitic ritual and religion in developing his account of the sacred and the profane, in his book *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912). He held that the function of collective ritual in renewing social bonds, rather than the content of individual belief, is the essence of the religious phenomenon. His influence also reached James Frazer, his friend and colleague at Cambridge, who acknowledged a debt to him in *The Golden Bough*. Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss, in their foundational study *Sacrifice: Its Nature and Function* (1899), likewise relied on Smith's analysis of Semitic sacrifice as a starting point for theory, although they later developed the framework toward a more abstract structural analysis of the mechanism of sacrifice as a mediator between the realms of the sacred and the profane.
