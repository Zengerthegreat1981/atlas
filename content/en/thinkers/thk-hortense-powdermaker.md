# Hortense Powdermaker

Hortense Powdermaker (1896–1970) was an American anthropologist and an early practitioner of what is now called "anthropology at home": the application of ethnographic method to the anthropologist's own society. A student of Bronislaw Malinowski at the London School of Economics, she carried out fieldwork in Melanesia, in the racially segregated American South, and in Hollywood, treating each as a culture with its own structure, values, and rituals. Her books — *Life in Lesu* (1933), *After Freedom: A Cultural Study in the Deep South* (1939), *Hollywood, the Dream Factory* (1950), and her memoir *Stranger and Friend: The Way of an Anthropologist* (1966) — are classics of a discipline learning to look at modern institutions with ethnographic eyes.

## Life and Career

Powdermaker was born on 24 December 1896 in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, into a Jewish family, and she first worked as a labour organiser before turning to anthropology. She studied at the London School of Economics under Malinowski (PhD 1928), absorbing his method of long-term participant observation, and she carried out her first fieldwork in Lesu, in the New Ireland province of Melanesia (now Papua New Guinea), in 1929–1930, publishing the results as *Life in Lesu* (1933). Returning to the United States, she taught for many years at Queens College of the City University of New York, and she undertook major studies of race relations in Indianola, Mississippi, of the Hollywood film industry, and, later in her career, of the Zambian Copperbelt, published as *Copper Town: Changing Africa* (1962). She died on 15 June 1970 in Berkeley, California.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

*After Freedom* (1939) was Powdermaker's first major contribution to American anthropology. In a small Mississippi Delta town, which she called "Cottonville," she documented the structure of racial segregation — the economic dependence of Black sharecroppers on white landowners, the etiquette of deference and insult, the role of the church and the school — with the same systematic attention that anthropologists had given to kinship in Melanesia. At a time when American anthropology still concentrated on distant, supposedly "primitive" peoples, she showed that the method could illuminate the anthropologist's own society, and that race in America was a cultural system that could be studied ethnographically.

*Hollywood, the Dream Factory* (1950) applied the same method to a modern institution. Powdermaker spent about a year in Hollywood in the late 1940s, interviewing producers, directors, writers, and stars, and she analysed the film industry as a "tribe" with its own hierarchy, its own rituals of success and failure, and its own values — above all, the worship of the box office. She showed how the studio system shaped the films it produced, and how the insecurity of the industry — no one knew why a film succeeded or failed — produced a culture of anxiety, superstition, and imitation. It was one of the first serious anthropological studies of a modern media institution, and it anticipated by decades the anthropology of organisations and of the media.

Her memoir, *Stranger and Friend* (1966), is the most personal of her books and the one most widely used in the training of fieldworkers. In it she reflected with unusual candour on the difficulties of fieldwork across four very different settings (Lesu, Mississippi, Hollywood, and the Zambian Copperbelt), on the loneliness and the ethical ambiguities of the ethnographer's role, and on the experience of being a woman in a discipline dominated by men. The title captures her central insight: the anthropologist is always both a stranger and a friend, an outsider who must become an insider without ceasing to see.

## Influence and Critique

Powdermaker's influence has grown rather than faded. She is now recognised as a forerunner of the anthropology of modern institutions, and her Hollywood study is cited as a pioneering work in the anthropology of media, while *After Freedom* is recognised as an early model of the ethnography of race in the United States. Her memoir remains a standard text in courses on fieldwork method and ethics, and she is remembered, with Ruth Benedict, Margaret Mead, and Zora Neale Hurston, as one of the pioneering women of American anthropology.

Anthropologists have noted the limits of her approach: her account of the South, for all its detail, said less about Black resistance and political organisation than later studies would, and her Hollywood study, written before the collapse of the studio system, belongs to a particular historical moment. Yet her methodological wager — that strangeness is a matter of perspective, not of distance, and that the familiar can be made strange by the ethnographic eye — has become one of the founding premises of contemporary anthropology.
