# Guy Standing

A British economist and researcher, research professor at SOAS, University of London, who popularized the term "precariat" with which he described an emerging global social class that lives and works under permanent job and social insecurity, thereby making a cross-disciplinary contribution at the heart of the sociology of contemporary classes.

## The Central Idea

In his central book *The Precariat: The New Dangerous Class* (Bloomsbury, 2011), Standing popularized the term "precariat" (a blend of "Precarious," meaning fragile or unstable, and "Proletariat") to describe an emerging global social class united by a shared experience of repeated short-term, intermittent work, without a stable occupational identity, and without actual social or organizational protection (temporary contracts, casual work, the digital gig economy). Standing argued that neoliberal policies and accumulated global institutional changes have produced a large and growing number of individuals whose shared experiences suffice to classify them as an emerging social class distinct from both the traditional industrial working class (the proletariat) and the stable middle class. He warned that its rapid growth produces dangerous social and political instability: it is a "dangerous class" precisely because it is internally divided (which may lead to the demonization of migrants and other vulnerable groups) and, lacking organized collective agency, may become vulnerable to extremist political appeals. Standing proposed a universal basic income as a main policy response to this structural insecurity. He has argued that a regular, unconditional payment to every individual would provide a measure of income security and bargaining power, and he took part in pilot basic-income projects in India (including with the Self-Employed Women's Association) in the early 2010s.

## Historical Context

Guy Standing developed his concept after a long career at the International Labour Organization of the United Nations, where he studied global labor markets and their transformations since the 1980s. His analysis came in the context of a sharp historical decline of forms of stable industrial work tied to permanent contracts and strong union protection (the "Fordist" model that prevailed in the mid-twentieth century), and the rise of the flexible service economy and economic globalization that facilitated the movement of production between countries in search of the lowest labor cost, in addition to the later rise of the digital "Gig Economy" in the second decade of the twenty-first century, which widened the scope of fragmented temporary work through digital platforms.

## Main Ideas or Method

Standing distinguished the precariat from the traditional industrial working class by the absence of seven forms of "labor security" that were relatively available to industrial workers in the Fordist era: labor-market security (adequate employment opportunities), employment security (protection from arbitrary dismissal), job security (the ability to retain a niche and an ascending career path), work security (protection from accidents and occupational diseases), skill-reproduction security (training opportunities), income security (a stable income on which one can plan), and collective-representation security (effective unions). Standing described three broad groups within the precariat: people from the former industrial working class and its communities who feel they have lost their footing (which he called "atavists"), migrants and minorities lacking a secure home or status ("nostalgics"), and educated young people whose qualifications have not delivered the careers they expected ("progressives").

## Major Works or Representatives

His book *The Precariat: The New Dangerous Class* (2011) is the most important founding work, followed by his book *A Precariat Charter* (2014), in which he proposed specific policy reforms, and the book *The Corruption of Capitalism* (2016), which broadened his analysis of contemporary economic rent. Standing engaged with Richard Sennett in his analysis of the erosion of stable occupational identity, and with Arne Kalleberg, who documented quantitatively the rise of "Bad Jobs" in the American economy, and he co-founded the Basic Income Earth Network to defend his main policy proposal.

## Impact and Critique

Standing's concept has been influential beyond his economic discipline in debates on contemporary class, offering a framework that merges the phenomena of job insecurity, documented quantitatively by Kalleberg and qualitatively by Sennett and Silva, into the concept of a single coherent emerging social class, and has been prominent in the political and academic debate on universal basic income and social-protection policies in the age of the gig economy. However, some sociologists doubted the accuracy of classifying the precariat as a "class" in the strict sociological sense (with unified interests and shared class consciousness), considering it closer to describing a shared economic condition or position that brings together individuals from differing backgrounds and political orientations who may not in fact unite in organized collective agency as Standing predicted.
