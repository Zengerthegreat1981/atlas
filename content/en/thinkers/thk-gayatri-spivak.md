# Gayatri Spivak

Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (born 1942) is an Indian-born literary theorist and critic, based in the United States, who is among the founding figures of postcolonial theory. She is best known for her essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?", her translation of Jacques Derrida, and her critiques of how Western knowledge represents colonized and marginal peoples.

## Life and Context

Spivak was born in Calcutta in 1942 and studied there before moving to the United States, where she completed a doctorate at Cornell University. She taught at the University of Iowa and the University of Pittsburgh, and since the 1990s at Columbia University, where she is a University Professor. She collaborated with the Subaltern Studies group of historians, notably Ranajit Guha, and she has long been engaged in rural education work in India.

## Main Ideas

- The subaltern: marginalized groups outside the colonial and elite structures of representation. In "Can the Subaltern Speak?" she argues that the subaltern woman is doubly silenced, and that well-meaning intellectuals who "speak for" the oppressed risk reproducing their erasure.
- Epistemic violence: colonial and imperial knowledge systems suppress other ways of knowing.
- Strategic essentialism: provisional use of group identity for political ends, while recognizing its limits; she later distanced herself from the phrase.
- Deconstruction, Marxism and feminism: she combines these traditions to examine labor, gender and globalization.
- Ethics of reading and teaching, including attention to language and translation.

## Major Works

- Translator's preface to Jacques Derrida, Of Grammatology (1976)
- In Other Worlds: Essays in Cultural Politics (1987)
- "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988)
- Selected Subaltern Studies (co-edited with Ranajit Guha, 1988)
- Outside in the Teaching Machine (1993)
- A Critique of Postcolonial Reason (1999)
- Death of a Discipline (2003)

## Case Studies

"Can the Subaltern Speak?" discusses the debate on the abolition of widow immolation (sati) in colonial India and the case of Bhuvaneswari Bhaduri, a young woman who took her own life in 1926, to examine how the subaltern woman's act was interpreted by others. Spivak has also translated the fiction of the Bengali writer Mahasweta Devi, including *Imaginary Maps* (1995) and *Breast Stories* (1997).

## Method

Her method is close reading of literary, philosophical and historical texts, informed by deconstruction and Marxist analysis, rather than empirical social research.

## Influence and Criticism

Her work shaped postcolonial theory, feminist theory and subaltern studies, and it is widely used to question who can represent whom in research. Critics, including Benita Parry, objected that she underplays evidence of subaltern resistance and voice; many readers also find her prose dense and difficult. Spivak has herself commented on the essay's reception and said it has often been misread.
