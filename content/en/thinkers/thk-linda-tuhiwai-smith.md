# Linda Tuhiwai Smith: Decolonizing Methodologies and Indigenous Research

Linda Tuhiwai Smith (b. 1957) is a leading indigenous scholar whose *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (1999) fundamentally transformed understandings of research ethics, power, and knowledge production by centering indigenous perspectives and asserting indigenous sovereignty over research conducted with and about indigenous communities. Her work demonstrates that Western research methodologies—including anthropology—have historically served colonial projects, extracting knowledge from indigenous communities for Western benefit while reinforcing indigenous marginalization. Her decolonizing methodologies insist that indigenous communities should control research processes, determine research agendas, maintain ownership of knowledge, and ensure that research serves indigenous interests. Her work has inspired a global movement toward indigenous-led research, community-based participatory research, and indigenous intellectual sovereignty that fundamentally challenges anthropological authority and epistemology.

## Biographical Context and Intellectual Formation

Linda Tuhiwai Smith is of Ngāti Awa descent from Aotearoa/New Zealand. She studied at the University of Auckland and the University of Otago, receiving her doctorate in education. She held positions at the University of Otago and later at the University of Waikato, where she founded the Maori Education Development research centre. Her intellectual formation combined indigenous knowledge systems, critical scholarship on colonialism, and engagement with education as a site of both oppression and liberation.

Smith's positioning as an indigenous scholar gave her distinctive insights: she experienced firsthand how Western research has operated in indigenous communities, how knowledge has been extracted and appropriated, and how indigenous peoples have been represented and misrepresented in Western scholarship. Her work emerged from indigenous communities' demands for self-determination and intellectual sovereignty, grounded in indigenous struggle and decolonial movements.

## Methodological Innovations: Decolonizing and Indigenous-Led Research

Smith's central methodological innovation was to articulate **decolonizing methodologies** as a distinct approach to research that prioritizes indigenous interests, knowledge, and sovereignty. Rather than adopting Western research methods and adding indigenous "perspectives" as supplementary voices, decolonizing methodologies fundamentally restructure research to center indigenous leadership, epistemology, and benefit.

Her key innovations included: (1) **centring indigenous sovereignty**—asserting that indigenous communities have the right to control research processes, agendas, and outcomes; (2) **decolonizing the research relationship**—transforming research from extraction of knowledge to reciprocal exchange serving community interests; (3) **validating indigenous knowledge systems**—asserting that indigenous epistemologies and ways of knowing are legitimate and valuable, not subordinate to Western science; (4) **community-controlled research design**—ensuring that research questions, methods, and interpretations reflect community priorities; (5) **indigenous ownership of knowledge**—maintaining community control over how knowledge is used, circulated, and represented.

Smith outlined *kaupapa Māori research* (research by, for, and with Māori people, organized according to Māori values and epistemologies) as a concrete example of decolonizing methodology. Rather than individual researchers imposing external methods, kaupapa Māori research is collaborative, community-defined, and grounded in Māori intellectual traditions.

## Major Works and Theoretical Development

Smith's publications established decolonizing methodologies as central to indigenous research and have influenced anthropology, education, health research, and other fields:

- **Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples** (1999, revised 2012) — Her foundational text, arguing that Western research has been implicated in colonialism and that indigenous communities must reclaim research sovereignty. Proposes decolonizing strategies and kaupapa Māori research as alternative approach.

- **Indigenous and Decolonial Futures in Higher Education: Pedagogies and Policies** (with others, 2019) — Applying decolonizing methodologies to higher education, asserting indigenous intellectual authority and calling for structural transformation of universities.

- **Theorizing Indigenous Methodologies: Transforming What Happens in the Classroom* (various publications, ongoing) — Extending decolonizing methodologies to pedagogy and classroom practice.

- Articles and chapters on indigenous data sovereignty, indigenous intellectual property, and indigenous leadership in research and knowledge production.

These works established decolonizing methodologies as essential framework for ethical research with indigenous communities.

## Fieldwork Practice: Community-Based Participatory Research and Collaboration

Smith's research practice exemplified decolonizing methodology. Rather than individual fieldwork, her research involved sustained collaboration with Māori communities, organizations, and scholars. Research questions, methods, and interpretations were developed collaboratively, reflecting community priorities and values.

Her research on Māori education involved working with whānau (family) groups, hapū (subtribe), and iwi (tribe) to understand educational experiences, aspirations, and barriers. Community members were partners in research design, data interpretation, and dissemination. Research findings were returned to communities in accessible formats; communities controlled how findings could be used and circulated.

This collaborative practice challenged traditional notions of the independent researcher conducting fieldwork. Instead, Smith emphasized team-based, community-accountable research in which researchers were accountable to communities and supported community-determined goals.

## Theoretical Impact: Decolonial Epistemology and Knowledge Sovereignty

Smith's epistemological contribution fundamentally repositioned indigenous knowledge and indigenous peoples within research frameworks. Rather than treating indigenous knowledge as data to be analyzed, she asserted indigenous knowledge systems as legitimate epistemologies worthy of respect and integration. Rather than positioning indigenous peoples as research subjects, she centered indigenous peoples as researchers and knowledge producers.

Her work demonstrated that the presumed neutrality and objectivity of Western research methodologies obscured their colonial implication: research had historically served colonial projects—documenting indigenous peoples for administrative control, extracting resources and knowledge, representing indigenous peoples as exotic or primitive to justify colonial domination.

Decolonizing methodologies required not merely adding indigenous voices but fundamentally restructuring research—changing who controls the research process, what counts as knowledge, whose interests research serves, and how knowledge is owned and circulated. This meant research accountability to communities, research that generates knowledge useful for indigenous liberation and self-determination, and research that respects indigenous intellectual sovereignty.

## Critics and Responses to Decolonizing Methodologies

Smith's work has been enthusiastically received in many quarters but has also generated debate. Some researchers and institutions have adopted the language of "decolonization" while maintaining extractive research practices—a form of co-optation Smith and others have critiqued as "decolonial washing."

Some Western researchers have questioned whether they can conduct decolonizing research; Smith's response has been that non-indigenous researchers can support decolonizing work by respecting indigenous leadership, ceding control over research processes, and genuinely serving indigenous communities' interests—what she calls being a "good ally."

Questions remain about how decolonizing methodologies can address transnational research, research on indigenous diaspora, and research engaging indigenous peoples from multiple communities with potentially divergent interests. Smith's work continues to engage with these complexities.

## Postcolonial Positioning and Global Indigenous Movements

Smith's work emerged from and continues to engage with indigenous decolonial movements worldwide. Her framework has inspired indigenous scholars and researchers globally—in Australia, Canada, the USA, Latin America, Africa, and beyond—to assert indigenous research sovereignty and develop indigenous-specific methodologies.

Her work explicitly positions indigenous peoples not as victims of history but as agents of decolonization, as producers of knowledge, and as intellectual authorities. This represents a fundamental repositioning of indigenous peoples from objects of research to subjects and leaders of knowledge production.

Smith's work has also engaged with indigenous movements for land rights, language revitalization, cultural sovereignty, and political self-determination, understanding research as integrally connected to these struggles.

## Contemporary Relevance and Legacy

Decolonizing methodologies have become increasingly central to research ethics across fields. Major research funding agencies, universities, and professional organizations now recognize indigenous data sovereignty and community-controlled research as ethical requirements. Smith's framework has inspired institutional changes—requirements for community consent, protocols for indigenous data, positions for indigenous scholars in research institutions.

However, significant challenges remain. Decolonizing methodologies require substantial restructuring of research institutions and power relations—changes that institutions have often resisted. The commercialization of research, pressure for rapid publication, and disciplinary hierarchies often conflict with community-controlled, long-term research relationships that decolonizing methodologies demand.

Contemporary indigenous scholars continue developing and refining decolonizing methodologies, extending them to digital research, addressing transnational indigenous communities, and asserting indigenous leadership in emergent research areas. Smith's foundational work continues to inspire these developments.
