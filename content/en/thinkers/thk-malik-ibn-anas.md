# Malik ibn Anas

A jurist and traditionist from Medina, founder of the Maliki school, one of the four major Sunni schools of jurisprudence, and author of the ***Muwatta'***, the oldest systematically organized jurisprudential-hadith compilation that has survived complete from the second century AH. His method rested on combining the prophetic hadith with **the practice of the people of Medina** ('*amal ahl al-Madina*), meaning the continuous judicial and juristic practice of the people of Medina, as a reliable source of knowledge for understanding the living Sunna of the Prophet.

## Biography

Malik ibn Anas ibn Malik al-Asbahi was born and raised in Medina, and barely left it throughout his life except to perform the pilgrimage, to the point that he came to be known as "the Imam of the Abode of Emigration" (*Imam Dar al-Hijra*). He took knowledge from the senior Successors and their followers in Medina, and held the chair of teaching and legal opinion there for decades, so that students of knowledge from the various Islamic regions came to him; among his most prominent students was Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i. During the reign of the Abbasid caliph Abu Ja'far al-Mansur, Malik underwent a political ordeal: it is reported that he was harmed and flogged (around 146 AH) on account of a legal opinion attributed to him concerning the oath of allegiance under compulsion, which in its context was linked to the revolt of Muhammad ibn Abd Allah, the "Pure Soul" (*al-Nafs al-Zakiyya*), of the Alids against the Abbasids, although the historical accounts differ on the details and precise causes of this incident. Malik died in Medina in 179 AH, after more than sixty years of nearly uninterrupted teaching and legal opinion there.

## What He Gave

### The Muwatta': Between Hadith and Jurisprudence

The *Muwatta'* combines authenticated prophetic hadiths, reports from the Companions and the Successors, and fatwas and juristic judgments of Malik himself, arranged under jurisprudential chapters. Malik did not intend it to be a "hadith book" in the sense that later became widespread (like the *Sahihayn*), but a jurisprudential-hadith corpus that reflects the actual **practice** inherited in Medina, that is, the continuous judicial practice and legal opinion which Malik regarded as a living continuation of the Sunna of the Prophet, and which he considered superior in authority, in case of conflict, to an isolated solitary report according to some jurists.

### The Practice of the People of Medina as a Legislative Source

Malik's method distinguished a source known as "the practice of the people of Medina": when the jurists and judges of Medina agree in practice on a ruling in a continuous, inherited manner, Malik regarded that as strong evidence that this practice reflects a prophetic Sunna that was not transmitted verbally, or that was transmitted in multiple reports weighed against each other through living practice. This principle distinguished the Maliki school from other schools that relied on the strictly transmitted hadith or on analogy more broadly.

### Imam of the Abode of Emigration: Medina as a Source of Knowledge

Malik's reliance on Medina as a jurisprudential source was not a mere geographical bias, but rested on a specific epistemic conception: that Medina, as the home of the Prophet, the place of his emigration, and the seat of the first rightly-guided caliphs, preserves in its inherited practice, generation after generation, a practical memory of the Prophet's Sunna that is more precise than isolated reports transmitted in distant regions, where error or forgetfulness may creep into individual chains of narrators. This conception made "the inherited collective practice" in Malik's view an argument that at times outweighs an isolated solitary report when the two conflict, and it is the methodological foundation that distinguished his school from others that relied primarily on evaluating the chains of individual hadiths.

## Impact

The Maliki school spread through Malik's students, among them Abd al-Rahman ibn al-Qasim and Ibn Wahb, and became historically the dominant jurisprudential school in the Maghreb, al-Andalus and West Africa, and it remains so in many of these regions. Malik's method in synthesizing hadith and practical application influenced the formation of al-Shafi'i himself, who studied the *Muwatta'* under him in his youth before later formulating a more abstract and systematic theory of usul in *al-Risala*. The *Muwatta'* is among the oldest sources of Islamic jurisprudence and hadith available today in relatively complete form, and an essential reference for studying the legal and religious practice in Medina in the second century AH, as well as an indispensable source for historians seeking to reconstruct the picture of early Islamic society beyond its purely literary or political sources.

## Limitations

Malik's reliance on the "practice of the people of Medina" as a quasi-independent argument drew objections from jurists of other regions (such as Iraq), who held that this source is regional in nature and cannot be generalized to all the Islamic regions, whose "practice" might also differ. Moreover, the *Muwatta'* has reached us in multiple transmissions that differ in the number and order of hadiths (the transmission of Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi of al-Andalus is the most famous today, and it differs from other transmissions such as that of Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani), which makes speaking of a "single text" of the *Muwatta'* an oversimplification that requires methodological caution, and obliges the researcher to identify precisely the transmission adopted when citing a particular passage from it before attributing it directly to Malik.

### His Relation to Abu Hanifa, al-Shafi'i and Ibn Hanbal

Malik was a younger contemporary of Abu Hanifa, founder of the Hanafi school in Iraq (d. 150 AH); there is no documented student-teacher relationship between them, despite scattered reports of meetings whose details the sources differ on. As for Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i, he studied under Malik directly in Medina and transmitted the *Muwatta'* from him by audition, before later developing in Iraq and Egypt a more abstract methodological approach combining Malik's hadith heritage with the Iraqi heritage of opinion. Ahmad ibn Hanbal, in turn, attended al-Shafi'i's circle in Baghdad, so that the founders of the four Sunni schools are linked by overlapping lines of scholarly contact, beginning practically with Malik in Medina.

## His Principal Works

- **The Muwatta'**: A comprehensive compilation combining hadith, jurisprudence and the practice of the people of Medina.
- Scattered letters and fatwas later collected into the works of the Maliki school (such as the *al-Mudawwana al-Kubra*, which was compiled by the North African jurist Sahnun on the basis of the teachings of Malik's student Ibn al-Qasim, and which Malik did not write himself).

### His Personal and Scholarly Standing in Medina

Malik was known for his strictness in choosing whom he would narrate from and sit with, and for his great care in the accuracy of transmitting hadith, to the point that it is reported that he would prepare himself with ablution and care before narrating hadith, out of reverence for the status of the prophetic hadith in his view. This personal strictness was reflected in his scholarly method: he preferred to limit himself to a relatively small number of hadiths that he regarded as completely reliable, rather than to an unregulated quantitative expansion, which partly explains the moderate size of the *Muwatta'* (a few thousand texts) compared with much larger later hadith collections such as the *Musnad* of Ibn Hanbal.

## Methodological Note

Modern editors and scholars of the *Muwatta'* differ in adopting a particular transmission as the basis of their edition: the most widely known today is the Andalusian transmission of Yahya ibn Yahya al-Laythi (about 1,700 hadiths), while other transmissions, such as that of Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani, contain additional material and a partially different arrangement. In recent decades critical editions have appeared that attempt to compare the different transmissions and trace their differences with greater precision than was possible in the older traditional editions.
