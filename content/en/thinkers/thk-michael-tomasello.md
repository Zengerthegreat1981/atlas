# Michael Tomasello

An American evolutionary, comparative, and developmental psychologist and linguist, professor at Duke University, who previously served as co-director of the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig. He is one of the most influential developmental and comparative psychologists of his generation, known for his research comparing the cognition of children and great apes, and for his theory of **shared intentionality** as the decisive cognitive difference between humans and other primates.

## What He Contributed

### The Theory of Shared Intentionality

Through decades of comparative experiments on infants and young children on the one hand, and on chimpanzees and bonobos on the other, Tomasello developed the thesis that humans uniquely possess the capacity for **shared intentionality**: cooperating in a collective activity with shared goals and intentions that both parties are aware of, and in which each knows that the other is aware of it too. He showed that this capacity develops in two developmental stages: **joint intentionality** around the ninth month of age, and then **collective intentionality** around the third year. This capacity opens the way for language, social norms, and institutions, abilities that are absent or very weak in non-human great apes despite their high individual intelligence.

### Usage-Based Grammar and Language Development in Children

In *Constructing a Language: A Usage-Based Theory of Language Acquisition* (2003), Tomasello applied the framework of **usage-based linguistics** to child language acquisition. He argued that the child does not activate a ready-made innate "universal grammar" (as generative linguistics assumes), but gradually builds grammatical rules from concrete usage patterns that they hear and repeat, drawing on general social-cognitive abilities (such as reading the speaker's intention), rather than on a specialized innate linguistic module.

### The Cultural Origins of Knowledge

In *The Cultural Origins of Human Cognition* (1999), he advanced the hypothesis that the decisive difference between human cognition and that of primates lies not in isolated individual abilities but in **cumulative cultural learning**: the human capacity to learn the tools and ideas of others accurately enough to improve them generation after generation (the "ratchet effect"), unlike apes, which rediscover solutions almost anew each generation.

## Impact

- The theory of shared intentionality has reshaped the debate about human cognitive uniqueness in evolutionary psychology and cognitive anthropology.
- His model of language acquisition has had a major influence on cognitive usage-based linguistics, as a competitor to the innate generative model.
- His comparative research with great apes (in collaboration with the Max Planck Institute) has become a basic reference in comparative cognition.

## Limitations

- Generative linguists (followers of Chomsky) have criticized his model of language acquisition as unable to explain how children acquire complex grammatical structures so quickly from relatively limited linguistic input (the "poverty of the stimulus" argument).
- Some of his comparative experiments on apes have been criticized methodologically by some other primatologists, who argued that certain test protocols may have underestimated what apes can do.
