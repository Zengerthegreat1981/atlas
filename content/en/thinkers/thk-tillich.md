# Paul Tillich

Paul Johannes Tillich (1886–1965) was a German-American theologian, Christian existentialist, and philosopher of religion. Alongside Søren Kierkegaard and Martin Heidegger, he was among the thinkers who investigated the ontological structure of anxiety. As an influential Protestant theologian of the twentieth century, Tillich brought theological reflection into sustained dialogue with philosophy, depth psychology, and culture. His ontological analysis of anxiety and courage entered American existential psychotherapy primarily through his long friendship and intellectual collaboration with Rollo May.

## Life and Career

Tillich was born on August 20, 1886 in Starzeddel (now Starosiedle, Poland), in the Province of Brandenburg, Prussia, the son of a conservative Lutheran pastor. He studied theology and philosophy at the universities of Berlin, Tübingen, and Halle, earning his doctorate in philosophy at Breslau in 1910 with a dissertation on F. W. J. Schelling's philosophy of religion, followed by a licentiate in theology at Halle in 1912. 

During World War I, Tillich served from 1914 to 1918 as a military chaplain in the German Imperial Army on the Western Front. The slaughter he witnessed on the Western Front profoundly altered his worldview, permanently shattering his trust in nineteenth-century bourgeois cultural optimism and Hegelian historical teleology. 

In the Weimar Republic, Tillich taught at Berlin (1919–1924), Marburg (1924–1925), and Dresden (1925–1929), before being appointed professor of philosophy at the University of Frankfurt in 1929. Active in the Religious Socialist movement, he published *Die sozialistische Entscheidung* (*The Socialist Decision*, 1933), a direct ideological critique of National Socialism. Following Adolf Hitler's rise to power, Tillich was suspended from Frankfurt under the Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service in April 1933. At the invitation of Reinhold Niebuhr, he emigrated to the United States in late 1933, joining Union Theological Seminary in New York, where he taught until 1955. He subsequently served as University Professor at Harvard Divinity School (1955–1962) and Nuveen Professor of Theology at the University of Chicago Divinity School (1962–1965). He died in Chicago on October 22, 1965.

## Key Works

- *The Shaking of the Foundations* (1948), his influential collection of sermons interpreting human existential crises through theological symbols.
- *The Courage to Be* (1952), derived from the Terry Lectures at Yale University, providing his classic phenomenological and ontological taxonomy of anxiety.
- *Systematic Theology* (3 volumes: 1951, 1957, 1963), his magnum opus expounding the "method of correlation" and interpreting God as "Being-itself."
- *Dynamics of Faith* (1957), defining faith as the state of being grasped by an "ultimate concern."

## Anxiety and the Courage to Be

Tillich maintained a rigorous distinction between fear and anxiety: fear has a definite, determinate object that can be faced, analyzed, and negotiated, whereas existential anxiety (*Angst*) has no specific object because its threat is non-being (*Nichtsein*) itself—the permanent awareness of radical finitude and contingency.

In *The Courage to Be*, Tillich classified existential anxiety into three fundamental forms:
1. **The anxiety of fate and death** (ontological anxiety): The awareness that one's biological and temporal existence is contingent and will perish. This form was culturally dominant in the Greco-Roman world of late antiquity.
2. **The anxiety of guilt and condemnation** (moral anxiety): The awareness of profound estrangement from one's authentic moral potential and moral accountability. This form was culturally dominant in the late medieval period and Reformation.
3. **The anxiety of emptiness and meaninglessness** (spiritual anxiety): The threat of the collapse of all ultimate meaning, purpose, and foundational values. This form characterizes modern and contemporary secular culture.

Tillich emphasized that existential anxiety is not a pathological neurosis or a medical disorder to be eliminated; it is an inescapable, universal structure of finite human existence. Pathological or neurotic anxiety arises precisely when an individual lacks the courage to face existential anxiety directly, retreating instead into defensive, restricted patterns of self-affirmation to ward off non-being.

Courage, for Tillich, is the ethical and ontological act in which an individual affirms their own being in spite of the constant threat of non-being. It operates through:
- The courage to be as a part (participation in a community and collective identity).
- The courage to be as oneself (individual autonomy and self-determination).
- The absolute faith directed toward the "God above God"—a radical self-affirmation rooted in Being-itself when conventional theistic concepts and religious certainties are shattered by doubt.

## Systematic Theology and the Method of Correlation

In *Systematic Theology*, Tillich formulated his "method of correlation": philosophy, psychological analysis, and human culture articulate the existential questions arising from the concrete human condition, while theology interprets the symbols of revelation to provide answers to these questions. Faith is understood as "ultimate concern" (*unbedingtes Angehen*)—the state of being unconditionally grasped by that which determines our ultimate meaning and being. Idolatry occurs when preliminary, finite concerns (such as nationalism, wealth, or ideological dogma) are elevated to ultimate status. Tillich insisted that God is not "a being" alongside or above other entities, but the ground of being (*Grund des Seins*) and "Being-itself" (*ipsum esse*). Speaking of God as a separate existing entity reduces the divine to a finite object, which Tillich considered an inadvertent form of atheism.

## Influence on Existential Psychotherapy

The primary bridge between Tillich's theology and clinical psychology occurred through his close friendship with Rollo May. May was Tillich's student at Union Theological Seminary and remained in lifelong dialogue with him. In *The Meaning of Anxiety* (1950; rev. ed. 1977), May drew directly upon Tillich's ontological framework to distinguish between normal anxiety (an intrinsic dimension of growth, responsibility, and freedom) and neurotic anxiety (a disproportionate reaction, tied to repressed conflict, that paralyzes action). May documented this relationship in *Paulus: Reminiscences of a Friendship* (1973). Through May, Tillich's insights significantly shaped the American school of existential-humanistic psychotherapy.

## Criticism and Reception

Tillich faced criticism from multiple traditions. Karl Barth and neo-orthodox theologians argued that the method of correlation subordinated divine revelation to human existential categories, turning theology into an anthropocentric enterprise. Analytic philosophers and secular critics questioned the conceptual coherence of "Being-itself," arguing that Tillich's symbolic theology lacked clear truth-conditions and verifiability. Conservative critics also contended that defining faith as "ultimate concern" was overly expansive, encompassing non-religious secular commitments. Clinically, contemporary therapists emphasize that Tillich's ontological analysis must not be confused with empirical psychiatric assessment or the clinical diagnosis and treatment of anxiety disorders.
