# Paul Tillich

Paul Tillich (1886–1965) was a German-American theologian and existential philosopher, and a leading figure in the philosophical treatment of anxiety, alongside Kierkegaard and Heidegger. He was among the most prominent Protestant theologians to bring theology into sustained dialogue with philosophy, psychology and art, and his analysis of anxiety and courage passed directly into existential psychotherapy through his friendship with Rollo May.

## Life and Career

Tillich was born on 20 August 1886 in the village of Starzeddel in Brandenburg, Prussia, the son of a Lutheran pastor. He studied theology and philosophy at Berlin, Tübingen and Halle, and took his doctorate in philosophy at Breslau in 1910 with a dissertation on Schelling. He served as an army chaplain in the First World War, and the experience of mass death at the front marked him permanently. He taught at Berlin, Marburg and Dresden, and in 1929 became professor of philosophy at Frankfurt. In 1933 he was dismissed from his post after the Nazis came to power, because of his early public criticism of the regime. At the invitation of Reinhold Niebuhr he emigrated to the United States and joined Union Theological Seminary in New York, where he remained for many years. In 1955 he moved to Harvard University, and in 1962 to the University of Chicago, where he died on 22 October 1965.

## Key Works

His best-known book, *The Courage to Be* (1952), grew out of his Terry Lectures at Yale University and remains the clearest statement of his thought on anxiety. His major systematic work, *Systematic Theology* (three volumes, 1951–1963), sets out his method of correlation and his doctrine of God as being-itself. *Dynamics of Faith* (1957) develops his famous definition of faith as "ultimate concern", and *The Shaking of the Foundations* (1948) collects sermons that show his theology addressed to ordinary human experience.

## Anxiety and the Courage to Be

Tillich's central distinction is between fear, which has a definite object, and anxiety, which has no object because its object is nothingness itself — the permanent possibility that one might not be. In *The Courage to Be* he classifies existential anxiety into three types: the anxiety of fate and death, the anxiety of guilt and condemnation, and the anxiety of emptiness and meaninglessness. He argues that each type has dominated a historical period: in late antiquity the anxiety of fate prevailed, in the late Middle Ages the anxiety of guilt, and in the modern age the anxiety of emptiness. Anxiety is thus not a symptom to be cured but a permanent structure of human existence.

Courage, for Tillich, is "the ethical act in which man affirms his own being in spite of those elements of his existence which conflict with his essential self-affirmation." It does not abolish anxiety; it endures it. Courage takes the form of the courage to be as a part of a community, and the courage to be as oneself alone, and culminates in a courage that goes beyond traditional theism — an absolute faith directed toward the "God above God" when the imagined God is lost in doubt.

## Systematic Theology and the Method of Correlation

In *Systematic Theology* Tillich developed his "method of correlation": the questions raised by the human situation find their answers in the symbols of revelation, so that theology answers the questions of existence rather than questions posed independently of it. Faith is "ultimate concern" — the state of being grasped by something that concerns one unconditionally — a definition wide enough to include hidden forms of religion such as nationalism and the pursuit of money. God, he insists, is not a being among beings but "being-itself", and to speak of God's existence in the sense of a thing existing is to end in a disguised atheism.

## Direct Influence on Rollo May

This is the practically most important link in Tillich's story. Tillich and Rollo May met in New York when May was a student at Union Theological Seminary, and they remained friends for life. May drew directly on Tillich's distinction between normal anxiety, which belongs to existence, and neurotic anxiety, which prevents a person from facing their possibilities, in his book *The Meaning of Anxiety*, first published in 1950 and revised in 1977. May also wrote about their friendship in *Paulus: Reminiscences of a Friendship* (1973). Through this route Tillich's ideas entered existential psychotherapy and the dialogue between theology and psychoanalysis, and they remain one of the clearest bridges between the two fields.

## Influence and Critique

Karl Barth and the dialectical theologians objected to the method of correlation on the grounds that it makes philosophy, or the human situation, the measure of revelation rather than the reverse. Critics from the analytic tradition found his language of "being-itself" and of symbols wavering between suggestion and precision, so that its conditions of truth are unclear. He was also accused of making "ultimate concern" so broad that any deep commitment could be called faith. From the therapeutic side, his analysis of anxiety is an existential-ontological analysis, not a clinical guide: it does not distinguish between anxiety disorders in the medical sense, which is why May himself had to connect it to clinical practice. His lasting contribution is to have restored anxiety to a cognitive dignity — not a symptom to be cured, but a sign that human beings belong to what transcends them.
