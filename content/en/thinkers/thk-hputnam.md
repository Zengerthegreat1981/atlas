# Hilary Putnam

An American philosopher (1926–2016), one of the most versatile philosophers of the second half of the twentieth century, who contributed to the philosophy of mind, language, science, metaphysics, mathematics, logic, and ethics. He was born in Chicago and received his doctorate from the University of California, Los Angeles in 1951 under Hans Reichenbach, and taught at Princeton, MIT, and then Harvard until his retirement in 2000. He became known for himself overturning many of the major positions that he had formulated.

## Life and Formation

Putnam began in the analytic tradition he inherited from Reichenbach and Carnap, and worked in the philosophy of science and logic, and took part in important mathematical work: with Martin Davis and Julia Robinson he contributed to the preliminaries that led to the solution of Hilbert's tenth problem, which Matiyasevich settled in 1970, and he also took part in the Davis-Putnam algorithm for logical problems. He then turned to the philosophy of mind and language, and in the 1960s was a politically active leftist who opposed the Vietnam War. In his later years he drew close to Jewish religious thought, and wrote on ethics and pragmatism, influenced by William James and John Dewey.

## What He Gave

- **"Functionalism" (1960)**: in "Minds and Machines," then in "The Nature of Mental States" (1967), he formulated functionalism: a mental state is what it does, not what it is made of, and so the same state can be realized in different materials (multiple realizability).
- **"Twin Earth" and "semantic externalism" (1975)**: in his essay "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" he imagined a planet identical to our Earth in everything except that its transparent liquid called "water" has the composition XYZ rather than H₂O. The Earth dweller and his twin are in an exactly identical mental state when each says "water," and yet each refers to something different. His famous conclusion: "meanings just ain't in the head," for meaning is determined by the actual environment and by the social division of linguistic labor, not by what is in the speaker's mind.
- **"Brain in a Vat" (1981)**: in *Reason, Truth and History* he used the same externalism against skepticism: if we were brains in vats, the sentence "I am a brain in a vat" could not refer to real brains and vats, so the hypothesis refutes itself by the mere fact of being formulated.
- **"Internal realism" (1981)**: in the same book he rejected the "God's-eye point of view," that is, that there is a single correct description of the world independent of every conceptual framework, without slipping into relativism.

## Later Shifts

In *Representation and Reality* (1988) he retreated from functionalism, arguing that mental states do not admit pure functional individuation because their content depends on environment and society, and so they cannot be defined by a computer's internal organization alone. In the 1990s he moved to what he called "natural realism" or "reflective naive realism," refused to treat sensory experience as a medium that veils the world, and defended the mind's ability to perceive the world directly, as in *The Threefold Cord: Mind, Body and World* (1999). In ethics he attacked the sharp separation between fact and value in *The Collapse of the Fact/Value Dichotomy* (2002).

## Influence

The functionalism he formulated influenced a generation of philosophers of mind (Fodor among them) and cognitive science and artificial intelligence, and his semantic externalism ran parallel to Saul Kripke's work on the causal theory of reference. What he is most remembered for methodologically is that he overturned with his own hand every major position he took, functionalism first and then internal realism, until he became a proverbial example in philosophy of readiness to retract.

## Limits and Criticism

Critics held that his repeated changes of position made it hard to fix a stable "Putnam position," that some arguments he offered, including the brain-in-a-vat argument, rely on controversial semantic premises, and that his model-theoretic argument against metaphysical realism has not been agreed upon by philosophers. Realists such as David Lewis (in "Putnam's Paradox", 1984) resisted the model-theoretic argument and saw internal realism as drifting toward idealism. Opponents of semantic externalism faulted it for not explaining how a speaker knows what he means by his words with special authority. Finally, his ethical position remained richer in advocacy than in the construction of a complete theory.

## Major Works

- "Minds and Machines" (1960), the first formulation of functionalism.
- "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975).
- *Reason, Truth and History* (1981).
- *Representation and Reality* (1988), his retreat from functionalism.
- *Realism with a Human Face* (1990).
- *Renewing Philosophy* (1992).
- *The Threefold Cord: Mind, Body and World* (1999).
