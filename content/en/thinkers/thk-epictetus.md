# Epictetus

An influential Stoic philosopher of the Roman imperial era who taught in Greek, born a slave in Hierapolis in Phrygia and later freed; he was expelled from Rome with the other philosophers under Domitian and founded his school in Nicopolis. He did not write a single word himself; everything that remains of him is the record of his student Arrian. He shaped late Stoicism as a purely practical method, and set out the principle of the "dichotomy of control", which distinguishes what is under our direct control (our thoughts, desires, judgments, and decisions) from what we do not control (our bodies, possessions, reputation, and the events of the external world).

## Life and Formation

He was born around 50 CE in Hierapolis (today Pamukkale, in western Turkey), and was a slave of Epaphroditus, a freedman of Nero and one of his aides in government. Accounts speak of a defect in his leg, and some ancient writers attributed its cause to his master's ill-treatment. In Rome he was able to attend the lessons of the Stoic Musonius Rufus, one of the most prominent teachers of Stoicism of that age, and then his master freed him. He taught philosophy in Rome until the emperor Domitian issued a decree banishing philosophers around 89 or 93, whereupon he moved to Nicopolis in the region of Epirus in western Greece and founded his school, and he remained there teaching until his death around 135. He lived poor and unmarried for a long time, then adopted a child to save him from perishing.

## Method and Teaching

Epictetus did not write; he conversed with students orally and used a dialectical style resembling Socrates. His student Flavius Arrian (later the historian) recorded his lectures in the eight books of the *Discourses* (Diatribai), of which four have survived, and then summarized their essence in the *Enchiridion* (Handbook). He held that philosophy is a way of life and a training, not an exposition of doctrines. He directed his teaching to three complementary domains: the domain of desire and aversion (to desire what is in our power), the domain of action (to perform our social roles in the fitting manner), and the domain of assent (to judge impressions soundly so that we are not swept along by the first appearance of things).

## The Dichotomy of Control and Moral Choice

The *Enchiridion* begins by saying that some things are "up to us" (eph' hēmin) and some are not up to us. Opinion, impulse, desire, and aversion are ours; body, property, fame, and office are not ours. Whoever believes that what is not his is his will be exposed to frustration, anxiety, and grief. According to Epictetus, souls are disturbed not by things themselves but by the opinions they form about them. His teaching revolves around the faculty of "moral choice" (prohairesis), the capacity for judgment and choice that no one can compel, not even a tyrant; so a slave who uses it well is freer than an emperor who is enslaved by fear. He tied this to the idea of divine providence: the world is governed by a divine reason, and a person should accept his fate with love and see himself as a citizen of the city of the world.

## Works

- *Enchiridion (Handbook)*: a brief summary extracted by Arrian from the Discourses, the most widely circulated of his texts through history.
- *Discourses (Diatribai)*: recorded by his student Arrian from his oral lessons; four of the eight books have come down to us.
- Scattered fragments transmitted by ancient authors such as Stobaeus.

## Reception and Influence

The emperor Marcus Aurelius admired Epictetus and mentioned him in the *Meditations*, and Simplicius commented on the Handbook in the 6th century CE; then the monasteries of the Byzantine age adopted the Handbook and reworked it in Christian versions. In the modern age he became a source for cognitive psychology: Albert Ellis, founder of Rational Emotive Behavior Therapy (REBT), and Aaron Beck, founder of cognitive therapy, cited Epictetus's statement on opinions and their effect on emotion. James Stockdale, the American prisoner in Vietnam who had read the Handbook before his capture, applied his teachings on endurance and published his reflections in *Courage Under Fire* (1993). Michel Foucault studied him in his lectures on the "hermeneutics of the subject" (1981–1982), and Pierre Hadot saw in the Stoic training "spiritual exercises". The dichotomy of control has today become material in self-help books.

## Limits and Criticism

Hegel, in the *Phenomenology of Spirit*, criticized Stoic consciousness as an abstract freedom that withdraws inward and ignores the social reality in which people are enslaved. Other critics held that his advice to accept what we do not control may feed political passivity, although Epictetus himself lived under a despotic ruler and spoke freely. Martha Nussbaum, in *The Therapy of Desire* (1994), addressed the Stoic position on the emotions and held that the attempt to uproot the emotions deprives a person of the values of love and grief. Scholars also point out that the sharp dichotomy between what is ours and what is not ours simplifies reality, since many matters fall between the two states. All our knowledge of his teaching depends on Arrian's record, so one cannot say with certainty how much he added by his own formulation.
