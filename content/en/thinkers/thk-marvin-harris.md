# Marvin Harris

An American anthropologist and the founder of "cultural materialism," a theoretical and methodological strategy for explaining cultural phenomena by reference to measurable material, environmental and economic factors, rather than symbolic or abstract meaning-based explanations.

## Cultural Materialism

In *The Rise of Anthropological Theory* (1968) and *Cows, Pigs, Wars, and Witches: The Riddles of Culture* (1974), Harris set out an analytical framework that divides any cultural system into three levels: "infrastructure" (technology, production and environment), "structure" (social and economic organization) and "superstructure" (ideology, religion and the arts). He held the methodological premise (the "principle of infrastructural determinism") that changes in infrastructure, especially in modes of production and reproduction, best explain changes in the other levels. This is a strategic theoretical preference rather than an absolute determinism.

## The Most Famous Example: The Sacred Cow of India

Harris used the example of the sacred status of the cow among Hindus, whose slaughter is forbidden despite widespread historical hunger, to illustrate his method. Rather than being satisfied with a symbolic religious explanation, he analyzed the living economic value of the cow (pulling ploughs, dung as fuel and fertilizer, milk) in comparison with its value as meat slaughtered only once. He concluded that the prohibition on slaughter is a rational environmental-economic adaptation under a religious cover, and not merely a cultural "irrationality," as it was sometimes described in Western literature.

## The Prohibition of Pork

In *Cows, Pigs, Wars, and Witches*, Harris devoted a well-known chapter to analyzing the prohibition of pork in Judaism and Islam using the same method he applied to the sacred cow of Hinduism. Rather than accepting the scriptural religious explanation alone, he argued that the pig is an animal ill-suited ecologically to the semi-arid climate of the Middle East (it needs shade and wet mud, and competes with humans for grain rather than grazing on grasses as cattle and sheep do). In his account, the religious prohibition reinforced an ecological and economic logic in this environment. This materialist explanation provoked wide debate among scholars of religion and anthropologists, who saw it as reducing a religious and legal tradition to a merely utilitarian ecological function; critics such as Mary Douglas offered different, symbolic readings of the pork taboo.

## Impact and Critique

Harris became one of the best-known anthropologists outside academia through his books addressed to the general public. However, his method was criticized by interpretive and symbolic anthropology (Clifford Geertz in particular), on the grounds that it reduces cultural and religious meaning to a utilitarian material function and neglects the interpretive dimension that the bearers of a culture themselves give to their practices.

## Academic Formation and Institutional Position

Harris received his doctorate from Columbia University in 1953 and taught there for nearly three decades before moving to the University of Florida in 1980, where he spent the final part of his academic career. He was influenced by Julian Steward and by Steward's method of "cultural ecology," as well as by Charles Wagley and Leslie White, which Harris later developed in a broader and more theoretically ambitious direction to explain entire cultural systems, not only technical and economic practices.

## Cultural Wars between Cultural Materialism and Interpretive Anthropology

Harris engaged in sharp, public intellectual confrontations with Clifford Geertz and his interpretive school during the 1970s and 1980s. He argued that excessive focus on the internal "meaning" of culture risks sliding into an unlimited relativism, in which it becomes impossible to build a comparative, empirically testable science of human culture. He insisted that the aim of a scientific anthropology must remain the explanation of cultural patterns through material causes that can be measured and compared across cultures, rather than being satisfied with the "thick description" of unique local meanings that are difficult to generalize.

## Emic and Etic

One of Harris's most important methodological contributions is his strict distinction between the "emic" perspective (how the members of a culture understand their own practices and beliefs in their own terms) and the "etic" perspective (the analytical categories set by the researcher from outside for the purposes of scientific comparison across cultures). He borrowed these two terms from the linguist Kenneth Pike. Harris insisted that scientific anthropology specifically needs the etic level to build generalizations that are comparable across cultures, even when this sometimes conflicts with the emic understanding of a particular practice. This position distinguished him from interpretivists, who regarded the emic understanding alone as the goal of anthropological analysis.

## War and Population in Ecological Anthropology

Harris also applied his materialist method to explaining warfare in small-scale societies. He argued that recurring warfare is linked to population pressure on resources and is often supported by an ideology of male supremacy, and he proposed that competition over scarce animal protein helped explain Yanomamö warfare, a view disputed by Napoleon Chagnon and others. He also offered materialist explanations of Aztec sacrifice in *Cannibals and Kings* (1977). These explanations provoked debate similar to that over his account of the sacred cow, concerning the limits of material reduction of complex cultural behavior.
