# Robert Lucas Jr.

**Robert Emerson Lucas Jr.** was an American economist at the University of Chicago and a central figure of the new classical school in macroeconomics, alongside Thomas Sargent, Neil Wallace and others. His work in the 1970s changed how many macroeconomists built models. He was awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1995 "for having developed and applied the hypothesis of rational expectations, and thereby having transformed macroeconomic analysis and deepened our understanding of economic policy".

## From a mathematical hypothesis to a general method

Lucas took the "rational expectations" hypothesis, which the economist John Muth had originally put forward in 1961 as a narrowly used mathematical tool, and made it a standard assumption in a large part of macroeconomics. Individuals do not form their expectations of the future in a simple adaptive way based only on the past. They use all available information efficiently, including their understanding of how the economy itself works and of government policies. They therefore do not make systematically repeated errors in their expectations that the government could exploit consistently.

## The Lucas critique

In his influential 1976 article, Lucas presented what became known as the "Lucas critique". Statistical relationships estimated from historical data (such as the Phillips curve) are not structurally stable. They reflect the behaviour of individuals within a particular policy regime. When the policy itself changes, rational individuals change their expectations and behaviour accordingly, and the old statistical relationship breaks down. The critique weakened confidence in large Keynesian forecasting models and pushed much of the field toward models with explicit micro-foundations.

## The ineffectiveness of anticipated monetary policy

The "policy ineffectiveness proposition" was developed by Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace (1975), building on Lucas's work on rational expectations. If rational individuals anticipate an announced expansionary policy, they adjust their inflation expectations and their contracts in advance, which, in that model, cancels its real effect on output and employment and leaves only a nominal effect on prices. A policy becomes effective only when it is surprising and unanticipated. In that setting, fully anticipated fine-tuning of demand has no lasting real effect. New Keynesian models later disputed the assumptions (especially flexible prices) on which the proposition rests.

## Business cycles: from monetary shocks to the full model

Lucas developed early business-cycle models that explain fluctuations in output and employment by unanticipated monetary shocks, which temporarily confuse producers' ability to distinguish a change in the relative price of their own good from a general change in the overall price level (the "Lucas island model"). This line of thought was later taken further by other economists toward real business cycle theory, which, in the work of Finn Kydland and Edward Prescott (1982), links fluctuations to real technological shocks rather than monetary ones. Lucas also contributed to growth theory with his 1988 paper "On the Mechanics of Economic Development".

## Micro-foundations: a lasting methodological legacy

Lucas pressed a methodological standard that became dominant in academic macroeconomics for decades: every macroeconomic model should be built from explicit micro-foundations describing how representative families and firms rationally optimize their decisions over time under clear constraints, rather than relying on aggregated behavioural equations without theoretical foundation. This standard remained dominant even after the New Keynesian models later restored the argument for intervention within a micro-founded framework of the kind Lucas had pressed for.

## Nobel Prize and later assessment after the 2008 crisis

Lucas received the Nobel Prize in 1995. His ideas, and the new classical school in general, faced wide criticism after the global financial crisis of 2008. Their models were accused of neglecting the role of the financial sector and the possibility of genuine market failure. This is often contrasted with a remark in Lucas's 2003 presidential address to the American Economic Association ("Macroeconomic Priorities"), in which he argued that the central problem of depression prevention had, for practical purposes, been solved. The judgment was widely reassessed after 2008, though Lucas is not held directly responsible for the crisis.

## A central role in shaping the Chicago economists

Lucas taught at Carnegie Mellon University from 1963 and returned to Chicago in the mid-1970s. He supervised many graduate students who later worked in macroeconomics. His career links Friedman's Chicago monetarism to the rational-expectations models of the new classical school, of which he was a central, not a sole, author. He died on 15 May 2023.

## A well-known anecdote

His 1988 divorce settlement with his first wife, Rita Cohen Lucas, included a clause giving her a share of any Nobel Prize awarded before a date in late 1995. The 1995 prize fell inside that window, and the clause was honoured.
