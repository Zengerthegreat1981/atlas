# Abu Hamid al-Ghazali

A Persian jurist, legal theorist, theologian, and Sufi, born in Tus in Khurasan in 450 AH/1058 CE, and counted among the most influential Muslim thinkers in the history of later Islamic thought. He combined Ash'arite theology, Shafi'i jurisprudence, Peripatetic philosophy, and Sufism, and produced an intellectual synthesis in which he tried to reconcile the sharia with rational knowledge on the one hand, and jurisprudence with spiritual experience on the other.

## Biography

Al-Ghazali received his early education in Tus and Gorgan, then moved to Nishapur, where he studied under Imam al-Haramayn Abu al-Ma'ali al-Juwayni at the Nizamiyya madrasa and gained depth in Ash'arite theology, the principles of jurisprudence, and logic. After al-Juwayni's death in 1085 CE he joined the entourage of the Seljuk vizier Nizam al-Mulk, who in 1091 CE appointed him professor at the Nizamiyya madrasa in Baghdad, where he attained wide fame in teaching and disputation.

In 1095 CE al-Ghazali went through an acute intellectual and psychological crisis that deprived him, by his own description in *Deliverance from Error*, of the ability to speak and teach. He left his post in Baghdad and set out on pilgrimage, then lived in retreat in Damascus and Jerusalem, and later performed the pilgrimage, for a period of about ten years, moving between spiritual discipline and writing, before returning to teach in Nishapur for a few years before his death in Tus in 505 AH/1111 CE.

## What He Gave

### The Critique of Peripatetic Philosophy: *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*

In this book al-Ghazali directed a methodical critique at the philosophy of al-Farabi and Avicenna, presenting their arguments in a precise dialectical style before refuting them. He declared the philosophers unbelievers on three questions that he considered an outright departure from the foundations of religion: the doctrine of the eternity of the world, the denial of God's knowledge of particulars, and the denial of bodily resurrection; and he counted the rest of their views as innovation rather than unbelief. This critique was not a wholesale rejection of logic and philosophy: al-Ghazali himself used the tools of Aristotelian logic in his critique of them, and he later wrote *Mi'yar al-'Ilm* (The Standard of Knowledge) and *Mihakk al-Nazar* (The Touchstone of Reasoning) to make logic accessible to students of the religious sciences.

### Sufism and Religious Revival: *The Revival of the Religious Sciences*

In this encyclopedic work, in forty books distributed over four quarters (acts of worship, customs, the destructive vices, the saving virtues), al-Ghazali tried to integrate outward jurisprudence with inner purification, recasting jurisprudence and doctrine within an ethical and spiritual framework. He relied in it on Sufi experience as direct, certain knowledge (dhawq, "taste") that surpasses theoretical demonstration in its certainty, without dropping adherence to the sharia.

### The Epistemic Crisis and Methodical Doubt: *Deliverance from Error*

An intellectual-spiritual autobiography in which al-Ghazali narrates his journey among the four paths of knowledge that he identified in his era: theology (kalam), philosophy, the teaching of the Batiniyya (the Isma'ilis), and Sufism. He describes passing through a comprehensive methodical doubt that extended even to the self-evident data of sense and reason, before emerging from it, according to his account, by a light that God cast into his heart rather than by an argument of inference, concluding that Sufism is the path of final certainty.

### The Principles of Jurisprudence: *al-Mustasfa min 'Ilm al-Usul*

One of the most important works on the principles of Islamic jurisprudence, in which al-Ghazali recast the science of the principles on logical foundations, drawing on Aristotelian logic in determining definitions and analogy. He discussed in it the theory of public interest (maslaha) and the controls on its use, and he gave in it a famous account of the ranking of the five necessities (the preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage, and property), which later became the cornerstone of the theory of the higher objectives of the sharia (maqasid) among those who came after him, such as al-Shatibi.

### Theology: *al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad*

A mid-length presentation of the creed of the People of the Sunna and the Community in the Ash'arite manner, treating the proof of God's existence, his attributes, prophecy, and the return, in an argumentative style closer to moderation than his other polemical works; it became one of the authoritative texts for teaching Ash'arite theology.

## Influence

Al-Ghazali profoundly influenced the course of later Islamic thought: his critique of the philosophers contributed to the decline of the influence of Peripatetic philosophy in the Islamic East in favor of Ash'arite theology, and he moved Sufism from the margin to the heart of the recognized religious sciences, as the *Revival* became an authoritative reference in spiritual education across the Islamic world to this day. He also directly influenced medieval Jewish and Christian philosophy through Latin and Hebrew translations of some of his works, and he has sometimes been compared with Thomas Aquinas in his effort to reconcile reason and religion. In the principles of jurisprudence, his account of the necessary objectives of the sharia formed the foundation on which al-Shatibi later built his more comprehensive theory of the objectives.

## Limits

Criticisms were directed at al-Ghazali from several quarters. From the side of the philosophers, Averroes (Ibn Rushd), in his book *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*, answered al-Ghazali's critique of the philosophers, accusing him of misunderstanding some positions of Avicenna and al-Farabi, of inaccuracy in attributing certain views to them, and of hastiness in declaring them unbelievers on disputed metaphysical questions. From another side, some historians have argued that al-Ghazali's criticisms of philosophy contributed to a decline of philosophical inquiry in parts of the later Sunni tradition, a view that Frank Griffel and others dispute, pointing out that al-Ghazali himself did not call for dropping logic but for harnessing it, and that Avicennian philosophy continued to flourish within Ash'arite theology.

Some Hanbali scholars, among them Ibn al-Jawzi and later Ibn Taymiyya, also criticized al-Ghazali's blending of philosophical and Sufi concepts (such as the theory of emanation) with Sunni doctrine, considering that some of his expressions in the *Revival* and in *Mishkat al-Anwar* approach the positions of the philosophers for which he himself declared them unbelievers in the *Incoherence*. Some contemporary researchers see here a genuine tension in his project between his outward critique of philosophy and his absorption of some of its propositions, while others (such as Griffel) read his work as a deliberate integration of Avicennian philosophy into Ash'arite theology.

## Major Works

- **The Incoherence of the Philosophers**: a critique of the philosophy of Avicenna and al-Farabi.
- **The Revival of the Religious Sciences**: an encyclopedia of jurisprudence, Sufism, and ethics.
- **Deliverance from Error**: an intellectual-spiritual autobiography.
- **al-Mustasfa min 'Ilm al-Usul**: on the principles of jurisprudence.
- **al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad**: on Ash'arite theology.
- **Mi'yar al-'Ilm**: on logic.
- **Mishkat al-Anwar**: on Sufism and the interpretation of light.
