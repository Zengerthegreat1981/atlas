# Franz Steiner

Franz Baermann Steiner (1909–1952) was a Czech-born British ethnologist, poet, and aphorist whose short life left an outsized mark on British social anthropology. A refugee from Nazism who became a lecturer at Oxford, he wrote almost no books in his lifetime, yet his posthumous lectures on taboo became a classic of the discipline, and his unpublished work on slavery, labour, and the sociology of religion influenced a generation of Oxford anthropologists — most famously Mary Douglas. He is remembered as a thinker of extraordinary erudition whose ideas reached print largely after his death.

## Life and Career

Steiner was born in Prague on 12 October 1909, into a German-speaking Jewish family, and grew up in the multilingual world of Bohemia. He studied Semitic languages at the German University in Prague, spent a period of study in Jerusalem, and took his doctorate in Prague in 1935 with a linguistic thesis on Arabic, reflecting a wide command of languages. After studying ethnology in Vienna, he moved to England in 1936; his parents were later murdered in the Holocaust. At Oxford he studied anthropology under A. R. Radcliffe-Brown and then E. E. Evans-Pritchard, completing a DPhil in 1949 with a comparative study of slavery. He was subsequently appointed to a lectureship in social anthropology at Oxford. He died suddenly of a heart attack in Oxford on 27 November 1952, aged forty-three. In his last years he was close to the novelist Iris Murdoch. Much of his work survived only in lecture notes, notebooks, and drafts.

## Key Works

Steiner's best-known work is *Taboo* (1956), published posthumously from his Oxford lectures, with Laura Bohannan and E. E. Evans-Pritchard involved in preparing it. It remains the most searching anthropological treatment of prohibition, danger, and the sacred. His essay "Towards a Classification of Labour" (published in *Sociologus* in 1957) and the paper "Chagga Truth" (*Africa*, 1954) show the range of his interests, from the definition of work to the ethnography of truth-telling. His wider study of slavery and servile institutions, which grew out of his Oxford doctoral thesis, was never turned into a book. His scattered writings were gathered much later in the two volumes of *Selected Writings* edited by Jeremy Adler and Richard Fardon — *Taboo, Truth, and Religion* (1999) and *Orientpolitik, Value, and Civilization* (1999) — which made the full breadth of his thought visible for the first time. As a poet he wrote in German, and his collection *Eroberungen* appeared in 1964; in London he belonged to a literary circle of German-language exiles that included Elias Canetti and H. G. Adler.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Steiner's work on taboo broke with the explanations offered by his predecessors. Where earlier theorists treated taboo as a primitive fear of danger or as a set of irrational prohibitions, Steiner questioned whether "taboo" named a single problem at all, and treated taboos as ways of identifying and delimiting danger: attitudes of avoidance mark out what is socially significant and so bear on a society's moral order. His analysis drew on linguistics, philosophy, and comparative ethnography, and it treated "primitive" thought as intellectually serious rather than as a defective version of modern rationality. His study of slavery pursued a similar strategy, comparing forms of servitude across societies to ask what made an institution count as slavery — a question of definition rather than of moral condemnation alone. In his lectures on labour he asked how different societies draw the line between work and other activity, arguing that European categories of "labour" distort non-European realities. Across all of this ran a distinctive sensibility, shaped by his Central European intellectual background, that treated anthropology as a branch of the study of civilisation, not merely as the description of small-scale societies.

## Influence and Critique

Steiner's influence was quiet but deep. Mary Douglas's *Purity and Danger* (1966), a highly influential book on pollution and taboo, draws on his work, and Douglas herself acknowledged the debt. His insistence that anthropological categories must not simply translate European concepts into other languages shaped later critiques of the comparative method, and his personal example — the refugee scholar, the poet-anthropologist — has made him a touchstone for recent writing on exile, interdisciplinarity, and the humanities in anthropology. The critique of Steiner is inseparable from the circumstances of his career: because so much of his work was unpublished, unfinished, or delivered as lectures, his arguments often reach us in fragmentary form, and their influence is hard to disentangle from that of the colleagues who edited and transmitted them. Some scholars also note that his sweeping comparative ambitions — a theory of civilisation, a sociology of slavery — outran the evidence he had time to assemble. Yet the very incompleteness of his work has kept it alive: each generation of readers finds in Steiner a thinker who asked the discipline's hardest questions about meaning, classification, and value.
