# Franz Steiner

Franz Baermann Steiner (1909–1952) was a Czech-born British ethnologist, poet, and aphorist whose short life left an outsized mark on British social anthropology. A refugee from Nazism who became a lecturer at Oxford, he wrote almost no books in his lifetime, yet his posthumous lectures on taboo became a classic of the discipline, and his unpublished work on slavery, labour, and the sociology of religion influenced a generation of Oxford anthropologists — most famously Mary Douglas. He is remembered as a thinker of extraordinary erudition whose ideas reached print largely after his death.

## Life and Career

Steiner was born in Prague on 12 October 1909, into a German-speaking Jewish family, and grew up in the multilingual world of Bohemia. He studied at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, at the University of Vienna, and at the German University in Prague, where he took his first doctorate in 1935 with a thesis on the history of Arabic word formation — evidence of a linguistic range that eventually covered some twenty languages, from classical Arabic and Hebrew to Malay. He did fieldwork in Sub-Carpathian Ruthenia in the 1930s and spent time in London researching at the British Museum. After the German occupation of Czechoslovakia in 1939 he remained in Britain as a refugee; his parents were murdered at Treblinka in 1942. At Oxford he studied under A. R. Radcliffe-Brown and then E. E. Evans-Pritchard, completing a second doctorate in 1949 on the comparative study of slavery. He was appointed lecturer in social anthropology at Oxford in 1949 and became a fellow of Magdalen College. He died suddenly of a heart attack in Oxford on 27 November 1952, aged forty-three, shortly after the novelist Iris Murdoch had accepted his proposal of marriage. Much of his work survived only in lecture notes, notebooks, and drafts.

## Key Works

Steiner's best-known work is *Taboo* (1956), published posthumously from his Oxford lectures and edited by Laura Bohannan. It remains the most searching anthropological treatment of prohibition, danger, and the sacred. His essay "Towards a Classification of Labour" (1957) and the posthumous paper "Chagga Truth" (1954) show the range of his interests, from the definition of work to the ethnography of truth-telling. His doctoral thesis on slavery, on which he had planned a major book called *Servile Institutions*, remained unfinished at his death. His scattered writings were gathered much later in the two volumes of *Selected Writings* edited by Jeremy Adler and Richard Fardon — *Taboo, Truth, and Religion* (1999) and *Orientpolitik, Value, and Civilization* (1999) — which made the full breadth of his thought visible for the first time. As a poet he published in German, and his aphoristic essays circulated among a literary circle that included Elias Canetti and H. G. Adler.

## Main Ideas and Contributions

Steiner's work on taboo broke with the explanations offered by his predecessors. Where earlier theorists treated taboo as a primitive fear of danger or as a set of irrational prohibitions, Steiner argued that taboo is a system of classification: it marks out what is socially significant by treating it as dangerous, and it operates through attitudes of avoidance that define the boundaries of a society's moral order. His analysis drew on linguistics, philosophy, and comparative ethnography, and it treated "primitive" thought as intellectually serious rather than as a defective version of modern rationality. His unfinished study of slavery pursued a similar strategy, comparing forms of servitude across societies to ask what made an institution count as slavery — a question of definition rather than of moral condemnation alone. In his lectures on labour he asked how different societies draw the line between work and other activity, arguing that European categories of "labour" distort non-European realities. Across all of this ran a distinctive sensibility: a Central European humanism, formed by Simmel and by the Viennese intellectual world, that treated anthropology as a branch of the study of civilisation, not merely as the description of small-scale societies.

## Influence and Critique

Steiner's influence was quiet but deep. Mary Douglas's *Purity and Danger* (1966), the most influential anthropological book on pollution and taboo of the twentieth century, builds directly on his lectures, and Douglas herself acknowledged the debt. His insistence that anthropological categories must not simply translate European concepts into other languages shaped later critiques of the comparative method, and his personal example — the refugee scholar, the poet-anthropologist — has made him a touchstone for recent writing on exile, interdisciplinarity, and the humanities in anthropology. Jeremy Adler's biography, *Franz Baermann Steiner: A Stranger in the World* (2021), renewed interest in his life and work. The critique of Steiner is inseparable from the circumstances of his career: because so much of his work was unpublished, unfinished, or delivered as lectures, his arguments often reach us in fragmentary form, and their influence is hard to disentangle from that of the colleagues who edited and transmitted them. Some scholars also note that his sweeping comparative ambitions — a theory of civilisation, a sociology of slavery — outran the evidence he had time to assemble. Yet the very incompleteness of his work has kept it alive: each generation of readers finds in Steiner a thinker who asked the discipline's hardest questions about meaning, classification, and value.
