# Emmy van Deurzen

Emmy van Deurzen (born 13 December 1951) is a Dutch-born existential philosopher, psychologist and psychotherapist who did more than anyone to organise the British school of existential therapy, turning it from scattered individuals into an institution with training programmes, a professional society, a journal and academic degrees.

## Life and Career

Van Deurzen was born in the Netherlands in 1951, studied philosophy and psychology (including in Paris), and settled in Britain in 1977. She combined philosophical training with psychotherapy training, and she went on to create the first master's programme in existential psychotherapy in Britain in the early 1980s. That programme was later based at Regent's College in London, where she led the School of Psychotherapy and Counselling. In 1988 she founded the Society for Existential Analysis and its journal, the *Journal of Existential Analysis*, giving the movement a professional and academic home in Britain. In the 1990s she left Regent's to found the New School of Psychotherapy and Counselling in London with Digby Tantam, and later founded the Existential Academy, where she directs training. She has held a professorship in partnership with Middlesex University.

## Key Works

Her first book, *Existential Counselling in Practice* (1988; later editions retitled *Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy in Practice*), became the standard textbook of the British school. *Everyday Mysteries: Existential Dimensions of Psychotherapy* (1997) is her most widely read theoretical work, and *Paradox and Passion in Psychotherapy* (1998) explores the tensions and contradictions at the heart of therapeutic work. With Claire Arnold-Baker she edited *Existential Perspectives on Human Issues* (2005), a handbook for therapeutic practice, and with Martin Adams she wrote the practical training text *Skills in Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy* (2011). In *Psychotherapy and the Quest for Happiness* (2009) she presents therapy as an "art of living" drawing on philosophy from Socrates to Sartre.

## The Four Dimensions of Existence

Van Deurzen's central theoretical contribution is the four dimensions of existence, offered as an alternative to Irvin Yalom's four "ultimate concerns" — death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness. Instead of four topics that confront the person, she proposes four worlds the person lives in simultaneously: the physical, the social, the personal and the spiritual. She builds on Ludwig Binswanger's distinction between the surrounding world, the shared world and the self-world, and adds the spiritual dimension, or the world beyond. Each dimension carries its own tensions: the physical between attraction and repulsion, life and death; the social between love and hate, belonging and isolation; the personal between identity and loss of self; the spiritual between value and absurdity.

## Why This Is a Fundamental Difference

The difference matters because "concerns" are things to be faced, while "dimensions" are worlds to be lived in. The first makes therapy a confrontation; the second makes it a survey of a whole life — including money, work, power, illness and the body. The therapist can ask where the person is stuck between dimensions, and how a problem in one dimension connects with a problem in another, so that anxiety is not reduced to an inner psychological problem but seen in its physical, social and moral context. The framework has become the organising map of British existential training, and it travelled widely through the centres she helped to found, including the World Confederation for Existential Therapy (2015).

## Her Stance on Technique and Her Critique of the Americans

Van Deurzen gives technique a secondary place. For her, existential therapy is a philosophical practice, not a set of tools, and the therapist is a companion in exploring a life rather than an expert treating symptoms. The work proceeds through philosophical dialogue with the client about the meaning of their life, their values and their choices, rather than through a diagnosis imposed from outside the encounter. Difficulties and crises — bereavement, illness, disappointment — are treated as material for understanding the self, not merely as disorders to be removed.

She has distinguished the British approach from some American branches of existential therapy, including those of Yalom and Rollo May, which she has regarded as more individualistic and more technique-oriented. The British school, influenced by R. D. Laing and the Philadelphia Association, puts the emphasis instead on relationship, social context and precise philosophical interpretation of existence.

## Influence and Critique

Van Deurzen's institutional achievement is inseparable from her intellectual one: she built master's and professional doctorate programmes in existential therapy in partnership with British universities, established a training curriculum combining the reading of philosophical texts with clinical practice and supervision, and trained a generation of existential therapists across Europe and beyond.

Critics from the evidence-based therapy tradition object that existential therapy lacks sufficient experimental studies, and that her four dimensions are an interpretive framework that cannot be tested by clinical measurement standards. Philosophers have argued that her language is so broad that it becomes hard to distinguish existential therapy from philosophical counselling in general. Some practitioners worry that her criticism of psychiatric classification may lead to neglecting patients who benefit from diagnosis and medication. She has replied that attending to meaning does not deny the need for medical treatment, but prevents suffering from becoming merely a symptom to be silenced. Her institutional influence on the training of the newest generation of existential therapists in Europe remains very large.
