# David M. Clark

A British clinical psychologist (born 20 August 1954 in Darlington) and emeritus professor of experimental psychology at the University of Oxford. He developed cognitive treatment models for panic attacks, social phobia, posttraumatic stress disorder, and health anxiety that have been widely tested and adopted in clinical guidelines.

## Career

Clark studied experimental psychology at Oxford (M.A., Lincoln College) and trained in clinical psychology at the Institute of Psychiatry in London (M.Phil., 1979), then completed a D.Phil. at Oxford in 1983. He began as a university lecturer in the Oxford department of psychiatry, in a group that included Michael Gelder, Paul Salkovskis, and, later, Anke Ehlers, at a time when Aaron T. Beck was a regular visitor. In 2000 he became professor of psychology at the Institute of Psychiatry, King's College London, and with Ehlers and Salkovskis founded the Centre for Anxiety Disorders and Trauma at the Maudsley Hospital. He returned to a chair at Oxford in 2011. From the start of the **Improving Access to Psychological Therapies (IAPT)** programme he has been national clinical adviser to the Department of Health and NHS England. The programme, developed with the economist Richard Layard and launched in England in 2008, trains therapists to deliver evidence-based psychological treatments in the National Health Service and publishes session-by-session outcomes. Layard and Clark set out the case in *Thrive* (2014).

## Contributions

### The Cognitive Model of Panic Attacks

In 1986, in the journal *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, Clark presented a model that explains a panic attack as the product of a **catastrophic misinterpretation** of normal bodily sensations (such as a racing heart or shortness of breath) as a sign of an imminent disaster (a heart attack or losing one's mind). Anxiety then increases and the sensations intensify in a vicious cycle. He based the model on the observation that panic patients hyperventilate, producing sensations similar to their attacks, and that persuading them toward an alternative interpretation reduces the attacks. Clinical trials conducted by Clark, Salkovskis, and colleagues in 1994 supported the effectiveness of cognitive therapy for panic compared with applied relaxation and imipramine.

### Cognitive Treatment of Social Phobia

In 1995, Clark and Adrian Wells developed a cognitive model of social phobia that focuses on excessive self-focused attention and negative mental images of the self during social situations, and on safety behaviors that prevent the correction of the mistaken belief. A treatment built on it proved effective in a randomized controlled trial in 2003, which compared it with fluoxetine and a placebo, and in which cognitive therapy outperformed the other conditions.

### Posttraumatic Stress Disorder and Health Anxiety

With Anke Ehlers, Clark proposed in 2000 the cognitive model of posttraumatic stress disorder, which holds that persistence is linked to threatening appraisals of the event and of the self, and to poorly organized memory. They built a cognitive treatment on it, which was evaluated in clinical trials, including a randomized trial reported in 2005. Building on the work of Salkovskis and Warwick, he contributed to cognitive accounts of health anxiety, which hold that the misinterpretation of normal bodily symptoms feeds the fear of illness.

### Brief Cognitive Therapy and the IAPT Program

Clark contributed to developing brief and effective versions of cognitive therapy for panic attacks and social anxiety, and led the scientific and policy effort to widen national access to cognitive behavioral therapies, which influenced similar models in other countries. In 2011 he published an account of implementing National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence (NICE) guidelines in the IAPT programme. The institute was later renamed the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence.

## Honors

He is a Fellow of the British Academy and of the Academy of Medical Sciences, and has received national and international awards for his research.

## Limits and Criticism

His contribution is concentrated specifically on anxiety disorders. His cognitive models were developed and tested mainly in Western clinics in Britain and the United States, and their full cultural generalizability beyond this context is an ongoing research question, because the interpretation of bodily sensations and safety behaviors are shaped by culture. The IAPT program has faced criticism that the "recovery" rates it announces depend on short-term measures, that some patients with complex conditions do not benefit from brief treatments, and that therapists have complained of pressure from numerical targets. The cognitive literature in general also debates whether changing interpretation is in fact the mechanism of improvement, or whether exposure and behavioral experiments are the basis.
