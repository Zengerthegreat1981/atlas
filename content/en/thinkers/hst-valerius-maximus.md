# Valerius Maximus

Valerius Maximus compiled the best-known surviving handbook of historical anecdotes and moral exempla from early imperial Rome. His thematic organization of historical narratives into Roman and foreign categories transformed traditional historiography into a functional resource for rhetorical persuasion, public education, and moral philosophy under the Principate.

## Definition and Historical Significance

Valerius Maximus (fl. c. 14–37 CE) was a Roman writer and historian who flourished during the reign of Emperor Tiberius. His principal surviving work, *Facta et dicta memorabilia* (*Memorable Deeds and Sayings*, in nine books), is a vast compendium of historical exempla systematically organized by moral, religious, and political topics. 

Rather than following a continuous chronological narrative like Livy or Tacitus, Valerius fragmented Roman and Mediterranean history into modular pedagogical episodes. His work represents the institutionalization of the Roman *exemplum* tradition—the cultural practice of citing historical precedents (*mos maiorum*) to validate moral conduct, legal judgments, and political decisions. For later antiquity, the Middle Ages, and the Renaissance, Valerius served as a primary repository of classical historical memory.

## Historical Context

Little is known of Valerius Maximus's personal life except that he was not of high aristocratic birth and possessed modest financial means. He secured the patronage of Sextus Pompeius (consul in 14 CE), whom he accompanied to the province of Asia around 27 CE. 

Valerius composed his work during the reign of Tiberius, dedicating the collection directly to the emperor in an elaborate preface that praised Tiberius as the terrestrial guarantor of peace, justice, and religion. His writing reflected the consolidation of the imperial Principate following the death of Augustus, an era when public rhetoric moved from contentious Republican assembly debates to schools of declamation (*controversiae* and *suasoriae*) and imperial administrative circles. In this transformed political environment, orators, magistrates, and students required ready access to well-indexed historical precedents that affirmed civic loyalty and traditional virtues without threatening imperial authority.

## Key Contributions and Methodology

Valerius's chief methodological innovation was his rigid thematic classification. Each of his nine books is subdivided into specific rubrics, and within nearly every rubric, he systematically separated his material into two distinct categories: Roman examples (*domestica*) and foreign examples (*externa*, drawn largely from Greek, Carthaginian, or Persian history). This deliberate juxtaposition served both to establish the moral superiority of traditional Roman institutions and to acknowledge universal ethical principles across cultures.

His compendium covers a vast spectrum of human activity:
- **Book 1**: Religious observances, cult practices, omens, prodigies, and the punishment of sacrilege.
- **Book 2**: Ancient social customs, marriage rites, military discipline, and the censorship.
- **Books 3–6**: Cardinal moral virtues, including bravery (*fortitudo*), patient endurance (*patientia*), fidelity (*fides*), ancestral moderation (*moderatio*), justice, and chastity (*pudicitia*).
- **Books 7–8**: Shifts of fortune, stratification, legal wills, military stratagems, and celebrated judicial trials.
- **Book 9**: Systematic catalogs of moral vices, such as luxury, lust, cruelty, greed (*avaritia*), and treachery.

Valerius drew, by his own account and by scholarly inference, upon earlier authors, probably including of the late Republic and early Empire—predominantly Cicero, Livy, Sallust, Marcus Terentius Varro, Coelius Antipater, and Pompeius Trogus. Because substantial portions of Livy's later decades and other early annalistic histories are now lost, Valerius's summaries preserve useful historical notices and variants not found elsewhere in the surviving record.

## Theoretical Framework

Valerius's historical philosophy rested on moral didacticism and social conservatism. He viewed history as an active ethical theater designed to inspire virtue and deter vice through vivid, emotionally resonant exemplars. In his framework, Roman military dominance was the direct result of divine favor secured through punctilious religious observance (*pietas*) and strict preservation of ancestral discipline (*disciplina militaris*). 

By organizing history thematically rather than chronologically, Valerius flattened historical time, placing early Republican heroes (such as Horatius Cocles, Mucius Scaevola, and Lucretia) alongside figures of the late Republic and Augustan eras as eternal archetypes of civic conduct.

## Contemporary Relevance and Reception

During the Middle Ages and early modern period, Valerius Maximus was among the most widely read and copied of all classical Latin prose authors. His work was epitomized in late antiquity by Julius Paris and Januarius Nepotianus, and served as a standard school text and rhetorical manual across medieval Europe, influencing writers from John of Salisbury and Petrarch to Montaigne. Modern historians study his collection to understand Roman collective memory, religious mentality, and the ideological values promoted during the Tiberian Principate.

## Limitations and Critical Perspectives

From the perspective of modern critical historiography, Valerius's methodology presents distinct limitations. His commitment to rhetorical effect and moral lessons frequently led him to compress historical context, exaggerate anecdotes, and smooth away complex political motivations. His anecdotes isolate actors from systemic socio-economic structures, reducing complex historical crises to simple illustrations of individual virtue or depravity. Moreover, his overt flattery of Tiberius and the Julio-Claudian dynasty reflects the political constraints of early imperial authorship.
