# Julius Axelrod

An American biochemist and pharmacologist (1912–2004), one of the founding figures of modern neuropharmacology. He shared the 1970 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Bernard Katz and Ulf von Euler for their discoveries concerning neurotransmitters in nerve endings, and the mechanisms of their storage, release, and inactivation. His work linked pharmacological chemistry with psychiatry by clarifying how brain-acting drugs work at the synapse.

## Origins and Training

Axelrod was born in the Lower East Side of New York City to a family of Jewish immigrants from Poland. He graduated from the City College of New York with a degree in biology in 1933. His financial circumstances did not allow him to study medicine, so he worked as a technician in the industrial hygiene laboratory of the New York City Department of Health, and then in the laboratory of Bernard Brodie at Goldwater Memorial Hospital (from 1949, at the National Heart Institute of the NIH). He did not earn his doctorate until 1955, which he received from George Washington University while working at the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH), an unusual path for a Nobel laureate.

## Paracetamol and Drug Metabolism

In the late 1940s Axelrod worked with Brodie on why acetanilide, a common analgesic, was associated with methemoglobinemia. In a paper published in 1948, they showed that acetanilide is converted in the body into paracetamol (acetaminophen), and that this metabolite accounts for the analgesic effect with lower toxicity, which paved the way for its use as a drug in its own right. He then studied the microsomal liver enzymes at the National Institutes of Health that oxidize and transform drugs, thereby establishing the field of drug metabolism on which the study of drug interactions and individual differences in response rests.

## Reuptake and Termination of Signaling

In 1957 Axelrod identified the enzyme catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT), which inactivates norepinephrine and epinephrine. The prevailing belief at the time was that enzymes alone terminate the action of a transmitter. Then, in 1961, he and Hertting showed in the journal *Nature*, using radiolabeled norepinephrine, that nerve endings reabsorb most of the released transmitter rather than letting it all be broken down, and that this reuptake is the main route for terminating the signal. In 1964, with Glowinski, he showed that the antidepressant imipramine blocks this uptake, which provided early direct evidence that some antidepressants prolong the presence of the transmitter in the synapse. This led to the monoamine hypothesis of depression and to the development of reuptake inhibitors, including the later selective class, which connects his work to the research of Carlsson and Snyder.

## The Pineal Gland and Melatonin

In 1960 Axelrod, with Weissbach, identified the enzyme that completes the synthesis of melatonin in the pineal gland. He then showed with Wurtman and others that the gland's activity is influenced by environmental light, which helped establish that it translates a light signal into a hormonal signal. This paved the way for the study of the daily rhythm and of sleep and mood disorders, a line connected to research on social rhythms in depression.

## Mentor and School Builder

Axelrod was known as a sparing speaker who generously credited his students. At the laboratory of the National Institutes of Health he trained a number of the leading neuroscientists, among them Solomon Snyder, who trained with him in the early 1960s and later became a leader in the study of opiate and dopamine receptors, and Richard Wurtman, with Irwin Kopin among his close collaborators. He continued to work at the National Institutes of Health into his late years.

## Impact

Axelrod's work transformed psychiatric drugs from agents discovered by clinical chance into tools whose molecular mechanisms could be understood. Through the concept of reuptake, psychiatric pharmacology came to design drugs according to specific neurotransmission targets, and research moved from knowing that a drug works to knowing how it works.

## Limits and Critique

Axelrod's work was characterized by biochemical experimentation more than by clinical method, and his research does not offer an explanation of psychiatric disorders; it identifies the mechanisms by which drugs act. Moreover, the monoamine hypothesis that drew on his work does not explain why antidepressants take weeks to show an effect when reuptake blockade is immediate, which led researchers to look for later changes in receptors and neural plasticity. Paracetamol itself had been synthesized in the nineteenth century; the contribution lay in clarifying its metabolic and pharmacological role. This entry is educational and not clinical advice.
