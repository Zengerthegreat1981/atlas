# Robert Fogel

Robert William Fogel (1926–2013) was an American economic historian and professor at the University of Chicago. A principal founder of **Cliometrics** (the systematic application of econometric models and quantitative methods to historical study), Fogel was awarded the 1993 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences jointly with Douglass C. North for "renewing research in economic history by applying economic theory and quantitative methods in order to explain economic and institutional change."

## Academic Career and Methodological Innovations

Born in New York City to Russian-Jewish immigrant parents, Fogel studied economics and history at Cornell and Columbia before earning his doctorate at Johns Hopkins University in 1963 under Simon Kuznets. He held professorial appointments at the University of Rochester, the University of Chicago (1964–1975, 1981–2013), and Harvard University (1975–1981).

Cliometrics represented a significant shift away from traditional narrative and institutional economic history. Fogel insisted that historical hypotheses must be stated in explicit mathematical and statistical models, tested against large-scale archival datasets, and evaluated through rigorous counterfactual modeling.

## Major Research and Historiographical Debates

### *Railroads and American Economic Growth* (1964)
In his first major monograph, Fogel attacked the "axiom of indispensability"—the long-standing historiographical consensus that nineteenth-century American industrialization and economic growth were almost entirely driven by the invention and expansion of the railroad. 

To test this assumption, Fogel constructed an elaborate counterfactual econometric model estimating what the American economy would have looked like in 1890 had the railroads never been built. Calculating the concept of "social saving" (the difference between the actual transportation costs of the railroad network and the hypothetical costs using alternative transport, such as canals, navigable rivers, and improved wagon roads), Fogel concluded that the aggregate social saving in 1890 was under 5 percent of Gross National Product. He argued that nineteenth-century economic growth was the cumulative result of numerous interrelated technological adaptations rather than any single indispensable innovation.

### The Slavery Controversy: *Time on the Cross* and *Without Consent or Contract*
In 1974, Fogel and Stanley L. Engerman published *Time on the Cross: The Economics of American Negro Slavery*. Utilizing computerized plantation records, census schedules, and slave market invoices, the authors argued that antebellum southern slave agriculture was commercially efficient, profitable, and technologically viable, rather than a decaying, backward economic institution destined for imminent peaceful collapse.

The book provoked intense methodological, sociological, and moral criticism. Leading historians and economists—including Paul A. David, Herbert Gutman, Richard Sutch, Peter Temin, and Gavin Wright in *Reckoning with Slavery* (1976)—argued that Fogel and Engerman had relied on biased plantation accounting records, imposed flawed neoclassical optimization assumptions onto coerced labor, and inadvertently sanitized the terror and human cruelty inherent in chattel bondage.

In response, Fogel dedicated fifteen years to comprehensive re-investigation, culminating in *Without Consent or Contract: The Rise and Fall of American Slavery* (1989). In this work, Fogel reaffirmed the economic viability of the plantation complex while drawing a sharp, explicit boundary between economic efficiency and moral legitimacy. He condemned slavery as a brutal system of exploitation, arguing that its demise did not result from economic exhaustion, but required the mobilization of moral and political movements that culminated in the Civil War.

### Technophysio Evolution and Biodemography
In his final decades at the University of Chicago's Center for Population Economics, Fogel was a leading contributor to historical biodemography and nutritional economics. In *The Escape from Hunger and Premature Death, 1700–2100* (2004), he introduced the theory of "technophysio evolution," tracking changes in human body size, caloric intake, and biological efficiency over three centuries to explain the dramatic reduction in chronic morbidity and the modern expansion of human life expectancy.
