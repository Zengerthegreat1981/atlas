# Piero Sraffa

**Piero Sraffa** was an Italian economist who spent most of his academic career at the University of Cambridge. He published little, yet his reputation is large: it rests mainly on a critical edition of the works of David Ricardo and on a short book that reopened debate on value and distribution.

## From Fascist Italy to Cambridge

Sraffa left Italy in 1927, under pressure from the Fascist regime, and settled at the University of Cambridge at the invitation of John Maynard Keynes, who had been impressed by Sraffa's 1926 article in the *Economic Journal*, "The Laws of Returns under Competitive Conditions", which criticised the Marshallian theory of competitive supply. Sraffa remained a friend and supporter of the Italian Marxist thinker Antonio Gramsci, whom he helped materially during Gramsci's imprisonment, and he was involved in efforts to safeguard Gramsci's prison writings.

## Editing the complete works of Ricardo

Sraffa devoted decades of his academic work to editing and publishing *The Works and Correspondence of David Ricardo* (eleven volumes, 1951–1973, with the collaboration of Maurice Dobb), a meticulous scholarly project that reconstructed the development of Ricardo's ideas from his drafts and personal correspondence. Sraffa's introduction to the edition offered an influential interpretation of Ricardo's theory of profits. His work drew attention to a problem Ricardo had not fully solved: finding an invariable measure of value that is not affected by changes in the distribution of income itself.

## Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities: a return to the classics

In 1960, after decades of silent reflection, Sraffa published his short book *Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities: Prelude to a Critique of Economic Theory*, in which he reformulated the problem of price determination and income distribution in a rigorous mathematical manner, entirely independent of the neoclassical concepts of marginal utility and marginal productivity. Sraffa showed that relative prices can be fully determined starting from the actual production technique (the quantities of commodities needed to produce other commodities) together with a given wage share, without any assumption about subjective utility or an aggregate marginal productivity function. To handle the problem of an invariable measure, he introduced the "standard commodity", a composite commodity whose proportions make the relation between the wage share and the rate of profit linear.

## The Cambridge capital controversy: the decisive spark

Sraffa's book provided a technical foundation for the **Cambridge capital controversy**, which Joan Robinson had opened in 1953. His analysis implied that measuring "capital" as a single homogeneous quantity, independent of the rate of profit, runs into circularity, because relative prices depend on distribution. Later work in that framework, summed up by Samuelson in 1966, established "reswitching": a technique can be the most profitable at more than one, non-adjacent, level of the rate of profit. That result conflicts with a simple neoclassical story in which a lower interest rate always goes with more capital-intensive methods.

## A work of limited circulation but deep influence

Although Sraffa's main book is small in size, written in an extremely concise mathematical style, and difficult to read for non-specialists, its influence spread deeply in shaping the "Sraffian" branch of post-Keynesian economics. This branch continues to develop a theory of prices and distribution based on the actual production technique rather than neoclassical marginal foundations, and influenced later economists such as Pierangelo Garegnani and Luigi Pasinetti in Italy.

## A reserved personality and a rare method of work

Sraffa was known for a relatively isolated personality and extreme caution in publishing his ideas. He spent decades revising drafts of his short book before finally publishing it, a pattern of work that is rare in contemporary academia, which usually presses for faster and more prolific publication. This extreme caution made every word in his few published texts the object of close scrutiny and intensive interpretation by later researchers, who have tried to extract more of his unpublished ideas from his personal papers.

## An unpublished archive

At his death in 1983, Sraffa left a large archive of unpublished manuscripts and notes, held in the Wren Library of Trinity College, Cambridge, where he had been a fellow. Specialists in the history of economic thought have worked on these papers to understand the development of his ideas in more detail than his single concise published text allows. Sraffa was also a friend of the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein at Cambridge, who acknowledged Sraffa's influence on his later thought.
