# Augustine of Hippo

A philosopher and theologian from North Africa (Hippo Regius, present-day Annaba, Algeria), bishop of Hippo, a major thinker of late antiquity and author of the *Confessions*.

## What he gave

He is often credited with a decisive turn toward inner subjectivity and the psychology of time in Western thought. In the *Confessions* he offered an influential analysis of lived time (the past as memory, the present as attention, and the future as expectation), and analyzed existential anxiety, inner longing, guilt, and the inner division of the human will.

## His place in the movement

Often cited as a precursor of religious existentialism; his analyses of anxiety and time influenced Kierkegaard, Heidegger (who taught the phenomenology of Augustine in 1921), and Karl Jaspers.

## Limitation

His framing of all existential experiences within the Christian theological system of sin and divine grace.

## Main works

Confessions (Confessiones, 397–400 CE) · The City of God (De Civitate Dei, 413–426 CE) · On the Trinity (De Trinitate, about 400–420 CE).

## Selected quotations

"Late have I loved you, O Beauty ever ancient and ever new, late have I loved you." — Confessions, about 400 CE

## Biography and formation

Aurelius Augustinus was born on 13 November 354 CE in Thagaste (Souk Ahras in present-day Algeria) to a pagan father, Patricius, and a devout Christian mother, Monica. He studied grammar at Madauros and then rhetoric at Carthage, where he was influenced by Cicero's *Hortensius*, which awakened in him the love of wisdom about 373 CE. He then joined the Manichaeans for about nine years; they had promised him a rational explanation of the problem of evil, and he abandoned them after his disappointment in one of their scholars (Faustus of Milevis). He worked as a professor of rhetoric in Carthage, Rome and then Milan (384 CE), where he listened to the sermons of Bishop Ambrose and read Platonic books (by Plotinus and perhaps Porphyry) through the Latin translation of Marius Victorinus, so that his idea of the soul and of God was freed from a material conception. In the summer of 386 CE, in a garden in Milan, occurred the experience he recounts in Book 8 of the *Confessions*, when he heard a voice saying "Take up and read", and was led to Paul's Letter to the Romans; he was baptized at Easter 387 CE by Ambrose.

He then returned to Africa and lived a communal ascetic life in Thagaste, was ordained a priest at Hippo (Annaba) in 391 CE, and became bishop in 395 or 396 CE, remaining so until his death on 28 August 430 CE during the Vandals' siege of his city.

## Time, memory and will

In Book 11 of the *Confessions* Augustine posed his famous question: What is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it to one who asks, I no longer know. He concluded that the past and the future exist only in the soul: the past in memory, the present in attention, and the future in expectation, and that time is an extension of the soul (distentio animi). In Book 10 he made memory a vast storehouse in which the soul keeps images and knowledge and even God. As for the will, in his view it is the site of conflict: a person knows the good and does not do it, because his will is divided, which he analyzed in Book 8 as an inner sickness that only radical conversion can heal.

On the problem of evil he offered the thesis that "evil is a privation, not a substance": God created everything that exists and all of it is good, and evil is a deficiency of good and a deviation of the will, not an independent being as the Manichaeans said. He discussed this in *On Free Choice of the Will* (388–395 CE). He also set out an argument close to the cogito: "If I err, I am" (City of God, 11: 26).

## Grace and the two cities

In his struggle with Pelagius and his followers after 411 CE, he held that human nature was corrupted by Adam's sin, and that salvation is a free gift of grace and not the fruit of human effort. This position deeply influenced Catholicism and the theology of the Reformation that arose after him in the sixteenth century. After the sack of Rome by the Goths in 410 CE he wrote *The City of God* (413–426 CE) to answer those who accused Christianity of causing Rome's fall, and conceived history as a struggle between two cities distinguished by what their inhabitants love: the city of earth, ruled by love of self, and the city of God, ruled by love of God. He did not identify either completely with the state, nor the other with the Church.

In *On the Trinity* (about 400–420 CE) he sought an analogy for the Trinity in the human soul: memory, understanding and will, or the knower, the known and love.

## Influence and reception

Augustine was a reference point for all medieval Latin thought, from Anselm and Bonaventure to Thomas Aquinas. The Reformers, Luther and Calvin, re-read his teaching on grace. In the modern age he was read by Descartes, Pascal and Kierkegaard, then by Heidegger, who gave lectures on him in 1921; Hannah Arendt wrote her dissertation on the concept of love in him (1929), and Wittgenstein opened the *Philosophical Investigations* with a quotation from the *Confessions* on the learning of language.

## Critique and Limitations

Pelagius and his successors, among them Julian of Eclanum, criticized the doctrine of original sin because it makes a person responsible for what he did not do. Many modern scholars hold that his position toward the Donatists, when he accepted the use of official force against them, conflicts with the spirit of his discourse on love. Feminist and analytic readings criticize his position on the body and sex. His theory of time also remains psychological and does not explain physical time, which physics and philosophy after him took up.
