# David Bloor

A British sociologist, founder of the "Strong Programme" in the sociology of scientific knowledge, which demanded that the content of scientific theories themselves, even the correct ones, be studied as a social product.

## The Central Idea

In *Knowledge and Social Imagery* (1976), Bloor put forward the "Strong Programme" in the sociology of scientific knowledge, based on four methodological tenets. **Causality**: explanations should identify the (social and other) conditions that bring about beliefs. **Impartiality**: the approach should be impartial with respect to truth and falsity, rationality and irrationality, success and failure, since both sides of these dichotomies call for explanation. **Symmetry**: the same types of cause should explain true and false beliefs alike. **Reflexivity**: the explanations should in principle apply to sociology itself. The programme goes beyond the earlier position associated with Robert Merton, who studied the institutional norms of science as a social system but left the content of accepted scientific knowledge to philosophical or empirical explanation, and beyond Karl Mannheim, who exempted the natural sciences and mathematics from his sociology of knowledge. The Strong Programme holds that scientific knowledge itself, and not only error, is open to sociological explanation.

## Major Works

Besides *Knowledge and Social Imagery*, Bloor wrote *Wittgenstein: A Social Theory of Knowledge* (1983), *Wittgenstein, Rules and Institutions* (1997) and *Scientific Knowledge: A Sociological Analysis* (1996, with Barry Barnes and John Henry), a synthesis of the sociology of scientific knowledge that sets out the case for the "finitist" and interest-based approach to knowledge. In his 1976 book Bloor also discussed mathematics, drawing on Imre Lakatos's *Proofs and Refutations* and the case of Martin Ohm's views on mathematical rigour, to argue that even mathematical knowledge could be treated sociologically.

## Impact and Criticism

The Edinburgh programme was one of the founding contributions to the sociology of scientific knowledge and influenced Science and Technology Studies, history and philosophy of science. It also provoked sharp controversy over alleged relativism, including in the "Science Wars" of the 1990s. Critics such as Larry Laudan and the philosopher David Stove argued that the symmetry principle undermines any normative basis for distinguishing sound knowledge from superstition. Bloor defended the programme as a method of causal explanation rather than a denial of scientific truth.

## Historical Context

The Strong Programme took shape in the Science Studies Unit at the University of Edinburgh in the 1970s, where Bloor worked with Barry Barnes and others (the "Edinburgh School"). It followed Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962), which opened the way to studying science historically and socially, although Kuhn later criticized the programme's more radical conclusions.

## Difference from Merton

Merton was interested in science as an institution (norms, incentives, reward systems) and not in the content of accepted scientific knowledge. Bloor argued that the content of knowledge, and not merely the institutional setting around it, should be subjected to the same kind of causal analysis.
