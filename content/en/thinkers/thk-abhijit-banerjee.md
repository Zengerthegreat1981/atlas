# Abhijit Banerjee

**Abhijit Banerjee** is an Indian-American economist, professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and co-recipient of the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2019, shared with Esther Duflo and Michael Kremer, for "their experimental approach to alleviating global poverty." He is among the leading pioneers of the use of rigorously controlled randomised field experiments in development economics.

## From India to MIT

Born in Bombay (Mumbai) in 1961, Banerjee grew up in Calcutta, studied economics at the University of Calcutta and Jawaharlal Nehru University, and received his PhD from Harvard University in 1988. He then settled as a professor at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where he co-founded with Esther Duflo and Sendhil Mullainathan the Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab (J-PAL) in 2003. J-PAL became one of the largest global platforms for designing and conducting randomised trials of development policies across dozens of countries.

## From the Grand Debate to Partial Testing

Banerjee explicitly criticised the traditional pattern of the development economics debate. For decades it focused on a grand theoretical argument about "what causes national poverty as a whole" (lack of institutions? historical colonialism? lack of capital?). He considered this grand debate difficult to settle empirically with sufficient precision. Instead he proposed a radically different approach: breaking the problem of poverty down into specific, testable questions. Examples include whether extra teaching resources or remedial education improve learning outcomes, and whether small incentives raise immunisation rates. Each question is tested through a randomised controlled field experiment borrowed from the methodology of clinical medical trials.

## The Randomised Controlled Trial Methodology

This methodology divides a population sample randomly into a group that receives the policy intervention under study (such as additional remedial teaching) and a control group that does not. It then measures the statistical difference in outcomes between them. This allows a more precise causal inference than traditional observational studies, which may confuse causation with apparent correlation. Once adopted, the methodology brought a wide transformation in how development economics research is conducted and in how the effectiveness of actual aid policies is evaluated.

## *Poor Economics*

Banerjee and Esther Duflo summarised the results of a decade of these experiments in their joint book *Poor Economics* (2011). They argued that many of the poor's economic decisions that appear "irrational" on the surface, such as low take-up of free immunisation or spending on festivals rather than saving, in fact reflect rational responses to real constraints. These include a lack of trust in the health system, or social needs necessary for survival within a local support network. They contrasted this with earlier analyses that treated such decisions as mere ignorance or cognitive shortcoming.

## A Critique of the Methodology

The randomised trial methodology has faced criticism from other economists, including William Easterly. They question its ability to generalise results from a narrow local context (a particular village or region) to comprehensive national policies. They argue that the success of a small intervention in a particular context does not guarantee the success of a comprehensive national policy built on it. This methodological debate remains present in the field, despite broad recognition of the methodology's value in evaluating specific programmes.

## Influence on Public Policy Design

Banerjee's research, and that of his colleagues at J-PAL, which also runs trials in health and education policy in rich countries, influenced the design of actual development programmes for many governments and international organisations. Proving the effectiveness of a programme through a randomised trial became an increasingly important condition for obtaining funding from many donor agencies. This raised the standards of experimental accountability in the field considerably compared with earlier practice.

## A Long Intellectual Partnership with Duflo

The long research partnership between Banerjee and Esther Duflo, which also developed into a personal marriage, was a central pillar in developing this experimental direction within development economics. Over two decades they published together dozens of research papers and joint books, including the book *Good Economics for Hard Times* (2019). They were awarded the 2019 prize jointly, together with Michael Kremer.

## A Debate on the Limits of Cross-Cultural Generalisation

A question is sometimes raised about the extent to which the conclusions of randomised trials conducted in a particular cultural and social context (villages in India or Kenya, for example) can apply to radically different contexts such as the Arab region. In the Arab region, family, religious and political structures differ in ways that may change the population's response to the same incentives. Replicating an experiment in multiple contexts before generalising a conclusion is a standard response to this concern, and J-PAL has promoted it.
