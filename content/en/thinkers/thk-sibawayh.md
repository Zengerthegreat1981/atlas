# Sibawayh (Amr ibn Uthman ibn Qanbar)

Sibawayh (Amr ibn Uthman ibn Qanbar) was a grammarian of Persian origin. In Basra he studied under Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi and Yunus ibn Habib. He wrote *al-Kitab*, the first comprehensive and systematic Arabic grammatical treatise, and the most extensive description of Classical Arabic in his era.

## Method

Sibawayh adopted an inductive method. He gathered evidence from the Quran and from the speech of eloquent Arabs (poetry and prose), then derived rules from it. He distinguished between **well-formed speech** (conforming to analogy), **well-formed and fine speech**, and **absurd speech (*muḥāl*)**, along with other degrees of grammatical acceptability. This was an early distinction between grammatical correctness and acceptability of usage. It is sometimes compared with modern distinctions such as Chomsky's competence and performance, but Sibawayh did not aim at a general theory of human language, only at describing Arabic, so a literal parallel between the two projects should not be drawn.

## Historical Context

Sibawayh emerged in the middle of the second century AH, in Basra, which was then the most important center of Arabic language sciences alongside Kufa. This was a period when Arabs mixed with non-Arabs after the Islamic conquests, and there arose a need to codify the rules of the Arabic language to preserve the accuracy of Quranic recitation and understanding, and to protect eloquence from the solecisms (*lahn*) spreading among the muwallads (non-Arab-born Muslims who spoke Arabic). Sibawayh, of Persian origin and from al-Bayda in Fars, studied under Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi, the founder of the science of prosody and author of the lexicon *al-Ayn*. He also learned from Yunus ibn Habib, Isa ibn Umar and Abu al-Khattab al-Akhfash al-Akbar. He gathered from all of them a large body of grammatical material and organized it into a single work.

## Key Ideas and Approach

*al-Kitab* offers a precise description of Arabic constructions through a rigorous classification of grammatical chapters, beginning with the nominative, accusative and genitive cases and ending with morphological and phonological chapters. Sibawayh relied on Quranic, poetic and proverbial evidence from the speech of eloquent Bedouins. He made precise distinctions between degrees of grammatical acceptability, as noted above. He also recorded the dialectal differences between the speech of various tribes (such as the dialect of Tamim and that of the people of the Hijaz) without decisively preferring one over the others. This reveals an early descriptive sensibility which, by the standards of his time, parallels the modern scientific approach to linguistic variation. He also used, and helped fix, many grammatical terms that remained in use, such as *al-fa'il* (subject), *al-maf'ul bihi* (direct object), *al-hal* (circumstantial accusative) and *al-tamyiz* (specification), and he laid the foundation for the theory of the grammatical operator (*'amil*), which dominated Arabic grammar for centuries.

## Legacy

*al-Kitab* became the basic reference for every subsequent Arabic grammar. Ibn Jinni and other grammarians studied it for centuries afterward, and many scholars commented on it, including al-Mubarrad, al-Rummani and al-Sirafi. Many later authors of rhetoric and morphology relied on it. Its grammatical terminology and inductive method became the foundation of the Basra and Kufa schools, which competed for primacy in Arabic grammar for centuries. Modern Western scholars studied it extensively, and some have compared it with Panini's Sanskrit grammar as two of the great descriptive grammars of antiquity and the medieval period, although each has its own context and independent method. *al-Kitab* remains the subject of modern philological studies that seek to understand its internal structure and terminology in light of the history of general linguistics.

## His Death and the Debate over His Biography

Accounts differ on the year of Sibawayh's death and its cause. Sources give dates ranging from the 170s to the early 190s AH; 180 AH / 796 CE is the commonly cited figure, and he is said to have died young after returning to Fars. Narratives are also related about a debate between him and al-Kisai at the court of Caliph Harun al-Rashid, known as the "Zunburiyya question." According to the well-known account, Sibawayh lost the debate because the audience favored al-Kisai, after which he returned to Persia dejected and died shortly afterward. Modern scholars have questioned the details of this account, but it is a standard part of his biography in classical sources, such as the *Tabaqat al-Nahwiyyin wa-al-Lughawiyyin* of al-Zubaydi and the *Wafayat al-A'yan* of Ibn Khallikan.

## Relation to the Islamic Arabic Heritage

Sibawayh represents the peak of the early Islamic Arabic grammatical tradition. His work arose directly from the religious need to fix the recitation of the Quran and preserve Arabic eloquence as the Islamic state expanded and Arabs mixed with other peoples. It was closely tied to the movement of Quranic readings (*qira'at*) and to the sciences of Quranic exegesis, which benefited from his codification of inflection (*i'rab*) and meanings. *al-Kitab* remained present in religious and grammatical curricula across the Islamic world for centuries, and it remains a foundational source studied in traditional and academic Arabic grammar.
