# James Hillman

An American psychoanalyst and philosopher (1926–2011), founder of "Archetypal Psychology" as a branch split off from classical analytical psychology, who served as **Director of Studies** at the Jung Institute in Zurich (1959–1969). He was born in Atlantic City and received his doctorate from the University of Zurich in 1959; after Zurich he was editor of the journal *Spring* and then a professor and co-founder of the Dallas Institute of Humanities and Culture. He died in Connecticut.

## Hillman and Corbin

Some of Hillman's concepts draw on Islamic philosophy through Henry Corbin, a reader of Suhrawardi and Ibn Arabi: the "imaginal world" (mundus imaginalis), with which Hillman broadened the concept of imagination as an objective world and not a mere subjective illusion.

## What He Gave

In "Re-Visioning Psychology" (1975) he redefined analytical psychology: the move from "individuation of the ego" to "soul-making" (borrowing the phrase from John Keats), and from the "unity of the psyche" to **"polytheistic psychology"** (Polytheistic Psychology): that the psyche is not one thing striving for integration but is inhabited by a throng of quarreling archetypal figures — Zeus, Aphrodite, Hermes, and Hades — and that the demand for "unification" under a single ego is itself the source of illness, not its cure. As he formulated his definition of soul in the preface (1975, p. x):

> "By soul I mean, first of all, a perspective rather than a substance, a viewpoint towards things rather than a thing itself."

He also formulated "the Acorn Theory" in "The Soul's Code" (1996) — that every person is born with an individual daimon that calls forth the course of his life, so that whoever tries to fix himself against it is fighting his own fate.

Among his other methodological concepts is "staying with the image": the analyst does not interpret the dream by reducing it to a meaning outside it, but stays with its image and deepens into it. In "The Dream and the Underworld" (1979) he read dreams as journeys to the world of Hades and not as messages about waking life. In "Suicide and the Soul" (1964) he held that suicide carries a psychological desire for transformation that the therapist can read, rather than merely preventing it. He then widened his interest to the city and the world: "We've Had a Hundred Years of Psychotherapy — And the World's Getting Worse" (1992) is the thesis that psychotherapy confined the psyche inside the individual and neglected the "world soul" (anima mundi), so that his project in its last phase became closer to an environmental-urban psychology.

## His Place in the Current

The "deepest split" within analytical psychology: he himself, from the official Zurich school, moved from rediscovering it to transforming it. Andrew Samuels, in his book *Jung and the Post-Jungians* (1985), classified later Jungians into three schools: the classical, the developmental (Fordham), and the archetypal (Hillman). He stepped down as Director of Studies at the Zurich institute in 1969 and moved to the United States. His life is documented in the first volume of Dick Russell's biography *The Life and Ideas of James Hillman: Volume I, The Making of a Psychologist* (2013), published after his death.

## The Spring Circle and Collaborators

During his tenure as editor of the journal *Spring: An Annual of Archetypal Psychology and Jungian Thought* (transferred from Zurich to Dallas, and later developing into Spring Publications), Hillman worked with an influential circle of scholars who extended archetypal psychology across myth, literature, and the clinic. Foremost among them was Venezuelan analyst **Rafael López-Pedraza** (1920–2006), whose *Hermes and His Children* (1977) and *Dionysus in Exile* (2000) brought mythological archetypes directly into cultural psychology; classicist and poet **Charles Boer** (1936–2021), who translated the *Homeric Hymns* and collaborated closely with Hillman on mythological and literary essays; analyst **Patricia Berry**, whose *Echo's Subtle Body* (1982) contributed foundational archetypal and aesthetic perspectives; and philosopher of religion **David L. Miller**, author of *The New Polytheism* (1974). Together, this circle developed a distinct post-Jungian discourse that privileged poetic metaphor, aesthetic sensibility, and mythological complexity over diagnostic categorisation.

## Limits and Criticism

He was accused of weakening the clinical dimension of analytical psychology in favor of "beautiful philosophy," along with his own reciprocal accusation that academic analytical psychology had lost its relationship to the soul. From another angle, the "Acorn Theory" assumes a pre-existing daimon that directs life, and this is a framework that can always be interpreted retroactively and cannot be tested, in which it is hard to distinguish a real "calling" from a mere story a person tells himself about his past. Also, the "polytheistic" model uses Greek myth as a metaphor, but does not make clear how this metaphor is carried over into treating a patient who is actually suffering. His ideas have not been tested in empirical studies, so his value lies in the cultural and philosophical horizon and not in clinical evidence.

## His Most Important Works
