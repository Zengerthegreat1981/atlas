# Joan Bybee

An American linguist and Distinguished Professor Emerita of Linguistics at the University of New Mexico. She is a central figure in **usage-based linguistics**, which holds that phonological, morphological and syntactic structure is not supplied ready-made by an innate universal grammar but is built up from repeated experience of language. She was president of the Linguistic Society of America in 2004.

## Contributions

### Usage-Based Grammar

In her pivotal work *The Evolution of Grammar: Tense, Aspect, and Modality in the Languages of the World* (1994, with William Pagliuca and Revere Perkins), Bybee showed, by comparing a wide sample of world languages, that grammatical tense, aspect, and modality markers (such as future and perfect markers) historically arise from concrete lexical words (such as verbs of volition or motion) through the process of **grammaticalization**: the gradual erosion of concrete meaning (semantic bleaching) alongside growing functional abstraction. This process recurs in similar patterns across languages with no historical relation to one another.

### The Effect of Frequency

Bybee developed a usage-based model that links the **frequency** with which a word or construction is used to its degree of mental entrenchment: highly frequent words (such as highly used irregular verbs) resist standard regularization and are stored as whole units in memory, while rare words are built each time by the general morphological rule. She presented this framework in detail in *Phonology and Language Use* (2001), showing that frequency also affects sound erosion (such as the reduction of highly frequent phrases).

### Dismantling the Competence–Performance Distinction

Unlike generative linguistics (Chomsky), which separates an abstract rule system (competence) from marginal actual use (performance), Bybee holds that the mental representation of language itself is **built from accumulated usage patterns** (exemplars), not from abstract rules prior to use. She elaborated this position in *Language, Usage and Cognition* (2010).

## Impact

- Her work is a main reference for usage-based alternatives to generative grammar.
- Her comparative project on grammaticalization (together with Perkins and Pagliuca) has become a standard reference in **comparative historical linguistics** and linguistic typology.
- She influenced research on language acquisition and psycholinguistics by emphasizing the role of repetition and frequency in learning.

## Limitations

- Generative linguists (including followers of Chomsky) have criticized the excessive reliance on frequency evidence as insufficient to explain linguistic creativity and speakers' ability to produce entirely new sentences never heard before.
- Some of the grammaticalization generalizations she proposed have been criticized as statistical tendencies rather than inevitable laws, since languages exist that deviate from the proposed typical paths.
