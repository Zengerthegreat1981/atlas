# Howard S. Becker

Howard Saul Becker (born 1928) is an American sociologist associated with the second Chicago school, whose studies of deviance, occupations and the arts shaped labelling theory and the sociology of culture.

## Life and Context

Becker was born in Chicago and trained there, receiving his doctorate in sociology in 1951. He worked as a professional jazz pianist while studying, an experience that informed his research on musicians. He taught at Northwestern University and later at the University of Washington. He was influenced by Everett C. Hughes, Herbert Blumer and the interactionist tradition.

## Main Ideas

- **Labelling and deviance.** In *Outsiders* (1963), Becker argued that deviance is not a quality of an act but arises from the application of rules and labels by social groups. The deviant is someone to whom the label has been successfully applied.
- **Moral entrepreneurs.** He drew attention to the people who create and enforce rules, such as in the campaign against marijuana.
- **Deviant careers.** In the article "Becoming a Marihuana User" (1953), he showed that users have to learn techniques, perceive effects and define them as pleasurable.
- **Art worlds.** In *Art Worlds* (1982), he treated art as collective activity involving many cooperating participants, not just the individual genius.
- **Whose side are we on?** In a 1967 article, he argued that researchers inevitably take sides, so they should be open about it.

## Case Studies

*Boys in White* followed students at the University of Kansas medical school. In *Outsiders*, Becker drew on his own fieldwork on dance musicians and on interviews with marihuana users. He also studied Chicago public-school teachers, work that fed his analysis of occupations and careers.

## Method

Becker championed participant observation, interviewing and analytic care in qualitative research, and wrote practical guides on writing and on thinking about method. He was open to quantitative data where appropriate.

## Influence and Criticism

Labelling theory had a lasting effect on criminology, the sociology of medicine and cultural sociology. Critics argued that the approach neglects the causes of initial rule-breaking and understates the harm of some deviance. Alvin Gouldner's "The Sociologist as Partisan" (1968) criticised the sympathy toward underdogs. Becker's accessible, craft-oriented style remains widely taught.
