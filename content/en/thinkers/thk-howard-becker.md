# Howard S. Becker

Howard Saul Becker (1928-2023) was an American sociologist associated with the second Chicago school, whose studies of deviance, occupations and the arts shaped labelling theory and the sociology of culture.

## Life and Context

Becker was born in Chicago and trained there, receiving his doctorate in sociology in 1951. He worked as a professional jazz pianist while studying, an experience that informed his research on musicians. He taught at Northwestern University and later at the University of Washington. He was influenced by Everett C. Hughes, Herbert Blumer and the interactionist tradition.

## Main Ideas

- **Labelling and deviance.** In *Outsiders* (1963), Becker argued that deviance is not a quality of an act but arises from the application of rules and labels by social groups. The deviant is someone to whom the label has been successfully applied.
- **Moral entrepreneurs.** He drew attention to the people who create and enforce rules, such as in the campaign against marijuana.
- **Deviant careers.** In the article "Becoming a Marihuana User" (1953), he showed that users have to learn techniques, perceive effects and define them as pleasurable.
- **Art worlds.** In *Art Worlds* (1982), he treated art as collective activity involving many cooperating participants, not just the individual genius.
- **Whose side are we on?** In a 1967 article, he argued that researchers inevitably take sides, so they should be open about it.

## Case Studies

*Boys in White* followed students at the University of Kansas medical school. In *Outsiders*, Becker drew on his own fieldwork on dance musicians and on interviews with marihuana users. He also studied Chicago public-school teachers, work that fed his analysis of occupations and careers.

## Method

Becker championed participant observation, interviewing and analytic care in qualitative research, and wrote practical guides on writing and on thinking about method. He was open to quantitative data where appropriate.

## Related Concepts

Becker's work connects with a family of interactionist concepts. Labelling theory is closely related to Edwin Lemert's distinction between primary deviance, the initial rule-breaking, and secondary deviance, which develops as a person responds to social reaction. Becker's idea of a master status, borrowed from Everett Hughes, describes how a deviant label can override a person's other identities, and it can be compared with Erving Goffman's account of stigma. Deviant careers apply the concept of career, developed in the study of occupations, to a sequence of changing positions and self-understandings. Moral entrepreneurs and moral crusades lead to the concept of moral panic, associated with Stanley Cohen, and to the social construction of social problems. Social norms and social control form the broader background, while *Art Worlds* introduces conventions as the shared understandings that let many people cooperate. This invites comparison with Pierre Bourdieu's concept of field, which gives greater weight to competition and power.

## Influence and Criticism

Labelling theory had a lasting effect on criminology, the sociology of medicine and cultural sociology. Critics argued that the approach neglects the causes of initial rule-breaking and understates the harm of some deviance. Alvin Gouldner's "The Sociologist as Partisan" (1968) criticised the sympathy toward underdogs. Becker's accessible, craft-oriented style remains widely taught. He died in 2023.

## Collective Activity, Credibility, and Later Career

Becker often framed the question as how people do things together: institutions are ongoing coordinated activity, held together by shared conventions. In "Whose Side Are We On?" (1967) he named a hierarchy of credibility: people in superordinate positions are commonly granted the right to define what is going on, so a study that takes subordinates seriously is apt to be charged with bias. His work on dance musicians showed an occupational group marking itself off from the audiences it played for. *Boys in White* described medical students at the University of Kansas collectively deciding what to study and how to meet the demands of the faculty. He treated the argument of *Outsiders* as an account of how rules and labels are applied, and he did not present it as a complete explanation of why people break rules in the first place. *Evidence* (2017) examines how data become evidence and the characteristic errors of both quantitative and qualitative work. He taught at Northwestern University from 1965 to 1991 and at the University of Washington from 1991 until he retired in 1999. In later years he spent part of each year in Paris, where his books had a large readership, and he died in San Francisco on 16 August 2023.
