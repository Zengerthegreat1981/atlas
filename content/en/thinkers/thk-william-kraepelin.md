# Emil Kraepelin

A German psychiatrist (15 February 1856 – 7 October 1926) whose textbook supplied the classification of endogenous psychoses that later psychiatry treated as a reference point. His system rested on long-term clinical observation of course and outcome, not only on a cross-section of symptoms. He received his medical degree from the University of Würzburg in 1878 and, in Leipzig, worked with the experimental psychologist **Wilhelm Wundt**. He was professor at the University of Dorpat (now Tartu) from 1886 to 1891, then at Heidelberg, and from 1903 at Munich. In 1917, with support that included a gift from James Loeb, he founded the German Research Institute for Psychiatry in Munich, a forerunner of the Max Planck Institute of Psychiatry.

## Arab Roots of Classification

Kraepelin did not invent the clinical pictures of mania and melancholia. Greek medicine, and Arabic compilers after it, already described both. Al-Razi's *al-Hawi* and Ibn Sina's *al-Qanun* transmit accounts of melancholia, mania and other disorders of mind within humoral theory. Ishaq ibn Imran, active in Kairouan around 900, wrote a treatise on melancholia. Wards for people judged insane existed in the bimaristans of Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo, as Michael Dols documented. Those texts do not contain Kraepelin's disease entities. What he added was a grouping of cases by course and outcome, rather than by humor or by the symptom of the moment.

## Formation and Career

Kraepelin was born in Neustrelitz in 1856. In Leipzig, Wundt's laboratory encouraged him to measure psychological performance. He published studies of alcohol, drugs and fatigue, including *Über die Beeinflussung einfacher psychischer Vorgänge durch einige Arzneimittel* (1892). In the clinic he kept diagnostic cards so that histories could be followed over years rather than at one admission; Weber and Engstrom later analyzed those cards. He took the Heidelberg chair in 1891 and the Munich chair in 1903. The research institute of 1917 became a center for neuropathology, experimental psychology and psychiatric genetics. His textbook *Psychiatrie: Ein Lehrbuch für Studierende und Ärzte* went through eight editions from 1883 (first issued as a *Compendium*) to 1915. A ninth, incomplete edition was issued in 1927, after his death, with Johannes Lange. The sixth edition (1899) is the one usually cited for the mature contrast between dementia praecox and manic-depressive insanity. The term dementia praecox already appears in the fourth edition (1893), and the fifth (1896) organizes illness by course.

## Contributions

### The Kraepelinian Dichotomy

In the sixth edition of *Psychiatrie* (1899), Kraepelin set out two large groups of endogenous psychoses: **dementia praecox** (Eugen Bleuler's later name was schizophrenia) and **manic-depressive insanity** (a source of the modern bipolar concept, not identical with it). He separated them mainly by **course**. He described dementia praecox as tending toward chronic deterioration, and manic-depressive insanity as episodic, with intervals that could include substantial recovery. Later writers called this the **"Kraepelinian dichotomy."** DSM-III and ICD classifications reused the broad separation, while also revising it. Within manic-depressive insanity he grouped mania, periodic depression and mixed states as one disease, gathering forms that nineteenth-century authors had often kept apart (Falret's *folie circulaire* and Baillarger's *folie à double forme*, both described in 1854, among them).

### Subtypes of Dementia Praecox

Within dementia praecox he distinguished **catatonic**, **hebephrenic** and **paranoid** patterns, drawing catatonia from Karl Kahlbaum and hebephrenia from Ewald Hecker, and uniting them by what he took to be a common outcome. Later editions added further distinctions, including paraphrenia in the eighth edition. The three patterns remained visible in the classical subtypes of schizophrenia for much of the twentieth century. DSM-5 (2013) dropped those subtypes from the main criteria.

### The Longitudinal Method in Clinical Observation

Kraepelin broke with the tradition of momentary descriptive psychiatry. He called for following patients **over time** (longitudinal studies) and recording their illness histories precisely. He held that the identity of a mental disease is determined not by a single symptom but by the full pattern of its development. This method has influenced the philosophy of psychiatric classification to the present day.

### Beginnings of Comparative Psychiatry

In 1904 Kraepelin traveled to Java and observed patients in the colonial asylum at Buitenzorg (now Bogor). His short paper "Vergleichende Psychiatrie" (1904) reported that the broad clinical pictures resembled those he knew in Europe, with differences in content and frequency. The essay is an early document of cross-cultural comparison in psychiatry. It assumes that the illnesses are natural entities whose coloring varies by culture, an assumption later medical anthropology has disputed. The observations were made inside a colonial institution and are not a systematic epidemiology.

## Legacy

- Descriptive systems from DSM-III (1980) onward again gave course and symptom clusters a central place, after decades in which psychoanalytic formulation had dominated much of American teaching. In 1978 Gerald Klerman called the Washington University group associated with Eli Robins, Samuel Guze and John Feighner "neo-Kraepelinian."
- Bleuler proposed the name "schizophrenia" in 1908; the monograph *Dementia Praecox oder Gruppe der Schizophrenien* appeared in 1911 and changed the emphasis from inevitable deterioration to a group of psychoses marked by what he called splitting of psychological functions. Kurt Schneider's later first-rank symptoms belong to the same German clinical tradition.
- Family and molecular studies have found overlapping genetic risk for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. That overlap is why Nick Craddock and Michael Owen, in 2005, argued that the dichotomy no longer fits the genetic evidence as a sharp biological split. Clinical differences in course remain in use even so.

## Limits and Critique

The model is criticized for sharp boundaries. Schizoaffective syndromes and mixed family histories sit poorly inside a strict split. Kraepelin himself wrote in 1920, in "Die Erscheinungsformen des Irreseins," that the boundaries were not absolute and that many cases combined features of both groups. Outcome is also less uniform than the early dichotomy implied: some patients diagnosed with dementia praecox improve, and some with manic-depressive illness do not return to their former state. Craddock and Owen (2005) pressed the genetic version of this criticism. Kraepelin also wrote in favor of hereditary explanations of degeneration, warned against alcohol, and took part in the German discussion of racial hygiene (*Rassenhygiene*). He died in 1926, before the National Socialist sterilization and killing programs, but historians treat his eugenic passages as part of the background those programs later exploited. This entry is educational and not a clinical classification guide.
