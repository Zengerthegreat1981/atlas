# Saskia Sassen

Saskia Sassen (born 1949) is a Dutch-American sociologist who popularized and developed the concept of the global city and has written widely on globalization, migration and the transformation of states and territory.

## Life and Context

Sassen was born in The Hague and grew up in Argentina and Italy before studying in several countries and receiving her doctorate in sociology at the University of Notre Dame (1974). She taught at several universities and became a professor at Columbia University (the Robert S. Lynd chair), and has also been associated with the London School of Economics. Her research brings together urban sociology, political economy and the study of migration.

## Main Ideas

In *The Global City*, Sassen argued that globalization does not remove the importance of place. Instead, a few major cities such as New York, London and Tokyo became strategic sites for the command functions of the global economy, concentrating finance and advanced producer services. This concentration also generates a large low-wage workforce, many of them immigrants, so global cities are marked by social and economic polarization. In *Losing Control?* and *Territory, Authority, Rights*, she examined how globalization partly denationalizes components of the state and reorganizes authority beyond national borders. She also studied the links between capital mobility, labor migration and the way that economic restructuring in poorer countries generates emigration. In *Expulsions*, she argued that recent economic dynamics push people, firms and parts of the biosphere out of the systems that once included them, through displacement, land grabs and environmental damage.

## Applications and Cross-References

*The Mobility of Labor and Capital* (1988) argued that foreign investment and export-oriented production in poorer countries mobilize workers (especially women) and create the conditions for emigration, so that migration is linked to the same restructuring that moves capital. *The Global City* is often read alongside John Friedmann's "world city" hypothesis and Manuel Castells's network-society thesis, and with Immanuel Wallerstein's world-system analysis as a framework for thinking about unequal global integration. Her analysis of New York, London and Tokyo has been used to study the growth of finance, producer services, gentrification and informal low-wage work in those cities, and later comparative work (for example *Cities in a World Economy*, 1994) extended it to other cities.

## Method

Sassen combines comparative urban analysis, data on economic sectors and migration, and conceptual work aimed at reframing familiar categories such as the national and the global.

## Influence and Criticism

Her concept of the global city framed a large body of research on world city networks and urban inequality. Critics have argued that her account overstates the importance of finance and producer services, that the ranking and definition of global cities is imprecise, and that the framework pays limited attention to cities outside the wealthy core.
