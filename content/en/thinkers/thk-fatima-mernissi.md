# Fatima Mernissi

Fatima Mernissi (1940-2015) was a Moroccan sociologist and writer who examined gender, Islam, politics and modernity in the Arab world. She combined academic sociology with memoir and public writing.

## Life and Context

Mernissi was born in Fez, Morocco, and grew up in a household shaped by the urban, segregated domestic life she later described in her memoir. She studied in Rabat and at the Sorbonne, and received her doctorate at Brandeis University in 1973. She taught sociology at Mohammed V University in Rabat and worked as a researcher and public intellectual. She received the Prince of Asturias Prize for Literature in 2003, shared with Susan Sontag.

## Main Ideas

- **Gender relations in Muslim societies.** In *Beyond the Veil* (1975), she argued that sexual segregation and the fear of women's sexuality reflect social and economic organisation and the need to control women's roles, rather than being a fixed feature of Islam.
- **Critique of religious misogyny.** In *The Veil and the Male Elite* (1987), she examined early Islamic sources and argued that certain misogynistic traditions were later constructions linked to male political interests, and that early Islam contained egalitarian potential.
- **Women in Muslim political history.** *The Forgotten Queens of Islam* (1990) studied women who held political power.
- **Democracy and modernity.** *Islam and Democracy: Fear of the Modern World* (1992) discussed Arab anxieties about modernity, the state and political participation.

## Major Works

- *Beyond the Veil: Male-Female Dynamics in a Modern Muslim Society* (1975)

- *Scheherazade Goes West* (2001)

## Method

She worked with interviews, field studies and close textual analysis of historical religious sources, and often wrote in an accessible style intended for a broad readership.

## Influence and Criticism

Mernissi is a key figure in Islamic and Arab feminism and in debates about the veil and women's rights. Critics have questioned her reading of the sources and her historical claims, and some Western readers have been accused of using her work in simplified ways. Others see her critique of Orientalist stereotypes, notably in *Scheherazade Goes West*, as a corrective to Western images of Muslim women.
