# Gilles Fauconnier

A French cognitive linguist who, together with Mark Turner, founded the theory of **conceptual blending**, a framework that explains how the human mind builds new meanings by combining multiple conceptual structures within a single mental "space."

## Mental spaces

In *Mental Spaces* (1985), Fauconnier developed a theory of how speakers build temporary "mental spaces" during discourse: partial conceptual packages constructed on the fly to interpret sentences that involve hypotheses, tenses or differing points of view (such as conditional sentences or indirect reports), without confusing them with the direct "reality" of the discourse.

## The theory of conceptual blending

Together with Mark Turner, Fauconnier extended this framework into a more general theory: the human mind builds a new meaning by blending two input mental spaces into a third "blended space," which inherits a partial structure from each and produces an emergent meaning that is not reducible to the sum of the two parts. An example often cited in the literature is the expression "that surgeon is a butcher," which blends the space of surgery with the space of butchery to produce a new critical evaluation.

## Legacy

This theory extended the scope of cognitive linguistics from simple conceptual metaphor (Lakoff and Johnson) to a more dynamic model of momentary semantic creativity. It influenced the analysis of humor, linguistic creativity and counterfactual reasoning.

## Emergent structure: composition, completion and elaboration

Fauconnier and Turner identified three mental operations that explain how the blended space produces a new meaning not reducible to its inputs: **composition** (combining elements from the two input spaces into a single structure that did not exist in either alone), **completion** (drawing on additional cultural or encyclopedic background knowledge to complete the image arising from composition with implicit information not explicitly stated), and **elaboration** (running the blended space mentally "as if it were a live scenario" to derive new conclusions and inferences that were not available in either of the original spaces before the blending operation).

## Academic career between France and America

Fauconnier began his academic career in France and later became a professor of cognitive science at the University of California, San Diego, where he was a leading figure in the cognitive linguistics movement alongside Ronald Langacker and in dialogue with George Lakoff, Leonard Talmy and others. He died in 2021, after a research career of several decades developing the theory of mental spaces and conceptual blending.

## An additional example: blending imaginary races

Among the well-known examples used by Fauconnier and Turner to illustrate conceptual blending is a newspaper report that the catamaran *Great America II* was sailing some distance "ahead of" the record pace set by the clipper *Northern Light* in 1853 on the San Francisco-Boston route, as if the two ships were racing each other. Here the mind blends two separate temporal spaces (the 1853 voyage and today's voyage) into a single blended space in which the race is depicted as if it were actually taking place between the two ships at the same time, despite its impossibility in reality. This is a direct illustration of how the human mind builds complex inferential scenarios that do not literally exist in reality, yet are fully comprehensible and internally logical to the listener.

## Applications of the theory to humor and political metaphor

Tools of conceptual blending have been widely used to analyze the mechanisms of verbal humor (where comic surprise often arises from an unexpected collision between two semantically distant blended spaces), and to analyze political discourse and complex conceptual metaphors (such as the metaphor of the nation as a family). The blending framework provides finer tools than the simple theory of conceptual metaphor for explaining how these metaphors produce new inferences that actually affect general political understanding, rather than being mere passing rhetorical decoration of discourse.

## The variety of blending networks

Fauconnier and Turner distinguished several types of blending networks: simplex networks (in which one input supplies a frame with roles and the other supplies values for those roles), mirror networks (in which all spaces share the same organizing frame), single-scope networks (in which only one input's organizing frame is projected to the blend), and double-scope networks (in which both inputs contribute different, often conflicting, organizing frames to the blend, a type they associate with much creative thought). This classification gives a more precise tool for describing different patterns of semantic creativity.
