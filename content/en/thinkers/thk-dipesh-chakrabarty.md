# Dipesh Chakrabarty

Dipesh Chakrabarty (born 1948 in Kolkata, India) is the Lawrence A. Kimpton Distinguished Service Professor of History and South Asian Languages and Civilizations at the University of Chicago. A prominent second-generation member of the Subaltern Studies Collective, Chakrabarty is widely recognized for his theoretical contributions to postcolonial theory, historiography, and the philosophy of history, most notably through his critique of Eurocentric historicism and his subsequent interventions on the Anthropocene and climate change.

## From Subaltern Labor to Postcolonial Historiography

Chakrabarty studied physics at Presidency College, Kolkata, and management at the Indian Institute of Management Calcutta, before earning his doctorate in history at the Australian National University under the supervision of Anthony Low and Ranajit Guha. His early research focused on industrial labor in colonial India, culminating in *Rethinking Working-Class History: Bengal 1890–1940* (1989). In this monograph, Chakrabarty challenged orthodox Marxist labor historiography, arguing that the social consciousness and organizing principles of Calcutta's jute mill workers could not be understood merely through universal categories of class consciousness or bourgeois contractual relations. Instead, workers' identities were deeply mediated by hierarchical notions of kinship, religious community, and rural cultural idioms that resisted reduction to classical European models of proletarianization.

## Provincializing Europe and the Critique of Historicism

Chakrabarty's most influential contribution to historiographical theory is *Provincializing Europe: Postcolonial Thought and Historical Difference* (2000). The book addresses a fundamental paradox: European political and social thought—including concepts of citizenship, the state, civil society, and human rights—is simultaneously "indispensable and inadequate" for understanding non-Western modernities. It is indispensable because modern social science and anti-colonial movements themselves rely on these Enlightenment categories; it is inadequate because these concepts were forged in the parochial context of European history and universalized through imperialism.

Central to this critique is Chakrabarty's rejection of "historicism"—the developmental schema that relegated non-Western societies to an imaginary "waiting room of history," where they were perpetually judged as "not yet" ready for self-rule or complete modernity. To dismantle this teleology, Chakrabarty introduces the distinction between two pasts:
- **History 1**: The analytical past posited by the logic of capital, which seeks to assimilate all social practices and human experiences into its own self-reproduction.
- **History 2**: Multiple, local antecedents and affective life-worlds that coexist with capital but do not originate from its logic, retaining cultural and ontological differences that cannot be subsumed by universal historical narratives.

"Provincializing" Europe does not mean rejecting Western philosophy or retreating into cultural relativism, but rather acknowledging the geographical and historical specificity of European thought and questioning its claim to represent the universal human trajectory.

## The Anthropocene and the Planetary Turn

In the late 2000s, Chakrabarty expanded his historical philosophy beyond postcolonialism to confront the global climate crisis. In his landmark essay "The Climate of History: Four Theses" (2009) and the monograph *The Climate of History in a Planetary Age* (2021), he argued that anthropogenic climate change radically alters the discipline of history. The Anthropocene collapses the foundational distinction, maintained since Vico and Collingwood, between natural history and human history: humans have become geological agents capable of altering the Earth system.

Chakrabarty posits a crucial distinction between the "global" and the "planetary." The global pertains to human-centered histories of empires, capital, globalization, and postcolonial justice; the planetary situates humanity within the deep time of Earth systems and geobiological processes that precede and transcend human survival. This planetary perspective forces historians to rethink humanist assumptions of agency, progress, and historical justice in the face of ecological limits.
