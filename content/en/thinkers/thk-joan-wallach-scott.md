# Joan Wallach Scott

Joan Wallach Scott (born 1941) is an American historian and Professor Emerita in the School of Social Science at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. An influential theorist of gender history and modern French history, she helped move women's history from an empirical recovery project toward a critical, post-structuralist analysis of how gender functions as a constitutive category of historical knowledge and political power.

## Intellectual Trajectory and Historical Context

Scott completed her doctorate at the University of Wisconsin and began her career as a social historian of nineteenth-century French labor, publishing *The Glassworkers of Carmaux* (1974) and co-authoring *Women, Work, and Family* (1978) with Louise A. Tilly. During the early 1980s, influenced by French post-structuralism—particularly the discourse analysis of Michel Foucault and the deconstructive methods of Jacques Derrida—Scott recognized the methodological limitations of traditional social history, which treated social categories like "class" and "woman" as stable, unexamined empirical givens.

## Gender as a Category of Historical Analysis

In December 1986, Scott published her landmark theoretical essay, "Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis," in *The American Historical Review*. Scott formulated a two-part definition of gender that became highly influential in feminist historiography:
1. Gender is a constitutive element of social relationships based on perceived differences between the sexes.
2. Gender is a primary way of signifying relationships of power.

Scott argued that gender does not merely concern the history of women or biological sex; rather, it is a primary discursive language through which political regimes, economic systems, and religious authorities articulate hierarchies, legitimacy, and control. Metaphors of masculinity and femininity structure realms that seem far removed from private life, such as statecraft, warfare, and economic theory. In *Gender and the Politics of History* (1988), Scott further examined how gendered discourses shaped nineteenth-century French labor politics and the political exclusion of women.

## Critique of Experience and Epistemology

In her influential 1991 essay "The Evidence of Experience," Scott challenged the foundationalism of social historians who treated historical subjects' personal "experience" as an uncontaminated, authentic source of historical truth. Scott argued that experience is not self-evident reality; rather, individuals are constituted as subjects through pre-existing discursive frameworks and ideological categories. The task of the critical historian is not merely to document lived experience, but to historicize the discursive processes that produce that experience.

## Secularism, Republicanism, and *The Politics of the Veil*

In later works, including *Only Paradoxes to Offer: French Feminists and the Rights of Man* (1996), *The Politics of the Veil* (2007), and *Sex and Secularism* (2017), Scott applied her critical framework to French republican universalism and contemporary controversies surrounding the Islamic headscarf (*hijab*). Scott argued that French republicanism historically resolved the contradiction between universal individual rights and gender inequality through the exclusion of women, defining the universal citizen as implicitly male. In her analysis of the 2004 French headscarf ban, she argued that the state's defense of *laïcité* (secularism) weaponized gender equality as an essentialized civilizational marker against Muslim immigrants, obscuring persistent gender inequalities within secular Western societies.

## Historiographical Impact and Debate

Scott's post-structuralist turn provoked vigorous debates. Materialist and Marxist feminist historians (such as Linda Gordon and Bryan Palmer) cautioned that an exclusive focus on discourse, language, and deconstruction risked detaching gender from concrete material conditions, such as labor exploitation, wages, and social institutions. Nevertheless, Scott's conceptual reformulation remains one of the most widely cited contributions to late twentieth-century historical methodology, and gender analysis is now widely used across the humanities and social sciences.
