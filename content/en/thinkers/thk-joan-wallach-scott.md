# Joan Wallach Scott

American historian and Professor Emerita at the Institute for Advanced Study at Princeton University. She formulated one of the most influential texts in turning gender into a basic and central analytical tool in the writing of modern history.

## Contributions

- **Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis (1986)**: Her best-known essay argues that gender is not only a subject of study (the history of women) but an analytical lens that reveals how relations of power and social meaning are built in any historical field (politics, economics, war), through symbols and hierarchies linked to sexual difference, even in fields that do not appear to be "about women" at first sight.
- **Moving from "recovering voices" to "reformulating questions"**: She pushed the field beyond the stage of biographies of exceptional women toward a deeper question: how does the inclusion of gender reinterpret events and institutions that historians believed they had fully understood?
- **Gender and the Politics of History (1988)**: A collection of essays that expanded the thesis theoretically, drawing on post-structuralist theory (Foucault, Derrida) to analyze how discourse itself produces the categories of "man" and "woman," rather than reflecting them neutrally.

## Position in the Field

Scott is the central figure in transforming "women's history" into "feminist history" in the deeper analytical sense. Her 1986 essay influenced generations of historians outside the field of gender itself, serving as a model of how a new analytical category can reformulate the questions of an entire field.

## Major Works

- "Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis" (essay, *The American Historical Review*, 1986).
- *Gender and the Politics of History* (1988).
- *Only Paradoxes to Offer: French Feminists and the Rights of Man* (1996).

## Historical Context

Scott's academic formation took place in the context of the development of "women's history" in the 1970s as a recovery project aimed mainly at rediscovering marginalized women in the traditional historical narrative (writing biographies of women of exceptional achievement, or recovering the experiences of ordinary women who had been silenced). Scott studied history at the University of Wisconsin and began her professional career with studies in the history of the French working class. She then gradually moved, influenced by the wave of French post-structuralist theory arriving in American universities in the 1980s, toward theoretical questions about gender itself as an analytical category, rather than merely an additional research subject added to the list of traditional historical topics.

## Main Ideas and Method in Detail

The core of Scott's thesis rests on a distinction between "gender" as a socio-cultural structure of meaning associated with sexual difference, and "biological sex" itself. Whereas biological sex is regarded as a fixed given, Scott's perspective holds that the meanings of "masculinity" and "femininity," and the values, roles, and hierarchies connected to them, are not natural but socially and historically constructed. They change over time and across cultures, and they are used as a tool to organize power relations in almost every social field, even in fields that seem far removed from questions of women, such as economic discourse, foreign policy, or war. These fields nonetheless borrow gendered symbols and metaphors (such as describing nations in the language of masculine strength versus feminine weakness) to build their legitimacy.

## Impact and Criticism in Detail

Scott's 1986 essay had an exceptional impact on the field of history in general. It has been cited thousands of times and became a standard text in graduate curricula around the world, training historians to think critically about basic analytical categories other than gender, such as class and race. Some Marxist and materialist feminist historians criticized it from a different standpoint, arguing that its strong focus on "discourse" and "symbolic representation" (influenced by Michel Foucault) relatively marginalizes the concrete material conditions of women's actual lives (wages, domestic labor, poverty), and that its post-structuralist method may bring gender analysis closer to a textual, literary analysis than to a social history open to direct empirical verification. Nevertheless, there remains broad agreement among historians in the field that her contribution constituted a decisive turning point that cannot be bypassed in the history of writing about gender and women.
