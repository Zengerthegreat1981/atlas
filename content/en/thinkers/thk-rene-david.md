# René David

**René David** (1906–1990) was a French jurist, professor of comparative law at the University of Paris, and a leading figure in modern **comparative law** as an academic discipline of global scope, known for his ambitious project of classifying the world's contemporary legal systems into major "families" amenable to systematic comparative study, and his treatment of **Islamic law** and other non-Western systems as independent legal systems within this global classification.

## Major Contemporary Legal Systems: The Classification of Families

In his foundational work *Les grands systèmes de droit contemporains* (Major Contemporary Legal Systems, 1964), David offers an influential classification of the world's legal systems into major groupings that share historical roots, intellectual methods and similar legal sources: the **Romano-Germanic family** (the European continental civil law and its extensions), the **common law family**, the **socialist family** (the legal systems of the former Soviet bloc), and finally a group of **other conceptions of law and social order**, to which David devoted a special section that includes Muslim law, Hindu law, Far Eastern law, Jewish law and the customary systems of Africa and Madagascar. David's method combined the **technical criterion** (modes of legal thinking, sources of law and techniques of interpretation) with the **ideological criterion** (the philosophical and political values underlying the system), so that the classification does not stop at the formal appearance of rules, but delves into the deeper logic governing each family.

## Islamic Law in Global Comparative Classification

One of David's notable contributions is his serious treatment of Islamic law as an independent legal system with its own internal logic, based on religious sources (the Quran and the Sunna) and on jurisprudential interpretive mechanisms distinct from both the Romano-Germanic and the common law logic. He rejected treating it as a marginal "exception" or a primitive version of Western law. David also addressed the phenomenon of **mixed legal systems** in the Arab and Islamic world, which combine civil laws derived from French or European models with rulings from Islamic law (especially in personal status matters), a phenomenon that remains central to the study of contemporary Arab legal systems.

## Practical Undertaking: Drafting the Ethiopian Civil Code

Alongside his theoretical work, David undertook a rare practical experience in comparative codification when the Ethiopian government commissioned him to draft the **Ethiopian Civil Code** (enacted 1960), drawing on French, Swiss and other civil law models and some Ethiopian elements, in an effort to modernize the Ethiopian legal system through a deliberate use of studied legal transplantation, rather than blind copying. This experience provided him with material for later reflection on the limits and possibilities of transferring legal models between cultural contexts; the Code is often cited in discussions of how little an imported code may shape actual practice.

## His Relations with Contemporary Arab Jurists

David was a contemporary of a generation of major Arab civil law scholars, foremost among them Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri, who drafted or helped draft the Egyptian, Syrian, Iraqi and Libyan civil codes on the basis of a blend of French-inspired sources and Islamic jurisprudence, in a project that in many respects parallels David's own experience in comparative codification, although the contexts and ideological starting points differed between them. The implicit dialogue between the projects of David and al-Sanhuri represents an early model of comparative thinking about how to modernize non-Western legal systems without a complete break with their authentic legal heritage.

## Criteria of Classification: Between Technique and Ideology

David explains that classifying a legal system within a particular family requires more than superficial similarity in terminology or even in the historical source of its codification. It requires examining the **deep mode of legal thinking**: how the concept of the "legal rule" itself is defined, which sources of binding force are formally recognized, and how judges and lawyers are trained in legal reasoning. By this criterion, an Arab system may formally borrow French civil legislative texts while its actual legal thinking and judicial practice remain closer to a different logic that reflects continuous interaction with Islamic jurisprudence and local customs, which explains the difficulty of classifying many Arab systems within a single pure family.

## Legacy

David's classification of legal families, despite later criticism by jurists such as H. Patrick Glenn, who considered it at times excessively simplifying of the diversity of non-Western systems, remains a common starting point in the teaching of comparative law. The book was later revised in collaboration with Camille Jauffret-Spinosi, and appeared in English as *Major Legal Systems in the World Today*, co-authored with John E. C. Brierley.
