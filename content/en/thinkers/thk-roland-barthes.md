# Roland Barthes

A semiological critic, a demystifier of everyday myths, and the author of the manifesto "The Death of the Author."

## What He Gave

- **"Mythologies" (1957) and the deconstruction of the obvious**: a series of short essays in which Barthes reads aspects of everyday French life (professional wrestling, the face of Garbo, the new car, laundry detergent, steak and chips) as **contemporary myths**. His thesis is that myth **"transforms history into nature"**: it takes what is the product of a class, a time, and an interest, and presents it as self-evident and eternal, needing no justification. The function of criticism is to restore to the thing its **historicity**, so that its enchantment disappears. This analytical tool became fundamental to **cultural studies** as a whole.
- **Two-level signification: the technical mechanism of myth.** He took the binary of the signifier and the signified from Saussure and, building on Louis Hjelmslev's notion of connotation, added a second layer: a first-order semantic system (the image of a young African soldier saluting the French flag) becomes in its entirety a **signifier** within a second system whose signified is "the French empire is just and its sons serve it faithfully." Ideology thus does not lie openly; it **operates on the second layer**, where we do not see it.
- **"The Death of the Author" (1968)**: his most widely known essay. His thesis is that interpreting a text by reference to its **author's intention and biography** closes the text and assigns it a single final meaning: to give a text an author is, in his words, "to impose a limit" on it. The alternative is to read the text as **a tissue of quotations** drawn from an entire culture, in which the reader, not the author, is the place where meaning is unified. The essay appeared first in English in 1967 (*Aspen*) and in French in 1968. The impact of this essay extended beyond literature to every field that asks: is the meaning of an act what its doer intended?
- **"Readerly" and "Writerly" (S/Z, 1970)**: his distinction between a **readerly** text (*lisible*), which the reader consumes passively through a closed plot, and a **writerly** text (*scriptible*), which involves the reader in producing its meaning because it is open and multiple. In the same book he broke a short story by Balzac into **561 units** distributed across five codes, which is the best-known application of structural analysis to a single text, and at the same time the work in which Barthes began to leave structuralism for what lay beyond it.
- **"The Pleasure of the Text" (1973) and pleasure versus bliss**: his late turn toward **the body and subjective experience**. He distinguished between *plaisir*, a comfortable pleasure tied to culture, and *jouissance*, a bliss that shakes the reader, breaks his certainty, and severs his connection to his language. Barthes's use of *jouissance* echoes its use in French psychoanalytic writing, notably Lacan's.
- **"Camera Lucida" (1980)**: his last book, on photography, and the most personal of his texts. He wrote it after his mother's death while searching her photographs for her. In it is his famous duality: the **studium** (the general cultural content in the image, which is read through knowledge) and the **punctum** (the small, wounding detail that "pricks" the viewer and concerns him alone, for no clearly understood reason). The punctum is a psychological concept par excellence: an effect that is not explained by the content and does not admit generalization, and that refers to the viewer's memory and loss.

## His Place in the Current

Barthes was one of the pillars of **structuralism** and then one of the first to depart from it toward **post-structuralism**. His own career condenses the transformation of French thought: from the scientific analysis of codes to the celebration of the multiplicity of meaning and of subjective experience. His influence on **cultural studies** and on the criticism of the media and the image has been very wide, and "The Death of the Author," "myth," and "punctum" have become shared vocabulary beyond philosophy. His place in this atlas is that he offers tools for analyzing **how meaning is made and naturalized**, tools necessary for any understanding of how people's conceptions of themselves and their bodies are shaped through images and discourse.

## Life and Career

Roland Barthes was born in Cherbourg on 12 November 1915. His father, a naval officer, was killed in a naval engagement in 1916 when Barthes was a baby, and he grew up with his mother Henriette, who remained the centre of his emotional life. In his youth he contracted tuberculosis, which disrupted his conventional university career. He worked as a teacher abroad (in Romania and Egypt) before turning to criticism. His first book, *Writing Degree Zero* (1953), was an attempt to describe a neutral writing that carries no style or commitment. He then published *Elements of Semiology* (1964-65), *The Fashion System* (1967), and *Empire of Signs* (1970), about Japan, and in 1976 he took up the chair of literary semiology at the Collège de France. He died on 26 March 1980, a month after being struck by a van in Paris.

## Text and Author in the Arab Heritage

The idea of "the death of the author" carries a paradox when read alongside classical Arabic criticism. The familiar saying "the meaning is in the belly of the poet" makes the poet's intention the authority of interpretation, which is the position Barthes attacks. By contrast, 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani (d. 1078), in *Dala'il al-I'jaz*, presents the theory of *nazm* (composition), which makes meaning the result of the relations of words in construction, rather than of isolated words or of the speaker's intention alone. Arab scholars have compared it to Saussurean structuralism, though this is a structural comparison rather than an influence. 'Abd al-Fattah Kilito, in *L'Auteur et ses doubles* (Seuil, 1985; English translation *The Author and His Doubles*, Syracuse University Press, 2001), studied the image of the author in classical Arab culture, through Barthes and French narratology, and showed that in classical Arabic the author often hides behind narrators, narration, and chains of transmission.

## Criticism and Reception

Barthes faced resistance from the start. Raymond Picard attacked him in *New Criticism or New Fraud?* (1965), and Barthes replied in *Criticism and Truth* (1966) that criticism has the right to state multiple meanings. Michel Foucault, in "What Is an Author?" (1969), argued that the death of the author is not enough, and that analysis must study the function of the author in discourse. Seán Burke, in *The Death and Return of the Author* (Edinburgh University Press, 1992), criticized the outright abolition of intention. A standing objection to *Mythologies* is that it analyzes cleverly but assumes that the critical reader stands outside the myth. Louis-Jean Calvet, in *Roland Barthes: A Biography* (1990), and the more recent study by Tiphaine Samoyault (Seuil, 2015), present a life story that shows how his personal life intersected with his theories.
