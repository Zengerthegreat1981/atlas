# Carol S. Dweck

A professor of psychology at Stanford University, a pioneer of experimental research on motivation and mindset theory, and the author of the classic distinction between the fixed mindset and the growth mindset.

## What She Contributed

- **The "fixed mindset" and the "growth mindset"**: her central distinction. A person with a **fixed mindset** believes that ability is a given that does not change, so every situation becomes a test of his worth. He therefore avoids difficulty, sees effort as evidence of deficiency, and collapses in the face of failure because he reads it as a verdict on his essence. A person with a **growth mindset** believes that ability can be developed, so he sees difficulty as an opportunity, effort as a path, and failure as **information** rather than a verdict.
- **The founding research on learned helplessness in children**: it began from an experimental observation in the 1970s: children of equal ability faced impossible problems, and some collapsed and gave up while others became more enthusiastic and said "I love a challenge." The difference was not in intelligence but in **how they explained failure**: as evidence of a fixed incapacity, or as a sign that the strategy had not yet worked.
- **The effect of praise (1998, with Mueller)**: her most famous experiments and the most widely applied. Children were praised after success either **for their intelligence** ("you're smart") or **for their effort** ("you worked hard"). Those praised for intelligence later chose the easy tasks, their performance declined after failure, and they **lied** in reporting their result to a stranger. Those praised for effort chose the difficult tasks and persevered. The conclusion is that **praising a trait** produces fragility, and **praising the process** produces resilience.
- **"Not Yet"**: her best-known applied formulation in education: that the verdict "you failed" be replaced with the phrase **"you haven't reached it yet,"** so that the result changes from a position in a fixed ranking to a **point on a path**.
- **Extension beyond education**: she applied the framework to relationships (one who sees compatibility as fate leaves at the first disagreement, and one who sees it as work stays), to leadership and organizations, and to stereotypes (**stereotype threat** eases when ability is presented as capable of growth).

## Limits: The Objections

It is only honest to record them, for they are among the widest debates in contemporary educational psychology:

- **Failure to replicate the interventions**: a large randomised trial in English schools, **Foliano and colleagues (2019)**, with about five thousand pupils, found no detectable effect of a growth-mindset programme on attainment, and **Li and Bates (2019)** failed to replicate the link between mindset and children's achievement and responses to failure. Meta-analyses (for example Sisk and colleagues, 2018) likewise found only weak effects overall.
- **The effect is conditional, not general**: the broadest national experiment (the **National Study of Learning Mindsets**, 2019, with more than twelve thousand students) found a **real but small** effect, concentrated among **lower-achieving students** and in schools whose culture supports this orientation. That is, mindset alone is not enough if the environment belies it.
- **The "false growth mindset"**: Dweck herself acknowledged that the concept spread in a distorted form: it came to be understood as **mere optimism** or praise of effort even without strategy, whereas its essence is **changing the strategy** upon failure rather than redoubling the same effort. She warned against using it to blame the student for circumstances beyond his control.
- **The structural objection**: as in the criticism of grit, it was objected that turning educational disparities into a matter of **individual mindset** diverts attention from poverty and unequal resources.

## Her Place in the Current

Carol Dweck is a professor at Stanford, and an extension of the **attribution** line in **Bernard Weiner** and the **learned helplessness** of **Seligman**, but she moved the question from "why do people give up?" to "what implicit belief about the nature of ability makes giving up reasonable?" She intersects with **Angela Duckworth** (grit) and **Anders Ericsson** (deliberate practice); the three share the same shift and hence the same objections. Her place in this atlas is twofold: a **practical framework** of great influence in education and cognitive therapy (the latter in turn works on implicit beliefs about the self), and a **case study** in the gap between a concept's cultural spread and the size of its measured effect.
