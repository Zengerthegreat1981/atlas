# Ludwig von Mises

**Ludwig von Mises** was an Austrian economist and a central figure of the Austrian school in the generation after Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk. He is known for his early and radical critique of the possibility of a centrally planned socialist economy, for his theory of money and the business cycle, and for a distinctive philosophical methodology, which he called "praxeology," for the study of economic human action.

## From Vienna to Geneva and New York

Mises was born in the city of Lemberg (now Lviv) in 1881. He studied at the University of Vienna, where he was influenced by the writings of Carl Menger and by Böhm-Bawerk's seminar. In the 1920s and early 1930s he ran a private seminar in Vienna whose participants included Friedrich Hayek, Gottfried Haberler, Fritz Machlup and Oskar Morgenstern. He left Austria for Geneva in 1934, as the political situation worsened, and then emigrated to the United States in 1940. There he was a visiting professor at New York University from 1945 to 1969 without a regular salaried chair, relying partly on support from private foundations and institutions that backed his ideas.

## Monetary and Credit Theory

In *The Theory of Money and Credit* (1912), Mises developed an early analysis linking the expansion of bank credit unsupported by real saving to the emergence of unsustainable investment booms that end in economic crises. This is the framework that Hayek later developed into the more detailed Austrian business cycle theory. The book also applied marginal utility reasoning to the value of money.

## The Economic Calculation Problem

In 1920 Mises published his pivotal essay "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth." He argued that a socialist economy which abolishes private ownership of the means of production necessarily lacks real prices for those means, because prices arise from genuine competitive exchange between owners of alternatives. Without these prices it becomes rationally impossible to calculate which combination of means of production is more efficient than another. In his view this makes comprehensive central planning incapable of rational allocation. (He later used the phrase "planned chaos", the title of a 1947 work, for the interventionist economy.)

## The Socialist Calculation Debate

This thesis opened the **socialist calculation debate**, which extended through the 1920s and 1930s. Socialist economists, most prominently Oskar Lange and Abba Lerner, responded that a central planner could simulate the competitive market through calculation, using hypothetical accounting prices (shadow prices) that it adjusted experimentally by trial and error until it reached an equilibrium similar to the market. Mises and Hayek regarded this response as ignoring the dispersed and non-centralisable nature of knowledge.

## Praxeology: A Deductive Methodology

In his encyclopaedic work *Human Action* (1949), Mises developed a complete systematic methodology that he called "praxeology." It derives economic conclusions through strict logical reasoning from one basic axiom that cannot be logically refuted: that human beings act purposefully to improve their situation. He thus rejected reliance on statistical empirical testing as a basis for formulating general economic laws, treating economic theory as an a priori science of action. This contrasted with the positivist methodology that dominated American academic economics in the twentieth century, and mainstream critics have argued that praxeology is unfalsifiable. The methodological dispute has not been settled.

## Academic Marginality and Later Revival

Mises's ideas remained relatively marginal in the American academic establishment during his lifetime. This was partly due to his explicit rejection of the prevailing positivist methodology, and partly to his strictly free-market liberal political positions, which seemed outside the mainstream during the post-war Keynesian consensus. His legacy experienced a broad revival after his death in 1973, through students such as Murray Rothbard and through intellectual institutions later founded in his name (the Ludwig von Mises Institute, founded in 1982).

## His Students and the Division of the Heritage

Mises's students later divided into two paths. One is an academic path closer to the prevailing economic mainstream, represented by Israel Kirzner, who focused on entrepreneurship and the discovery of information as a dynamic market process. The other is a radical political path, represented by Murray Rothbard, which combined Mises's economic analysis with an extreme free-market liberal philosophy that rejects almost any role for the state in the economy or society.

## A Sharper Political Stance Than Hayek's

Mises's positions on government intervention were widely regarded as more uncompromising than those of his colleague Hayek, who in *The Constitution of Liberty* accepted some state provision such as a minimum income. Mises held that any partial government intervention in the market, such as setting a minimum wage, necessarily creates new distortions that call for successive further interventions to remedy them. He called this "interventionism," which he regarded as an inherently unstable path that ends either in withdrawing the intervention or in sliding toward a fully centrally directed economy.

## Influence on Development and Transition Economics

Mises's argument about the impossibility of central economic calculation was revived in discussions of the difficulties of the planned economies of Eastern Europe and the Soviet Union in the 1980s. Some economists saw in their difficulties with pricing, resource allocation and chronic shortages a belated confirmation of his argument, while others disputed that reading.
