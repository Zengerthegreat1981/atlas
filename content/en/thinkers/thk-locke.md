# John Locke

An English philosopher and physician (1632–1704), he studied at **Christ Church**, Oxford, met **Anthony Ashley Cooper**, later the first Earl of Shaftesbury, in 1666 and in 1667 moved into his service as physician and adviser, and was elected to the **Royal Society** in 1668. He spent his exile in the Netherlands between 1683 and 1689, and after the revolution of 1688 he published in a single year — 1689 — the three works that made his name.

## What He Contributed

### *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689)
It appeared in late 1689 with the date 1690 on its title page, in four books: **Of Innate Notions**, **Of Ideas**, **Of Words**, **Of Knowledge and Opinion**.

- **The rejection of innate ideas** is the whole subject of the first book: there are no theoretical or practical principles in the mind before experience. The two sources of ideas in the second book are **sensation** and **reflection**.
- **The image of the blank page**: in II.i.2 Locke writes that the mind is "white paper". The Latin phrase **tabula rasa** is older than Locke but does not occur in the text of the *Essay*, so as a label for his view it is not the author's own expression.
- **Primary and secondary qualities** in II.viii: solidity, extension, shape, motion and number are in the body itself, whereas color, sound and taste are powers in it to produce ideas in us, not qualities of it.
- **Personal identity** in II.xxvii, a chapter added in the **second edition (1694)**: the person is not a substance but **continuity of consciousness** extending to past actions — hence the example of the prince and the cobbler.
- **The association of ideas** in II.xxxiii, a chapter added in the **fourth edition (1700)** alone: accidental associations explain "unreasonableness" in judgments, and from it the line extends to the laws of conditioning.

### Politics and Toleration

- ***Two Treatises of Government*** (1689, title page 1690), published without an author's name: the **First Treatise** is a demolition of *Patriarcha* (1680) by **Robert Filmer** and of the theory of divine right; the **Second Treatise** is a construction of the rights to life, liberty and property, of government by the consent of the governed, and of the right of resistance.
- ***Epistola de Tolerantia*** (Gouda, 1689) appeared in Latin without a name, and **William Popple** rendered it into English in the same year; toleration in it is conditional: it excludes Catholics and atheists, and it was written in the context of his Dutch exile.
- ***Some Thoughts Concerning Education*** (1693) and ***The Reasonableness of Christianity*** (1695).

## The Best-Known Controversies

1. **The "thinking matter" controversy** with **Edward Stillingfleet**, Bishop of Worcester, from 1697: the bishop saw in Locke's statement in IV.iii.6 — that God might have given matter the power of thinking — a threat to the immortality of the soul, and Locke replied in successive letters until the bishop's death.
2. **Slavery**: Locke was a shareholder in the **Royal African Company** and, as secretary to Shaftesbury, was involved in the drafting of the *Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina* (1669), which provides for the absolute authority of the master over his slaves — while the Second Treatise opens with a rejection of slavery; a tension that remains a matter of Lockean dispute.
3. **Leibniz's reply**: Leibniz wrote a chapter-by-chapter discussion of the *Essay* titled *Nouveaux essais sur l'entendement humain*, which was not published until after Locke's death (1765).

## Influence

**George Berkeley** criticized the distinction between primary and secondary qualities and "abstract ideas" in the *Principles of Human Knowledge*; **David Hume** extended associative analysis to the self in *A Treatise of Human Nature*; and in **David Hartley**'s *Observations on Man* association became a complete psychological system.

## Medical and Scientific Formation

Locke was not an academic philosopher in the professional sense. He studied medicine at Oxford and collaborated with Thomas Sydenham in clinical observation, worked with Robert Boyle in the circle of experimental philosophy, and was a friend of Isaac Newton. In the days when he wrote the *Essay* he described himself as an "under-labourer" in the service of the great scientists, clearing away the obscurity of speech that stood in their way. Hence his view of knowledge: what is fit for inquiry is what the actual powers of understanding reach, not delving into the essences of substances that there is no way of knowing.

## The Last Years

After returning from the Netherlands he held a post on the "Board of Trade and Plantations" between 1696 and 1700, and then lived his last years at Oates in Essex under the care of the family of Sir Francis Masham and his wife Damaris Masham, daughter of the philosopher Ralph Cudworth, and he died there on 28 October 1704. After his death *Of the Conduct of the Understanding* (1706) was published, an educational work on correcting habits of thought, which shows the practical face of his epistemological project.

## Limits

- The *Essay* is not an experimental psychology: no measurement and no experiment, and its instrument is reflection — so Locke's relevance to psychology passes through the chapters on association (II.xxxiii) and personal identity (II.xxvii), not through a clinical work.
- His word "idea" wavers between mental image and cognitive content, and this ambiguity is the entry point for the criticism of Berkeley and Thomas Reid against him.

## His Most Important Works

*Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689; 2nd ed. 1694, 4th ed. 1700) · *Two Treatises of Government* (1689) · *Epistola de Tolerantia* (1689) · *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* (1693) · *Reasonableness of Christianity* (1695).
