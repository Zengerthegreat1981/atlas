# Andrew B. Newberg

An American physician and researcher in neuroimaging, one of the pioneers of what is known as "neurotheology": the experimental study of what happens in the brain during religious practice, meditation and prayer, through functional single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) and related imaging techniques. He is director of research at the Marcus Institute of Integrative Health at Thomas Jefferson University in Philadelphia.

## Definition

Neurotheology is an interdisciplinary field that combines neuroscience and religious studies. It assumes that religious and mystical experiences, despite their enormous cultural diversity, may share measurable physiological neural patterns, without necessarily settling the question of whether the metaphysical content of those experiences is true or false.

## Historical Context

Newberg began his collaboration with Eugene d'Aquili, a psychiatrist who had since the 1970s developed an earlier theoretical project known as "Biogenetic Structuralism", which links general neural structures with cultural symbolic systems. This collaboration, which continued until d'Aquili's death in 1998, formed the theoretical basis on which Newberg later built his broader experimental research program using modern imaging techniques.

## Main Ideas and Method

In collaboration with d'Aquili, Newberg imaged the brain activity of experienced Tibetan Buddhist meditators during deep meditation and of Franciscan nuns during contemplative prayer, in small samples, and reported a recurring pattern: decreased activity in the superior posterior parietal lobe (the area responsible for the sense of bodily boundaries and spatial orientation), in parallel with subjective reports of "dissolution of the self's boundaries" and "union with the whole", which mystics describe across very different religious traditions. The two researchers interpreted this pattern through what they called the "Deafferentation Hypothesis": the progressive blocking of sensory input to that area during deep concentration is what produces the subjective sense of the dissolution of individual self-boundaries.

## Key Works and Representatives

In his joint book with d'Aquili, *Why God Won't Go Away* (2001), the authors argued that the human brain is neurologically equipped to produce religious and mystical experiences, and that this does not by itself show such experiences to be illusory. This thesis places him in a cautious middle position between pure neural reductionism (religion as merely a passing neural activity) and the theological affirmation of the reality of the transcendent. Later, with Mark Robert Waldman, he published *How God Changes Your Brain* (2009), which extends the applied scope of the hypothesis to the effects of everyday religious practice (prayer, brief meditation) on general mental and psychological health, going beyond the deep mystical states of experienced practitioners alone.

## Impact and Critique

This research program has faced a double critique. On one side, it has been accused of being unable in principle to settle the metaphysical question (does the neural activity accompanying the mystical experience produce it, accompany it, or reflect the reception of an actual transcendent reality? A question outside what neuroimaging can measure). This critique was elaborated later by researchers such as Ann Taves. On the other side, the research samples have been criticized for their very small size (a small number of highly experienced practitioners) and for the difficulty of generalizing the results to ordinary believers. The field remains to this day a living example of the limits of neural reduction in explaining religious phenomena, and of the need for a careful dialogue between the neural sciences and religious studies rather than replacing one with the other.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Neuroimaging research on Islamic practices such as prayer and Sufi *dhikr* is much less developed than research on Buddhist meditation and Christian prayer, which is a recognised gap in the neurotheology literature.

## Later Expansion of the Research

Newberg later extended his method to other practices, including a SPECT study of glossolalia in Pentecostal women (Newberg and colleagues, *Psychiatry Research: Neuroimaging*, 2006), and to the effects of meditation and prayer on health and cognition, moving beyond rare mystical states toward everyday religious practice. This work is exploratory and its samples are small.

## Synthetic Conclusion

Newberg remains a central figure in the broader debate about the relationship between science and religion in the twenty-first century. He presents a model of a neuroscientist who seeks neither to reduce religion nor to refute it, but to understand its vital mechanisms without judging the truth of its content. This position finds acceptance among some believers and rejection from secularists and believers alike, who see in it an unresolved methodological ambiguity.
