# Thomas Aquinas

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) was an Italian Dominican friar and theologian, one of the most influential philosophers of Latin Scholasticism. He is called in Catholic tradition the "Angelic Doctor" (*Doctor Angelicus*) and the "Common Doctor" (*Doctor Communis*), and was canonized in 1323 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1567.

## Life

He was born at Roccasecca, in the Kingdom of Sicily, into a noble family, and was educated at Monte Cassino and at the University of Naples. He joined the Dominican Order around 1244 despite his family's opposition (they briefly held him in custody), and studied in Paris and Cologne under Albertus Magnus. He taught in Paris as a master of theology (1256–1259 and again 1268–1272), and also in Italy, and in Naples at the end of his life. He died on 7 March 1274 at Fossanova, on his way to the Council of Lyon, leaving the *Summa Theologiae* unfinished. According to a report by his companion Reginald of Piperno, after a religious experience in December 1273 he stopped writing and remarked that all he had written seemed like straw.

## What He Contributed

- **The reconciliation of Aristotle and Christian doctrine**: working with new Latin translations of Aristotle (notably by William of Moerbeke) and drawing on Arabic and Jewish philosophers (Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides), he reread Christian theology through Aristotelian concepts, against more strictly Augustinian approaches.
- **The *Summa Theologiae*** (c. 1265–1273): his major work, a systematic exposition of God, creation, human beings, ethics, Christ and the sacraments, built from *quaestiones* with objections and replies.
- **The Five Ways** (*quinque viae*): five arguments for God's existence, from motion, efficient causation, contingency and necessity, degrees of perfection, and the order of nature.
- **Natural law**: the participation of rational creatures in the eternal law of God, linking theology with an ethics of virtues and natural ends.
- **Hylomorphism**: the human being is a unity of soul (the form of the body) and body, not a soul merely lodged in a body; the soul is subsistent and survives death.
- **Esse and essence**: God is pure act of being; in creatures essence and existence are distinct.

## His Place in the Tradition

- **Basis of Thomism**: his work became the foundation of Thomism, later promoted as a model for Catholic philosophy and theology, notably after Pope Leo XIII's encyclical *Aeterni Patris* (1879).
- **Opposed positions**: Franciscans such as Bonaventure and, later, Duns Scotus and Ockham held different views, for example giving greater weight to the will than to the intellect; Bonaventure and others accepted a "universal hylomorphism" (matter in spiritual substances) deriving from Ibn Gabirol, which Aquinas rejected. Some of his positions were included in the Paris and Oxford condemnations of 1277, and the Paris condemnation of those propositions was lifted in 1325.
- **Contemporary influence**: natural-law ethics, virtue ethics, philosophy of mind and religion, and the relation of faith and reason.

## His Most Important Works

- *Summa Theologiae* (c. 1265–1273, unfinished)
- *Summa contra Gentiles* (c. 1259–1265)
- *Quaestiones disputatae* (including *De veritate*, *De potentia*, *De malo*)
- *De ente et essentia* (early work)
- Commentaries on Aristotle (*Metaphysics*, *Nicomachean Ethics*, *De anima* and others) and on the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard
- *De regno* (*De regimine principum*): the first book and part of the second are by Aquinas; it was completed by Ptolemy of Lucca.

## Limits and Criticisms

- Critics question whether his synthesis of Aristotle and Christian doctrine is faithful to both.
- His arguments for God's existence were criticized by Kant and by many later philosophers.
- The dating and authenticity of some works remain the subject of scholarly study.
