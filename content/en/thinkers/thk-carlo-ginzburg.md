# Carlo Ginzburg

Carlo Ginzburg (born 1939 in Turin) is an Italian historian who taught at the University of Bologna, the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA), and the Scuola Normale Superiore di Pisa. Alongside Giovanni Levi and Edoardo Grendi, he is a leading figure of Italian microhistory (*microstoria*), a methodology that uses intensive archival analysis of circumscribed, exceptional cases to illuminate wider cultural, social, and mental structures.

## Core Contributions and Methodology

### *The Night Battles* and Popular Beliefs
In *The Night Battles: Witchcraft and Agrarian Cults in the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Centuries* (*I benandanti*, 1966), Ginzburg examined Inquisition records from the Friuli region in northeastern Italy. He showed how peasants known as *benandanti* ("good walkers") believed that their spirits left their bodies on certain nights to battle witches (*malandanti*) and protect agrarian fertility. Over decades of interrogations, Roman Catholic inquisitors systematically reframed these archaic, shamanic agricultural fertility rites into the demonological categories of diabolical witchcraft and the witches' sabbath. This early study established Ginzburg's signature method: reading hostile inquisitorial trial transcripts against the grain to recover the authentic oral culture of early modern peasants.

### *The Cheese and the Worms*
Ginzburg's most celebrated work, *The Cheese and the Worms: The Cosmos of a Sixteenth-Century Miller* (*Il formaggio e i vermi*, 1976), reconstructs the mental world of Domenico Scandella, known as Menocchio, a miller from Montereale executed for heresy in 1599. Through two detailed inquisitorial trials, Ginzburg analyzes Menocchio's cosmology—in which the universe emerged out of chaos just as cheese forms out of milk, with angels and God emerging spontaneously like worms. Ginzburg argued that Menocchio's ideas resulted from a complex circular interaction between printed texts (which Menocchio read through the lens of traditional oral culture) and an ancient, subaltern peasant worldview, challenging top-down models of cultural diffusion.

### The "Exceptional Normal" and the Evidential Paradigm
Ginzburg embraced the concept of the "exceptional normal" (*l'eccezionale normale*), originally formulated by Edoardo Grendi, arguing that exceptionally detailed archival documents—such as inquisitorial trials—act as dense diagnostic windows into normal cultural assumptions that standard historical sources leave unrecorded. In his landmark methodological essay "Clues: Roots of an Evidential Paradigm" (1979), Ginzburg traced an epistemological model shared by art connoisseur Giovanni Morelli, detective Arthur Conan Doyle (Sherlock Holmes), and Sigmund Freud. This "evidential paradigm" (*paradigma indiziario*) relies on deciphering minute, marginal, and indirect clues rather than quantitative or deductive laws.

## Position in the Historiographical Current

Ginzburg's microhistory developed in close dialogue with the journal *Quaderni storici* and the book series *Microstorie* (co-edited with Giovanni Levi for Einaudi). While sharing the French *Annales* school's interest in mentalities, Ginzburg broke with its structuralist and quantitative homogenizations, insisting on individual agency and internal cultural tensions. In later works such as *Ecstasies: Deciphering the Witches' Sabbath* (1989) and *History, Rhetoric, and Proof* (1999), Ginzburg entered into vigorous debate with Hayden White and post-structuralist relativism, arguing that historical narratives remain bound by evidential proof and the distinction between truth and fiction.
