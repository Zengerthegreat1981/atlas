# Carlo Ginzburg

An Italian historian, professor at the University of Pisa and later at the University of California, Los Angeles, and one of the founders of Microhistory (Microstoria) as a method that uses an exceptional individual case to illuminate broader cultural complexities.

## What He Gave

- **The Cheese and the Worms (1976)**: his most famous work, which reconstructs the world of a sixteenth-century Italian miller (Domenico Scandella, known as Menocchio), who was burned for heresy. Relying on the records of two Inquisition trials, it reveals a layer of peasant "popular culture" that rarely leaves any direct written trace.
- **The method of the "exceptional normal"**: he formulated a central methodological principle. A single individual case documented in exceptional detail (through a trial or an investigation) is not a worthless anomaly, but a dense window onto broader cultural patterns that would have remained hidden without the chance survival of the archival record.
- **Clues (Clues, Myths, and the Historical Method, 1979)**: an influential methodological essay that compares the historian's method of reading indirect evidence (like a physician reading symptoms or a detective reading traces) with the "evidential paradigm" (Paradigma indiziario). It draws a parallel with the methods of police investigators and Freudian psychoanalysis, which read significant marginal details.

## Position in the Current

Ginzburg, together with Giovanni Levi, is considered the founder of the Italian school of microhistory. His work is distinguished from the French history of mentalities (Le Goff, Chartier) in that it uses an individual case as a tool to challenge large structural generalizations, rather than searching for a single unified collective mental structure.

## Major Works

- *The Night Battles: Witchcraft and Agrarian Cults in the Sixteenth and Seventeenth Centuries* (I benandanti, 1966).
- *The Cheese and the Worms: The Cosmos of a Sixteenth-Century Miller* (Il formaggio e i vermi, 1976).
- *Clues: Roots of an Evidential Paradigm* (article, 1979).
