# Eric Wolf

An American anthropologist born in Vienna, whose family fled to escape Nazi persecution. He conducted fieldwork in Puerto Rico, Mexico, and the South Tyrol, and is regarded as one of the leading theorists of anthropological political economy and global history.

## Biographical Context: Exile, Migration and the Anthropology of World Systems

Eric Wolf (1923–1999) was born in Vienna. His family left Austria in 1933 for Czechoslovakia, then moved to England and, in 1940, to the United States. He studied at Queens College in New York, served in the US Army in the Second World War, and earned his doctorate at Columbia University under Julian Steward in 1951. He did fieldwork in Puerto Rico (1948–1949) as part of Steward's project, and later in Mexico, studying peasant communities undergoing capitalist transformation. Wolf taught at the University of Chicago and the University of Michigan before moving to Lehman College and the Graduate Center of the City University of New York in 1971. Unlike contemporaries who focused on small, isolated groups, Wolf insisted on placing local communities within larger historical and economic systems. His early work on Mexico examined how indigenous groups adapted to Spanish colonialism and capitalist development. This perspective, that anthropological fieldwork requires an understanding of the global economic forces that shape local life, became increasingly central to his later work. Wolf engaged closely with Marxist thought and world-systems theory, combining anthropology, economic history and political economy. He received a MacArthur Fellowship in 1990 and remained an active scholar until his death in 1999, having strongly influenced how anthropologists conceive of their subjects of study.

## Theoretical Contributions: World Systems, Commodification and Peasant Agency

Wolf's central theoretical innovation is a global-historical approach in anthropology, related to world-systems thinking: the argument that no community can be understood in isolation from the global circuits of capital, trade, labor and colonialism. Beginning in the sixteenth century with European expansion, a capitalist world system emerged that incorporated societies across the globe into networks of commodity production, extraction of labor and political domination. Local "traditions" are not timeless reservoirs but historical responses to these global forces. Wolf emphasized the **political economy of colonialism**: how European conquest disrupted indigenous economies, extracted resources, reorganized labor systems (the encomienda system, slavery and wage labor), and subordinated indigenous peoples to colonial states and capitalist markets. More importantly, Wolf highlighted **peasant agency**: rather than portraying peasants as passive victims or prisoners of tradition, he showed how they adapt strategically, negotiate and sometimes resist incorporation into capitalist systems. His work on **peasant wars** showed that peasant revolution does not arise from the most exploited or the poorest groups, but from **middle peasants**: those with sufficient resources to organize and sufficient grievances to rebel when their position is threatened by capitalist commodification. He also emphasized the **cultural politics of identity**: how concepts such as "ethnicity" and "nationality" arise as responses to colonial domination and capitalist inequality, rather than as timeless cultural essences. His work on **the anthropology of Europe** (challenging the assumption that anthropology concerns only "non-Western" peoples) argued that capitalist societies also require ethnographic attention, and that the formation of Europe itself is entangled with its colonial relations.

## Major Works: Peasant Wars and Europe and the People Without History

**Peasant Wars of the Twentieth Century** (1969) examines six major peasant revolutions (Mexico 1910, Russia 1917, China 1949, Vietnam 1954, Algeria 1954 and Cuba 1959) in order to understand the material and political conditions that generate peasant rebellion. Wolf argues that peasant wars do not arise when peasants are exploited in the harshest manner, but when their position is threatened, when the commodification of agriculture or external conquest endangers their autonomy and livelihoods. By comparing these cases, Wolf identifies common structural conditions: the disruption of traditional subsistence patterns, incorporation into capitalist commodity markets, the breakdown of local power structures under colonial or capitalist forces, and the availability of ideological frameworks (nationalism, communism) that offer alternative visions. Drawing openly on Marxian analysis, the book became a standard reference in anthropology, offering a sophisticated analysis of agrarian change and rebellion.

**Europe and the People Without History** (1982) is Wolf's most important work. Its central argument is that classical anthropology constructed its objects of study as isolated, timeless and ahistorical "peoples" or "cultures," obscuring the historical reality that all these societies had for centuries been entangled in global trade networks, colonial conquests and capitalist expansion. On this view, anthropology's focus on small groups, without attention to how they arrived at their condition, hides the very processes of conquest and trade that shaped them. Wolf rewrites world history from the perspective of these interconnected societies, showing how the capitalist world system was shaped not through European ingenuity alone but through the exploitation of colonized labor, the extraction of resources and the disruption of indigenous systems. The book's methodological innovation is the study of "peoples without history" through the restoration of their historical agency and their connections to global processes.

**Envisioning Power: Ideologies of Dominance and Crisis** (1999), published in the year of his death, extends his analysis to the relation between ideas and power through three cases: the Kwakiutl, the Aztecs, and National Socialist Germany.

## Ethnographic Focus: Mexico, Puerto Rico and Peasant Communities under Capitalism

Wolf's ethnographic work centered on Mexico and Puerto Rico, communities undergoing capitalist transformation. In Mexico, he studied peasant communities and wrote a synthetic history of Mesoamerica, *Sons of the Shaking Earth* (1959), and his essays on the "closed corporate peasant community" examined how Spanish colonialism shaped Indian communities. In the 1960s he also carried out fieldwork with John W. Cole in the South Tyrol (published as *The Hidden Frontier*, 1974). Rather than treating culture as a fixed entity, he examined how it was produced through responses to colonial domination and economic change. In Puerto Rico, he studied a coffee-growing community and its place in a changing colonial economy. His ethnographic method was distinctive: he insisted on linking fine-grained local ethnography to broad historical forces. No ritual, kinship practice or pattern of land tenure can be understood except by examining how colonialism, capitalism and state policy shaped it. This approach influenced generations of anthropologists to place their own fieldwork within the framework of global political economy. His attention to **class dynamics** within communities, to how some members benefited from capitalism while others were marginalized, introduced class differentiation and inequality into anthropological analysis in ways that functionalist anthropology had obscured.

## Influence and Debate: Political Economy and Historical Anthropology

Wolf became a founding figure of **anthropological political economy** and **historical anthropology**. His insistence that anthropology must engage with history, colonialism and capitalism had a profound influence on younger scholars. His historical approach, which developed in dialogue with (and partly in criticism of) the world-system theory of Immanuel Wallerstein, became influential in development anthropology, postcolonial anthropology and global ethnography. Scholars studying indigenous peoples, peasants, working-class communities and subordinate groups have drawn on Wolf's framework to analyze how global capitalism incorporated and marginalized diverse populations. His work was foundational for **development anthropology** and for the critique of development discourse as a form of colonial domination. Some critics, however, argue that Wolf's Marxist framework **sometimes overstates** the role of capitalism, which may underestimate how local communities, indigenous agency, cultural logics and local knowledge shaped outcomes beyond capitalist logic. Others note that his focus on class sometimes marginalized attention to gender relations within peasant and working-class communities. In addition, some researchers debate whether the "world-systems" framework adequately explains the multiple non-capitalist economic systems or whether it treats capitalism as an all-encompassing force. Indigenous scholars have engaged with Wolf appreciatively in analyzing colonial disruption, but sometimes critically when discussing whether his framework adequately represents indigenous self-determination and sovereignty.

## Position on Colonialism: Decolonizing Anthropology and Global Justice

Wolf's stance on colonialism is explicitly critical. His work is fundamentally anti-colonial: it reveals how colonialism disrupted indigenous societies, extracted resources, reorganized labor and subjugated peoples. By insisting that anthropology must engage with the effects of colonialism, he helped to decolonize the discipline by rejecting the myth that societies can be studied in isolation from colonial domination. His concept of **"peoples without history"** pointedly critiques the erasure of non-European histories: the phrase captures how colonialism made subordinated peoples appear ahistorical in Western scholarship, while Wolf's project was to restore their history. In doing so, he supports anti-colonial and emancipatory projects. Some postcolonial scholars argue, however, that Wolf's framework, though anti-imperialist, still privileges European/Western analytical categories (capitalism, class and ideology) and may not sufficiently center indigenous conceptual frameworks and epistemologies. Nevertheless, his work has been important for postcolonial anthropology and for supporting indigenous sovereignty movements.

## Legacy and Contemporary Relevance

Wolf remains foundational for contemporary anthropology. **Political economy** and **historical anthropology** have become standard frameworks in the field, and Wolf is widely credited with helping to establish them. In **development anthropology** and **environmental anthropology**, researchers studying how global capitalism integrates and marginalizes communities draw on Wolf's framework. Activists and scholars working on **indigenous sovereignty** and **land rights** cite Wolf's analysis of how colonialism disrupted indigenous economies and undermined the autonomy of peasants and indigenous peoples. In the context of climate change and environmental justice, Wolf's analysis of how capitalism commodifies nature and incorporates marginal communities into extractive relations has become relevant. His work on **peasant agency and resistance** enriches contemporary studies of social movements and political struggle. Academic anthropology regards Wolf as one of the field's major theorists and historians, and his books are widely taught. **Marxist anthropology** and **critical anthropology** continue to build on Wolf's foundations. His critique of capitalism as a world system of exploitation and domination resonates with contemporary critiques of neoliberalism and imperialism.

## Gaps and Contested Interpretations

Several important debates about Wolf's work continue. First, **the power of capitalism**: some researchers argue that Wolf's world-systems framework sometimes treats capitalism as an absolute capacity, which may underestimate how local communities preserved their autonomy or how non-capitalist logics persisted. Second, **gender blindness**: Wolf's analysis of peasant wars and class struggle sometimes marginalizes the roles of women and gender relations. Feminist scholars have noted this gap and developed work that extends Wolf's framework through attention to gender. Third, **the concept of the "peasant"**: some researchers question whether "peasant" is a precise enough category or whether it obscures significant social differences. Fourth, **cultural resistance**: some researchers worry that Wolf's emphasis on material economic factors and class struggle sometimes marginalizes cultural and ideological resistance and alternative value systems. Fifth, **colonialism and indigenous resistance**: although Wolf's analysis of colonialism developed considerably, some indigenous scholars argue that his framework concentrates more on how colonialism incorporated societies than on sustained indigenous resistance and cultural survival. Sixth, **the persistence of non-capitalist logics**: some researchers argue that Wolf's analysis may overstate the totalizing effects of capitalism and underestimate how gift and reciprocity economies and redistributive systems persist even within capitalist contexts. Finally, **temporality and periodization**: historians and anthropologists debate whether Wolf's periodized account of capitalist expansion and the formation of the world system adequately captures regional variation and alternative pathways of global interconnection.
