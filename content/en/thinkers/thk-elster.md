# Jon Elster

A Norwegian philosopher and sociologist (born 1940), a professor at Columbia University, and an important influence on "analytical sociology" through his emphasis on the importance of precise psychological and rational mechanisms in explaining social action.

## What He Contributed

- **Critique of functionalism and explanatory Marxism**: In his early works he criticized the uncritical "functional" use of concepts such as class consciousness or collective interest in traditional Marxism. He demanded a more precise explanation that identifies the actual psychological and behavioral mechanism at the individual level linking any alleged social cause to its outcome, instead of assuming that a "function" or "class interest" explains the phenomenon in itself without detailing the intermediate mechanism.
- **Social mechanisms**: In *Nuts and Bolts for the Social Sciences* (1989) he stressed that sound sociological explanation always requires specifying a particular causal "mechanism" linking cause to effect, rather than settling for an abstract general law or a statistical correlation.
- **Bounded rationality and the emotions**: He later developed influential analyses of the limits of individual rationality (such as the phenomenon of "sour grapes," in which the individual readjusts his desires to fit what can actually be achieved) and of the role of emotions in explaining individual decisions (developed at length in *Alchemies of the Mind*, 1999), extending the framework of analytical sociology beyond pure rationality.

## Historical Context

Jon Elster was born in Oslo in 1940 and studied philosophy and the social sciences in Norway before completing his studies in Paris under Raymond Aron, where he was influenced both by the Anglo-Saxon analytic philosophy tradition and by French social theory. He worked as a professor at the universities of Oslo and Chicago before settling academically at Columbia University in the United States, holding positions in both the philosophy and political science departments, and later held a chair at the Collège de France (2006-2011), which reflects the interdisciplinary nature of his intellectual project, hard to confine to a single academic field.

## Main Ideas or Method

In his early works on Marxism (including *Making Sense of Marx*, 1985), Elster criticized the uncritical "functional" use of concepts such as class consciousness or collective interest in traditional Marxist explanation. He demanded a more precise explanation that identifies the actual psychological and behavioral mechanism at the individual level linking any alleged social cause to its outcome, instead of assuming that a "function" or "class interest" explains the phenomenon in itself without detailing the actual intermediate mechanism that drives individuals to perform their roles. In *Nuts and Bolts for the Social Sciences* (1989) he stressed that sound sociological explanation always requires specifying a particular causal "mechanism" linking cause to effect, rather than settling for an abstract, all-encompassing general law (in the manner of the natural sciences) or for a statistical correlation alone without a causal explanation. In *Sour Grapes* (1983) he later developed influential analyses of the limits of individual rationality. He borrowed Aesop's tale of the fox who persuades himself that the grapes he could not reach are "sour" anyway, and proposed that individuals often readjust their desires and preferences retroactively to fit what can actually be achieved, not the reverse, which complicates any simple model of free rational choice.

## Key Works or Representatives

Elster's writing on mechanisms, together with the volume *Social Mechanisms* edited by Peter Hedström and Richard Swedberg (1998), helped provide the philosophical-methodological foundation of the "Analytical Sociology" program, which Hedström later developed in a more systematic form in *Dissecting the Social* (2005). Elster's other major works include *Ulysses and the Sirens* (1979), on precommitment and imperfect rationality, *Explaining Technical Change* (1983), *The Cement of Society* (1989) on social norms and cooperation, and *Alchemies of the Mind* (1999). Elster also worked on a comparative analysis of transitional justice in societies emerging from authoritarian regimes or civil conflicts, most notably in *Closing the Books* (2004).

## Impact and Critique

Elster provided a deeply influential philosophical-methodological foundation for the contemporary analytical sociology program, stressing that any trustworthy social explanation must identify the actual mechanism at the level of individual action, and not settle for an abstract overall generalization or a superficial statistical correlation. Elster has sometimes been criticized on the grounds that his strict methodological insistence on specifying precise individual mechanisms may make social theorizing slower and more fragmented, and make it harder to build grand comprehensive theoretical generalizations in the manner of the classical traditions in sociology.

## A Methodological Note

Elster distinguishes carefully between "functional explanation," which he rejects methodologically in its naive form (assuming that a beneficial outcome for some system on its own explains the existence of the cause that produced it), and "explanation through mechanisms," which always requires specifying the actual causal link connecting individual motives to the observed social outcome. This distinction has been widely discussed in debates on the standards of social explanation.
