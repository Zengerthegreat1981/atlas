# George C. Homans

An American sociologist (1910–1989), a professor at Harvard University for many long decades, and the founder of behavioral social exchange theory, who developed an explanation of social behavior drawn from behavioral psychology and elementary economics, as a direct response to the structural abstraction of his Harvard colleague Talcott Parsons.

## What He Contributed

- **Social exchange theory**: In *Social Behavior: Its Elementary Forms* (1961) he argued that social interaction, at its deepest core, is a process of "exchange" in which each party seeks to maximize "rewards" (material or symbolic, such as recognition and affection) and minimize "costs" (effort or loss). From this starting point he formulated several general behavioral "propositions" borrowed from Skinner's behavioral reinforcement theory and elementary economic principles of marginal utility.
- **Methodological reductionism**: He strongly rejected the idea of "social structure" as an independent entity with its own explanation independent of individuals (as in Émile Durkheim and Talcott Parsons), insisting that all social phenomena, however complex they appear at the level of overall observation, can ultimately be methodologically reduced to repeated individual interactions that can be fully explained by simple behavioral principles at the level of the individual.

## His Place in the Current

Homans was an outspoken methodological critic of Parsonian structural functionalism, accusing it of being merely a descriptive classificatory system without real causal explanatory power. He founded a whole current in American sociology ("exchange theory") that Peter Blau later developed in a more structural direction, restoring the importance of the influence of social structures on the conditions of exchange itself, and his propositions were a point of reference for later sociological rational choice theory, such as that of James Coleman.

## The Rationality Proposition

Homans also formulated the "rationality proposition" that unifies the logic of his earlier propositions: when an individual faces a choice among several possible actions, he chooses the action for which he expects the highest product of the value of the expected reward multiplied by the probability of its actual attainment. This brings his theory close to the logic of economic rational choice theory, although he remained insistent that the "value" meant goes beyond material utility to include symbolic elements such as social approval, status, and affection, which distinguished his formulation from the purely economistic reduction that he himself criticized in some of the more abstract proponents of rational choice theory.

## Academic Career

Homans was born in Boston into a wealthy family whose ancestry included the Adams family of Massachusetts, and studied English literature before turning to sociology by joining the informal "Pareto circle" at Harvard University during the 1930s, a discussion group around L. J. Henderson that also drew other Harvard scholars, influenced by the ideas of the Italian sociologist Vilfredo Pareto on elites and non-logical action. In *The Human Group* (1950) Homans reanalyzed material from the Hawthorne studies of the Western Electric plant, among other field studies, which formed an early empirical basis for his later ideas on small-scale social exchange within work groups. He spent most of his career as a professor at Harvard University, where for decades he worked in the same department that Parsons dominated intellectually, despite the radical theoretical disagreement between them over the nature of correct sociological explanation.

## Elementary Behavior as an Explanatory Foundation

Homans distinguished between "elementary behavior," which occurs in small-scale face-to-face interactions among a limited number of individuals, and the larger, more complex social structures (institutions, states, markets). He considered elementary behavior the ultimate explanatory foundation of all other social phenomena, however large and apparently complex, for he held that even the largest bureaucratic or economic institutions are, in the final analysis, nothing but a vast accumulation of millions of repeated elementary exchanges among individuals each seeking to maximize his own gains, and that any sociological explanation that ignores this individual foundation in favor of independent structural abstractions remains, from his perspective, methodologically incomplete.

## The Distributive Justice Proposition

Among his further influential contributions is the concept of "distributive justice": individuals expect their rewards to be proportionate to their investments and costs relative to their peers in a similar situation, and the violation of this expected proportion, whether through relative deprivation or even through an undeserved surplus of reward that the individual himself feels uncomfortable about, generates measurable tension and social discontent. This idea later influenced studies of organizational equity and the social psychology of justice.

## The Human Group and the English Villagers

In *The Human Group* Homans treated small groups through three mutually related classes of variables — activities, interactions, and sentiments — and distinguished an external system, through which the group meets demands from its surroundings, from an internal system that grows out of interaction among members. The book reworked several field studies, among them the Bank Wiring Observation Room of the Hawthorne research, William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (the Norton Street gang), and Raymond Firth's study of Tikopia. *English Villagers of the Thirteenth Century* (1941) applied a comparable analysis of related variables to a medieval English agrarian community, linking open and non-open field systems, nucleated or dispersed settlement, and partible or impartible inheritance.

## Named Propositions and the 1964 Address

Alongside the rationality and distributive-justice propositions, Homans set out elementary propositions drawn from behavioral psychology: the success proposition (an action that has been rewarded is more likely to be repeated), the stimulus proposition (a situation resembling one in which an action was rewarded is more likely to call out that action), the value proposition (the more valuable the result, the more likely the action), the deprivation-satiation proposition (the more often a person has recently received a reward, the less valuable a further unit of it becomes), and the aggression-approval proposition (when an expected reward is withheld the person is apt to be angry and to act aggressively; when the reward exceeds what was expected the person is apt to approve the source and to repeat the action). In the presidential address "Bringing Men Back In," delivered at the 1964 meeting of the American Sociological Association and published in the *American Sociological Review*, he argued that an explanation must show how a phenomenon follows from general propositions in a deductive system, and that functionalist statements about the conditions of social equilibrium do not yield such conclusions, whereas propositions that do explain turn out to be propositions about the behavior of persons.

## Career Landmarks and Later Exchange Research

Homans graduated from Harvard College in 1932 in English literature, without a later doctorate. Two years later he published *An Introduction to Pareto: His Sociology* with Charles P. Curtis, Jr. From 1934 to 1939 he was a junior fellow of Harvard's Society of Fellows. He joined the Harvard faculty in 1939, served as a naval officer during the Second World War, and in 1946 became an original member of the Department of Social Relations. He was president of the American Sociological Association in 1963–1964 and, from 1970 to 1975, the first chairman of Harvard's revived Department of Sociology. Other books include *Marriage, Authority, and Final Causes* (1955), with David M. Schneider, the essay collection *Sentiments and Activities* (1962), and *The Nature of Social Science* (1967), which restates his account of deductive explanation. Exchange theory was carried further by Richard Emerson's power-dependence analysis and by later research on exchange in networks. Critics held that a psychological reduction of this kind does not account for emergent features of norms and institutions, and that the propositions are too general to explain complex social phenomena.
