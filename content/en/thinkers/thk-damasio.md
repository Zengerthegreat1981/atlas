# Antonio Damasio

A Portuguese-American neuroscientist, university professor, and one of the most important contemporary theorists of the relationship between the brain, consciousness and feeling. His work is a bridge between neuroscience, philosophy and clinical psychology, and he is among the most widely cited neuroscientists writing on the "self."

## What He Contributed

### The Somatic Marker Hypothesis (1994)

In his foundational work **Descartes' Error (1994)**, Damasio formulated his pioneering theory:
- Individuals with damage to the **ventromedial prefrontal cortex** (emotional decisions) make disastrous decisions, despite their intelligence and intact memory.
- The reason: they lost the **bodily signals** (somatic markers) that link decisions to emotions.
- On his account, "pure" rational decision (as Descartes imagined it) is not how people actually decide: decisions are biased by somatic markers (heartbeat, changes in the stomach, muscle tension).

The hypothesis challenged Cartesian dualism (mind/body) and drew new attention to the role of **emotion in rational thought**, though it remains debated (see Criticism).

### Theory of Consciousness (1999)

In **The Feeling of What Happens (1999)**, Damasio developed a theory of consciousness:
- **Core Consciousness**: the immediate feeling of the world and the self in the present. Its basis: interoception and the state of the body.
- **Extended Consciousness**: the feeling of the self across time, remembering the past and imagining the future. Its basis: memory, language, the sense of self.

Damasio criticizes the idea of "pure consciousness" without a body. Consciousness needs a **body** that feels, and the self needs **memory**.

### Homeostasis as the Origin of Mind and Culture (2018)

In **The Strange Order of Things (2018)**, Damasio developed a broader framework:
- **Homeostasis**: not merely keeping the internal state within its limits but, for Damasio, the **original drive** from which life, emotion, mind and culture emerged. He uses the word in an expanded sense: striving toward a state that is not merely viable but **flourishing**.
- **Emotion**: a rapid bodily response to the environment (a change in heartbeat, dilation of the pupil, and so on).
- **Feeling**: the perception of the emotion from within (what you feel when your heart pounds).
- **Consciousness**: the feeling of the self in time.
- **Culture**: a natural outcome of consciousness.

Damasio says that **emotions and feeling precede culture**: they are an invention of life itself, not an invention of the human brain.

## Biography and Formation

Antonio Damasio was born in Lisbon on 25 February 1944, received his medical degree from the University of Lisbon in 1969 and then a doctorate in medical sciences in 1974, and moved to the United States in 1976. He worked as a professor and head of the Department of Neurology at the University of Iowa until 2005, then moved to the University of Southern California, where he directs the Brain and Creativity Institute and holds the position of professor of neuroscience, psychology and philosophy. Throughout his career he collaborated with his wife Hanna Damasio, a specialist in neuroimaging, and with Antoine Bechara and Daniel Tranel. His work started from observing patients who had suffered specific brain damage, the school of clinical neurology that infers the function of a region from the deficit resulting from its injury.

## The Clinical Foundation: Patients and Experiments

The somatic marker hypothesis began with patients such as "Elliot," a man who was successful in his work and who, after the removal of a tumor from the frontal lobe, lost the ability to make simple everyday decisions despite intact intelligence, memory and speech; he was described in the study by Eslinger and Damasio in the journal Neurology in 1985. Damasio also offered a re-reading of the famous case of Phineas Gage, published in the journal Science in 1994 under the title "The Return of Phineas Gage," in which he reconstructed the path of the iron rod through the skull to conclude that the injury had affected the ventromedial prefrontal cortex. Then came the most famous experiment, by Bechara, Damasio, Tranel and Damasio in Science in 1997, titled "Deciding Advantageously Before Knowing the Advantageous Strategy," the "Iowa Gambling Task": participants draw cards from four decks, two of which are profitable in the long run and two losing. Healthy participants learned to avoid the losing decks and showed an anticipatory skin response before they could explain the strategy, whereas patients with ventromedial prefrontal damage continued to choose the losing decks without showing those responses. The experiment was taken as evidence that emotional signals operate before explicit awareness, an interpretation that has been contested.

## Influence

Damasio influenced the founding of the field of "affective and social neuroscience," as well as neuroeconomics, neuromarketing and the philosophy of mind, and became a reference point in the debate over the role of emotion in rationality. His books have been translated into many languages and have influenced readers outside the discipline, among them *Looking for Spinoza* (2003), in which he connected Spinoza's ideas about emotion and the body with modern neuroscience. In *Feeling & Knowing: Making Minds Conscious* (2021) he summarized his conception that feelings are the foundation of consciousness and that interoception precedes cognition.

## Criticism

Many critics directed their objection at the interpretation rather than the phenomenon: Maia and McClelland (PNAS, 2004) argued that participants in the Iowa task know the correct strategy earlier than Damasio thought, so there is no need to assume unconscious signals. Dunn, Dalgleish and Lawrence reviewed the evidence in 2006 in Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews and concluded that the hypothesis is not specified precisely enough to be tested well, and that the evidence for it is less secure than often claimed. Affective neuroscientists such as Joseph LeDoux and Jaak Panksepp offered competing accounts of emotion and feeling, giving more weight to dedicated brain circuits than to perception of the body. In addition, Damasio's most recent formulations, on homeostasis and the origin of culture, are closer to speculative philosophy than to empirical hypotheses.

## His Place in the Field

- **Versus Descartes**: he rejected mind/body dualism. He said that the mind is embodied in the body.
- **Versus Freud**: he agrees with him that much of what directs behavior takes place outside awareness, but he replaces the Freudian instinctual apparatus with a measurable **physiological basis**: the body maps in the brain and homeostatic loops.
- **Versus William James**: he re-grounded James's theory of emotion (the James-Lange theory) with modern neural data.
- **Versus LeDoux and Panksepp**: he is with them in affective neuroscience, but he focused more on consciousness and feeling, whereas LeDoux focused on fear, and Panksepp on the primary emotional systems.

## His Most Important Works

- **Descartes' Error (1994)**: the somatic marker.
- **The Feeling of What Happens (1999)**: the theory of consciousness.
- **Self Comes to Mind (2010)**: the theory of the embodied self.
- **The Strange Order of Things (2018)**: the theory of emotions, culture and consciousness.
