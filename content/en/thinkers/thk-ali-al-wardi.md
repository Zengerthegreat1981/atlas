# Ali Al-Wardi

Ali Al-Wardi (1913-1995) was an Iraqi sociologist and public intellectual, a leading figure in the development of modern sociology in Iraq. He is known for interpreting Iraqi society through the tension between bedouin and urban values and for books that reached readers well beyond the university.

## Life and Context

Al-Wardi was born in Kadhimiya, now part of Baghdad. He studied at the American University of Beirut and then at the University of Texas, where he completed a doctorate around 1950 with work on Ibn Khaldun. Returning to Iraq, he taught sociology at the University of Baghdad. His life spanned the end of Ottoman rule, the British Mandate, the monarchy and the republican governments that followed, and this turbulent political history supplied much of the material for his analysis. He died in 1995.

## Main Ideas

- Bedouinism and urban civilisation: drawing on Ibn Khaldun's contrast between nomadic and settled life, Al-Wardi argued that Iraqi society was marked by a long struggle between tribal values, such as solidarity, honour and the prestige of force, and the values of settled life.
- Duality of personality: he suggested that individuals exposed to conflicting value systems could profess one set of ideals while acting according to another.
- Social dissonance: rapid and uneven change, in which new institutions and ideas coexist with older loyalties, produces strain and contradiction.
- Critique of preaching: he argued that moral exhortation which ignores social conditions and human nature does little to change conduct, and he criticised preachers who served rulers.

## Major Works

- *The Personality of the Iraqi Individual* (1951)
- *Preachers of the Sultans* (1954)
- *The Farce of the Human Mind* (1955)
- *The Logic of Ibn Khaldun* (1962)
- *A Study in the Nature of Iraqi Society* (1965)
- *Social Glimpses of Modern Iraqi History* (several volumes, from 1969)

## Method

Al-Wardi worked mainly as an interpretive historical sociologist. He combined historical narratives, travel accounts, personal observation and concepts from sociology and social psychology, and he wrote in an accessible style. He treated Ibn Khaldun as a source of analytical tools for understanding modern society, not only as a historical figure.

## Influence and Criticism

His books stimulated lasting debate in Iraq and the wider Arab world about identity, tribalism, religion and sectarianism, and they provoked criticism from religious, literary and political opponents. Later readers have questioned how far generalisations about an Iraqi personality can be supported without systematic data, and whether the bedouin and urban contrast simplifies a society divided along many other lines, including class, region and political power. His work nevertheless remains a common starting point for discussion of Iraqi social history.
