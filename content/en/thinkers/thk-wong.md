# Paul T. P. Wong

Paul T. P. Wong (1937–2024) was a Chinese-Canadian clinical psychologist, researcher, and educator who carried Viktor Frankl's tradition of logotherapy and meaning-oriented psychology into contemporary empirical science and positive psychology. A professor emeritus at Trent University in Ontario, Wong was the founding president of the International Network on Personal Meaning (INPM) and the Meaning-Centered Counselling Institute in Toronto, as well as the founding editor-in-chief of the *International Journal of Existential Psychology and Psychotherapy*.

## Context and Intellectual Synthesis

Wong was born in China in 1937 and emigrated to Canada, completing his PhD in psychology at the University of Toronto in 1970. His intellectual project stands at the crossroads of three major traditions:
1. **Franklian Logotherapy and Existential Analysis**: The foundational conviction that the will to meaning is the primary human motivation, and that meaning can be discovered even within inevitable suffering.
2. **Empirical and Cross-Cultural Psychology**: The development of psychometrically sound instruments to measure subjective meaning, death attitudes, and coping strategies across diverse ethnic and cultural backgrounds.
3. **Eastern Philosophical Dialectics**: Drawing on Taoist and Confucian perspectives (specifically the yin-yang dialectic of complementary opposites), Wong conceptualizes psychological flourishing not as the elimination of negative states, but as their dynamic integration.

## Core Contributions and Frameworks

### Existential Positive Psychology (Positive Psychology 2.0)
In his influential paper *"Positive Psychology 2.0: Towards a Balanced Interactive Model of the Good Life"* (2011), Wong offered a critique of the initial wave of positive psychology associated with Martin Seligman. Wong argued that the first wave was culturally biased toward Western individualism and hedonism, naive in its focus on positive emotions and optimism, and deficient in addressing the reality of unavoidable human suffering, trauma, and death.

Wong formulated **Existential Positive Psychology (PP 2.0)**, which posits that genuine, sustainable flourishing arises from courageously confronting and transforming existential adversity rather than avoiding it. Suffering and distress are viewed not as pathogenetic failures to be eradicated, but as fertile soil for spiritual growth, tragic optimism, and deep meaning.

### Meaning Therapy (MT) and the PURE Model
Wong developed **Meaning Therapy** (formerly Meaning-Centered Counselling), an integrative, pluralistic therapy designed to help clients navigate crisis, depression, and loss. Central to this approach is the **PURE** model:
- **Purpose**: Clarifying core life directions, values, and attainable goals.
- **Understanding**: Making coherent sense of one's identity, lived experience, and suffering.
- **Responsible Action**: Committing to ethical choices, self-transcendence, and constructive behavioral steps.
- **Evaluation**: Continuously assessing whether one's chosen path yields authentic meaning and life satisfaction.

To help clients navigate acute trauma and unchangeable suffering, Wong devised the **ABCDE** intervention framework:
- **A (Acceptance)**: Acknowledging reality and emotional pain without denial.
- **B (Belief)**: Cultivating faith, hope, and trust in life's ultimate value.
- **C (Commitment)**: Committing to actionable, meaningful goals and personal growth.
- **D (Discovery)**: Uncovering new perspectives, lessons, and spiritual insights from adversity.
- **E (Evaluation)**: Reviewing progress and celebrating small existential victories.

### Death Attitudes and the DAP-R
In collaboration with Gary T. Reker and George Gesser, Wong developed the **Death Attitude Profile-Revised** (DAP-R, 1994), a standardized instrument that treats attitudes toward death as multidimensional rather than as fear alone. The DAP-R distinguishes five distinct orientations toward death:
1. **Fear of Death**: Dread of pain, non-being, or the unknown.
2. **Death Avoidance**: Refusal to think or speak about death.
3. **Neutral Acceptance**: Perceiving death as an inevitable, natural biological fact.
4. **Approach Acceptance**: Viewing death as a peaceful transition to an afterlife.
5. **Escape Acceptance**: Viewing death as a welcome release from unendurable psychological or physical agony.

## Reception and Critique

Wong's integration of meaning-centered existentialism with quantitative research has been widely adopted in palliative care, oncology counseling, bereavement support, and multicultural clinical practice. His edited volume *The Human Quest for Meaning* (1998; 2nd ed. 2012) is a widely cited reference on meaning research.

Critics have noted that the concept of "meaning" remains broad and multifaceted, raising challenges for precise construct operationalization. Others argue that his critique of early positive psychology overstated its neglect of adversity, or that his clinical models blend psychological interventions with spiritual philosophies in ways that may feel prescriptive for purely secular clients. Nonetheless, his work remains a prominent bridge between existential therapy and empirical well-being research.
