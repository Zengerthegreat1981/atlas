# Paul Wong

Paul T. P. Wong is a Canadian psychologist of Chinese origin who carried Viktor Frankl's line of meaning into a contemporary wave that joins it with positive psychology. He is one of the few non-Western voices in this field. He was a professor at Trent University in Peterborough, Ontario, founded and chaired the International Network on Personal Meaning, and heads the Meaning-Centered Counselling Institute in Toronto.

## Existential Positive Psychology

He combined the tradition of meaning with positive psychology, on one essential condition: that the dark side not be denied. Meaning is made from within suffering, not in spite of it, and flourishing requires absorbing pain, loss and death rather than escaping them. He calls this approach existential positive psychology and has developed it in meaning-centered counselling and meaning therapy, an approach to the human need for meaning. One summary of the model is the acronym PURE: purpose, understanding, responsible action and evaluation.

## The Second Wave

In "Positive psychology 2.0: Towards a balanced interactive model of the good life" (*Canadian Psychology*, 2011) he criticised the first wave of positive psychology: focusing on positive emotions and strengths alone is superficial and dishonest because it neglects the pain, limits and loss inseparable from life. The second wave integrates the two sides, recognising that negative thoughts and feelings can be a route to maturity. The term has since been adopted by other scholars and organisations.

## Culture, Death and Meaning

Wong worked on the cultural dimension of meaning and of coping with stress, editing with Lilian C. J. Wong the *Handbook of Multicultural Perspectives on Stress and Coping* (2006). He also studied attitudes toward death and contributed to the Death Attitude Profile-Revised (DAP-R, 1994, with Reker and Gesser), which distinguishes three kinds of death acceptance: neutral (death as a natural fact), approach (death as a gateway to an afterlife) and escape (death as release from a painful life). The distinction shows that acceptance of death is not one thing and that its effects on well-being differ with its kind. He also developed the Personal Meaning Profile, described in the book *The Human Quest for Meaning* (1998), and helped found the *International Journal of Existential Psychology and Psychotherapy*.

## Main Edited Works

The Human Quest for Meaning: A Handbook of Psychological Research and Clinical Applications (1998, edited with Prem Fry), whose second edition under the revised title *The Human Quest for Meaning: Theories, Research, and Applications* appeared in 2012. It collects research on meaning, health, death, religion and culture.

## Position in the Field

Wong stands at the meeting point of three currents: Frankl's meaning, empirical research on well-being, and a comparative cultural perspective. He differs from the first positive psychology in his stance toward pain, and from philosophical existentialism in adopting measurement. He is closer to practice and field research than to building a philosophical system.

## The State of His Evidence

Stronger than the school's average because it lends itself to measurement, using instruments such as the Personal Meaning Profile and the death attitude scale, and weaker than the branches of palliative care, where larger trials of meaning-based therapy have been run. Criticism centres on the breadth and vagueness of the concept of meaning, the reliance of its measures on self-report, and the unclear boundary between the model and religion or spirituality. Some also argue that announcing a "second wave" exaggerates what came before, since early positive psychologists also dealt with suffering. His ideas circulate more in counselling than in controlled trials.
