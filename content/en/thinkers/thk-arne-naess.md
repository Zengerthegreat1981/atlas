# Arne Naess

A Norwegian philosopher (1912–2009), professor at the University of Oslo, and the originator of the term "deep ecology" and a leading figure of the movement. He began as a specialist in logical empiricism, semantics and the philosophy of science, was influenced by Spinoza and Gandhi, and in 1973 introduced the distinction between "shallow" and "deep" ecology, which made him a central figure of environmental philosophy.

## Life and Context

Arne Dekke Eide Naess was born in Slemdal, Oslo, in 1912. He studied at the University of Oslo, spent time in Vienna in the early 1930s, where he met members of the Vienna Circle, and in Paris, and received his doctorate in 1936. At twenty-seven, in 1939, he was appointed professor of philosophy at Oslo, becoming the youngest holder of such a chair in Norway at the time, and he remained there until 1970, when he retired early in order to devote himself to ecological and political work. During the German occupation of Norway he was involved in the resistance. He was also a dedicated mountaineer who led a first ascent of Tirich Mir in the Hindu Kush in 1950 and who kept a mountain hut, Tvergastein, where he did much of his writing. He died in January 2009.

## Main Ideas

**Deep ecology.** In a short 1973 article in the journal *Inquiry*, "The Shallow and the Deep, Long-Range Ecology Movement," Naess contrasted a shallow movement concerned mainly with pollution and resource depletion for the benefit of humans, with a deep movement that questions underlying values and asks for a fundamental change in how we relate to nature. Its central claims are that non-human life has intrinsic value independent of its usefulness to humans, that richness and diversity of life forms are valuable in themselves, and that human interference with the non-human world is excessive.

**The "Ecosophy T" and self-realization.** Naess called his own philosophy "ecosophy T," named after his mountain hut, and presented it as one personal articulation among many possible ones. Drawing on Spinoza and on Gandhi, he developed the idea of an expanding Self with a capital S: through identification with other beings, a person comes to see their interests as part of one's own, a process called "self-realization."

**The platform.** With George Sessions he formulated an eight-point "platform" in 1984 that individuals from varied religious or philosophical backgrounds could accept, so that agreement on policy would not require agreement on ultimate premises.

**Empirical semantics and pluralism.** Earlier in his career he argued for empirical investigation of how ordinary people actually use philosophical terms, and he defended a form of scepticism and pluralism, stressing that there are many legitimate ways of interpreting the same text or question.

## Major Works

His early works include *Erkenntnis und wissenschaftliches Verhalten* (1936), *Interpretation and Preciseness* (1953) and *Scepticism* (1968). His environmental philosophy appeared in *Økologi, samfunn og livsstil* (1974), later translated and revised as *Ecology, Community and Lifestyle* (1989). Other works include *Gandhi and Group Conflict* (1974) and his writings on Spinoza. His collected works were published in English as *Selected Works of Arne Naess* (2005).

## Method

Naess combined analytic habits, such as close attention to meaning and to how terms are used, with a more holistic, intuitive style in ecosophy. He was explicit that his "platform" is not a deduction from first principles. Instead there are layers: ultimate philosophical or religious premises (level one), the platform principles (level two), more concrete policy conclusions (level three), and particular actions (level four). He called this the "apron diagram," and it was meant to make dialogue and cooperation possible across different worldviews. He also valued direct experience of nature, and he emphasised practice and lifestyle as well as theory.

## Influence and Reception

Deep ecology inspired radical environmental movements and organisations, such as Earth First!, and had a strong effect on environmental ethics, ecophilosophy and ecopsychology, including the work of writers like Bill Devall, George Sessions, Joanna Macy and Fritjof Capra. Naess was one of the figures behind the debate on whether nature has intrinsic value, which became a defining topic of environmental ethics. In Norway he was a public intellectual and received several honours, including the Fritt Ord Honorary Award.

## Criticisms and Debates

Critics from social ecology, notably Murray Bookchin, accused deep ecology of misanthropy and of ignoring the social causes of environmental destruction, such as capitalism and hierarchy. Ecofeminists argued that deep ecology's focus on anthropocentrism overlooked androcentrism, and writers from the global South, notably Ramachandra Guha, charged that its emphasis on wilderness preservation reflects Western, privileged concerns and can harm poor communities. Philosophers have also questioned the metaphysical basis of self-realization and whether the intrinsic value of all life forms can be defended without implying either that all lives have equal value or that human needs matter little. Naess himself held that these value statements are not strict equalities and that vital human needs may take priority, and he stressed that the movement is pluralistic.
