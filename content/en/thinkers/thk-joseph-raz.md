# Joseph Raz

An Israeli-British jurist (1939–2022), born in Mandatory Palestine, who studied law at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem before moving to Oxford to pursue a doctorate under the supervision of **H.L.A. Hart** in the 1960s. He later became a fellow of Balliol College and Professor of the Philosophy of Law at Oxford, and also held posts at Columbia University in New York and King's College London. He was co-editor, with Penelope Bulloch, of the Postscript to *The Concept of Law* after Hart's death (1994 edition), and the most prominent theorist of **exclusive legal positivism** in the generation after Hart.

## The Sources Thesis: The Validity of Law Is Known by Its Source Alone

In *The Authority of Law* (1979), Raz develops a strict position known as the **Sources Thesis**. The existence and validity of a legal rule can be verified **by reference to its social source alone** (who enacted it, and under what procedure), with no need to refer to moral arguments. This leads him to reject what Hart allowed in his Postscript: that the rule of recognition may include a moral criterion among the conditions of validity. See **exclusive and inclusive positivism** for a detailed account of this dispute.

## Why Exclusivity Matters: The Argument from Authority

Raz justifies his position with his theory of **authority**. The function of law is to operate as an **authoritative** guide to conduct, one that replaces our direct moral reckoning in every situation, saving effort and enabling social coordination. To perform this function, it must be possible to **determine its content without reference to the moral arguments that it was supposed to replace in the first place**. Otherwise, law loses its practical advantage as an independent guide to action.

## Exclusive versus Inclusive Positivism

Raz takes his position in a debate that arose within legal positivism itself after Hart. Hart, in the Postscript to *The Concept of Law*, allowed the rule of recognition to incorporate a moral criterion (such as justice or human rights) among the conditions of validity of a legal rule. This position is known as "inclusive" or "soft" positivism, and it was later defended by **Jules Coleman**, W. J. Waluchow and others. Raz argues that this leniency empties positivism of its original content. He insists that the validity of a rule must **always and only** be known by its social source, with no possibility of introducing a moral criterion, even by reference.

## The Service Conception of Authority

Raz builds his argument on a broader theory of the nature of legitimate **authority**, which he calls the "Service Conception of Authority." Legitimate authority is that which helps those subject to it act in accordance with the reasons that apply to them more effectively than if they tried to assess those reasons directly themselves (the "Normal Justification Thesis"), so that its directives replace their direct calculations. For legal directives to perform this intermediary function, it must be possible to identify their content without returning to the arguments they were supposed to replace. Otherwise, law would lose its practical advantage as an independent guide to action. In Raz's account, the directives of an authority are meant to pre-empt (replace) the reasons they are based on, which is why their content must be identifiable independently of those reasons.

## An Echo in Islamic Political Jurisprudence on Obedience

Raz's theory of authority meets, from a distance, an Islamic juristic debate on the legitimacy and limits of obedience to the ruler (*wali al-amr*). Jurists (such as al-Mawardi in *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* and Ibn Taymiyya in *al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya*) discussed the function of the ruler's commands in discretionary punishments and regulatory policies, as a practical substitute that spares the subjects individual judgment on every question, provided they do not contravene Sharia. This parallels Raz's idea of authority as an intermediary that spares one direct calculation. It differs fundamentally from it, however. For the jurists, the upper limit of obedience to the ruler is set in advance by the scriptural text, whereas for Raz the limits of authority remain a matter determined by philosophical analysis of the practical function of authority itself, not by a transcendent textual source.

## His Style and Teaching Standing

Raz was known for an analytical style of great precision and logical rigor, shaped by the traditions of Oxford analytic philosophy, and for his ability to break complex legal concepts (such as authority, practical reason and autonomy) into primitive elements that can be analyzed separately. Over his decades at Oxford and Columbia he supervised a large number of doctoral students who later became leading contemporary legal scholars. His work became a leading current within analytic philosophy of law, in dialogue with Dworkin's interpretive approach and with the inclusive positivism represented by Coleman.

## Impact and Assessment

Raz's thesis drew a clear dividing line within contemporary legal positivism. It prompted Jules Coleman, W. J. Waluchow and others to defend inclusive positivism in response, while Raz's exclusive positivism remained the stricter version of the separation of law and morality within the positivist camp. Raz's influence also extended beyond analytic legal theory, through his work in practical philosophy and freedom, especially *The Morality of Freedom* (1986). In that work he developed a theory of personal **autonomy** and value pluralism that influenced contemporary political philosophy alongside his impact on legal theory.
