# Sherry Ortner: Practice Theory and Ritual Agency

Sherry B. Ortner (born 1941) is an American cultural anthropologist, a leading figure in feminist anthropology and one of the main advocates of the **practice approach** in anthropology, an effort to combine the study of social structure with attention to human action and agency. She is known for her early essay on gender and nature ("Is Female to Male as Nature Is to Culture?", 1974), for her ethnographic and historical work on Sherpa Buddhism and Himalayan mountaineering, for her synthetic essay "Theory in Anthropology since the Sixties" (1984), and for later ethnographies of class and culture in the United States.

## Life and Career

Ortner grew up in New Jersey and studied at Bryn Mawr College before taking her PhD in anthropology at the University of Chicago, where the symbolic anthropology of Clifford Geertz and David Schneider formed part of her training. She carried out fieldwork among the Sherpa of Nepal beginning in the 1960s. She has taught at the University of Michigan, Columbia University, and the University of California, Los Angeles, where she is Distinguished Professor of Anthropology.

## Key Works

- "Is Female to Male as Nature Is to Culture?", in *Woman, Culture, and Society*, edited by Michelle Zimbalist Rosaldo and Louise Lamphere (1974). She argued that women are widely devalued because they are symbolically associated with nature, which cultures seek to transcend, while men are associated with culture. Ortner later revised and qualified this universalizing claim, and the essay has been much criticised, but it was foundational for the anthropology of gender.
- *Sherpas through Their Rituals* (1978), an analysis of Sherpa religious practice in terms of symbols, ritual, and social relations.
- "Theory in Anthropology since the Sixties" (*Comparative Studies in Society and History*, 1984), an influential survey that traced the shift from structuralist and symbolic approaches towards a focus on practice and action.
- *High Religion: A Cultural and Political History of Sherpa Buddhism* (1989), a historical ethnography of the founding of Sherpa monasteries and the relation between religion and Sherpa social structure.
- *Making Gender: The Politics and Erotics of Culture* (1996), a collection of essays on gender, culture, and power.
- *Life and Death on Mt. Everest: Sherpas and Himalayan Mountaineering* (1999), on the relationship between Sherpas and Western climbing expeditions.
- *New Jersey Dreaming: Capital, Culture, and the Class of '58* (2003), an ethnography of her own high-school class, tracing class and social mobility.
- *Anthropology and Social Theory: Culture, Power, and the Acting Subject* (2006), a collection of essays setting out her practice approach.
- *Not Hollywood: Independent Film at the Twilight of the American Dream* (2013), a study of independent film-making.

## Main Ideas: The Practice Approach

In "Theory in Anthropology since the Sixties" and later essays Ortner argued that anthropology had moved away from both functionalism and a purely symbolic view of culture, toward the study of how people act within, reproduce, and sometimes transform social structures. She drew on Pierre Bourdieu's theory of practice, Anthony Giddens's structuration theory, and Marshall Sahlins's work on history and structure. Her view is that structure shapes action without determining it: people are agents with intentions and projects who act within historically given constraints, and their actions have consequences that can both reproduce and change those constraints.

In *Anthropology and Social Theory* she links this to the notion of "serious games": social life as a field of culturally organised activity in which actors pursue goals within relations of power and inequality. The idea allows attention to agency without assuming that actors are free of structure.

## Gender and Power

Ortner's feminist work, from the 1974 essay through *Making Gender*, is concerned with how cultural systems construct gender and how gender hierarchies are made and contested in practice. In her Sherpa work she also analysed how religious institutions, especially monasticism, were tied to the organisation of kinship, wealth, and prestige.

## Influence and Critique

Her essays on the practice approach and on resistance have been widely used in teaching anthropological theory, and her Sherpa books are standard references on Himalayan Buddhism. Critics of the 1974 essay argued that the nature/culture opposition is not universal and reflects Western categories; Ortner herself later acknowledged that criticism. Others have argued that practice theory risks privileging human intentionality and underplaying non-human or structural forces, and that "agency" can be a vague term. In "Resistance and the Problem of Ethnographic Refusal" (1995) she herself criticised romanticised portrayals of resistance in anthropology.

## Further Work and Career

Born in Newark, New Jersey, Ortner received her Chicago doctorate in 1970. Her other posts included Sarah Lawrence College and the University of California, Berkeley, and she was awarded a MacArthur Fellowship in 1990. "On Key Symbols" (1973) distinguished summarising symbols, which condense a set of attitudes, from elaborating symbols, which supply categories or scenarios for ordering action. With Harriet Whitehead she edited *Sexual Meanings* (1981), which linked ideas about gender to systems of prestige. In the essay on nature and culture, her grounds for the symbolic association of women with nature included bodily processes, social roles tied to child-rearing and ideas about psychic structure; the argument concerned cultural construction, not natural inferiority. *Sherpas through Their Rituals* used public rites to bring out tensions such as that between household self-interest and Buddhist ideals of detachment and compassion. In "Dark Anthropology and Its Others" (2016) she considered the discipline's turn to inequality and suffering alongside work on ethics, wellbeing and resistance.
