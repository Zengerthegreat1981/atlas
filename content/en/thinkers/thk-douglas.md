# Mary Douglas: Purity, Danger, and Categorical Thought

Dame Mary Douglas (born Margaret Mary Tew, 1921–2007) was a British social anthropologist whose work on classification, pollution, and risk shaped anthropology, religious studies, and the sociology of risk. Her central argument in *Purity and Danger* (1966) is that purity concerns are not mainly about hygiene. They express how a society draws boundaries between categories and treats what falls between them. She also argued that modern industrial societies use classificatory logic of the same general kind as the societies anthropologists had called traditional.

## Biographical and Intellectual Context

Mary Tew was born in San Remo, Italy, on 25 March 1921, into an English Roman Catholic family, and was educated at the Sacred Heart convent school at Roehampton. At St Anne's College, Oxford, she read philosophy, politics, and economics (B.A. 1943) and then worked at the British Colonial Office. She returned to Oxford for anthropology, studying with E. E. Evans-Pritchard and M. N. Srinivas, and took a doctorate in 1951. In 1951 she married the economist James A. T. Douglas. They had three children.

Her fieldwork was with the Lele of the Kasai, in what was then the Belgian Congo (now the Democratic Republic of the Congo), in 1949–50, with a return visit in 1953. The monograph from that research, *The Lele of the Kasai*, appeared in 1963. From 1951 she taught at University College London, receiving a personal chair around 1970 and leaving in 1977. She then spent 1977–81 at the Russell Sage Foundation in New York, was Avalon Professor of the Humanities at Northwestern University from 1981 to 1985, and afterwards held visiting appointments, including at Princeton. She was appointed CBE in 1992 and DBE in the 2007 New Year Honours, and died in London on 16 May 2007.

Her later writing moved from ethnography to consumption, institutions, risk, and the Hebrew Bible. She remained a practising Catholic, and comparison of ritual and cosmology stayed at the centre of her work. She engaged structuralism, including Claude Lévi-Strauss, while treating classification as socially situated rather than as a closed mental code.

## Core Theoretical Framework

### Purity, pollution, and order

Douglas argued that **purity and pollution are about order**. Dirt, in her formula, is **matter out of place**: nothing is dirty in itself, only relative to a system of categories. Pollution rules teach which boundaries a society treats as important, including boundaries of the body, the group, and the cosmos.

Her best-known case is the dietary law of Leviticus. Against the idea that the rules were ancient hygiene, she read them as a classification of animals by their proper sphere of life. Land animals that chew the cud and have a parted hoof fit the class; animals that only partly fit, such as the pig, are treated as abominations. She later revised this reading. In *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) she interpreted the food rules through the literary structure of the book, and she accepted that the earlier account had been too neat.

Among the Lele she analysed the cult of the pangolin. The pangolin is a scaled mammal that climbs trees and does not sit easily in ordinary animal classes. For Douglas it showed that anomaly is not only avoided. In some ritual settings it is singled out and honoured by initiates.

### The body and the social group

In *Purity and Danger* and more fully in *Natural Symbols* (1970), Douglas treated the human body as a symbol of the social body. Boundaries of the skin and the orifices, and rules about blood, food, and excreta, can stand for the boundaries of a group. She used this to compare cosmologies, not to claim that one code of the body is natural.

*Natural Symbols* sets out **grid and group**. "Group" is the strength of the boundary around a social unit. "Grid" is the strength of internal classification and regulation. Different combinations, she argued, go with different cosmologies, ritual styles, and uses of the body. With Aaron Wildavsky she applied the scheme to technological and environmental danger in *Risk and Culture* (1982): people select and rank dangers in ways that fit the form of social life they inhabit. That argument became a main source of what risk research calls cultural theory. It has been widely used and widely disputed. It does not claim that physical hazards are unreal.

### Institutions and consumption

With Baron Isherwood, *The World of Goods* (1979) treats consumption as a system of communication: goods mark categories and social relations. *How Institutions Think* (1986) argues that classification and memory are carried by institutions, drawing on Durkheim and on the sociology of knowledge. *Thought Styles* (1996) returns to the claim that standards of rationality and taste are styles sustained by particular institutions.

## Major Works

- *The Lele of the Kasai* (1963) — ethnography of Lele kinship, economy, ritual, and animal symbolism.
- *Purity and Danger: An Analysis of Concepts of Pollution and Taboo* (1966) — pollution as a marking of categorical boundaries, with the Leviticus food rules and the Lele pangolin among the main cases.
- *Natural Symbols: Explorations in Cosmology* (1970) — the body as a social symbol, and the grid-group comparison.
- *The World of Goods* (1979, with Baron Isherwood) — an anthropology of consumption.
- *Edward Evans-Pritchard* (1980) — a short study of her teacher.
- *Risk and Culture* (1982, with Aaron Wildavsky) — cultural selection of technological and environmental dangers.
- *How Institutions Think* (1986).
- *In the Wilderness* (1993) — defilement and the Book of Numbers.
- *Thought Styles: Critical Essays on Good Taste* (1996).
- *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) — a revision of her earlier reading of Leviticus.

## The Lele

The Lele are a Bantu-speaking people of the Kasai, living between the Kasai and Loange rivers on the edge of the former Kuba kingdom. They farmed, hunted, and produced raffia cloth. Douglas described a matrilineal society in which marriage, age, and control of raffia wealth gave older men considerable authority over access to wives. Hunting ritual and animal classification, including the pangolin cult, later supplied one of her main examples of how an anomalous creature can be ritually marked. The ethnography is a colonial-period study. It is the empirical base of her early theory, not a claim about classification in every society.

## Influence and Critique

Douglas's account of pollution influenced the anthropology of ritual and religion, biblical studies, food studies, and research on risk and consumption. Scholars of Judaism, Christianity, and other traditions have used her questions about dietary rules and bodily boundaries, while also correcting her readings of particular texts.

Critics have raised several objections that the work itself does not settle. The scheme can become circular if every rule is read, after the fact, as a defence of social order. Binary contrasts such as clean and unclean can flatten ambiguity. A stress on order can underplay conflict, history, and the colonial setting of the Lele research. Feminist readers have noted that an analysis of menstrual and bodily rules as cosmology still has to reckon with the constraints those rules place on women. Douglas's own later books on Numbers and Leviticus are part of this revision, especially on the dietary laws.
