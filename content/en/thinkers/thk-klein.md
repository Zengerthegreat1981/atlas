# Melanie Klein

A British psychoanalyst of Austrian origin, pioneer and founder of the "object relations" current (Object Relations Theory) and a founder of the psychoanalysis of children through play technique.

## What She Contributed

- **Play technique**: the starting point. She held that **a child's play is the equivalent of an adult's free association**, so that what the child does with dolls and small figures is read and interpreted as the patient's speech on the couch is read. This made child analysis possible without waiting for the child's language to mature, and it opened to her what Freud had not seen.
- **Positions, not stages**: the most precise thing to understand about her. She did not call them "stages" because a stage is passed through and left behind, whereas a **position** is a structure organizing experience to which one returns throughout life:
  - **The paranoid-schizoid position** (the first three or four months): the infant does not perceive a single mother but a **good breast** and a **bad breast**; the governing defense is **splitting**, and the anxiety is **persecutory** (the infant fears being attacked).
  - **The depressive position** (from about the fourth month onward): the infant realizes that the good and the bad are **one person**, that is, that the one it loves is the one it attacked in fantasy. From this arise **guilt**, **sadness**, and **the wish for reparation**. The anxiety here is not fear for the self but **fear for the loved object**. Reaching this position is, for her, a **psychological achievement**, not a setback, and from it arise concern for others and the capacity for creativity.
- **Projective identification**: her concept with the furthest reach in contemporary practice, and more complex than projection. The person does not merely attribute to another what he cannot bear in himself, but **places it in** the other and unconsciously presses on him until he **actually feels it and acts accordingly**. That is why the therapist finds himself filled with despair or anger that has no source in himself, and this becomes data about the patient, not about the therapist. **Bion** built on this the concept of **containment**, and **Sandler** built on it "role-responsiveness."
- **Envy and gratitude** (1957): her last major work and the one that provoked the most controversy. She distinguished **jealousy** (triadic: I fear a third party will take what I love) from **envy** (dyadic: I resent the good in the other because it is in him and not in me, so I want to spoil it). Envy, for her, is **innate** and derived from the death instinct, and it is what most obstructs treatment, since the patient attacks what benefits him because it comes from someone else.

## Her Place in the Current

Leader of the Kleinian school in the British Psychoanalytical Society, and the most important figure to have produced a theoretical and clinical revolution parallel to Freud's in the twentieth century.

## Limitations

There are three objections to Klein, the first of which is the strongest:

**First: attributing a complex imaginary world to an infant of a few weeks.** The theory assumes that an infant in its first months carries fantasies of attacking inside the mother's body and of spoiling the breast out of envy, and that it distinguishes between object and self. This is **not supported by developmental research**: the cognitive capacities these fantasies presuppose appear much later than she claimed. The likelier explanation among her opponents is that she **projected material from the analysis of adults and older children back onto the infant**.

**Second: innate aggression and the death instinct.** The whole edifice of envy for her rests on the **death instinct**, a Freudian concept rejected by most of the field, including most analysts. A weighty clinical position follows from it: if aggression is **innate** and not a reaction, then what happens to the child in the way of neglect or abuse becomes less important in explaining the child's illness. This is what **Fairbairn**, **Winnicott**, and **Bowlby** objected to, and after them the whole literature on trauma.

**Third: method.** Her interpretation of children was **early, deep, and explicit** (she told a three-year-old about his fantasies of his mother's body in the first sessions), which **Anna Freud** saw as dangerous for a child whose defensive apparatus had not yet formed. This was the core of the **Controversial Discussions** (1941–1945).

To this may be added that her basic early material came from **the analysis of her own children** under pseudonyms, a practice that was common at the time and is in no way acceptable today, and her daughter **Melitta Schmideberg** became one of her fiercest public opponents.

Even so, much of her remained: the positions, projective identification, and reparation are concepts used today in currents that accept nothing of her instinctual foundation.

## Her Most Important Works

The Psycho-Analysis of Children (1932) · **A Contribution to the Psychogenesis of Manic-Depressive States** (1935) · Notes on Some Schizoid Mechanisms (1946), the paper in which the paranoid-schizoid position and projective identification were presented · Envy and Gratitude (1957) · Narrative of a Child Analysis (published posthumously in 1961).
