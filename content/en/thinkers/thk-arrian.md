# Arrian

Lucius Flavius Arrianus wrote the most reliable surviving historical account of Alexander the Great. Combining high Roman imperial command experience with philosophical training, his critical evaluation of primary sources in the *Anabasis of Alexander* set enduring standards for ancient military historiography.

## Definition and Historical Significance

Arrian (c. 86–after 160 CE) was a Greek historian, philosopher, and Roman consular senator from Nicomedia in Bithynia. Writing during the Second Sophistic in clear, archaizing Attic prose, he modeled himself consciously upon Xenophon, often referring to himself as "the younger Xenophon" (*Xenophon ho neoteros*). 

His premier historical masterpiece, the *Anabasis of Alexander* (*Anabasis Alexandri*, in seven books), provided the most sober, tactically sound, and critically grounded narrative of Alexander the Great's campaigns against the Persian Empire and expedition into India. In an ancient historiographical landscape dominated by sensationalized romances and rhetorical embellishments of Alexander's life, Arrian distinguished himself by articulating and applying strict source-critical principles that prioritized verified eyewitness accounts by military professionals.

## Historical Context

Arrian was born into the Greek provincial aristocracy of Bithynia and obtained Roman citizenship. As a young man, he studied Stoic philosophy at Nicopolis under the celebrated philosopher Epictetus; Arrian took meticulous shorthand notes of his master's lectures, publishing them as the *Discourses of Epictetus* (*Diatribai*) and the *Enchiridion* (*Manual*), which remain our primary sources for late Stoic ethics.

Entering the imperial administrative service, Arrian enjoyed the patronage of Emperor Hadrian, who appointed him to the Roman Senate. He served as suffect consul (c. 129 CE) and subsequently governed the sensitive frontier province of Cappadocia as imperial legate (*legatus Augusti pro praetore*) from 131 to 137 CE. 

During this tenure, he commanded two Roman legions along the eastern frontier, successfully deterring and repelling an invasion of nomadic Alani through sophisticated tactical deployments documented in his military treatise *Ektaxis kata Alanon* (*Array against the Alans*). After retiring from Roman administrative office, he was elected eponymous archon of Athens (145/146 CE), devoting his later decades to composing historical, geographical, and hunting treatises.

## Key Contributions and Methodology

A notable feature of Arrian's work is his explicit statement of a hierarchical source-critical methodology. In the preface to the *Anabasis*, he announced that he would rely primarily on two specific primary authorities: Ptolemy, son of Lagus (the Macedonian general who became King Ptolemy I Soter of Egypt), and Aristobulus of Cassandreia (a military architect and engineer on Alexander's staff). 

Arrian defended this choice on methodological grounds:
1. Both men were personal companions and eyewitness participants in Alexander's campaigns;
2. Both composed their memoirs after Alexander's death, when neither fear of punishment nor the desire to curry imperial favor could distort their writing;
3. Ptolemy, having achieved royal status as king of Egypt, would have found mendacity especially disgraceful.

Where Ptolemy and Aristobulus agreed, Arrian accepted their narrative as established truth. Where their accounts diverged, he recorded both versions and assessed their relative probability. Furthermore, he explicitly separated these verified accounts from popular legends and rhetorical fabrications (the "Vulgate" tradition stemming from Cleitarchus), labeling the latter merely as "tales told" (*legomena*) to be reported with critical skepticism.

## Major Works

Beyond the *Anabasis*, Arrian composed an extensive corpus:
- **The *Indica***: a companion monograph to the *Anabasis* written in the Ionic dialect in imitation of Herodotus, describing the geography, flora, fauna, and caste divisions of India, and preserving the journal of Alexander's admiral Nearchus detailing the fleet's voyage from the Indus delta to the mouth of the Euphrates.
- **The *Events after Alexander* (*Ta meta Alexandron*, in ten books)**: an extensive history of the Wars of the Diadochi (Successors) down to 320 BCE, which survives only in epitomes preserved by the Byzantine patriarch Photius in his *Bibliotheca*.
- **The *Parthica* (in seventeen books)**: a contemporary history of Trajan's campaigns in Mesopotamia, preserved in fragments.
- **The *Periplus Ponti Euxini* (Circumnavigation of the Black Sea)**: an administrative and geographical report addressed directly to Emperor Hadrian detailing ports, garrisons, and distances along the Black Sea coast.

## Theoretical Framework

Arrian's historical worldview synthesized Stoic moral philosophy with practical military realism. He viewed Alexander as a figure of unmatched genius and ambition (*pothos*), driven by a superhuman desire to achieve noble deeds. However, Arrian did not hesitate to condemn Alexander's moral degeneration in his later years: his adoption of Persian court ceremonial and prostration (*proskynesis*), his bouts of heavy drinking, his murder of Cleitus the Black, and his execution of the philosopher Callisthenes. 

Arrian treated history as an arena for ethical contemplation, arguing that without philosophical wisdom and self-control, supreme worldly conquest yields personal catastrophe.

## Contemporary Relevance and Reception

Arrian remains the principal foundational text for modern historians of Alexander the Great and the Hellenistic world. Modern scholarship has generally regarded his military geography, tactical descriptions, and chronological sequence as more reliable than alternative accounts in Diodorus Siculus, Quintus Curtius Rufus, Plutarch, or Justin. His methodological preface is frequently cited in the history of historiography as an early milestone in the development of systematic source criticism.

## Limitations and Critical Perspectives

While Arrian's reliance on Ptolemy and Aristobulus yielded military clarity, modern scholars—notably A. B. Bosworth—have argued that Arrian was sometimes overly trusting of his primary authorities. Ptolemy had political interests of his own during the Wars of the Diadochi, leading him to minimize the contributions of rivals like Perdiccas and Antigonus while exaggerating his own role. Furthermore, Arrian's elite military and administrative focus left little room for the social, fiscal, and cultural transformations experienced by the subjugated Persian and Asian populations.
