# Ali Shariati

Ali Shariati (1933-1977) was an Iranian sociologist and Islamic thinker whose lectures recast Shi'i Islam as a language of social critique and political mobilization. Trained in sociology and the study of religion in Paris, he became one of the most influential ideological voices of the Iranian opposition in the decade before the 1979 revolution.

## Life and Context

Shariati was born in 1933 in the village of Mazinan in Khorasan province and grew up in Mashhad in a religious family. He studied in Mashhad and Tehran, then went to France on a state scholarship and completed a doctorate in Paris in the early 1960s. In Paris he encountered anti-colonial writing, including Frantz Fanon's work, which he helped to translate into Persian, and studied with scholars such as Louis Massignon and Georges Gurvitch. After returning to Iran he was imprisoned for political reasons, then lectured widely, notably at the Hosseiniyeh Ershad in Tehran. He left Iran in 1977 and died in Southampton, England, that year.

## Main Ideas

- Islam as a liberating ideology: religion should be read as a force for justice and against oppression, not only as ritual or inherited custom.
- "Return to the self" (bazgasht be khishtan): a call to recover indigenous cultural and religious identity against Western cultural domination and imitation.
- Critique of clerical and state-aligned religion: he contrasted a revolutionary reading of Shi'ism with a version he saw as passive and tied to established power.
- Tawhid as a worldview: divine unity read as a rejection of social hierarchies and class division.
- Critical dialogue with Marxism and existentialism, while rejecting materialism.

## Major Works

- Hajj (lectures, published in Persian in the 1970s)
- Marxism and Other Western Fallacies (English translation 1980)
- On the Sociology of Islam (English translation by Hamid Algar, 1979)
- Fatima is Fatima (Persian, 1970s)

Much of his output survives as edited lectures rather than systematic treatises.

## Method

Shariati combined comparative religion, historical sociology and polemic. He interpreted religious symbols and narratives sociologically, as models for social action, rather than offering empirical research in the strict sense.

## Influence and Criticism

His writings circulated widely among students and activists in Iran in the 1970s and are often cited as an ideological influence on the revolutionary movement, although he died before the revolution. Critics, both clerical and secular, questioned his theological credentials, his selective use of Marxist and Western concepts, and the rigor of his sociology. Scholars still debate how far his thought anticipated the political system that followed 1979.
