# Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Abd al-Malik ibn Tufayl

An Andalusian philosopher, physician and statesman, famous for his philosophical romance *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*, regarded as a major narrative-philosophical text of the Islamic heritage, which discusses whether a human being can attain the great metaphysical truths by unaided reason, apart from any teaching or transmitted revelation. He served as physician and vizier at the Almohad court, and it was he who presented Ibn Rushd to the caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf.

## Biography

Ibn Tufayl was born in Wadi Ash (Guadix) near Granada around 500 AH / 1105 CE (the year is an estimate). He studied medicine and philosophy and worked as a secretary in Granada before entering the service of the Almohads, becoming physician and chamberlain to the caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, and then a vizier close to him. He was known for his wide learning in medicine, astronomy, mathematics and philosophy, and according to the historian Abd al-Wahid al-Marrakushi in his book *al-Mu'jib fi talkhis akhbar al-Maghrib*, it was Ibn Tufayl who presented the young Ibn Rushd to the caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf and recommended him for the task of commenting on Aristotle's books, a role that contributed to launching Ibn Rushd's commentary project. Ibn Tufayl withdrew from court duties late in life, when Ibn Rushd succeeded him as physician to the caliph (c. 1182), and died in Marrakesh in 581 AH / 1185 CE, the caliph Abu Yusuf Ya'qub al-Mansur himself attending his funeral in his honor.

## What He Contributed

### *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*: Philosophy in the Form of a Romance

His best-known work, and the only philosophical one to have reached us complete, is the treatise *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*, a philosophical tale about a child who grows up alone on an isolated tropical island (either born by spontaneous generation according to the first account in the story, or cast into the sea by his mother out of fear for him according to the second), who is nursed by a gazelle, and who grows up relying only on his senses and his unaided reason. Through successive stages of life (each seven years a stage) Hayy discovers by himself, with no teacher, book or revelation, physics and anatomy (by dissecting the body of the gazelle that reared him) and astronomy, arriving at a rational knowledge that the world has one creator and that the human soul must connect with the First Principle. At the end of the story Hayy meets a man named Asal who has come from an inhabited island where people follow a transmitted law, and the two discover that what Hayy reached by pure reason corresponds in essence to what the law carries in its outward symbolic form; but their later attempt to convey the abstract rational truths to the common people fails, and they return to the isolated island to live in private contemplation.

### The Thesis of the Independence of Reason from Revelation

The central philosophical thesis of the work is the possibility that unaided human reason, without prophecy or social teaching, can reach the highest degrees of metaphysical knowledge, including knowledge of God and his unity and the necessity of concern for the afterlife. This position intersects with broader philosophical discussions of the relation between reason and revelation in al-Farabi, Ibn Sina and Ibn Bajja (whose idea of the "regime of the solitary" directly influenced Ibn Tufayl), but he presents it in a narrative form accessible to a wider audience than the specialized philosophical one. At the same time the work holds a cautious position on generalizing rational philosophical truths to the common people, since Ibn Tufayl holds that religious symbols suit the minds of the public better than abstract demonstrations, which makes the work close in spirit to al-Farabi's distinction between demonstrative and rhetorical modes of persuasion.

### The Introduction: A Critique of His Predecessors

In the introduction to *Hayy ibn Yaqzan* Ibn Tufayl surveys, in brief critical fashion, the positions of al-Farabi, Ibn Sina, al-Ghazali and Ibn Bajja on the relation between philosophy and intellectual happiness. He criticizes al-Farabi for a contradiction between his two statements, in *al-Siyasa al-madaniyya* and in his book on *al-Milla al-fadila*, about the immortality of the soul, and he describes Ibn Sina's "Eastern" project as not having disclosed its secrets fully. This passage is one of the most important historical sources for understanding how the late Andalusian philosophers read the heritage of the Eastern philosophers before them.

## Influence

*Hayy ibn Yaqzan* was rendered into Latin by Edward Pococke the Younger in 1671 under the title *Philosophus Autodidactus*, then into English, Dutch and German, and some scholars have speculated about its influence on later European thought on the "state of nature" and the formation of knowledge, and have compared Daniel Defoe's *Robinson Crusoe* with it for the shared motif of island isolation, though direct influence is debated. Within the Islamic heritage, Ibn Tufayl paved the way for his indirect disciple Ibn Rushd at the Almohad court, and *Hayy ibn Yaqzan* remained a unique model of philosophy composed in literary form, sometimes compared with al-Ma'arri's *Risalat al-Ghufran* in literary-intellectual ambition although the subject differs entirely.

## Limitation

The greatest problem facing the study of Ibn Tufayl is the loss of nearly all his other works: he is said to have written on medicine and astronomy (including a critique of Ptolemy's astronomical model mentioned by his disciple al-Bitruji). A long medical poem in rajaz metre attributed to him survives in manuscript, but his philosophy has to be judged essentially from a single text. The central thesis of *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*, that pure reason attains the great religious truths apart from any society and language, has also been subjected to methodological critique: how can sophisticated conceptual thinking (including abstract concepts such as unity and causality) form without any language or social interaction? This is a philosophical problem of the possibility of abstract thought arising apart from language that Ibn Tufayl did not treat in sufficient detail, since he passed over it narratively rather than addressing it demonstratively. Some scholars also note a deliberate ambiguity at the end of the work concerning Ibn Tufayl's final position on the usefulness of philosophy for the general public, as he seems inclined to a reserved elitist position closer to al-Ghazali's than the outward philosophical text suggests.

## Major Works

- *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*
- *al-Urjuza fi al-Tibb*, a long medical poem in rajaz metre attributed to him
- Lost works on medicine and astronomy (mentioned in the sources of his disciples, such as al-Bitruji)
