# Guy L. Beck

An American researcher in religious studies, specializing in Hinduism and sacred sound, who has taught at Tulane University, and who developed the idea of "sonic theology" as a framework for studying ritual sound and religious music in a comparative academic manner.

## Main Contribution

In his book *Sonic Theology: Hinduism and Sacred Sound* (1993; see wrk-sonic-theology-beck), Beck puts forward a central thesis: sacred sound in Hinduism (particularly the concepts of *nada* and *mantra*, and Vedic chanting) is not merely a means of transmitting theological content, but a theological category in itself. That is, spoken or chanted sound has an existential and cosmic power independent of its verbal meaning. This thesis helped bring the study of religious sound closer to the concerns of comparative religious studies.

## Comparative Expansion

Beck later broadened his framework in an edited collective volume (2006) that compares the religious sound traditions of six major religions (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism and Sikhism), thereby contributing to a sub-field distinct from secular comparative musicology (for example the tradition of Curt Sachs) through its explicit focus on the theological and ritual function of sound rather than on musical form alone.

## The Hindu Roots of the Field

Beck's first thesis is Indian in origin: the concept of *Nada Brahman* in Hindu philosophy, which sees in eternal sound the cosmic origin from which existence itself emanated, and the Indian grammatical-philosophical idea of *shabda* (sound or word), which gives the spoken word ontological and ritual weight. Beck also traces the Caitanya Bhakti movement (sixteenth century) and its communal singing practice of *kirtan* as a living model of this sonic theology, in which chanting the names of the deity aloud in a group is a salvific act in itself rather than an accompanying emotional expression.

## Methodological Questions

Beck's edited volume *Sacred Sound: Experiencing Music in World Religions* (2006) gathers contributions on Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism and Sikhism. Extending a category formulated in a Hindu context (*nada* and mantra) to other traditions raises a methodological question: is it valid to generalise it where theologies of sound differ substantially? In Islam, for example, Qur'anic recitation is understood as the speech of God, not as an eternal cosmic sound. This tension between the Indian foundation and comparative ambition is a natural point of critique.

The difficulty can be tested point by point. The Hindu model draws on schools that disagree among themselves: the Mimamsa defence of the eternal Vedic word, for instance, does not depend on a creator deity, while devotional movements centre on the names of a personal god. Elsewhere the differences widen. Mainstream Buddhist philosophy rejects a permanent ultimate substance, so efficacious recitation there need not imply that sound is eternal reality. Many Christian discussions, from Augustine's reflections on singing onward, subordinate musical sound to the understanding of the words, and Jewish liturgy ties chant closely to text and commandment. A comparison that starts from sound as divinity may therefore find only weaker versions of its own starting point, or overlook categories internal to other traditions. The stronger use of the model is as a question to put to each tradition (what is sound held to do, and why?) with the answer left open. An edited volume with separate treatments of each tradition permits this, but juxtaposition does not by itself establish a shared sonic theology. This is a methodological assessment, not a report of a settled scholarly verdict.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The 2006 volume includes treatment of Islamic sacred sound, notably Qur'anic recitation (*tajwid*, *tartil*), which can be compared with the Hindu case as a practice whose authority exceeds the transmission of verbal meaning. The Islamic theological framework, however, differs substantially from the Hindu one in affirming that the Qur'an is the literal speech of God sent down, not an eternal cosmic sound emanating from existence itself.

## Teaching and Academic Standing

Beck has taught religious studies at Tulane University in New Orleans (in a visiting or adjunct capacity). His work has contributed to making sacred sound and music a recognised topic within academic religious studies, where it had often been treated as marginal.

## Other Works

Beck also edited *Alternative Krishnas* (2005), a collection on regional and vernacular variations in the worship of Krishna in India.

## Conclusion

Beck is a frequently cited reference for the study of sacred sound in Hindu and comparative perspective, having given ritual sound a theoretical standing within religious studies.
