# Albert O. Hirschman

**Albert O. Hirschman** was an economist born in Berlin to a Jewish family, who fled Nazi Germany and later became one of the most original and diverse thinkers of development economics in the twentieth century. He is known for his methodological scepticism toward grand, comprehensive economic theories with a single prescription, and for his preference for more flexible and complex analyses that take account of the particularities of each development context.

## From Escaping the Nazis to American Academia

Hirschman left Germany in 1933 as a teenager after the Nazis came to power. He studied in Paris, at the London School of Economics, and in Trieste, Italy (doctorate, 1938), and fought on the Republican side in the Spanish Civil War. In 1940-41 he helped Varian Fry's Emergency Rescue Committee in Marseille get refugees out of Vichy France. He then settled in the United States, served in the US Army, worked on post-war European reconstruction at the Federal Reserve Board, and in the 1950s worked in Colombia as a financial adviser to the government and then as a private consultant. This direct field experience formed the basis of his foundational book in development economics.

## Unbalanced Growth: An Alternative to the Idea of Comprehensive Balance

In his book *The Strategy of Economic Development* (1958), Hirschman offered a radical critique of the comprehensive, balanced planning models prevailing at the time. These assumed that investment had to be made simultaneously in multiple interconnected sectors all at once (the idea of the "big push"). Hirschman argued that developing countries lacked the administrative and organisational capacity to plan and implement such complex simultaneous investments. He proposed the opposite alternative: deliberately unbalanced growth, through focused key investments that create bottlenecks and pressures. These force the rest of the economy to invest sequentially to fill those bottlenecks. Strategically, this exploits the scarcity of decision-making capacity rather than trying to overcome it all at once.

## Forward and Backward Linkages

Hirschman developed the concepts of "forward linkages" (when an investment project stimulates the creation of industries that use its output) and "backward linkages" (when it stimulates demand for inputs from other industries). He proposed that developing countries choose their first investments carefully, according to their capacity to generate the greatest number of these successive stimulating linkages across the rest of the economy, rather than spreading limited investment across scattered sectors with no links between them.

## Exit, Voice and Loyalty

In a later work that influenced fields well beyond development economics, Hirschman presented a general analytical framework entitled *Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*. When the performance of an organisation, a state or a firm declines, affected individuals have two basic options: exit (leaving the organisation, like emigration from a struggling country) or voice (protest and demands for reform from within). He argued that easy availability of the exit option may paradoxically weaken the incentive to use voice for reform. The framework has since been used to analyse emigration from many developing countries and its effect on internal political reform.

## An Approach Resistant to Generalisation

Hirschman was known for a methodological scepticism toward grand economic theories with a single prescription valid in all contexts. In a later book, *The Rhetoric of Reaction* (1991), he analysed the rhetoric of reactionary arguments, identifying three recurrent theses (perversity, futility and jeopardy), namely the arguments that have repeatedly been raised historically against any social reform: that a reform will produce the opposite of what is intended, that it is futile, or that it threatens earlier gains. This analysis intersects with contemporary debates about resistance to economic reform in many countries.

## A Legacy Spanning Multiple Fields

Today Hirschman is a reference point that extends beyond development economics into political and social science. His contribution was not a single analytical model. Rather, he offered a series of varied conceptual frameworks that are still used to analyse institutional and political change in contexts going beyond narrow economic development, toward a broader understanding of how societies respond to crises and opportunities.

## Intellectual Relations with His Generation of Development Pioneers

Hirschman's work intersected with that of his fellow founders of development economics, though he differed from them methodologically. Gunnar Myrdal proposed a general framework of circular cumulative causation that perpetuates underdevelopment, and W. Arthur Lewis proposed a relatively simple mathematical model of labour transfer between the two sectors. Hirschman remained closer to a contextual approach that warns against imposing a single general model on highly diverse development realities. This was an explicit methodological disagreement within the founding generation of the field itself, over how far development theory can be generalised.

## Late Years and Self-Criticism

Hirschman was known for what he called "self-subversion": he repeatedly revisited and qualified his own earlier arguments. In later writings he stressed the limits of economic theory in predicting the complex actual paths of development, and linkage claims such as his own have been tested empirically by later economists with mixed results.
