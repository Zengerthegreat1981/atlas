# Leopold von Ranke

Leopold von Ranke (1795–1886) was a German historian and professor at the University of Berlin from 1825 until his retirement. He was a central figure in establishing modern academic history as a rigorous, autonomous discipline grounded in systematic archival research, philological source criticism, and professional seminar training, distinguishing historical scholarship from literary rhetoric and speculative philosophy.

## Methodological Contributions

- **"As it actually happened" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*)**: In the preface to his first major work, *Histories of the Latin and Germanic Peoples from 1494 to 1514* (*Geschichten der romanischen und germanischen Völker von 1494 bis 1514*, 1824), Ranke famously disclaimed the traditional task of history to judge the past or instruct the present for the benefit of future generations. He stated that his work aimed merely to show "how it actually was." While later positivist interpreters read this as a demand for bare factual neutrality, Ranke conceived it as a rejection of anachronistic moralizing and ideological schemes.
- **The Archival Revolution and Primary Sources**: Ranke shifted historical inquiry toward contemporary official documents, particularly diplomatic correspondence, treaties, and state papers. His discovery and analysis of the Venetian ambassadorial dispatches (*Relazioni*) in the 1820s contributed to new standards of primary archival documentation, used in works such as *Fürsten und Völker von Süd-Europa* (1827).
- **The Advanced University Seminar (*Historisches Seminar*)**: At Berlin, Ranke institutionalized the advanced research seminar, where students were trained directly in the critical scrutiny of primary documents, diplomatic palaeography, and philological analysis. The seminar trained leading nineteenth-century historians—including Georg Waitz, Heinrich von Sybel, and Wilhelm von Giesebrecht—and became an influential model for graduate education in history.
- **Rejection of Speculative Teleology**: Ranke formulated his method in sharp contrast to the speculative idealism of G. W. F. Hegel, who held the chair of philosophy in Berlin until 1831. Where Hegel saw world history as the teleological self-realization of the Spirit through grand historical stages, Ranke defended the unique individuality of each historical phenomenon. In his 1854 lectures to King Maximilian II of Bavaria, he proclaimed that "every epoch is immediate to God" (*jede Epoche ist unmittelbar zu Gott*), meaning that no period exists merely as a stepping-stone to the next.

## Historiographical Position and Critical Limitations

Ranke's historiography was underpinned by a conservative Lutheran providentialism that perceived the workings of the divine in the organic development of sovereign states. Consequently, his scholarship concentrated overwhelmingly on great power politics, statecraft, and foreign relations (*Primat der Außenpolitik*), largely neglecting social structures, economic forces, and popular classes.

In France, the methodical school of Gabriel Monod, Charles-Victor Langlois, and Charles Seignobos institutionalized Rankean documentary criticism. In the twentieth century, however, the French Annales School (Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre) attacked Rankeanism as narrow *histoire événementielle* (event-based history). In the United States, as Peter Novick argued in *That Noble Dream*, the American historical profession transformed Ranke's nuanced historicism into a simplified positivist ideal of scientific detachment.

## Major Works

- *Geschichten der romanischen und germanischen Völker von 1494 bis 1514* (1824).
- *Fürsten und Völker von Süd-Europa im sechszehnten und siebzehnten Jahrhundert* (1827).
- *Die römischen Päpste in den letzten vier Jahrhunderten* (The History of the Popes, 3 vols., 1834–1836).
- *Deutsche Geschichte im Zeitalter der Reformation* (History of the Reformation in Germany, 6 vols., 1839–1847).
- *Französische Geschichte, vornehmlich im sechzehnten und siebzehnten Jahrhundert* (5 vols., 1852–1861).
