# Paul Ricœur

A French philosopher born in Valence in 1913 and died in 2005 near Paris. He taught at Strasbourg, the Sorbonne, Nanterre, and Chicago. A leading figure of hermeneutic phenomenology and of the philosophy of narrative and narrative identity.

## Background and Course

Ricœur lost his mother a few months after his birth and his father in the First World War, and grew up with his grandparents in a devout Protestant environment. He studied philosophy in Rennes and in Paris studied under Gabriel Marcel. He was taken prisoner in the Second World War in Germany between 1940 and 1945, and in captivity read Karl Jaspers and Edmund Husserl; he translated Husserl's *Ideas Pertaining to a Pure Phenomenology* into French and published it in 1950. He taught at the University of Strasbourg and then the Sorbonne, and moved to the University of Nanterre, where he became dean and resigned in 1970 amid the unrest that followed May 1968; he then worked at the University of Chicago and at Louvain, and continued producing philosophy until late in life.

## What He Gave

He developed a widely renowned philosophical and hermeneutic system that links phenomenology, Freudian psychoanalysis, narrative theory, and ethics; he formulated the concept of "narrative identity" (Narrative Identity: a human being understands himself by retelling his story), and analyzed the phenomenology of the will, human fallibility, guilt, forgiveness, and "oneself as another" (Oneself as Another).

## Core Ideas

Ricœur began from a philosophy of the will, analyzing in "The Voluntary and the Involuntary" the relation among decision, body, and motives. He then realized that a human being does not know himself by direct knowledge but by way of interpretation surrounded by signs, symbols, texts, and works, and so adopted what he called "the long route" in place of the direct intuition of Descartes. In "The Symbolism of Evil" (1960) he studied the symbols of defilement, sin, and guilt in myths and sacred books, and arrived at his phrase "the symbol gives rise to thought."

In "Freud and Philosophy" (1965) he distinguished between an interpretation that seeks to expose illusions, as in Marx, Nietzsche, and Freud, which is the "hermeneutics of suspicion," and an interpretation that seeks to recover the meaning deposited in the symbol, which is the "hermeneutics of recovery," and he made philosophy move between the two. In "The Rule of Metaphor" (1975) he took up how metaphor produces new meaning. In "Time and Narrative" (1983–1985) he held that human time is understood only when it is narrated, and that narrative is the answer to the paradoxes of time described by Augustine, Husserl, and Heidegger, and he formulated the theory of threefold mimesis: prefiguration in life, then configuration in the plot, then refiguration in the act of reading.

This course culminates in "Oneself as Another" (1990), where he distinguished between identity as sameness and identity as fidelity to a promise and commitment, tied narrative identity to moral responsibility, and defined the ethical aim as the pursuit of the good life with and for others within just institutions. In "Memory, History, Forgetting" (2000) he addressed collective memory, history, and forgiveness, and in "The Course of Recognition" (2004) he took up mutual recognition.

## Arab Reception

Ricœur became known in Arab culture through successive translations, among them "Oneself as Another," translated by George Zeenati (Arab Organization for Translation, 2005), and "Interpretation Theory: Discourse and the Surplus of Meaning," translated by Saeed al-Ghanimi (al-Markaz al-Thaqafi al-Arabi, 2003). Arabic studies on interpretation, narrative, and the reading of the religious text, and on the dialogue of hermeneutics with the inherited tradition of exegesis, benefited from him. Ricœur offered concepts such as hermeneutic distance and the surplus of meaning, which Arab critics have used in analyzing the text.

## His Place in the Current

He is among the most important figures in French hermeneutic phenomenology, and an influential mediator between continental philosophy, analytic philosophy and the human sciences.

## Limits

His encyclopedic hermeneutic expansion into the fields of linguistics, law, and theology makes him a comprehensive systematic philosopher who exceeds the narrow existential classification.

## Influence and Limits of the Project

Ricœur was a mediator among several traditions: phenomenology, psychoanalysis, structuralism, analytic philosophy, and theology, and he influenced interpretive sociology and the methods of history and law. Critics raised objections to him, among them that his project seeks mediation among currents to the point of softening the sharpness of their radical disagreement, and that the concept of narrative identity may be made to carry more than it can bear when measured against a life that does not narrate itself in a coherent story. Gadamer's followers criticized what they saw in Ricœur as an attempt to restrict interpretation by a method, while Habermas's followers held that his argument about ideology critique does not go far enough. He himself continued to hold that philosophy works between suspicion and faith, and that every interpretation has a share of truth.

## Major Works

Time and Narrative (Temps et Récit, 1983–1985) · Oneself as Another (Soi-même comme un autre, 1990) · Phenomenology of the Will: The Voluntary and the Involuntary (Le Volontaire et l'Involontaire, 1950) · Memory, History, Forgetting (La Mémoire, l'histoire, l'oubli, 2000).

## Selected Quotations

"The symbol gives rise to thought" (Le symbole donne à penser): a phrase from the conclusion of *The Symbolism of Evil* (La Symbolique du mal, 1960), the second volume of his project on finitude and guilt (Finitude et culpabilité).
