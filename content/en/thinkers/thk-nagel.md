# Thomas Nagel

An American philosopher, born in Belgrade (formerly Yugoslavia) on 4 July 1937, who received his doctorate from Harvard University in 1963 under the supervision of John Rawls, and has been professor of philosophy and law at New York University (NYU) since 1980. He is among the best-known philosophers of mind and ethics in contemporary analytic philosophy.

## Biography and Formation

Nagel graduated from Cornell University (1958), then studied at Oxford, where he received the B.Phil. in 1960, and afterwards received his doctorate from Harvard. He taught at Berkeley and Princeton before moving to New York University in 1980. He was formed in an Anglo-American analytic philosophical environment that seemed, in the early 1960s, preoccupied with the analysis of language, and among the most distinctive things about him was his return to the great questions about mind and ethics, rather than being content with the analysis of concepts.

## His Contribution

He reformulated the great existential themes (the absurd, death, and the meaning of life) with the tools of precise analytic philosophy. In his essay "The Absurd" (1971, published in the *Journal of Philosophy*) he offered a new definition of the absurd arising from the human being's unique capacity to look at his life and its seriousness from a transcendent, external standpoint, and from the contradiction between this standpoint and the inescapable seriousness with which we live our lives. In his most famous essay, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), he argued that subjective experience (qualia) resists reductive physical explanation, and it is among the most cited texts in contemporary philosophy of mind.

## Main Ideas

Nagel's project revolves around the tension between the subjective standpoint and the objective standpoint. In *The View from Nowhere* (1986) he holds that thought seeks to move away from its particular position in order to arrive at an objective conception of the world, but this distancing cannot encompass everything that is real, especially consciousness, so that subjective experience remains a part of reality that the physical description alone does not capture. In ethics he defends the view that moral reasons have an objective character that does not depend on the individual's desires, as in his book *The Possibility of Altruism* (1970). In politics and justice he raised in *Equality and Partiality* (1991) the question of reconciling the demands of moral impartiality with the demands of personal attachments.

In 2012 he stirred controversy with his book *Mind and Cosmos*, in which he criticizes the purely materialist Darwinian explanation of consciousness, knowledge and value, and proposes the search for non-religious teleological explanations, a position rejected by many naturalists.

## His Place in the Tradition

He represents a bridge between Anglo-American analytic philosophy and continental existential questioning, since he treats the issues of death, the absurd and meaning with the tools of analytic logic instead of the French phenomenological style.

## Reception

The bat essay is a key reference point for discussions of what Joseph Levine later (1983) called the "explanatory gap," and is among the most taught texts in philosophy of mind. His "what it is like to be" argument is cited in the debate over animal consciousness and artificial intelligence. The essay "The Absurd" treats the existential subject in a concise and ironic manner, and so became a meeting point between analytic philosophy and reflection on life.

## Critique and Limits

Naturalists, such as Daniel Dennett, fault him that the bat argument assumes that "subjective experience" is a fact that does not admit functional explanation, which they reject. In *Mind and Cosmos* he was accused of going beyond the scientific evidence and of offering a proposal about cosmic teleology that is untestable. His objective moral position is not free of objections that doubt the existence of moral facts independent of the mind.

## Method and Style

His method is analytic, rationalist and critical, differing from the phenomenological and dramatic style of French existentialists such as Sartre and Camus.

His style is marked by concision and clarity: his essays are relatively short but open questions that continue after reading them, which explains their spread in university courses beyond the bounds of the specialty.

He has also written on the philosophy of law and the philosophy of religion from the position of an atheist who admits that his own wish that there be no God may influence his judgment, as in his book *The Last Word* (1997).

## His Principal Works

"The Absurd" (essay, 1971) · "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (essay, 1974) · *Mortal Questions* (collection of essays including "Death," 1979) · *The View from Nowhere* (1986) · *What Does It All Mean?* (1987).
