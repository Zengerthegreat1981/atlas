# Nagarjuna (Founder of Madhyamaka)

An Indian Buddhist philosopher, the traditional founder of the "Madhyamaka" school (the Middle Way). He is credited with "developing" the Buddha's original idea of "emptiness" (Śūnyatā) and "dependent origination" (Pratītyasamutpāda) into an organized philosophical system. According to various estimates he lived in the period 150-250 CE, and later tradition associates him with south India (the Andhra region, and Śrīparvata in particular). The dating of his life is disputed (second or third century CE).

## Biography and historical context

No documented biography of Nagarjuna exists in the historical sense; everything related about him goes back to later Buddhist biographies in the Tibetan and Chinese traditions, which differ in their details, and some of which add miracles to him. The likelier view in modern studies is that he lived in the second and third centuries CE in south India, perhaps in the Andhra region. His environment belonged to a sharp dialogue between the schools of Abhidharma Buddhism, especially the Sarvastivada, which held to specific temporal elements (dharmas) with an intrinsic nature, and the Prajnaparamita current that appeared in the "Perfection of Wisdom" sutras. Nagarjuna read these sutras in a logical-dialectical way, making "emptiness" an analytic thesis against those who claimed a fixed substance for phenomena.

## What he contributed

- **The principle of "emptiness" (Śūnyatā)**: "every phenomenon is empty of intrinsic existence (svabhāva)", that is, nothing has an existence independent in itself. This is not "nihilism" but a "principle of the absence of intrinsic substantiality" of all phenomena.
- **The principle of "dependent origination" (Pratītyasamutpāda)**: "if this exists, that exists; if this arises, that arises". All phenomena arise in relation to other phenomena, not independently.
- **The principle of the "tetralemma" (Catuṣkoṭi)**: not A, not not-A, not both A and not-A, not neither A nor not-A. The fourfold negation is used to reject both extreme positions: that things exist inherently and that they do not exist at all.
- **The critique of "the intrinsic nature of things" (Svabhāva)**: things have no "intrinsic nature" in the sense of a "fixed substance"; what we call a "thing" is the result of conditions and relations.

## Influence in Asian cultures and the link to the Arabic heritage

The school's works passed to China when Kumarajiva translated works attributed to Nagarjuna and his school at the beginning of the fifth century CE, giving rise to the Chinese Sanlun school; the tradition then passed to Japan and Tibet, where Madhyamaka became the official theory of most schools. In the medieval Islamic world the name "Nagarjuna" appears in al-Biruni's *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind* (Researches on India), which mentions him as an Indian alchemist from a region near Somnath, and he is most likely connected with the tradition of the Siddhas and Indian alchemy, not with the disputing philosopher, although the two names were mixed in later accounts. This Arabic mention is therefore a witness to the name's reaching Muslims, not to the transmission of his philosophical method.

## His place in the current

Nagarjuna is regarded as the "second pillar" of philosophical Buddhism (after the Buddha), and one of the greatest philosophers of Asia. His Madhyamaka doctrine later divided into two branches:
- **Prāsaṅgika (the critical / refuting)**: content to expose the contradictions of opponents, without building a positive thesis (later associated with Tsongkhapa).
- **Svātantrika (the autonomous)**: accepts building provisional positive theses for dialectical purposes (associated with Bhāvaviveka; the two labels are later, largely Tibetan, classifications).

## Modern interpretations

Contemporary scholars differ in reading Nagarjuna: Jay Garfield and Jan Westerhoff, among others, read him as holding that all things lack intrinsic nature and exist only conventionally, in dependence on other things, while other interpreters hold that his negation of substantiality is a practical, therapeutic position, not a metaphysical one. Some have compared him with Kant's statements on the limits of reason, and others with Greek skepticism in Pyrrho and Sextus. The comparisons remain fruitful to the extent that differences in context and aim are respected, since his purpose was spiritual salvation by exposing attachment to substance, not academic skepticism.

## Limits

- Emptiness (Śūnyatā) is often misread as nihilism or sheer nothingness. In Madhyamaka it means the absence of intrinsic nature, and Nagarjuna rejects both the extreme of inherent existence and the extreme of non-existence.
- Scholars debate whether Nagarjuna advances a metaphysical position of his own or only a method that dissolves metaphysical theses. A common view is that he is a critic of essentialist (svabhāva-based) metaphysics who uses the tools of reasoning against metaphysical claims themselves.

Another matter deserves notice: the attribution of works to Nagarjuna is a subject of philological research; some, like the *Root Verses of the Middle Way* (Mūlamadhyamakakārikā), are considered authentic by consensus, while others, such as the books on alchemy and tantra attributed to him, are likely the work of people who bore the same name in later ages.

## Major works

- *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* (Fundamental Verses of the Middle Way, the foundational text).
- *Vigrahavyāvartanī* (Averting the Arguments, a reply to objections of realist logicians of the Nyāya type).
- *Vaidalyaprakaraṇa* (The Critical Chapters).
- *Yuktiṣaṣṭikā* (Sixty Verses on Reasoning).
- *Suhṛllekha* (Letter to a Friend, a letter of advice; attribution sometimes debated).
- *Ratnāvalī* (The Jewel Garland, a text on ethics and the path; attribution sometimes debated).
