# Martin Heidegger

Martin Heidegger (1889–1976) was a German philosopher, author of *Being and Time* (1927). He is the heaviest philosophical source of the existential and phenomenological tradition, and the most resistant of its figures to being used this way. He is also among the most controversial philosophers of the century because of his involvement with Nazism. This atlas has a separate entry on his philosophy of technology; this one focuses on being, Dasein, authenticity and his relation to the clinic.

## Life and Context

Heidegger was born on 26 September 1889 in Messkirch, in Baden, into a modest Catholic family. He studied theology and then philosophy at Freiburg and qualified as a lecturer in 1915 with a study of Duns Scotus. He was Edmund Husserl's assistant at Freiburg (1919–1923), taught at Marburg (1923–1928), where his reputation grew, and published *Being and Time* in 1927. In 1928 he succeeded Husserl in the Freiburg chair. In 1933 he became rector of the university and joined the Nazi Party, delivering the rectoral address on the self-assertion of the German university; he resigned the rectorate in April 1934. After the war he was barred from teaching until 1951. He died in Freiburg on 26 May 1976.

## Main Ideas

**The question of being.** *Being and Time* revives the question of the meaning of being, which Heidegger held philosophy had forgotten. He begins from the entity that asks the question, Dasein, and analyses its structure as being-in-the-world rather than an isolated subject facing objects.

**Everyday understanding.** He distinguishes practical engagement with tools (the ready-to-hand) from detached theoretical observation (the present-at-hand), arguing that human beings live first within a practical grasp of the world before any theory.

**The They, anxiety, death.** "The They" (das Man) names the mode of existence in which the individual dissolves into the average and familiar. Anxiety is a revealing experience that pulls Dasein out of familiarity and confronts it with its own possibilities. Being-toward-death and care (Sorge) structure Dasein's temporality, and authenticity means owning one's existence rather than living by anonymous norms.

**Later thought.** After the "turn" (Kehre) of the 1930s his attention shifted from the analysis of Dasein to the history of being and how it discloses and conceals itself. He wrote *Contributions to Philosophy* (composed 1936–1938, published 1989), essays on art, poetry (Hölderlin) and language, and the *Letter on Humanism* (1947), in which he distanced himself from Sartre's existentialism. "Truth" becomes unconcealment (aletheia).

## His Direct Relationship with the Clinic

For about a decade from 1959 he took part in seminars with psychiatrists at the invitation of Medard Boss, later published as the Zollikon Seminars. They are among the few cases of a major philosopher directly addressing clinicians, explaining Dasein, space, time and the body, and criticising purely causal-explanatory approaches in psychology. This encounter fed into Boss's Daseinsanalysis, and Binswanger's earlier work drew on his ideas as well, though Heidegger and Binswanger's readings diverged.

## Limits and Criticism

He offers no therapy, and does not regard anxiety as a problem to be solved. Transferring his concepts into the clinic means they lose their ontological meaning, which is legitimate on condition that it is declared. Critics include Adorno (*The Jargon of Authenticity*), feminist readings of Dasein's alleged neutrality, and Levinas, who charged that ethics and the other are neglected.

## A File That Is Not Hidden

His association with Nazism is part of his record; it is neither deleted nor used to dismiss his analyses without examination. He remained a party member until 1945, and the publication of the *Black Notebooks* from 2014 revealed antisemitic passages, reopening debate about how his thought relates to them. Separating the questions is possible and required, and concealing either is not honesty.

## Influence

He shaped Sartre, Merleau-Ponty, Levinas, Arendt, Gadamer, Hans Jonas, hermeneutics and deconstruction, and, through Boss and others, existential psychotherapy.
