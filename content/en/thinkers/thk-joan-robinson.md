# Joan Robinson

**Joan Violet Robinson** was a British economist from the University of Cambridge, and one of the leading founding figures of the post-Keynesian school. She is one of the most influential economists of the twentieth century, although she never received the Nobel Prize. Her work combined early contributions to the theory of imperfect competition with her later sharp critique of the simplification of Keynes's ideas in the prevailing neoclassical synthesis.

## Imperfect competition: an early neoclassical contribution

In her first book, *The Economics of Imperfect Competition* (1933), Robinson presented a new analytical framework for studying markets that are neither fully competitive nor pure monopolies, but combine elements of both — the situation of most real markets. She developed analytical tools (the marginal revenue curve under monopolistic competition) that remain a core part of standard microeconomics today, although this early work stayed within the traditional neoclassical framework that she later criticized sharply.

## A close colleague of Keynes

Robinson was one of the small circle of young economists in Cambridge (the "Cambridge Circus") who discussed Keynes's ideas with him while he was formulating the *General Theory* in the 1930s. She became one of his most committed defenders, but later sharply criticized what she regarded as a distortion of Keynes's original spirit in the neoclassical-Keynesian synthesis formulated by John Hicks and Paul Samuelson, arguing that this synthesis neglected the fundamental element of uncertainty at the heart of Keynes's thought.

## The accumulation of capital: an alternative theory of growth

In her principal book, *The Accumulation of Capital* (1956), Robinson developed an alternative theory of economic growth that links the rate of capital accumulation directly to the distribution of income between profits and wages. She held that capitalist investment decisions, not individual saving alone, are the main engine of growth, and criticized neoclassical models that treat "capital" as a homogeneous technical quantity detached from power relations and class distribution.

## The Cambridge capital controversy

Robinson, who opened the debate with her 1953 article "The Production Function and the Theory of Capital", was a central party to the **Cambridge capital controversy** against the economists of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (Samuelson and Solow). Working alongside Piero Sraffa, she argued that measuring "capital" as a single monetary value presupposes prior knowledge of the interest rate that is supposed to be determined by that same quantity, a circular logical contradiction that undermines the foundation of the neoclassical marginal productivity theory of capital. Samuelson acknowledged the validity of the technical point ("reswitching") in 1966 without abandoning the neoclassical model in practice.

## Economic Philosophy: a methodological critique

In her book *Economic Philosophy* (1962) and later essays, Robinson launched a broad methodological critique of what she called neoclassical economics' tendency to present conservative ideological judgments in the guise of neutral science. She called for greater awareness of the value assumptions hidden behind formal mathematical models, a methodological position that deeply influenced the critical current within post-Keynesian economics.

## Visits to socialist economies and a contested political stance

Robinson showed interest in the experiments in economic planning in China and North Korea, visited both, and wrote sympathetically about the Chinese experience; this provoked political controversy about her academic objectivity. This has remained a contested aspect of the assessment of her intellectual legacy, alongside her rigorous theoretical contributions.

## A founding legacy of post-Keynesian economics

Historians of economic thought regard her as one of the most important actual founders of the post-Keynesian school, because she combined high technical command of neoclassical tools with a radical critique of their basic assumptions. This rare combination made her critique more influential than criticism from outsiders less familiar with the details of the model being criticized.

## No Nobel Prize

Robinson never received the Nobel Prize in Economics and died in 1983. She was widely regarded as a leading candidate, and commentators have debated whether her political positions or her membership in a critical school then outside the mainstream played a part. The committee's deliberations are not public, so this remains speculation; her contributions to the theories of competition, growth and distribution are widely recognised.

## A long teaching career at Cambridge

Robinson spent her academic life at the University of Cambridge, from her undergraduate years at Girton College in the 1920s to a professorship that she held from 1965 until her retirement in 1971. She was not appointed to a professorship until relatively late, despite her large earlier contributions. Some historians have attributed this delay partly to institutional bias against women in British academia at the time, alongside her theoretical and political positions.
