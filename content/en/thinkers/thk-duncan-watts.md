# Duncan J. Watts

A sociologist (raised in Australia, long based in the United States) known for work on network structure, social contagion and, more recently, computational social science, which uses large-scale digital data and online experiments to study social behavior.

## The Central Idea

Watts argued, for example in his essay "A Twenty-First Century Science" (*Nature*, 2007), that the digital records people leave through phones, email and social media allow social scientists to study social networks and collective behavior at a scale and temporal resolution not previously possible, testing classical theories of information diffusion, influence and network dynamics. The programmatic article "Computational Social Science" (*Science*, 2009) by David Lazer and colleagues, a manifesto for the field, raised related methodological and ethical challenges, including privacy and data bias. Watts held posts at Columbia University, Yahoo! Research and Microsoft Research, and later at the University of Pennsylvania.

## The Music Lab Experiment

With Matthew Salganik and Peter Sheridan Dodds, Watts ran the "Music Lab" experiment ("Experimental Study of Inequality and Unpredictability in an Artificial Cultural Market," *Science*, 2006). Participants in an online music-download site were assigned to independent worlds, some in which they could see others' download counts. Social influence increased inequality of success and made outcomes less predictable across worlds, which the authors read as evidence of cumulative-advantage dynamics, while song quality still had some bearing on outcomes.

## Impact

The work helped establish computational social science as a recognized research area and influenced methods for analyzing digital social networks and large-scale behavioral data.

## His Early Work: Small-World Networks

His foundational work on "small-world networks" came a decade earlier. In his pivotal article with Steven Strogatz (his doctoral supervisor at Cornell), "Collective Dynamics of 'Small-World' Networks" (*Nature*, 1998), Watts developed a mathematical model that structurally explains the "six degrees of separation" phenomenon that Stanley Milgram had studied experimentally with his letter-forwarding experiments (1967; Travers and Milgram 1969). He showed that adding a very small number of "long-range random links" to a highly clustered local network is enough on its own to shrink the average distance between any two nodes abruptly, explaining mathematically how very large networks can be "small worlds" despite their apparent size, thereby complementing Granovetter's thesis on the strength of weak ties with a general quantitative model.
