# Immanuel Kant

A Prussian philosopher, and one of those who most changed the course of modern philosophy. He brought about the **Copernican turn** in epistemology by making the structure of the **mind**, not the structure of things, what determines the form of what we know; he founded **deontological ethics** on the **categorical imperative**; and in the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* he set out an immensely influential theory of **aesthetic judgment**.

## What He Gave

### *Critique of Pure Reason* (Kritik der reinen Vernunft, 1781)

A Copernican revolution: until then philosophers thought that **our knowledge** conforms to **things** (Aristotle, Locke). Kant **reverses** the equation: things are perceived according to the **forms of subjective knowledge** (Erkenntnisformen). Time and space (which Newton thought real) are our **pure sensible intuition** (Reine Sinnliche Anschauung), and the **categories of the understanding** (Kategorien des Verstandes: substance, causality, community, and so on) are the **pure forms of thinking**. The thing in itself (Ding an sich) remains **outside knowledge**, and is not grasped except as appearances (Erscheinungen).

### *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (Grundlegung zur Metaphysik der Sitten, 1785) and *Critique of Practical Reason* (Kritik der praktischen Vernunft, 1788)

In the *Groundwork* (1785) Kant first formulated the best-known version of the **principle of the categorical imperative** (Kategorischer Imperativ): "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law" (Handle nur nach derjenigen Maxime, durch die du zugleich wollen kannst, dass sie ein allgemeines Gesetz werde). He then rebuilt the full critical foundation of ethics in the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), where he tied the categorical imperative to the concept of the **autonomy of the will** (Autonomie des Willens) and to the proof of freedom, immortality, and the existence of God as "postulates of practical reason" (Postulate der praktischen Vernunft), not theoretical givens. This principle **unifies** duty (Pflicht) and freedom (Freiheit).

### *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (Kritik der Urteilskraft, 1790)

The bridge between nature and freedom: **aesthetic judgment** (Ästhetisches Urteil) concerning natural beauty, and **teleological judgment** (Teleologisches Urteil) concerning the "purposiveness of nature" (Zweckmäßigkeit). And the concept of the **"final end"** (Endzweck), that is, that the human being as a moral being is the end that is never a means to another, is what links for him the contemplation of nature and the contemplation of ethics.

### *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason* (Die Religion innerhalb der Grenzen der bloßen Vernunft, 1793)

An analysis of religion as **morality** in symbolic dress. **Radical evil** (Radikales Böse) in human nature: not an inclination to harm for its own sake, nor the victory of instinct over reason, but an **inversion of the order of incentives**: that a person makes self-love the condition to which the moral law is subordinated, instead of the law being the condition. That is, evil for him lies in the **structure of the will**, not in the force of desire, and therefore it was radical and was at the same time an act attributed to freedom and for which one is held accountable.

## Influence

- **German Idealism** (Fichte, Schelling, Hegel): all heirs of Kant.
- **Neo-Kantianism** (Marburg, Baden): a rereading in the 19th century.
- **20th-century philosophy**: **Husserl**, **Heidegger**, and **Carnap** were all influenced by the *Critique of Pure Reason* (or rejected it).
- **Contemporary ethics**: the formula "an end, not a mere means" is an important philosophical source for the concept of **human dignity** in discussions of rights and of medical research ethics (informed consent), and it is regularly invoked in bioethics and in debates about artificial intelligence.
- **Psychology**: his influence on it is indirect but deep: the idea that the mind **constructs** experience through its own categories is the philosophical grandfather of Gestalt psychology, of cognitivism, and of Piaget's constructivism; Piaget explicitly described his project as an attempt to make **Kant's categories genetic**, studied in the child's development instead of being assumed a priori.

## Limits

Three major objections have accompanied Kant since his lifetime:

**First: the thing in itself.** **Jacobi** formulated it in a phrase that became proverbial: one cannot enter Kant's system without presupposing the thing in itself, and one cannot remain in it with this presupposition. For the categories, including **causality**, apply only to appearances by Kant's own statement; so how can it be said that the thing in itself **produces** impressions in us? This objection is what led Fichte and Hegel to drop the thing in itself and build German Idealism.

**Second: the formalism of ethics.** **Hegel** charges that the categorical imperative is an **empty** criterion: the test of universalizability prevents logical contradiction and gives no moral content, since hideous maxims can be universalized without contradiction. **The utilitarians** charge him with neglecting consequences, and their famous example is Kant's answer on the question of **lying to the murderer** who asks about the location of his victim: he insisted on the prohibition of lying absolutely, a position most readers today regard as evidence of a flaw in the system rather than of its soundness. **Schiller**, and then **Bernard Williams**, charge that making moral worth lie in acting **from the motive of duty alone** removes love and feeling from ethics, so that one who helps his friend out of love for him becomes less moral than one who helps him reluctantly.

**Third: his writings on the human races.** In his lectures on anthropology and physical geography Kant wrote an explicit hierarchy among the races, in terms that read today as unambiguously racist. These texts are established; the dispute concerns their place in the system and how far his later writings revised them. One camp holds that they are the opinions of his age that do not touch the critical philosophy, and that his views changed in the 1790s; another camp (Charles Mills and Robert Bernasconi) holds that the extension of **dignity** to every human being in his moral theory directly contradicts the exclusion of peoples from it in his anthropology, and that this contradiction is structural in eighteenth-century Enlightenment, not incidental to it. The debate is alive and has not been settled.

## Politics and the French Revolution

In the political writings of the 1790s (*Toward Perpetual Peace*, 1795; *The Metaphysics of Morals*, 1797) Kant defended a republican constitution, the rule of law and a federation of free states. He also followed the French Revolution with sympathy: in *The Conflict of the Faculties* (1798) he described the disinterested enthusiasm of its spectators as a sign of humanity's moral progress, while denying that subjects have a right of active revolt.

## His Most Important Works

- **Kritik der reinen Vernunft** (1781): *Critique of Pure Reason*.
- **Kritik der praktischen Vernunft** (1788): *Critique of Practical Reason*.
- **Kritik der Urteilskraft** (1790): *Critique of the Power of Judgment*.
- **Die Religion innerhalb der Grenzen der bloßen Vernunft** (1793): *Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason*.
- **Zum ewigen Frieden** (1795): *Toward Perpetual Peace*.
- **Metaphysik der Sitten** (1797): *The Metaphysics of Morals*.
