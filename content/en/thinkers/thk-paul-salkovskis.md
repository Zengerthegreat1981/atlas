# Paul Salkovskis

British clinical psychologist who has held professorial posts at the University of Oxford, the Institute of Psychiatry at King's College London, and the University of Bath. He is a leading contemporary theorist of cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for obsessive-compulsive disorder, panic disorder, and health anxiety, through his account of how intrusive thoughts are interpreted.

## Training

Salkovskis attended Kelso High School and took his undergraduate degree at the University of Reading. He qualified as a clinical psychologist in 1979 at the Institute of Psychiatry and the Maudsley Hospital, where Monte Shapiro was among his teachers. He then worked for six years in the National Health Service in Yorkshire, in Huddersfield and Leeds, before moving to Oxford in 1985 as a research clinical psychologist. He received a PhD from Reading in 1990. At Oxford he became Professor of Cognitive Psychology. From 2000 to 2010 he was Professor of Clinical Psychology and Applied Science at the Institute of Psychiatry and clinical director of the Centre for Anxiety Disorders and Trauma in the South London and Maudsley trust, including its specialist OCD service. From 2010 he directed a new clinical-psychology doctorate at the University of Bath and a specialist NHS clinic for severe anxiety. In 2018 he returned to Oxford as professor of clinical psychology, directing the Oxford Institute of Clinical Psychology Training and Research and the Oxford Cognitive Therapy Centre. He was president of the British Association for Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapies from 2018 to 2020, and he edits *Behavioural and Cognitive Psychotherapy*. ## Obsession in the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Classical Arabic-Islamic writers described obsessional doubts (*waswas*) centuries before obsessive-compulsive disorder was medically defined, in two overlapping settings, one medical and one legal-religious. Abu Zayd al-Balkhi, in *Masalih al-Abdan wa al-Anfus*, discussed obsessional thinking among the disorders of the soul and recommended turning attention away from the intrusive thought. Ibn al-Qayyim, in *Ighathat al-Lahfan*, addressed obsessive doubts about purification, prayer, and intention, and advised the afflicted person to disregard them rather than yield to them. This advice has a loose resemblance to response prevention in modern treatment, although the framework and purpose differ. Clinical studies in Saudi Arabia and Egypt, including the study by Mahgoub and Abdel-Hafeiz in 1991 and the study by Okasha and colleagues in 1994, reported that the content of obsessions among Muslim patients often concerns purification and prayer, while the formal structure of the disorder remains shared with the West. This observation is compatible with Salkovskis's model, which regards intrusive thoughts as universal and their interpretation as shaped by beliefs, including cultural and religious ones: a person who regards a lapse in purification as a grave sin may interpret a passing thought with a heightened sense of responsibility.

## The Inflated Responsibility Model of OCD

In 1985, in his influential paper "Obsessional-Compulsive Problems: A Cognitive-Behavioural Analysis," Salkovskis presented an influential cognitive model to explain OCD. His theory starts from the premise that **intrusive thoughts**, such as passing images, urges, or disturbing ideas, are a normal and common phenomenon among most people. This was supported by Rachman and de Silva's 1978 study, which found that the content of ordinary people's thoughts resembles that of patients' thoughts in kind. The essential difference between healthy people and OCD patients is not the presence of these thoughts, but **the way they are interpreted**. The OCD patient interprets these passing thoughts as a sign of **inflated personal responsibility** for causing harm to themselves or others, which is known as **catastrophic misinterpretation**.

According to this model, the misinterpretation generates acute anxiety that drives the patient to **neutralizing** behaviors: visible rituals such as washing and repeated checking, or hidden mental rituals, aimed at reducing the sense of responsibility and the imagined danger. But this neutralizing, instead of solving the problem, reinforces the vicious cycle. It gives the intrusive thought excessive importance and entrenches its catastrophic interpretation, which perpetuates the disorder over the long term. In a 1989 paper he developed the idea that these acts increase the recurrence of the thought and make it easier to recall, and that the thoughts and behaviors one tries to control become a source of distress. He also explained why the patient does not benefit from repeated reassurance, since reassurance is itself a neutralizing ritual.

## From Obsession to Panic and Health Anxiety

Salkovskis worked with Clark and Gelder in Oxford on the idea that panic attacks occur when normal bodily sensations (such as a racing heartbeat) are catastrophically interpreted as a sign of imminent danger, such as a heart attack or loss of control. With them he published in 1996 a paper on cognition-behavior links in the persistence of panic, which examined the role of safety behaviors. In 1986, with Warwick, he proposed a cognitive-behavioral account of hypochondriasis (now health anxiety) based on the misinterpretation of minor bodily changes, and on the idea that reassurance reduces fear temporarily but can maintain it. This analysis later informed cognitive treatments for health anxiety.

## Therapeutic Impact

This model provided the theoretical basis for developing modern CBT protocols for OCD, which combine cognitive restructuring (challenging beliefs about inflated responsibility) with **exposure and response prevention** (ERP). In a 1999 review he set out the cognitive treatment approach, including behavioral experiments to test an alternative interpretation of the problem (for example, that the person is worried about harm rather than in real danger of causing it).

## Academic Standing

Salkovskis has been prominent in the training of cognitive behavioral therapists in Britain. His models are part of the evidence base on which cognitive behavioral treatments for anxiety disorders, such as those used in the National Health Service, are built.

## Limits and Criticism

This entry is educational and not clinical advice. The responsibility model does not enjoy consensus. Rachman and colleagues argued that "thought-action fusion," the belief that thinking about something increases its likelihood of occurring, is no less important than responsibility. Research by the Obsessive Compulsive Cognitions Working Group (1997 and 2005) indicated that inflated responsibility is neither the only relevant belief domain nor specific to OCD. Trials such as the study by Whittal, Thordarson, and McLean in 2005 showed that cognitive therapy and exposure and response prevention achieve similar results, which raises the question of whether changing interpretation is the actual mechanism. The causal relationship between responsibility and OCD also remains unresolved, since it is measured in correlational studies in which responsibility may be a result of the disorder rather than its cause. Cultural generalization has limits as well: in religious cultures, inflated responsibility takes religious forms that require adapting the therapeutic formulation.
