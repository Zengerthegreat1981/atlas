# Roger Bastide

French sociologist and anthropologist, born in Nîmes in 1898 to a Protestant family. He studied philosophy before turning to sociology. He moved to Brazil in 1938 to occupy the chair of sociology at the University of São Paulo, succeeding Claude Lévi-Strauss, and spent some sixteen years there studying Afro-Brazilian religions through deep field research. He then returned to France to teach at the Sorbonne until his death in 1974.

## What He Contributed

### The Study of Candomblé and Afro-Brazilian Religions

In his landmark work *Les Religions africaines au Brésil* (1960; English translation *The African Religions of Brazil*, 1978), Bastide provided the first systematic sociological-anthropological study of the African religions transplanted to Brazil through the slave trade and their transformations there: Candomblé, Umbanda, Macumba, Xangô and Batuque. He linked the structure of these rituals to the history of slavery and the social organization of Afro-Brazilian communities. His field participation reached an unusual academic extreme: he was initiated into Candomblé, as a "son of Xangô" (*filho de Xangô*).

### The Theory of "Interpenetration of Civilizations"

Bastide developed a theoretical framework as an alternative to the linear concept of "acculturation" then prevalent. He proposed that the meeting of African culture with European and Indigenous American culture in Brazil did not produce a fused "mixture" but a **structural coexistence** of distinct cultural systems, each of which retained its own internal logic even as they interacted. An individual could thus move between different religious and cultural systems (Catholicism and Candomblé, for example) without experiencing a lived contradiction.

### Sociology of Alienation and Madness

In *Sociologie des maladies mentales* (1965), Bastide studied the relationship between cultural transition, social alienation and psychological disorder, approaching questions that later became central to medical anthropology.

## Impact

- Bastide is regarded as a principal founder of the comparative study of Afro-American religions, and influenced later Brazilian scholars of religion and race.
- His theory of "interpenetration" reframed the debate on acculturation, moving it away from simple linear models of assimilation.
- His studies influenced the comparative sociology of religion and African Diaspora Studies.

## Limitations

- His deep ritual participation (as a "son of Xangô") was criticized by some colleagues as exceeding the limits of traditional scientific objectivity, although others argued that it gave him an insider (*emic*) understanding unavailable to an external observer.
- Some of his comparative classifications of Afro-Brazilian religions (such as his distinction between "preserved" and "mixed" religions) were later considered to carry an implicit hierarchy among degrees of cultural "authenticity," a point criticized by later Brazilian anthropologists.

## Major Works

- *Les Religions africaines au Brésil* (1960).
- *Sociologie des maladies mentales* (1965).
- *Le Candomblé de Bahia* (1958).
- *Les Amériques noires* (1967; English translation *African Civilizations in the New World*, 1971).

## Research with Florestan Fernandes and Ethnic Relations

Between 1951 and 1955, Bastide collaborated with the Brazilian researcher Florestan Fernandes on a UNESCO study of ethnic relations in Brazil. This produced the book *Brancos e Negros em São Paulo* (1955), which exposed actual racial discrimination in the city and criticized the image of racial democracy drawn by Gilberto Freyre. This work shaped the São Paulo school of race studies, from which researchers such as Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Octavio Ianni emerged, both of whom became among Brazil's most prominent sociologists.

## Islam in Bahia

A dimension connected to the Arab-Islamic tradition is that among the enslaved Africans in Bahia were Muslims known as *malês*, most of whom belonged to the Hausa and Yoruba peoples. They preserved written Arabic in amulets and tablets, and led the Bahia revolt of 1835. João José Reis reconstructed this revolt in *Slave Rebellion in Brazil: The Muslim Uprising of 1835 in Bahia* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1993). Islam was, however, peripheral to Bastide's interests compared with Candomblé, and later research has addressed the subject in detail.

## Dreams, Madness and Trance

In *Le Rêve, la transe et la folie* (1972), Bastide extended his work on psychological disorder to ritual trance. He argued that spirit possession in Candomblé is not an illness but an organized social technique that allows the individual to receive the gods according to controlled rules, and that cultures shape the boundaries of madness and normality differently depending on the individual's position. In *Les Amériques noires* (1967; English translation 1971) he compared the fates of African cultures in Brazil, the Caribbean and the United States.

## Review of His Classifications

Later Brazilian scholars argued that Bastide's dichotomy between purity and syncretism reproduces a notion of a pure African origin. Recent Brazilian research presents Candomblé communities as moving between origin and innovation to a greater degree than the 1950s studies had assumed.
