# Elizabeth F. Loftus

## Definition

An American cognitive psychologist (born 1944), distinguished professor at the University of California, Irvine, pioneer of research on the "plasticity" of human memory and its susceptibility to modification, discoverer of the "Misinformation Effect," and one of the cognitive psychologists with the greatest influence on the American criminal justice system.

## Historical Context

Loftus came to prominence in the 1970s, in a context in which cognitive psychology was taking shape as a research current independent of behaviorism, interested in studying the internal processes of memory and perception rather than confining itself to overt behavior. Her interest in the reliability of eyewitness testimony arose as a direct response to a practical need within the American judicial system, where the testimony of a self-confident witness was traditionally treated as strong evidence before the courts, without sufficient regard for the nature of human memory, which is open to modification after an event has occurred.

Loftus received her doctorate from Stanford University in 1970, and taught for a long time at the University of Washington in Seattle before moving to the University of California, Irvine in 2002. Part of her interest in the subject was her shift from studying semantic memory to real-world memory, and then she turned to criminal cases in which her testimony was sought as an expert.

## Main Ideas or Method

Loftus demonstrated experimentally, through a series of innovative laboratory studies, that human memory is not a fixed recording retrieved as it is, but an active process of reconstruction that can be contaminated by later information. In her founding experiment with John Palmer (1974), participants watched a film of a car accident and were then asked about the speed of the two cars, with only the verb used in the question changed ("when they collided" versus "when they smashed"). Those asked with the stronger wording ("smashed") estimated a higher speed, and, when asked a week later, a larger share of them reported having seen broken glass that was not in the film at all, which showed that the wording of the question itself can modify memory and not merely extract it. On this observation she built her central concept, the "misinformation effect": false information presented after an event merges with the original memory of the event, so that the person later cannot distinguish what they actually witnessed from what was suggested to them afterward. In another famous study, known as "Lost in the Mall" (1995, with Jacqueline Pickrell), she succeeded in convincing about a quarter of the participants that they had experienced in childhood an event that never took place (getting lost in a shopping mall), and some of them elaborated the false event with details of their own.

She extended the idea in a study with Miller and Burns (1978) in which participants saw a traffic accident involving either a stop sign or a yield sign and were then given a question that wrongly presupposed the other sign; many of them later chose the suggested sign at test, an experiment that became one of the best-known models of the misinformation effect. Loftus gathered three decades of this research in a review article titled "Planting Misinformation in the Human Mind" (2005) in the journal *Learning & Memory*, showing that the effect appears across different materials and ages and under multiple conditions.

## Principal Works or Representatives

Her most important books: *Eyewitness Testimony* (1979), which laid the theoretical and empirical foundation for her research on the reliability of eyewitness testimony; *Witness for the Defense* (1991, with Katherine Ketcham), in which she reviewed real court cases in which she took part as an expert; and *The Myth of Repressed Memory* (1994, with Katherine Ketcham), in which she critiqued the hypothesis of "repressed memories" recovered through therapy.

## Impact and Critique

Loftus's research fundamentally changed how American courts treat eyewitness testimony, and pushed toward procedural reforms in the methods of questioning witnesses and presenting suspect identification procedures, and she testified as an expert or was consulted in hundreds of cases. Her involvement in the "Memory Wars," the controversy over the credibility of memories of sexual abuse recovered during psychotherapy, drew her into sharp professional and legal disputes with therapists and patients who sincerely believed in their recovered memories, and she was subjected to institutional investigations and lawsuits because of her public critical positions. This controversy remains one of the most divisive debates in the history of contemporary psychology, between those who defend the possibility that traumatic memories can be repressed and later recovered accurately, and those who see a large part of these recovered memories as the product of unintended therapeutic suggestion.

On the critical side, Pezdek and colleagues (1997) argued that the possibility of implanting a memory depends on the plausibility of the event for the person, so the results of the lost-in-the-mall study cannot be generalized to extremely severe events such as sexual abuse. A later systematic review (Brewin and Andrews, 2017) showed that the proportion of participants who were fully convinced of the implanted event in these studies approaches a third, a proportion lower than popular headlines might suggest. In addition, laboratory studies capture the properties of ordinary memory, and it cannot easily be inferred from them whether a particular memory in a court file is true or false; and the case of Taus v. Loftus, decided by the California Supreme Court in 2007 largely in her favor, showed the personal cost of this role.
