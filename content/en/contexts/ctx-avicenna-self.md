# Avicenna and Kitab al-Nafs

Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Sina — known in the Latin West as Avicenna (980–1037) — composed the psychology section of his great philosophical encyclopaedia al-Shifa (The Healing) in the 1020s, during his years in Hamadan and Isfahan. The section, Kitab al-Nafs (The Book of the Soul), is the most systematic treatment of the soul produced in the Arab-Islamic tradition: a theory of the soul's powers that fuses Aristotelian philosophy with Galenic medicine and with Avicenna's own experience as the author of the Canon of Medicine. For this Atlas it matters as the model of a rigorous, methodical Arab-Islamic psychology — not scattered observations on the soul, but a complete science of it, worked out a millennium before modern psychology claimed the same ground.

## The context

Avicenna wrote in a world where philosophy and medicine were not separate professions. Trained as a physician, courtier, and philosopher in the courts of Persia and Central Asia, he inherited Aristotle's De Anima through the commentary tradition and Galen's physiology of the brain, and he set out to give the soul a single coherent account that satisfied both. Al-Shifa itself is an encyclopaedia of logic, natural philosophy, mathematics, and metaphysics; Kitab al-Nafs is its psychological core. Avicenna defines the soul as the first perfection of a natural body possessing organs — Aristotle's formula, taken seriously as a scientific definition — and then works out, power by power, what such a soul can do.

## The powers of the soul

Avicenna divides the powers of the soul into three ascending ranks: the vegetative (nutrition, growth, reproduction), the animal (sensation and movement, including the five external senses and five internal senses), and the rational (the theoretical and practical intellect). The internal senses are his most distinctive development of the psychology of perception, building on Aristotle, Galen and earlier Arabic thinkers: the common sense that gathers the deliverances of the outer senses, imagination (khayal) that stores images, the compositive imagination that combines and divides them, estimation (wahm) that perceives non-sensible meanings such as the hostility of the wolf as the sheep perceives it, and memory that retains them. These powers are assigned to the ventricles of the brain, following Galenic anatomy, so that psychology and physiology are parts of one inquiry.

## The Flying Man

Kitab al-Nafs contains one of the most famous thought experiments in philosophy: the Flying Man. Imagine a person created all at once, suspended in air, limbs separated so they do not touch, senses cut off from the outside world — and yet, Avicenna argues, such a person would still affirm their own existence. The experiment is meant to show that self-awareness does not depend on awareness of the body, and many readers take it to point to the soul as a self-aware substance independent of matter. The argument deserves careful comparison with Descartes' cogito, which it anticipates by six centuries, but without assuming identity between the two: Avicenna seeks to establish the substantiality of the soul, not a first certainty on which all knowledge can be rebuilt. Separately, Avicenna argues for the soul's immateriality and its survival after the body's death on the ground that the apprehension of universal intelligibles cannot take place in a divisible organ.

## Key figures

The context is dominated by Avicenna himself, but its meaning depends on the figures around him. Aristotle supplies the framework of De Anima; Galen supplies the medical physiology; al-Farabi, Avicenna's predecessor, supplies the political and logical context of falsafa. After Avicenna, the Latin translators Dominicus Gundissalvi and Avendauth carried Kitab al-Nafs into Latin in the twelfth century, where it shaped the scholastic psychology of Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas — a transmission studied in detail by Dag Hasse (2000). Within the Islamic world, al-Ghazali attacked Avicenna's doctrine of the soul's survival without bodily resurrection in The Incoherence of the Philosophers (1095), and Ibn Rushd (Averroes) is usually read as rejecting the immortality of the individual soul in his commentaries on Aristotle.

## Influence

Avicenna's psychology became the standard account of the soul in both the Islamic and the Latin worlds for centuries, and its vocabulary — the internal senses, the estimative faculty, the distinction between the soul as form and the soul as substance — structured discussions of mind long after his metaphysics was contested. For the Atlas, Kitab al-Nafs is also a caution: its theory of the soul belongs to a philosophical-medical framework that predates modern psychology by nine centuries, and its concepts should be compared with modern notions of consciousness and self-awareness critically, not translated into them without remainder.

## Key works

The primary text is Kitab al-Nafs itself, available in Fazlur Rahman's 1959 edition, Avicenna's De Anima, published by Oxford University Press. Lenn Goodman's Avicenna (1992) is the best short introduction in English, and Robert Wisnovsky's Avicenna's Metaphysics in Context (2003) situates the psychology within the wider system. Marmura's 1986 study of the Flying Man in context remains the classic treatment of the thought experiment.
