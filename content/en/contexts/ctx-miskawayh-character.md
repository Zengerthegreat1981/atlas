# Miskawayh and Tahdhib al-Akhlaq

Abu Ali Ahmad ibn Muhammad ibn Miskawayh (c. 932–1030) was a Persian philosopher, physician, and historian who wrote in Arabic and served as secretary and librarian at the Buyid courts of Rayy, Isfahan, and Baghdad. His ethical treatise Tahdhib al-Akhlaq wa Tathir al-Araq (The Refinement of Character and the Purification of Dispositions) is among the earliest and most influential systematic works of philosophical ethics in the Islamic tradition: a treatment of the emotions and the character that binds the cultivation of the soul to the stability of the self. For this Atlas it matters as the voice within the Arab-Islamic context that is closest to Greek moral philosophy — and therefore the voice that shows the tradition was not one voice but several.

## The context

Miskawayh worked in the fourth Islamic century, the age of the Buyid renaissance in Baghdad and Rayy, when Greek philosophy was being absorbed, debated, and rewritten in Arabic. He was a contemporary of the humanist circle around al-Tawhidi, and his great historical work, Tajarib al-Umam (The Experiences of Nations), shows the same practical intelligence as his ethics: history as a store of experience about how human beings behave. Tahdhib al-Akhlaq draws its framework directly from Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics — above all the doctrine of the mean between excess and deficiency — and from Galen's writings on the soul's three powers and on knowing the defects of the soul, with a clear Neoplatonic, Plotinian colouring in its account of the soul's ascent toward perfection. It is a model case of Greek philosophy received and restated within an Islamic horizon.

## The method of refining character

Miskawayh's central claim is that character is not a fixed endowment but something that can be shaped and refined through habit and training. The emotions are not givens to be endured; they are dispositions that can be trained toward the mean, and this training is the work of a lifetime, beginning in childhood with discipline and habituation. His account can be loosely compared with modern ideas of changing how one relates to an emotion, but it is not identical with them: for Miskawayh, the goal is not symptom relief but the formation of a virtuous character ordered toward happiness (eudaimonia), understood first on a rational, philosophical basis and then reconciled with religious ends. The book is organised in six treatises: the soul and its powers; character and the possibility of changing it; the good and happiness; justice; love and friendship; and the health of the soul and the treatment of its diseases. The four cardinal virtues — wisdom, temperance, courage, and justice — correspond to the three powers of the soul, with justice as the harmony that binds them together.

## Central ideas

Three ideas carry the work. First, the mean: every virtue stands between two vices of excess and deficiency, so that courage lies between recklessness and cowardice and generosity between prodigality and stinginess; emotional life is a matter of finding and holding that middle. Second, the treatability of the soul: the final treatise offers remedies for anger, fear of death, and sorrow, a theme anticipated by al-Kindi in his essay on dispelling sorrows, and Miskawayh's remedies mix argument, habituation, and companionship. Third, the education of the young: Miskawayh gives detailed guidance on the upbringing of children and adolescents, on the shaping of habit before reason is fully formed, which makes his ethics as much a pedagogy as a theory.

## Critical Edition and Philological Lineage

Constantine Zurayk established the critical Arabic edition of *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (American University of Beirut, 1966), based on manuscript sources, which he subsequently translated as *The Refinement of Character* (1968). Studies by Majid Fakhry (*Ethical Theories in Islam*, 1991), Mohammed Arkoun (1970), and Richard Walzer (1962) have traced Miskawayh's Greek and Arabic sources, including the Arabic tradition of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*, the pseudo-Aristotelian *Virtues and Vices*, and Galen's ethical writings. This scholarship describes how Miskawayh harmonized Aristotelian teleology and Galenic faculties with Islamic ethical ideals.

## Difference from al-Ghazali

Miskawayh and al-Ghazali are the two great voices of character ethics in the Arab-Islamic tradition, and the difference between them is instructive. Where al-Ghazali subjects the refinement of the soul to directly religious and legal ends — purification as a path to salvation — Miskawayh builds happiness on a philosophical foundation first, drawing on Aristotle, Galen, and Plotinus, and only then harmonises it with religious purposes. Al-Ghazali is closer to a spiritual-legal method; Miskawayh is closer to Greek moral philosophy in Islamic dress. The contrast is exactly what makes the Arab-Islamic context a context of many voices rather than one.

## Influence

Tahdhib al-Akhlaq shaped the later ethical tradition profoundly. Nasir al-Din al-Tusi drew on it extensively in his Nasirean Ethics (c. 1235), and through al-Tusi it passed into the Persian and Ottoman ethical traditions. Mohammed Arkoun saw in Miskawayh the representative of "Arab humanism" in the fourth Islamic century. Modern scholarship, notably Fakhry, cautions that most of Miskawayh's material is drawn from Greek sources, and that his originality lies in synthesis and harmonisation rather than in inventing a new ethical theory — a judgement that does not diminish the work, since the synthesis itself was the achievement.

## Key works

The primary text is Tahdhib al-Akhlaq, available in Constantine Zurayk's 1968 English translation, The Refinement of Character, and in Zurayk's 1966 Arabic edition. Arkoun's 1970 study of Arab humanism and Fakhry's Ethical Theories in Islam (1991) remain the essential secondary literature, and Peter Adamson's 2016 history of philosophy in the Islamic world gives the best recent account of Miskawayh's place in the tradition.
