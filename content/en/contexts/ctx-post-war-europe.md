# Post-WWI/WWII European Context

Existential therapy did not arise in an intellectual vacuum. It arose as a direct response to collective shocks without precedent: two world wars, the trenches of Verdun and the Somme, the Nazi concentration and extermination camps, the atomic bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945, and the collapse of shared meaning in Europe across three consecutive decades of civilisational violence. This entry documents the historical context in which the concepts of existential anxiety, limit situations, and the search for meaning were formed — and without which they are easily misread as abstract philosophical reflections, when in their historical roots they were responses to a real civilisational and existential crisis.

## The context

The First World War (1914–1918) destroyed the nineteenth century's confidence in progress, reason, and the moral order of Europe; the interwar years brought revolution, economic collapse, and the rise of fascism; and the Second World War (1939–1945) brought total war, genocide, and the first use of atomic weapons. Between the wars, philosophy itself changed register: the great systems gave way to philosophies of existence, anxiety, and absurdity, from Karl Jaspers and Martin Heidegger in Germany to Jean-Paul Sartre and Albert Camus in France. After 1945, Europe rebuilt itself physically — the welfare state, the European project, the long post-war boom — but the philosophical response to the catastrophe continued to shape psychotherapy, literature, and theology for a generation. Any account of existential therapy that ignores this setting mistakes its tone and its urgency.

## Key figures

Karl Jaspers (1883–1969) lived through the First World War and the rise of Nazism in Germany — he was married to a Jewish woman, was harassed by the regime, and was banned from teaching in 1937. A psychiatrist before he was a philosopher, he published General Psychopathology in 1913 and introduced the concept of "limit situations" (Grenzsituationen) — death, suffering, struggle, and guilt — in Psychology of Worldviews (1919): moments that rational philosophy cannot absorb and that demand an existential awareness beyond scientific knowledge. After the war he published The Question of Guilt (1946), distinguishing criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical guilt, a text that shaped the German debate on responsibility. Viktor Frankl is the second founding figure of this context, and his camp experience is treated in its own section below. Theodor Adorno's remark, written in 1949 and published in 1951, that writing poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric became the emblem of a crisis of confidence in European culture, and Primo Levi's If This Is a Man (1947) gave the catastrophe its most exact literary witness.

## Frankl and the concentration camps

Viktor Frankl's direct experience in four Nazi concentration camps — Theresienstadt, Auschwitz, and then the Kaufering and Türkheim camps of Dachau, between 1942 and 1945 — is the founding event on which logotherapy, the therapy of meaning, was built. His father died in Theresienstadt, his mother was murdered in Auschwitz, and his wife Tilly died in Bergen-Belsen. Frankl had begun formulating logotherapy in the 1930s, but the camps became the severe test of his ideas, and he recorded the experience in A Psychologist Experiences the Concentration Camp (1946), known in English as Man's Search for Meaning. This is not a metaphor but a documented lived experience, and it is the reason logotherapy's claim — that meaning can be found even in the most extreme suffering — carries the weight it does.

## Central ideas

The context produced three ideas that run through existential therapy. First, the limit situation: there are experiences — death, guilt, suffering, chance — that cannot be solved or explained away, only faced, and therapy must learn to work with them rather than around them. Second, the collapse of inherited meaning: after the wars, meaning could no longer be received from church, nation, or tradition as a matter of course; it had to be found or made by the individual, which is why the search for meaning became the central clinical question. Third, responsibility after catastrophe: Jaspers's distinctions of guilt and Frankl's insistence that the last human freedom is the choice of one's attitude made responsibility a therapeutic category, not only a moral one.

## Primary Historical Documentation

Beyond retrospective memoirs and secondary narratives, primary historical sources capture the immediacy of the mid-century European crisis:
- **Hannah Arendt's Field Report (1950)**: In "The Aftermath of Nazi Rule: Report from Germany" (published in *Commentary*, October 1950), Arendt documented firsthand the pervasive moral numbness, apathy, and psychological avoidance in post-war Germany, providing empirical specificity to the philosophical diagnoses of Jaspers and Frankl.
- **Sartre's Public Manifesto (1945/1946)**: Jean-Paul Sartre's address *L'Existentialisme est un humanisme* (delivered in October 1945 at the Club Maintenant in Paris, published 1946) articulated directly to a disoriented post-Liberation public why radical freedom, anguish (*angoisse*), and abandonment (*délaissement*) were unavoidable ethical responsibilities in the wake of the Occupation and collaboration.
- **Karl Jaspers's Forensic Lectures (1946)**: *Die Schuldfrage* transcribed Jaspers's 1945/1946 lectures at Heidelberg University immediately following its reopening under Allied administration, providing a precise contemporary dissection of German moral, political, and metaphysical responsibility before the verdicts of the Nuremberg trials.

## Influence

The post-war context shaped existential therapy in its content and its institutions. The British school of van Deurzen and Spinelli, the Swiss Daseinsanalyse of Binswanger and Boss, and the American school of Yalom all inherit its questions, even when they answer them differently. But historians warn against reducing the century to two shocks: post-war Europe was also a time of reconstruction, welfare-state building, and a European project, and experiences differed sharply between east and west, victors and defeated, occupiers and occupied. The existential reading of the wars is therefore a partial interpretation: the philosophical roots of existential therapy predate the wars in Kierkegaard, Heidegger, and Jaspers himself. This context is historical and descriptive; it must not be used to justify or politicise any later clinical reading.

## Key works

Frankl's Man's Search for Meaning (1946) and Jaspers's The Question of Guilt (1946) are the two primary texts of this context. Tony Judt's Postwar (2005) is the best single history of Europe after 1945, and Mark Mazower's Dark Continent (1998) gives the darker account of the century as a whole, including the war years themselves.
