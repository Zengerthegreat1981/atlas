# Al-Ghazali and Ihya' Ulum al-Din

Abu Hamid Muhammad ibn Muhammad al-Ghazali (1058–1111) composed the Ihya' Ulum al-Din (The Revival of the Religious Sciences) after the spiritual crisis of 1095, when he abandoned his teaching post at the Nizamiyya school in Baghdad and set out on years of wandering through Damascus, Jerusalem, and the Hijaz before returning to Tus. The work runs to forty books in four quarters — worship, customs, the deadly vices, and the saving virtues — and the last two quarters, on the diseases of the heart and their cure, are the richest source in the Arab-Islamic tradition for the understanding of the soul and the emotions. Al-Ghazali's treatment of the emotions is a detailed moral-psychological analysis, and the Ihya' is one of the most influential texts of the Arab-Islamic understanding of the self.

## The context

Al-Ghazali wrote at the height of Seljuk power, as the leading theologian of his generation, and his crisis was both personal and intellectual: having mastered philosophy, theology, and law, he found that knowledge alone did not transform the soul, and he turned to Sufism and the disciplined life. The Ihya' is the fruit of that turn — an attempt to revive the religious sciences by grounding them in the inward life rather than in legal formalism alone. It synthesises the ethical psychology of the philosophers, notably Avicenna, whose doctrines al-Ghazali had attacked in The Incoherence of the Philosophers (c. 1095), with the practical discipline of the Sufi tradition. The result is a work that is at once a legal manual, a moral psychology, and a spiritual guide.

## The structure of the heart and the soul

Al-Ghazali opens the quarter on the deadly vices with the Book of the Wonders of the Heart, in which he distinguishes the senses of the words heart, spirit, soul, and intellect, each of which can refer either to a bodily or to a spiritual reality. For the soul (nafs) he uses the Qur'anic ranks: the soul that commands evil (al-ammara bi'l-su'), the self-reproaching soul (al-lawwama), and the soul at peace (al-mutma'inna). These are moral-spiritual states describing a person's condition toward their appetites, not independent divisions of the soul.

## The Book of Condemnation of Anger

Al-Ghazali treats anger as a psychic power with a proper function: repelling harm. It is praiseworthy when it holds the mean between excess and deficiency and obeys reason and the law, and blameworthy when it escapes that governance. His treatment is practical as well as theoretical: he lists remedies for anger, including knowledge of the merit of restraining it, remembering the consequences of revenge, changing one's posture, and performing ablution, and he traces anger's consequences — rancour and envy — when it is allowed to persist. The discussion shows his method at its best: a power of the soul described in its function, its mean, its excess, and its cure.

## The Book of Fear and Hope

In the Book of Fear and Hope, al-Ghazali presents fear and hope as the two wings of the traveller on the spiritual path. He distinguishes fear that motivates action from fear that breeds despair and paralysis, and he compares hope to one who sows seed in good soil and waits for the crop — if one abandons work and merely waits, that is not hope but delusion (ghurur). William McKane's English translation of the book (1962) made it accessible to Western readers. The treatment of fear here is the natural point of contact with this Atlas's concepts of fear and anxiety, but the contact must be handled with care: al-Ghazali's fear is a spiritual state oriented toward the afterlife, not a clinical symptom.

## The method of purification

For al-Ghazali, the soul is not a fixed entity but a field of continuous training and purification: struggle against the appetites (mujahada), habit, companionship, contemplation, and self-accounting. This horizon differs fundamentally from the Western existential framework, which centres on authenticity and individual choice: where the existentialist asks whether a life is one's own, al-Ghazali asks whether a life is rightly ordered toward salvation. The difference lies more in ends than in means — the techniques of self-examination and habituation overlap considerably — but the ends should not be confused.

## Critical Editions and Philological Transmission

The *Ihya'* circulates in many editions; a widely used modern edition is the one published by Dar al-Minhaj (Jeddah, 2011), which is accompanied by Zayn al-Din al-Iraqi's hadith verification (*al-Mughni 'an Haml al-Asfar*). Modern scholarship, including Frank Griffel (2009) and Kenneth Garden (2014), discusses how the work draws on Ash'arite theology, Avicennian psychology, and Sufi asceticism.

## Affective States: Fear, Shame, and Guilt

Al-Ghazali's detailed analysis of the passions connects directly with fundamental psychological affects:
- **Fear (*khawf*)**: In Book 33 (*Kitab al-Khawf wa'l-Raja'*), al-Ghazali treats fear not as a psychological pathology or generalized anxiety, but as an affective motivator for moral vigilance. He contrasts constructive spiritual fear with paralyzing despair (*ya's*), which deadens volition and is deemed spiritually and morally destructive.
- **Shame (*haya'*)**: Treated in several places in the *Ihya'*, *haya'* is conceived as spiritual modesty and internal moral constraint born from an abiding awareness of divine witness (*muraqaba*). It functions as an active self-monitoring mechanism rather than paralyzing social inhibition or neurotic shame.
- **Guilt and Remorse (*nadam* / *tawba*)**: In Book 31 (*Kitab al-Tawba*), repentance is framed as an organic process requiring three consecutive elements: intellectual apprehension (*'ilm*) of wrongdoing, affective remorse or guilt (*nadam*), and purposeful behavioral resolution (*'azm*). Guilt is therefore not an end in itself or a source of chronic self-recrimination, but an indispensable functional catalyst for moral rehabilitation and psychic equilibrium.

## Reception and influence

The Ihya' was received with extraordinary breadth and became one of the most widely read books of ethics in the Islamic world. Accounts report that it was burned in Cordoba around 1109 on the orders of the Almoravid authorities after objections by Maliki jurists. Frank Griffel (2009) argues that al-Ghazali incorporated elements of Avicenna's philosophy into his own theology, despite his explicit criticism of the philosophers.

## A warning against projection

Al-Ghazali's terms must not be read as translations of modern concepts: the commanding soul is not anxiety, the soul at peace is not mental health, and his classification of fear is not a diagnostic manual. His framework for the emotions is a legal-moral one aimed at salvation in the afterlife, not at clinical diagnosis. Any use of the Ihya' in this Atlas must respect that frame, borrowing its precision without importing its conclusions into a different system.

## Key works

The primary text is the Ihya' Ulum al-Din itself, available in the Dar al-Minhaj edition of 2011, among others. McKane's translation of the Book of Fear and Hope (1962) and T. J. Winter's translation of On Disciplining the Soul (1995) are the most useful English entry points. Griffel's Al-Ghazali's Philosophical Theology (2009) is the essential modern study of al-Ghazali's thought as a whole.
