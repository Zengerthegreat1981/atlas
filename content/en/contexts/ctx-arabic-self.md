# The Self in Arabic-Islamic Intellectual Heritage

This entry maps a context, not a single idea: several centuries of Arabic-Islamic reflection on the self, the soul, and the emotions, written by thinkers in different disciplines, regions, and centuries. It is the overarching frame within which the Atlas's three more focused entries belong — Ibn Sina and the *Kitab al-Nafs* (ctx-avicenna-self), Miskawayh and the refinement of character (ctx-miskawayh-character), and al-Ghazali and the *Ihya Ulum al-Din* (ctx-ghazali). Those entries give the detail; this one gives the shared ground and the caution needed when reading them alongside modern psychology.

## Why a Context, Not a Concept

A concept in this Atlas is a defined theoretical construction, such as anxiety or envy, that can be stated and revised. A context is a whole intellectual tradition: many voices, internal disagreements, and several centuries of development, used as a reference frame through which many concepts can be read. The Arabic-Islamic heritage qualifies because its writers did not share one theory of the self. A physician-philosopher, a moral philosopher, and a theologian-mystic could use the same word — *nafs*, soul or self — and mean overlapping but different things by it. Treating it as one doctrine would flatten what makes it useful: several distinct models of inner life, not one.

The tradition is also not a translation of modern psychology into older language. Its writers were answering their own questions — about the soul's faculties, the discipline of character, the knowledge of God, and the soul's fate after death — shaped by medicine, Aristotelian philosophy, theology, and Sufi practice at once.

## Three Principal Voices

Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058–1111) writes at length on the emotions. In the *Ihya Ulum al-Din* (*The Revival of the Religious Sciences*, composed in the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries), he devotes whole books to anger, fear, and hope, classifying emotional states in a vocabulary that mixes law, theology, and the discipline of the heart. For al-Ghazali, an emotion is a state of the heart (*qalb*) with a moral direction, to be examined and disciplined.

Ibn Sina (980–1037), known in Latin as Avicenna, approaches the self from philosophy and medicine. His *Kitab al-Nafs* (*Book of the Soul*), part of the *Kitab al-Shifa* (*Book of Healing*, early eleventh century), gives a systematic theory of the soul's faculties: the senses, imagination, estimation, memory, and the rational soul. Where al-Ghazali writes as a spiritual guide, Ibn Sina writes as a theorist, asking what the soul is and how it relates to the body. His "flying man" argument holds that a person aware of no body would still affirm his own existence.

Ahmad ibn Muhammad Miskawayh (died 1030) takes the ethical angle. His *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (*The Refinement of Character*, eleventh century) treats the emotions as matters of character formation: anger, envy, fear, and desire are trained, through habit and reflection, toward a balanced disposition. Drawing on Greek ethics, he describes the good life as a mean between excess and deficiency in each passion.

These three do not agree in any simple way: faculty psychology, ethics of habituation, and spiritual discipline are different projects, and keeping their differences visible is part of this context's value.

## The Vocabulary of the Self

The tradition resists summary partly because its key terms do not map one-to-one onto modern ones. *Nafs* can mean the soul, the self, the appetitive part of the person, or the lower self that must be disciplined, depending on the writer and the passage. *Qalb*, the heart, is the seat of awareness and intention rather than only of feeling. *Ruh*, spirit, and *aql*, intellect or reason, mark further distinctions that different authors draw differently. The terms must be learned in use, author by author, before any comparison is attempted.

## The Projection Risk

The largest methodological danger here is projection: taking a modern psychological concept — existential anxiety in Kierkegaard's sense, or shame as an object of appraisal in a modern theory such as Richard Lazarus's — and reading it back into a heritage text as if the author had meant the same thing. The words may look similar, but the concepts were built inside different systems, for different purposes, centuries apart. Al-Ghazali's fear, for example, is fear of God and of the final reckoning inside a theology of salvation, not the same object as fear in a modern emotion taxonomy. The rule is therefore strict: read each text in its own terms first, and only then ask what it shares with a modern concept.

## First Cautious Bridges to the Emotion Entries

With that caution in place, a first exploratory bridge can be drawn — labelled exploratory, not established. Al-Ghazali's classification of fear in the Books of Fear and Hope invites a careful preliminary comparison with the account of fear in con-fear, which draws on Lazarus's appraisal theory: both treat fear as an evaluative response to a perceived threat, but differ on what counts as the threat and on what a good response looks like. Miskawayh's treatment of anger and envy in the *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* opens a similar preliminary comparison with con-jealousy-envy: both treat envy as a vice of character rather than a passing mood, though Miskawayh's remedy is habituation toward a mean, not a therapeutic technique. ## Comparison with the Four Emotion Entries

The four emotion entries do not line up evenly with this context's three authors. The fear entry treats fear as an appraisal of a present threat when coping looks limited. It also places Ibn Sina's fear in the irascible faculty, with innate heat drawing toward the heart, and al-Ghazali's fear, in the book on fear and hope, as pain of the heart at expected harm, parsed as knowledge, inward state and action, with both too little and too much fear counted as failures. Miskawayh appears there on fear of death as confusion about the soul. The jealousy entry separates two-party envy from three-party jealousy, and its Arabic section uses al-Ghazali's contrast between *hasad*, willing the loss of another's blessing, and *ghibta*, emulation without ill will. It does not use Miskawayh's mean. The sadness entry discusses *huzn* through al-Balkhi, al-Kindi and the public elegy, not through these three authors. The shame entry uses Miskawayh's *haya'* as an early sign of moral capacity and separates restraint that blocks base acts from timidity that blocks just action, which is a virtue of modesty rather than a condemnation of the whole self. The bridges stay local. They are not a collation of passages from a critical edition.

## What This Entry Does Not Claim

It does not claim that this heritage anticipates existential psychology, or that its authors were psychologists in the modern sense. It claims something smaller: that a rich, multi-voiced tradition of reflection on the self exists, that three of its major authors have their own entries in this Atlas, and that any engagement between that tradition and modern psychology must begin by respecting the distance between them.
