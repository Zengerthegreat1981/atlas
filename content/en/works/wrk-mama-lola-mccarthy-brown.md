# Mama Lola: A Vodou Priestess in Brooklyn

A landmark ethnographic book by Karen McCarthy Brown, published in 1991 by the University of California Press (an expanded edition appeared in 2001). It presents an intimate ethnographic biography of Alourdes ("Mama Lola"), a Haitian immigrant Vodou priestess who works from her home in Brooklyn, New York.

## Definition

The book is among the most widely read works in the academic study of Afro-Caribbean religions. It does not limit itself to describing Haitian Vodou rituals; it makes the long friendship between the researcher and her principal informant a methodological subject in its own right. It appeared at a time when the American popular image of Vodou was still loaded with the legacy of Hollywood films about "black magic" and "zombies," and Brown's work offered a serious academic alternative.

## Historical Context

The roots of Haitian Vodou lie in the blending of West African beliefs (especially Dahomean and Kongo) carried by enslaved people to the French colony of Saint-Domingue, with elements of the popular Catholicism that was imposed on them. This syncretic religion played a role in the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804). With the waves of Haitian emigration to the United States in the second half of the twentieth century, especially after the political upheavals under the Duvalier regime, groups of Vodou practitioners moved to American cities such as New York and Miami, where they reorganized their religious lives within new family-spiritual networks. In this context Brown met Alourdes in 1978, and a friendship and fieldwork began that lasted for decades.

## Main Ideas and Method

The book traces several generations of Vodou practitioners in Alourdes's family. It presents Haitian Vodou as a living religious practice rooted in the everyday life of immigrants: healing, kinship, migration, and the central role of women as priestesses and intermediaries between the human world and the world of the *lwa* (the intermediary spirits invoked in rituals). Brown adopts an intimate feminist ethnographic method that gives the native informant's voice a central narrative space. Entire chapters are presented in Alourdes's own voice, as the researcher transcribed and organized them literarily. This was regarded at the time as a daring methodological experiment within academic anthropology.

## Principal Works and Figures

Karen McCarthy Brown (d. 2015), professor of anthropology and sociology of religion at Drew University, is among the most prominent researchers of Haitian Vodou in the generation after that of Alfred Métraux, the Swiss ethnologist who carried out fieldwork in Haiti in the 1940s and wrote an influential early study of Vodou. Brown's work marked a clear break from this model. She replaced distant structural description with long-term participant fieldwork and a reciprocal human relationship with her research subject, to the extent that she herself took part in some Vodou rites as part of her fieldwork.

## Impact and Criticism

The book is regarded as a founding model of an intimate feminist ethnographic method in the study of Afro-Caribbean religions, combining anthropological precision with literary sensitivity. It entered the teaching curricula of religious studies and anthropology in American universities. Some critics have raised reservations about the limits of generalizing from a single family case study to Haitian Vodou as a whole, and about the complex ethical relationship between researcher and informant when the friendship itself becomes material for academic publication. Nevertheless, the work has remained consistently present in the core bibliography of any university course on Vodou or on contemporary religious ethnographic methodology.

## Concluding Notes on Method

Brown wrote some chapters in the first person in Alourdes's own voice, based on long recorded conversations that she organized and edited. This ethnographic technique provoked a broad methodological debate on the limits of textual mediation between the voice of the informant and the voice of the researcher. The 2001 expanded edition added new material updating the story of Alourdes's family after the first edition appeared.

The chapters also detail the rites of initiation (*kanzo*) that a person passes through to become an officially recognized Vodou priest or priestess, explaining its stages from ritual seclusion to the symbolic "rebirth" of the priest. Brown shows how this ritual trajectory intersects with the experience of migration itself: Alourdes, like many priestesses of her generation, carried to America a ritual knowledge inherited from her mother and grandmother, keeping it alive despite her move from rural Haiti to an urban neighborhood in Brooklyn. This partly explains the subtitle, which ties Vodou to a specific American place rather than to her homeland alone.
