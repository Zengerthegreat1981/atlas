# Theses of 1929

## Definition

The founding programmatic statement that the Prague Circle presented collectively to the First Congress of Slavic Philologists in Prague in 1929. It is the reference document that defined the functional-structural method distinguishing the Circle both from pure Saussurean structuralism and from the earlier historical-comparative tradition.

## Historical context

The Prague Linguistic Circle was founded in 1926 on the initiative of the Czech linguist Vilém Mathesius, and included Czech linguists and Russian émigrés, most prominently Roman Jakobson, who lived in Czechoslovakia, and Nikolai Trubetzkoy, who worked in Vienna and took part in the Circle's work largely by correspondence. The "Theses" were the fruit of the Circle's regular discussions in its first years, with Jakobson, Mukařovský and others among the main contributors. They were presented officially as a collective document signed in the name of the Circle (not in the names of individual members) to the First International Congress of Slavic Philologists held in Prague in 1929, at a moment of great intellectual and artistic activity in interwar Czechoslovakia.

## Core principle: language is a functional system

The Theses open by asserting that language is a system of expressive means serving a specific purpose, namely communication, and that any linguistic phenomenon (whether phonetic or grammatical) must first be understood through its function within this communicative system, not as an isolated datum. This principle distinguished the Prague "functional structuralism" from purely descriptive structuralism. In this way, the Circle combined the structural heritage of Saussure (language is a system of relations, not a sum of isolated elements) with a new functional orientation that rejects merely describing the system formally without linking it to its actual communicative aims. This distinguished it from other readings of Saussure that moved toward pure formalism, as in the Copenhagen Circle.

## Main provisions: phonology, synchrony and stylistic variety

The Theses contain specific methodological provisions: the establishment of phonology as a science independent of phonetics, studying the distinctive function of sounds rather than their physical properties; the call to study language synchronically without entirely neglecting the evolutionary dimension, as some extreme readings of Saussure did; and the recognition of the diversity of functional systems within a single language (such as literary language versus colloquial language) as functionally distinct subsystems rather than deviations from one norm.

## The literary language and the norm

A distinctive contribution of the Theses is their treatment of the status of the "literary language" (the language of elevated written culture) as a functionally distinct system with its own internal standards and distinctive social function (cultural unification, aesthetic expression and intellectual precision). It is not necessarily regarded as linguistically "higher" than other systems such as colloquial language or regional dialects, but as merely another subsystem serving a different social function. The idea has points of contact with later sociolinguistic debates about the standard language.

## Impact and critique

The Theses shaped the programmatic map that guided all subsequent research of the Prague Circle (from Trubetzkoy's phonology to Mathesius's functional sentence perspective), and are among the most important founding documents in the history of functional linguistic structuralism, and a founding statement of functional structuralism. Later functional approaches, such as Halliday's systemic functional linguistics and Dik's functional grammar, share the Prague emphasis on function, though their direct indebtedness varies. Some historians of linguistic thought have noted, however, that the collective character of the document sometimes concealed real internal disagreements among members of the Circle about the details of applying these general principles, especially between Trubetzkoy's more formal orientation in phonology and Mathesius's greater interest in the functional analysis of actual discourse. 

## Connection with the Arab-Islamic heritage

The Prague Circle's distinction between the literary language and its functional subsystems parallels the reality of Arabic diglossia between Standard Arabic (which serves the functions of formal writing, religion, literature and shared media) and the many regional colloquials (which serve everyday communication). The Prague framework provides a conceptual tool for understanding this diglossia as a legitimate functional differentiation between integrated linguistic systems, rather than as a deviation of the colloquial from an inherently "higher" standard Standard Arabic.
