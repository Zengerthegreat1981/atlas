# Theses of 1929

## Definition

The collective programmatic statement that the Prague Linguistic Circle presented to the First International Congress of Slavists (Slavic philologists) in Prague in 1929. The document sets out a functional-structural method, distinct both from a purely relational reading of Saussure and from historical-comparative description alone.

## Historical context

The Prague Linguistic Circle was founded in 1926 on the initiative of the Czech linguist Vilém Mathesius. Its early members included Czech scholars and Russian émigrés, notably Roman Jakobson, then in Czechoslovakia, and Nikolai Trubetzkoy, who held a chair in Vienna and joined the Circle's work largely by correspondence. The Theses grew out of the Circle's discussions in its first years. Jakobson, Jan Mukařovský, and others took a large part in drafting them. They were issued as a collective text in the Circle's name, not under individual signatures, at the congress held in Prague from 6 to 13 October 1929, and printed the same year in the first volume of *Travaux du Cercle Linguistique de Prague*.

## Core principle: language is a functional system

The Theses open by asserting that language is a system of expressive means serving a specific purpose, namely communication, and that any linguistic phenomenon (whether phonetic or grammatical) must first be understood through its function within this communicative system, not as an isolated datum. This principle is what later writers call Prague functional structuralism. The Circle kept Saussure's claim that language is a system of relations, not a sum of isolated elements, and added the requirement that each element be described by its communicative function. Glossematics, the strongly formal development of Saussure associated with Louis Hjelmslev and the Copenhagen Linguistic Circle (founded in 1931), is a later and separate line. It is not a position the 1929 text itself answers.

## Main provisions: phonology, synchrony and stylistic variety

The Theses contain specific methodological provisions: the establishment of phonology as a science independent of phonetics, studying the distinctive function of sounds rather than their physical properties; the call to study language synchronically without entirely neglecting the evolutionary dimension, as some extreme readings of Saussure did; and the recognition of the diversity of functional systems within a single language (such as literary language versus colloquial language) as functionally distinct subsystems rather than deviations from one norm.

## The literary language and the norm

A distinctive contribution of the Theses is their treatment of the status of the "literary language" (the language of elevated written culture) as a functionally distinct system with its own internal standards and distinctive social function (cultural unification, aesthetic expression and intellectual precision). The Theses treat that variety as a system with its own norm and social tasks, including cultural unity and intellectual precision, rather than as a mere corruption of everyday speech. Later sociolinguistics, including Charles A. Ferguson's account of diglossia (1959), takes up related questions about standard and vernacular varieties. The 1929 text does not use that later terminology.

## Impact and critique

The Theses outlined problems that later Prague work took up, including Trubetzkoy's phonology and Mathesius's functional sentence perspective (*aktuální členění větné*). They are a principal programmatic document of functional structuralism. Later functional theories, including M. A. K. Halliday's systemic functional linguistics and Simon C. Dik's functional grammar, share the emphasis on function. How directly each derives from the 1929 text varies. Because the Theses are collective, they do not record every difference of emphasis among members. Trubetzkoy's phonological work is more formal; Mathesius gave more space to the analysis of discourse. 

## Connection with the Arab-Islamic heritage

The Prague distinction between a literary language and other functional varieties offers one way of describing Arabic diglossia: Standard Arabic used in formal writing, religion, literature, and shared media, and regional colloquials used in everyday speech. The comparison is analytical. The Circle did not write about Arabic, and the Theses do not claim that every vernacular is equivalent to the literary language in social prestige.
