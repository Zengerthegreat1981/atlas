# The Cheese and the Worms: The Cosmos of a Sixteenth-Century Miller

## Definition

The founding work of Italian microhistory (*Microstoria*) by the historian **Carlo Ginzburg** (born 1939). It was published in Italian in 1976 under the title *Il formaggio e i vermi* and translated into English in 1980. The book is today one of the most widely read works in history faculties around the world, and one of the foundational texts of the microhistory current, which spread from Italy to the rest of Europe and to America from the late 1970s onwards.

## Content

The book reconstructs the world of **Domenico Scandella**, known as "Menocchio", a miller from the Italian village of Montereale in the Friuli region in north-eastern Italy, who was burned alive for heresy in 1599. Over the years he had developed a distinctive and cosmological religious outlook that was entirely foreign to official Catholic doctrine. Menocchio claimed that the original universe was a chaotic mass resembling cheese, in which the angels (including God himself) came into being just as worms spontaneously form in rotting cheese. This was a popular, materialist cosmology that combined scattered readings from printed books that reached him (a translated Bible, the travels of Marco Polo, Boccaccio's *Decameron*) with a long-established local oral peasant tradition that predated the invention of printing itself. For this reconstruction Ginzburg relied on the transcripts of two rare trials before the Roman Inquisition (in 1584 and 1599), in which Menocchio's interrogations were recorded with exceptional detail, and in which he repeatedly explained his strange ideas to his judges.

## Methodological Importance

The book embodies the principle of the "Exceptional Normal" that Ginzburg later formulated theoretically: Menocchio's individual case, though exceptional and documented in unusual detail only because of his trial, illuminates — when read with methodological caution — a wider stratum of the oral peasant "popular culture" that rarely leaves a direct written trace in official archives dominated by the voice of the educated elite. Ginzburg argues that Menocchio's ideas, despite their apparent uniqueness, reflect an ancient peasant cultural foundation (popular material conceptions of the cosmos) that interacted with the new printed reading material available after the invention of printing, producing a unique intellectual synthesis that can only be reached through this judicial "archival accident".

## Impact and Criticism

The book had a huge impact on the historical field worldwide. Together with contemporary works such as Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie's study of the French village of Montaillou (1975), it established the current of "microhistory", which builds broad cultural conclusions from the intensive, well-documented study of a single small case, in contrast to the quantitative and long-series history prevalent at the time. The book faced methodological criticism from historians who questioned how far broad cultural conclusions can be generalised from a single case that was itself atypical (precisely because it attracted the attention of the Inquisition). This discussion about "representativeness" remains alive in debates on microhistory today. Nevertheless, the book has remained a model for combining rigorous archival precision with an attractive narrative capacity.

## The Fate of Menocchio

After his first conviction in 1584, Menocchio was ordered to repent and refrain from publishing his ideas. But years later he returned to recounting his cosmology to those around him, which led to his second trial in 1599 before a far stricter Inquisition tribunal. By then the Catholic Church's anxiety about heresy had grown acute, shortly after the burning of the philosopher Giordano Bruno in 1600, and Menocchio was sentenced to death by burning. Ginzburg documents this judicial escalation to show how the level of tolerance of religious authority towards popular intellectual deviations changed between the two periods, reflecting a general European climate of increasing rigidity towards heresy at the end of the sixteenth century.

## Its Influence on Fiction and Film

Ginzburg's book inspired literary and cinematic works that dealt with similar marginal figures from the judicial archives of history. It became a reference model for writing "history from below", which restores the standing of the voices of obscure peasants and artisans who were usually left outside grand political history. Its narrative style combines academic rigour with a storytelling capacity that makes the book readable beyond specialised academic circles as well, which contributed to its unusually wide fame for a work of academic scholarship on medieval European history.

## Menocchio's Reading

Ginzburg details with striking precision the list of books that Menocchio read or had heard of (around eleven books identified from the interrogation records). These ranged from a popular translation of the Bible and the travels of Marco Polo to a translated copy of the Qur'an circulating in Italy. This shows how this peasant reader recombined these materials of disparate sources within an oral peasant mental framework older than printing itself, producing a unique cosmology that could not have arisen except at that specific historical moment of the early spread of printing.
