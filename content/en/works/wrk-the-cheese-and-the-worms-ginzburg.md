# The Cheese and the Worms: The Cosmos of a Sixteenth-Century Miller

*The Cheese and the Worms: The Cosmos of a Sixteenth-Century Miller* (*Il formaggio e i vermi: Il cosmo di un mugnaio del '500*, 1976; translated into English by John and Anne C. Tedeschi in 1980) by the Italian historian Carlo Ginzburg is a classic of Italian microhistory (*microstoria*). Through an intensive examination of inquisitorial court records, the book reconstructs the mental universe, reading practices, and materialist cosmology of a literate miller in the Counter-Reformation era.

## Menocchio and His Cosmos

The central subject of the work is Domenico Scandella (1532–1599), known as Menocchio, a miller from the village of Montereale Valcellina in Friuli, northeastern Italy. Interrogated by the Roman Inquisition during two distinct trials in 1583–1584 and 1599, Menocchio articulated a startling cosmological theory: the universe originated out of primeval chaos just as cheese forms out of milk, and God and the angels emerged spontaneously from this matter just like worms appearing in decaying cheese. 

Menocchio held deeply radical religious convictions: he questioned the virgin birth, denied the divinity of Christ, criticized the Latin liturgy as an instrument of clerical oppression against the illiterate poor, and espoused religious tolerance, claiming that God loved Muslims, Jews, and Christians equally. Despite being admonished and sentenced to life imprisonment in 1584 (commuted to house arrest in 1586), Menocchio continued expounding his beliefs. Following a second trial in 1599, Pope Clement VIII intervened personally, and Menocchio was executed at the stake in late 1599—just months before Giordano Bruno was burned in Rome in February 1600.

## Popular Culture and Circularity

Methodologically, Ginzburg intervened in major debates over early modern popular culture. Opposing both the thesis of top-down acculturation (advanced by Robert Mandrou) and Mikhail Bakhtin's view of popular culture as wholly autonomous and carnival-based, Ginzburg argued for a complex "circularity" (*circolarità*) between elite and subordinate cultures. 

Menocchio was literate, and Ginzburg traced the roughly a dozen books he read, including the vernacular Bible, John Mandeville's travels, Boccaccio's *Decameron*, and Andrea Arrivabene's 1547 Italian translation of the Qur'an. Rather than passively absorbing these printed works, Menocchio subjected them to a violent, selective reading filter grounded in an ancient, oral peasant materialism. The collision between the newly democratizing technology of the printing press and archaic peasant oral traditions produced Menocchio's idiosyncratic worldview.

## The Exceptional Normal

*The Cheese and the Worms* provided an applied template for what Italian microhistorians (notably Edoardo Grendi) theorized as the "exceptional normal" (*l'eccezionale normale*). While Menocchio's vocal defiance and trial records were atypical—the vast majority of sixteenth-century peasants left no archival trace—his very exceptionality reveals the otherwise invisible cultural currents and cognitive boundaries of his contemporary society. By reading judicial archives against the intentions of the inquisitors, Ginzburg retrieved subaltern agency and intellectual vitality, helping make microhistory an influential alternative to quantitative macrohistory.
