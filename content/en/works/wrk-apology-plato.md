# Plato's Apology of Socrates (Apologia)

A text that presents itself as a literary and philosophical recasting of Socrates' defense speech before the court of Athens in 399 BCE, when he was tried on the charges of corrupting the youth, denying the gods of the city and introducing new gods, and the trial ended in his execution. The Greek word Apologia means "defense" or "reply to an accusation," not "apology" in the familiar sense. Plato (c. 428–348 BCE), who names himself among those present at the trial, presents here the image of his teacher on which he built his philosophy.

## The Central Thesis

Philosophy as a mission that admits no bargaining: Socrates refuses to give up the practice of critical examination ("the unexamined life is not worth living," 38a) even at the price of his life, and presents his defense not to appease the judges but as an account of his mission, which, by his claim, the Delphic oracle assigned to him when the priestess answered his friend Chaerephon that no one was wiser than Socrates, so he devoted his life to testing this claim by questioning those who thought themselves wise, politicians, poets and craftsmen, and found them claiming a knowledge they did not possess. He concluded that his wisdom is "human wisdom" that knows the limits of its ignorance, not knowledge of divine matters. He links this to his role as the "gadfly" that rouses a sluggish city, and to the daimonion, the divine voice that prevents him from certain actions but never urges him to act.

Among his moral claims in the defense are that the good man suffers no real harm in life or in death (41d), that what matters more than survival is not committing injustice, and that he will obey God rather than the court if it orders him to abandon philosophy. These positions made the text a reference in the philosophical debate over the limits of obedience to political authority and freedom of thought.

## Structure of the Work

Three successive speeches rather than chapters:

1. **The first defense speech** before the jury (about 500 jurors), in which he answers the old accusations (Aristophanes' caricature of him in the play *The Clouds*) and the new official charges (Meletus, Anytus and Lycon), and introduces the idea of "self-examination" as a service to the city and not subversion of it, and cross-examines Meletus in a way that exposes the contradiction of the charge.
2. **The speech on the alternative penalty**, after the vote of conviction by a narrow margin, in which Socrates proposes, as provocation rather than abasement, that he be honored with meals in the Prytaneion at the city's expense, and then accepts a small fine with the guarantee of Plato and his friends.
3. **The final speech** after the death sentence, in which he addresses those who convicted him and those who acquitted him separately, reflecting on death: either a nothingness resembling sleep without dreams, or a passage to a place where he will meet the earlier dead, and he has no certainty about either.

## Historical Context

The text was written after Socrates' execution, within a literary wave produced by his students to defend his reputation (among them Xenophon's *Apology of Socrates*, a text different in style and content). The precise date of composition within the decades following 399 BCE is not known. The trial took place after Athens' defeat in the Peloponnesian War and the rule of the Thirty, and some of Socrates' students, such as Critias, were pillars of that regime, so the city kept a bitter memory, which probably contributed to the conviction. The Greek text has reached us through the Byzantine manuscript tradition; the standard critical edition is that of **John Burnet** in *Platonis Opera* (Clarendon Press, Oxford, vol. 1, 1900).

## Reception and Critique

It is regarded as one of the Platonic texts closest to the "historical" image of Socrates, since it lacks the complex theoretical apparatus (the theory of Forms, advanced dialectic) that marks Plato's mature dialogues, so many scholars hold that it is closer to the "early Socratic" dialogues. It is not to be confused with the dialogues *Crito* (the argument for obligation to the laws of the city, set in prison after the trial) and *Phaedo* (the death scene and the arguments for the immortality of the soul); the three form a narrative sequence but are texts distinct in subject and arguments.

Its reading is pulled by conflicting interpretations: some scholars see in it a Socrates who believes he is commanded by divine inspiration, making his defense religious in essence; others see it as purely rationalist; and others ask whether it is a record of what was said in court or a Platonic formulation. I. F. Stone, in *The Trial of Socrates* (1988), holds that the conviction of Socrates reflects a political conflict with Athenian democracy.

## Presence in the Arab Heritage

The text of the *Apology* has not come down to us in a known early Arabic translation, but Arab culture knew Socrates and his death through the books of biographical classes and wisdom literature, as in al-Qifti's *Akhbar al-Hukama'*, and in al-Shahrastani's *al-Milal wa-l-Nihal*, which presents Socrates in the chapter on the philosophers. Modern Arabic translations of the text, separately or within editions of Plato's dialogues, circulate today.
