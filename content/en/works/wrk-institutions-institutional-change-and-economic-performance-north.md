# Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance

**Douglass North** published his book *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* in 1990. In it he offered the most comprehensive theoretical formulation of his intellectual project: extending institutional analysis, which Coase and Williamson had applied mainly at the level of the individual firm, to explain long-run differences in economic performance between entire nations.

## Institutions as the Rules of the Game

The book opens with an influential general definition: **"institutions" are the rules of the game in a society**, or, more precisely, the humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction. These include formal rules (constitutions, laws and written property rights) and informal rules (customs, traditions and inherited codes of conduct), together with enforcement mechanisms that make compliance predictable and reliable for the parties dealing with one another. North draws a clear methodological distinction between these "rules" and the **"players" (organisations)**, such as firms, trade unions, political parties and government bodies, which arise and operate within the limits of these rules and sometimes seek to change them to their advantage through organised political and economic activity.

## Why Do Economies Not Converge Automatically?

North confronts a central question that standard neoclassical growth theory answered simply: if markets are free and technical knowledge is globally available, why do poor economies not converge automatically toward the income levels of rich countries? North argues that the answer lies in the quality of institutions, especially the extent to which they effectively protect property rights and reduce transaction costs in the wider market, rather than the mere formal existence of a market. He treats institutions as a decisive variable missing from much traditional analysis. Poor institutions (corruption, arbitrary expropriation, weak judicial enforcement) keep many economies stuck below their optimal level, despite the theoretical availability of resources and technical knowledge.

## Case Study: The Glorious Revolution and British Growth

North cites a central historical example: how the **English Glorious Revolution** (1688), which curbed the absolute power of the crown in favour of Parliament and protected property rights and creditors from arbitrary expropriation, improved the credibility of the state's commitments to its creditors and its economic citizens. North and Weingast argued that this helped enable the development of public debt and capital markets, which they linked to later British economic strength. This contrasts with other historical paths in which the absolute state remained able to seize private wealth without restraint.

## Path Dependence

North draws on the concept of **"path dependence"** (developed in economics by Paul David and Brian Arthur) to explain why inefficient institutions persist for long periods despite the clarity of their inefficiency. Early historical decisions create a network of entrenched interests, mutual expectations and sunk costs, which make the transition to an alternative institutional path, even one that is theoretically more efficient, politically and economically costly and resisted by those who benefit from the status quo.

## Impact on Development Economics and Governance

The book became a basic reference in comparative development economics. It contributed to a wider shift in the attention of economists and development institutions toward governance, property rights and the rule of law as determinants of development alongside traditional macroeconomic policies. North shared the 1993 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Robert Fogel for work renewing economic history; this book is among his best-known contributions.

## Earlier Joint Research

In presenting the Glorious Revolution argument, North drew on an earlier joint study with Barry Weingast published in 1989. In it they analysed in detail how the constitutional settlement between the crown and Parliament after 1688 changed the credibility of the English state's financial commitments to its creditors, which allowed deeper and more stable government and private debt markets to develop. The 1990 book is a general theoretical extension of this specific historical argument into a framework applicable to many other historical cases.

## A Later Debate on the Generality of the Framework

The book provoked an ongoing academic debate about how far its general framework can actually be applied to diverse historical contexts outside the Western European experience on which it was mainly based. Some economic historians have criticised the use of North's model in a circular way (assuming that any society that failed economically must have had poor institutions, without an independent measure of institutional quality established in advance). This methodological criticism remains present in debates in comparative development economics to this day.

## A Theoretical Maturity Crowning a Long Research Career

The book represents a stage of theoretical maturity in North's long research career. That career began with quantitative studies of American economic history and ocean shipping, passed through joint historical work with Robert Thomas, and culminated in this more abstract general formulation, which is intended to apply across historical and contemporary contexts. His intellectual trajectory can therefore be read as a movement from specific historical studies toward a general theory of institutions.
