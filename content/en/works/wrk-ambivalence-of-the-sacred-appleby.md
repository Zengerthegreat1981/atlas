# The Ambivalence of the Sacred: Religion, Violence, and Reconciliation (Appleby)

R. Scott Appleby's book, published in 2000 by Rowman & Littlefield in cooperation with the Carnegie Commission on Preventing Deadly Conflict, is a widely cited work in which he developed the thesis of the structural "ambivalence" of religious traditions toward violence and peace.

## Content

The book argues that the major religions carry both the potential for sacred violence and the potential for peaceful reconciliation structurally, neither in isolation from the other. It draws on the study of actual religious actors and their decisions in specific conflict contexts, rather than on an abstract analysis of doctrine apart from its historical practitioners. The book explicitly refutes the two common reductive explanations, that religion is "essentially" peaceful or that religion is "essentially" violent, through comparative examples from multiple religious traditions.

## Religious Actors rather than "Religion"

Appleby treats religion not as a single unified entity that is judged violent or peaceful all at once, but as a field of actors (militants, leaders, communities and institutions) who interpret and apply their traditions in particular circumstances. In his account, the same religious commitment, knowledge of texts and capacity to interpret a tradition can be turned toward sanctioning violence or toward peacemaking and reconciliation. The decisive difference lies less in the nature of religion itself than in how actors interpret their tradition and in the historical and political context in which they act.

## Case Studies and the Comparative Method

The book draws on case studies of conflicts in several regions of the world. It shows how religious actors played quite different roles in contexts that look similar on the surface. Some fueled sectarian conflict, while others led effective initiatives for interfaith dialogue and communal reconciliation, sometimes within the same religion and the same society.

## Critique of the Two Reductive Explanations

Appleby devotes a significant part of the book to refuting two opposing positions common in both public and academic discourse. The first, common among some religious apologists and proponents of interfaith dialogue, holds that the "true" or "authentic" religion is essentially peaceful, and that any violence committed in its name is necessarily a deviation from its essence, or its politicization by non-religious parties. The second, common among some critical secularists and theorists of the "clash of civilizations," holds the opposite: that religion carries a latent seed of violence that inevitably erupts whenever religion acquires genuine social and political influence. Appleby rejects both positions as reductive simplifications that ignore the abundant historical and contemporary evidence that both possibilities can coexist within the same religious tradition, and sometimes within the same religious community at the same historical moment.

## Impact on International Peacebuilding Policy

The book's influence extended beyond academic circles into the practical field of international peacebuilding policy. It is often cited in discussions urging policymakers and peacebuilding organizations to take local religious actors seriously as potential partners in mediation and reconciliation, rather than treating them as an obstacle to conventional secular conflict resolution or ignoring them in formal peace negotiations.

## Relation to Bruce Lincoln's Approach

Appleby's book is often compared with Bruce Lincoln's *Holy Terrors* (2003), which appeared shortly after the September 11 attacks. Lincoln focused more on analyzing extremist religious discourse itself, using tools from the history of religions and textual analysis. Appleby, by contrast, focused on the institutional and social structure of religious actors and their capacity to shift between the poles of violence and reconciliation. The two works complement each other as models of two approaches to the academic comparative study of religious violence: discourse analysis on one hand, and analysis of actors and institutions on the other.

## Appleby's Institutional Background

Appleby is a historian of religion at the University of Notre Dame, where he is associated with the Kroc Institute for International Peace Studies. The book was produced in cooperation with the Carnegie Commission on Preventing Deadly Conflict, which gives it an applied orientation toward policymakers as well as an academic one.
