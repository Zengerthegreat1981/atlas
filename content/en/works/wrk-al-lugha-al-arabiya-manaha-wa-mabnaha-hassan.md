# Al-Lugha al-Arabiyya Ma'naha wa Mabnaha (Arabic Language: Its Meaning and Its Form)

A reference work by Tammam Hassan, published in 1973. It is his best-known work in his project of rereading the traditional Arabic grammar with modern linguistic tools, without a complete break with the tradition and without blindly importing Western methods.

## Methodology: Between Traditional Grammar and Modern Linguistics

The book seeks to draw out modern descriptive insights that were already latent in classical Arabic grammar. It uses tools from structural and functional linguistics to reorganize and interpret the traditional grammatical material in a more coherent methodological way, rather than replacing the tradition entirely with imported Western terminology.

## The Intellectual Context of the Author

Tammam Hassan wrote this work after spending years studying Western linguistics in Britain, where he was influenced by the London school of J. R. Firth and its emphasis on context. He returned to Egypt with an ambitious intellectual project: that modern Arabic language study should be neither a mechanical importation of Western terminology (as some of his predecessors had done), nor a closed repetition of the grammatical heritage without renewal. Rather, it should be a critical rereading that draws from the early grammarians (especially Sibawayh) analytical distinctions that can be restated with the tools of contemporary linguistics.

## Structure and Topics of the Book

In its successive sections, the book treats topics including the nature of the Arabic nominal and verbal sentence, the relation between inflection (*iʿrab*) and meaning, the concept of fronting and postponement and its semantic and pragmatic function, questions of definiteness and indefiniteness, and the levels of morphological, syntactic and semantic analysis taken together. In each question it presents a dialogue between what the early grammarians said and what modern linguistic analytical tools allow in the way of a more coherent methodological reformulation.

## The Concept of Grammatical "Indicators" (*Qara'in*)

In the book Hassan develops a central concept: the "indicators" (*qara'in*), the linguistic and contextual signs that work together (such as morphological form, position in the sentence, linking particles, and semantic and situational indicators) to indicate the grammatical and semantic meaning of a construction together. This is an attempt at a methodical characterization of how multiple elements interact to produce meaning, rather than relying on a single isolated morphological rule. Hassan distinguishes between verbal indicators (such as inflection, word order, form, agreement and linking) and semantic indicators (such as predication and relation). The notion may owe something to Firth's emphasis on context, but it is developed to suit the structure of the Arabic sentence and the multiplicity of its means of signaling meaning (inflection, word order, particles).

## Parts of speech

A widely discussed proposal in the book replaces the traditional three classes (noun, verb and particle) with seven, assigned by function in context: the noun, the adjective, the verb, the pronoun, the adverbial (*ẓarf*), the *khalīfa* (a class that includes items such as the so-called names of verbs), and the particle (*adāt*). Class membership is read from a bundle of formal and semantic indicators, in line with the *qarāʾin* account, rather than from inflection alone. Later writers have debated both the inventory and how far it can be reconciled with Sibawayh's three-way division.

## Impact

The book became a widely cited reference in modern Arabic linguistics, and influenced generations of researchers who sought to develop an authentic Arab methodology for studying language that combines the depth of the grammatical heritage with the precision of modern linguistic tools. Together with Hassan's *al-Usul* (an epistemological study of Arab linguistic thought), it is often cited as a foundation of the modern effort to root Arabic language study in its heritage without a break from it.

## His Relationship to the London School of Firth

Tammam Hassan is reported to have studied with John Rupert Firth in London; Firth was the pioneer of "context of situation" theory, which holds that linguistic meaning is not determined in isolation from the context of social and situational use. Hassan's concept of "indicators" can be seen as an attempt to bring this contextual outlook into Arabic grammar. He himself looked for precedents in the Arabic tradition, where the concept of the governing agent (*ʿamil*) and the notion of *qarina* already appear, and he drew especially on Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani's theory of *nazm* (see *Dala'il al-I'jaz*).

## Reception of the Book in Arab Academic Circles

The book has received mixed reception since its publication. While many researchers welcomed it as a pioneering model for modernizing Arabic language study without falling into the trap of complete Westernization or traditional closure, some traditional grammarians criticized its use of modern terms and concepts that they regard as foreign to the authentic method of Arabic grammar. Some modern linguists, for their part, saw in it a tendency at times to reinterpret the heritage beyond what the early grammarians themselves intended. Despite this debate, the book remains widely cited in Arabic linguistics.

## An Illustrative Example of the Method of Indicators

The idea can be illustrated by a sentence in which two nouns lack visible case endings (as in *daraba Musa ʿIsa*, "Musa hit Isa"). The listener identifies subject and object by other indicators, here the conventional word order, and in other cases by agreement, context or world knowledge, rather than by a single isolated grammatical mark. This approaches what modern linguistics calls structural ambiguity and its contextual resolution.
