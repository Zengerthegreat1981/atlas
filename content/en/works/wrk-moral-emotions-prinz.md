# Moral Emotions

The American philosopher Jesse Prinz published *The Emotional Construction of Morals* in 2007 with Oxford University Press. It is an ambitious metaethical and psychological thesis that defends an updated and systematic version of "moral sentimentalism," drawing on evidence from cognitive psychology and cultural anthropology.

## Central Theses

Prinz defends a thesis with two layers. The first layer is semantic and psychological: he adopts what he calls "constructive sentimentalism," which holds that the moral judgment that an act is wrong consists essentially of a mental representation that includes an emotion of disapproval, such as anger, disgust, or guilt, rather than a neutral, cold, rational judgment. Moral concepts, in his account, are therefore "emotionally constituted concepts," meaning that our understanding of the meaning of "wrong" is structurally bound up with a disposition to feel a negative emotion toward it, rather than being purely intellectual knowledge. The second layer is metaphysical: Prinz argues for a version of "moral relativism" grounded in a social emotional basis. He holds that moral facts are not objective independently of the emotional attitudes of groups accumulated through their cultural practices. However, he avoids sliding into absolute individual subjectivity, since he ties the truth of a moral judgment to intersubjective emotional standards rooted within a particular culture.

## Structure and Arguments

Prinz builds his argument from multiple empirical sources. These include findings from neuroscience showing that damage to brain regions responsible for emotional processing (as in patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex) is associated with altered moral judgment and behavior despite preserved general reasoning; psychological studies linking judgments about sexual and dietary taboos to the emotion of disgust; and anthropological evidence for the diversity of moral standards corresponding to the diversity of prevailing cultural emotional patterns. Prinz devotes chapters to discussing particular emotions, such as disgust, anger, guilt, and shame, explaining their evolutionary and social functions in stabilizing moral rules. He distinguishes between "other-directed" emotions, such as anger, which targets another agent, and "self-directed" emotions, such as guilt, which targets the agent himself. He treats these as paradigm moral emotions and links them to different kinds of moral judgment.

## Historical and Intellectual Context

Prinz continues a philosophical tradition going back to David Hume and Adam Smith in the eighteenth century, who held that morality is "more properly felt than judged of," in Hume's well-known phrase. He seeks, however, to update this sentimentalist tradition with the tools of contemporary cognitive science, which were not available to Hume. He rejects both the Kantian rationalism that makes reason the sole source of moral obligation and non-natural moral realism, which posits moral properties independent of our emotional responses. The book also belongs to a renewed philosophical debate about the role of emotion in moral knowledge, prompted by the psychologist Jonathan Haidt's research on the "social intuitionist model."

## Impact and Criticism

The book was widely discussed in the emerging field of experimental philosophy, which combines philosophical analysis with empirical data, and became a frequent reference in discussions of the relationship between emotion and reason in moral judgment. It has, however, been criticized from several directions. Its cultural relativism has been objected to on the ground that it makes it difficult to condemn harmful cultural practices objectively, if those practices are supported by a local emotional consensus. Rationalist critics have questioned whether emotion alone is sufficient to explain complex moral judgments that require conscious reasoning, especially in moral dilemmas that require balancing conflicting principles. For Prinz, an emotion such as disgust or anger is not merely a reaction that follows a prior rational judgment of wrongness but is part of what the moral judgment consists in.

## The Book Within Prinz's Broader Project

This book belongs to a broader philosophical project by Prinz that seeks to explain different aspects of mental life using empirical, naturalistic tools. In his earlier book *Gut Reactions* (2004), he defended a perceptual theory of emotions, on which emotions register bodily changes, drawing on the tradition of William James, who linked emotion to the perception of physiological bodily changes. In *The Emotional Construction of Morals*, Prinz applies this bodily framework to the moral field specifically, holding that the moral judgment is not an abstract cognitive representation but an embodied emotional state. The book takes care to refute the common objection that relying on emotion makes morality arbitrary or irrational, arguing that moral emotions are themselves the product of long social and educational development, which gives them stability and capacity for correction through social interaction and self-reflection, rather than being momentary whims. The book also discusses the possibility of explaining historical moral change, such as the decline in acceptance of slavery or of gender discrimination, as the result of redirecting our emotional responses through the expansion of the circle of social sympathy, rather than as the result of discovering new objective moral truths that had previously been absent.
