# Diwan of Shams of Tabriz

The *Diwan of Shams of Tabriz*, also known as the *Great Diwan* (*al-Diwan al-kabir*), is the collection of ghazals and quatrains that Jalal al-Din Rumi (604–672 AH / 1207–1273 CE) composed in Persian in Konya after his encounter with the wandering dervish Shams al-Din al-Tabrizi. Rumi did not arrange the diwan himself as a book; his poems were collected after his death, and in the old manuscripts their order remained according to meters and rhymes rather than the chronology of composition. Studies note that it contains more than three thousand ghazals and nearly two thousand quatrains, which makes it larger than the *Masnavi* that Rumi later wrote for his disciples.

## Its Arabic and Islamic roots

Rumi grew up in a multilingual world. He was born in Balkh or Wakhsh and travelled with his father Baha' al-Din Walad, a preacher and jurist, to Baghdad, Damascus and Anatolia. According to the hagiographical biographies (*manaqib*), he studied in the schools of Aleppo and Damascus under masters of Hanafi jurisprudence and the Arabic sciences before settling in Seljuk Konya. His scholarly formation was therefore Arabic and juristic before it was Persian and poetic. The diwan accordingly includes Arabic verses and macaronic lines (*mulamma'at*) that combine Persian and Arabic, and a few verses in Turkish and Greek that reflect the languages of Konya at that time. It is full of Qur'anic phrases and hadiths, and its lexicon is connected to the tradition of the Arab Sufis. It mentions al-Hallaj and stands at his saying "I am the Truth" (*ana al-haqq*), praising him, and it uses the technical vocabulary of annihilation, love and listening (*sama'*) set out in earlier Sufi manuals such as al-Qushayri's *Risala*.

Konya also had Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi (d. 673 AH / 1274 CE), a disciple of Ibn Arabi and a friend of Rumi, which shows that the city was a meeting point between the heritage of the Andalusian Ibn Arabi and Persian lyric poetry. The account of Rumi's meeting with Ibn Arabi in Damascus, however, remains a late and undocumented report.

## Shams and the question of naming

Rumi met Shams al-Din al-Tabrizi in Konya in 642 AH (c. 30 November 1244 CE), whereupon he left teaching and legal opinion for companionship and listening (*sama'*), and his disciples were disturbed. Shams disappeared in 645 AH / 1247 CE in obscure circumstances, and some accounts suggest that he was killed. The absence became a principal source of love poetry: the poet speaks in the voice of the lover who has lost the Beloved, and places the name of Shams at the end of many verses as a signature in place of his own. The diwan came to be named after him rather than after the poet. Critics point out, however, that Shams here is not only a historical person but has become a symbol of what the Sufi finds in the divine Beloved, and in the diwan he has taken the place of the Beloved absolutely.

## Form and listening

Rumi composed his ghazals usually in extemporaneous improvisation connected with listening (*sama'*). Hagiographies relate that he would whirl and recite while his disciples wrote down what he said, and from this practice came the Mevlevi Order, which his son Sultan Walad established. The language of the diwan is marked by a dancing rhythm, repetition, frequent invocation and direct address, and images of love, wine, fire, sea and light. There is no ordered doctrine in the diwan as there is in the *Masnavi*; rather there are shifting experiences between ecstasy, sobriety and bewilderment.

## Edition and translation

No critical edition of the diwan was published until Badi' al-Zaman Furuzanfar edited it in ten volumes at Tehran University between 1957 and 1967, relying on the Konya manuscript dated 716 AH, which belongs to a generation after the poet's death. Reynold Nicholson presented a selection of translated poems from the diwan in 1898 at Cambridge University, the first major scholarly translation. Studies followed by Annemarie Schimmel, *I Am Wind, You Are Fire* (1992), and Franklin Lewis, *Rumi: Past and Present, East and West* (2000). The popular American free-English translations, including those of Coleman Barks, are reformulations rather than precise translations, and they may omit the Islamic references.

## Religious life after Rumi

The Mevlevi Order spread across the Ottoman state, and Mevlevi lodges (*tekkes*) were established in Istanbul, Aleppo, Damascus and Cairo, where the diwan and the *Masnavi* were recited in listening sessions. The Turkish Republic then closed the lodges and banned the Sufi orders in 1925. Performances moved to the stage of tourism and culture, so that the diwan came to be read as literature more than recited as remembrance (*dhikr*).

## Caveats

Some poems attributed to Rumi in the diwan are doubtful and entered later manuscripts, and determining what is authentic requires collating manuscripts. The dates of composition are unknown, so it is not valid to tie each ghazal to a specific episode of the Shams story. Scholars also warn against reading the diwan only as a general spiritual text detached from Islam and Sufism.
