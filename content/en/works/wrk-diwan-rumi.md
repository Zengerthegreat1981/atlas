# Diwan of Shams of Tabriz

The *Diwan of Shams of Tabriz*, also known as the *Divan-i Kabir*, is the collection of ghazals, stanzaic poems and quatrains that Jalal al-Din Rumi (604–672 AH / 1207–1273 CE) composed in Persian in Konya after his encounter with the wandering dervish Shams al-Din al-Tabrizi. Rumi did not leave the diwan as a book whose order he had fixed. Poems were taken down in his lifetime and gathered after his death, and in the old manuscripts they are ordered by meter and rhyme rather than by date of composition. In Badi' al-Zaman Furuzanfar's edition the collection contains 3,229 ghazals, 44 *tarji'-bands* and 1,983 quatrains, and in that count it is longer than the *Masnavi* that Rumi later wrote for his disciples.

## Its Arabic and Islamic roots

Rumi grew up in a multilingual world. He was born in Balkh or Wakhsh and travelled with his father Baha' al-Din Walad, a preacher and jurist, to Baghdad, Damascus and Anatolia. According to the hagiographical biographies (*manaqib*), he studied in the schools of Aleppo and Damascus under masters of Hanafi jurisprudence and the Arabic sciences before settling in Seljuk Konya. His scholarly formation was therefore Arabic and juristic before it was Persian and poetic. The diwan accordingly includes Arabic verses and macaronic lines (*mulamma'at*) that combine Persian and Arabic, and a few verses in Turkish and Greek that reflect the languages of Konya at that time. It is full of Qur'anic phrases and hadiths, and its lexicon is connected to the tradition of the Arab Sufis. It praises al-Hallaj and returns to his saying "I am the Truth" (*ana al-haqq*), and it uses the technical vocabulary of annihilation, love and listening (*sama'*) known from earlier Sufi manuals such as al-Qushayri's *Risala*.

Konya also had Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi (d. 673 AH / 1274 CE), a disciple of Ibn Arabi and a friend of Rumi, so the city was a meeting point between the heritage of the Andalusian Ibn Arabi and Persian lyric poetry. The story that the young Rumi met Ibn Arabi in Damascus belongs to the later hagiographies, and its historicity is doubtful.

## Shams and the question of naming

Rumi met Shams al-Din al-Tabrizi in Konya in 642 AH / 1244 CE, a date the later biographies place in late November. He left ordinary teaching for companionship and listening (*sama'*), and his disciples were disturbed. The same biographies describe a first departure of Shams around 1246, a return, and a final disappearance in 645 AH / 1247–48. Some of them say that he was killed. Historians treat the killing as unproven. The absence became a principal source of love poetry: the poet speaks as the lover who has lost the beloved, and he places the name of Shams at the end of many ghazals as a signature in place of his own. The diwan came to be named after Shams. In the poems Shams is also more than the historical companion. He becomes a figure of the divine beloved.

## Form and listening

Rumi composed his ghazals usually in extemporaneous improvisation connected with listening (*sama'*). Hagiographies relate that he would whirl and recite while his disciples wrote down what he said, and from this practice came the Mevlevi Order, which his son Sultan Walad established. The language of the diwan is marked by a dancing rhythm, repetition, frequent invocation and direct address, and images of love, wine, fire, sea and light. There is no ordered doctrine in the diwan as there is in the *Masnavi*; rather there are shifting experiences between ecstasy, sobriety and bewilderment.

## Edition and translation

No critical edition of the diwan was published until Badi' al-Zaman Furuzanfar edited it in ten volumes at Tehran University between 1957 and 1967, using early manuscripts, among them an early Konya copy. Reynold Nicholson published a selection of ghazals in English in 1898 at Cambridge University Press. Furuzanfar later judged some poems in that selection inauthentic. Studies followed by Annemarie Schimmel, *I Am Wind, You Are Fire* (1992), and Franklin Lewis, *Rumi: Past and Present, East and West* (2000). The popular American free-English versions, including those of Coleman Barks, are reformulations rather than close translations, and they often omit the Islamic references.

## Religious life after Rumi

The Mevlevi Order spread across the Ottoman state, and Mevlevi lodges (*tekkes*) were established in Istanbul, Aleppo, Damascus and Cairo, where the diwan and the *Masnavi* were recited in listening sessions. The Turkish Republic then closed the lodges and banned the Sufi orders in 1925. Performances moved to the stage of tourism and culture, so that the diwan came to be read as literature more than recited as remembrance (*dhikr*).

## Caveats

Some poems attributed to Rumi in the diwan are doubtful and entered later manuscripts, and determining what is authentic requires collating manuscripts. The dates of composition are unknown, so it is not valid to tie each ghazal to a specific episode of the Shams story. Scholars also warn against reading the diwan only as a general spiritual text detached from Islam and Sufism.
