# Lived Time: Phenomenological and Psychopathological Studies

*Le Temps vécu: études phénoménologiques et psychopathologiques* (1933), by the French psychiatrist Eugène Minkowski, is one of the most influential descriptions of the disturbance of time in mental illness, and a model of what phenomenology can do in the clinic. Minkowski's claim is that human beings do not live in clock time — homogeneous, measurable, divisible — but in a lived time that has direction, extension, and a rhythm of its own, changing with the person's existential state. Mental disorder, on this view, is not first a defect in an isolated function but a primary break in this living temporal structure, with the familiar symptoms appearing as secondary expressions of it. The book was published in Paris in 1933 and translated into English as *Lived Time* by Nancy Metzel in 1970. It is a founding text of French phenomenological psychopathology.

## The Idea: Time as the Face of Life

Minkowski borrows from Henri Bergson the idea of the *élan vital*, the vital impulse, and makes it the foundation of lived time. A healthy person lives in a forward thrust towards the future as an open horizon: action, waiting, desire, expectation, and prayer are all forms of this forward direction. The past is present as an extension, not as a burden, and through this structure a person keeps contact with things and other people. Time, here, is not something that happens to the mind; it is the first face of psychic life. Minkowski's method is phenomenological in the clinical sense: he starts not from an analysis of inner time-consciousness, as Husserl did, but from the moving life a clinician actually perceives in a patient's speech, movement, and rhythm. That is why he stands closer to Bergson than to Husserl, and why his book reads as a bridge between philosophy and psychiatry rather than as a work of either alone.

## The Structure of the Book

The book examines lived time from several angles: temporal extension, becoming, activity, hope, and the self's relation to the future as a living horizon rather than an abstract point on a line. Minkowski presents clinical cases, drawn chiefly from depression and schizophrenia, and reads their symptoms as deformations of this temporal horizon before anything else. The argument is cumulative rather than systematic: each chapter takes one aspect of lived time and shows how its distortion reorganises the patient's whole world.

## Time in Depression and Schizophrenia

The clinical heart of the book is the contrast between two temporal breakdowns. In depression, becoming is blocked: the forward impulse stops, the future becomes a wall, and the past takes over the whole of the patient's present with guilt and regret, so that time seems to stand still or to move unbearably slowly. In schizophrenia, the problem is different: the vital contact with becoming itself is lost, and a frozen, spatialised time takes the place of lived time, with dry rationality appearing as a substitute for life. Hallucinations, delusions, and retardation are then read as secondary results of this primary break, not as the disorder itself. This reading later shaped the way a generation of phenomenologists understood psychosis — not as a collection of defective functions but as a transformation of the structure of experience.

## Author and Context

Minkowski was born in St Petersburg in 1885, grew up in Warsaw, studied medicine in Munich and then in Zurich, where he worked in Eugen Bleuler's clinic. After settling in France he became one of the founders of the group *L'Évolution psychiatrique*, in whose series *Le Temps vécu* appeared. The book was published in 1933 in the middle of a wider European movement to bring phenomenology — Husserl, then Heidegger — into psychiatry, a movement that also included Ludwig Binswanger and Erwin Straus. Its influence was direct on the next generation of French phenomenological psychiatrists, notably Arthur Tatossian and Henri Ey, but it spread slowly outside francophone circles compared with Binswanger's German works.

## Later Influence

The English translation of 1970 opened the book to a wider readership, and contemporary phenomenological psychopathology has built directly on it. Thomas Fuchs's 2013 study of temporality and psychopathology connects the disturbance of time to depression and schizophrenia in terms Minkowski would have recognised. Louis Sass and Josef Parnas drew on Minkowski in their analysis of the self in schizophrenia, and Hubertus Tellenbach in his study of melancholia. The book's lasting contribution is methodological as much as substantive: it shows that a clinician can describe a patient's experience more precisely than any symptom checklist, and that such a description can be learned and taught. For the therapist, Minkowski offers a language for what the patient lives through — the closed future, the heavy past, the frozen present — that is more accurate than any list of symptoms.

## Limits

The book rests on clinical impression and philosophical description and offers no experimental measurement of lived time. Its language is Bergsonian and idiosyncratic, far from modern psychiatric terminology, and some of its concepts, such as the vital impulse, cannot be tested empirically. Generalising the thesis to all disorders goes beyond what the cases presented can support, since many symptoms show no clear link to a specific temporal disturbance. These limits do not diminish its standing as the founding text of the phenomenological study of time in mental illness — they mark the boundary between what phenomenology can describe and what it cannot prove.
