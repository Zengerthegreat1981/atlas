# Negara: The Theatre State in Nineteenth-Century Bali

***Negara: The Theatre State in Nineteenth-Century Bali***, by **Clifford Geertz**, published in **1980**, is one of the most influential works of interpretive anthropology. Its subject is the Balinese state in the century before Dutch conquest, and its argument is a provocation: in Bali, Geertz claimed, the state did not use ceremony to display power — ceremony *was* power. The elaborate rituals of the Balinese courts, the processions, the temple festivals, the cremations, were not a mask for a reality of force and taxation, but the reality itself. The state was a theatre, and the theatre was the state.

Geertz (1926–2006) was the leading figure of interpretive anthropology, and *Negara* was his most sustained historical work. It drew on his fieldwork in Bali (from 1957-58), on Dutch colonial records, and on Balinese texts, and it followed his earlier work in Indonesia, including *The Religion of Java* (1960), a study of Java rather than Bali, and the essay "Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight" (1972). The book's title takes the Balinese and Indonesian word *negara*, which Geertz glosses as meaning "state", "capital" and "court" — a fusion that already contains Geertz's argument, since in Bali the state was identified with its capital and its court.

## The Theatre State

Geertz's argument begins from a puzzle. Nineteenth-century Bali was divided into a number of small kingdoms, each centred on a royal court, and these kingdoms were in near-constant rivalry. Yet they were also extraordinarily elaborate: the courts maintained huge retinues, staged vast ceremonies, and competed in displays of splendour. A conventional political analysis would treat this as wasteful extravagance, a drain on resources that a rational state would have spent on administration or warfare. Geertz argued the opposite. The ceremonies were not a luxury the state indulged in; they were the state's purpose. The king's power consisted in his capacity to stage the cosmos — to make visible, through ritual, the divine order of which he was the centre — and his subjects' loyalty consisted in their participation in that staging.

The key concept is the **theatre state**: a state whose energies are directed toward spectacle rather than toward the exercise of administrative control. Geertz contrasted this with the European model of the state, in which power is measured by the ability to tax, conscript, and govern territory. In Bali, power was measured by the ability to command attendance, to mount a cremation that drew thousands, to maintain a court that shone with gold and music. The "expressive nature" of the Balinese state, as Geertz called it, meant that the distinction between politics and ritual, between power and pageantry, did not hold. A royal cremation was a political act; a temple festival was an act of state.

## Court, Capital, and Cosmology

The book reconstructs the Balinese state in detail. At its centre was the king, who was at once a political ruler and a divine figure, the axis of a cosmological order that mapped the kingdom onto the Hindu-Buddhist universe. Around him were the court, the nobility, and the priesthood, arranged in a hierarchy that mirrored the hierarchy of the gods. The capital was laid out as a microcosm: the palace at the centre, the temples at the cardinal points, the quarters of the various castes and occupations arranged by status. To live in the capital was to live inside a model of the cosmos, and the daily life of the court — its audiences, its feasts, its ceremonies — was a continuous enactment of that model.

Geertz also examined the relationship between the court and the countryside. The kingdoms were not administered in the European sense: there was no bureaucracy, no standing army, no systematic taxation. Instead, the court extracted resources through a system of tribute and corvée labour, and it held the countryside through a combination of ritual authority and the threat of violence. The villages, organised around their own temples and irrigation societies, had considerable autonomy, and the king's power over them was intermittent and ceremonial rather than continuous and administrative. This, Geertz argued, was not a failure of statecraft but a different kind of statecraft: the Balinese state ruled by being seen, not by governing.

## Reception and Influence

*Negara* was immediately recognised as a major work, and it has been one of the most discussed books in the anthropology of the state. Its concept of the theatre state has been applied to other Southeast Asian kingdoms, to the court societies of early modern Europe, and to the ceremonial politics of contemporary states. It also influenced the wider "cultural turn" in political science and history, encouraging scholars to treat ritual, symbol, and performance as constitutive of power rather than as decoration. Benedict Anderson's essay on the idea of power in Javanese culture, published in 1972, is often read alongside *Negara* as a complementary account of Southeast Asian political cosmology.

The book has also been vigorously criticised. Historians of Bali have argued that Geertz exaggerated the ceremonial character of the state and underestimated its administrative and coercive dimensions: Balinese kings did tax, did raise armies, and did govern, and the Dutch conquest of Bali was a military and political event, not a theatrical one. Others have argued that Geertz's Bali is too static, and that his reliance on Dutch sources and on the court perspective marginalised the experience of ordinary Balinese. Henk Schulte Nordholt's *The Spell of Power* (1996) offers a detailed historical corrective. These criticisms have not diminished the book's influence, but they have qualified its central claim: the theatre state is now usually read as an ideal type, a model that illuminates one dimension of Balinese politics rather than a complete description of it.
