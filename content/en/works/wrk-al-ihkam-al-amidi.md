# Al-Ihkam fi Usul al-Ahkam

One of the major classical works of usul al-fiqh in the method of the theologians (alongside al-Ghazali's *al-Mustasfa* and al-Razi's *al-Mahsul*). It was written by Sayf al-Din al-Amidi (d. 631 AH / 1233 CE), and it is regarded as a carefully organized usul text and a comprehensive survey of the opinions of the various schools.

## Method: Logical Rigor in the Formulation of Usul Questions

Amidi is distinguished by applying strict logical tools (precise definitions, divisions that are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive) to usul questions that had traditionally been treated in less formal language. This extends the method of **al-Ghazali** in *al-Mustasfa*, but with broader detail and a survey of more varied school opinions.

## The Study of the Signification of Words: His Best-Known Contribution

Amidi devotes an extensive section to classifying the types of **signification of legal words over their meanings** (the explicit meaning of the text, its implied meanings, and its necessary entailments, among other categories) with an analytical precision that became a standard reference for later usul scholars. This section is a detailed classical treatment of the question of **how a jurist derives a ruling from a text that does not state the ruling directly**. This question roughly parallels the study of **implicit meaning** in modern legal interpretation theory.

## Impact

Ibn al-Hajib's *Muntaha al-Sul wa al-Amal* is generally described as drawing largely on it, and Ibn al-Hajib then abridged that work in *Mukhtasar al-Muntaha*, which became the text of usul study in many schools for centuries. This reflects the centrality of al-Ihkam in shaping the later usul tradition.

## General Structure of the Book

Amidi divides his book into several major divisions, covering the full range of usul questions in a rising logical order. It begins by defining the **Sharia rulings** and their categories (obligatory, recommended, permissible, disliked, and prohibited) and the reality of goodness and badness (a purely theological question in which usul intersects with *kalam*). It then moves to the four **agreed-upon Sharia evidences** (the Book, the Sunna, consensus, and analogy) in broad detail, devoting to each a discussion of its authority and the conditions of its use. It then presents the **disputed evidences** (such as juristic preference, unrestricted public interest, the opinion of a Companion, and the law of earlier communities) in a critical survey that weighs the positions of the schools.

## His Position on Analogy and Consensus

Amidi devotes considerable space to the question of **analogy** (qiyas). He discusses its four pillars (the root, the branch, the ruling, and the rationale, 'illa) with logical precision, examining the methods for establishing the rationale (such as sabr and taqsim, suitability, and concomitant variation), and he responds to the Zahiri rejecters of analogy. In the section on **consensus** (ijma'), he discusses the possibility of its actual realization and its authority in usul. He presents objections from those who doubt that the opinions of all mujtahids in a single age can be ascertained, and he answers them with precise distinctions between types of consensus (explicit and tacit).

## His Place Among the Usul Schools

Amidi was born into the Hanbali school but later became a Shafi'i. He writes from a position that combines the **method of the theologians** (*tariqat al-mutakallimin*) in organizing usul questions, a tradition whose foundations are traced to al-Shafi'i's *al-Risala*, which al-Juwayni developed in *al-Burhan* and al-Ghazali refined in *al-Mustasfa*. That method abstracts usul rules from the branches of the juristic schools through a theoretical, rational approach. He also combines this with a broad absorption of the opinions of the Hanafis, who represent the **method of the jurists** (*tariqat al-fuqaha'*), which derives usul rules from the branches of the school of law itself. This synthesis makes al-Ihkam a comparative reference between the two methods rather than a text of one perspective alone, and it explains the continued citation of the book by usul scholars of different schools.

## The Question of Rational Goodness and Badness

One of the most subtle theological-usul questions Amidi treats at the start of the book is that of **goodness and badness** (husn and qubh). Can reason perceive the goodness and badness of acts independently of the Sharia (as the Mu'tazila hold), or are goodness and badness established only by the Sharia (as the Ash'aris hold)? Amidi leans toward a moderate Ash'ari position, which accepts that reason can perceive some worldly benefits and harms, but denies that such perception is an independent source of religious obligation apart from the divine address. This theological position in turn shapes his stance on the authority of reason in establishing the religious goodness and badness of acts, and it directly influences his later treatment of evidences such as juristic preference and unrestricted public interest. He approaches these with greater methodological caution than other usul scholars closer to Mu'tazilism on this question.

## Comparison with His Contemporaries and Successors

Al-Ihkam differs from al-Ghazali's *al-Mustasfa* by its broader presentation of the views and arguments of opponents before weighing them. It differs from al-Razi's *al-Mahsul* by greater precision in logical formulation and in the ordering of questions. Modern scholars have regarded al-Ihkam as a mature expression of the **method of the theologians** in usul, before the later works that sought to combine the two methods (such as Ibn al-Sa'ati's *Badi' al-Nizam*).

## Its Standing in Modern Western and Academic Studies

Al-Ihkam received special attention in modern Western studies of usul al-fiqh. Bernard Weiss made it the basis of his study of Islamic legal theory, regarding Amidi's method of classifying evidences and their ranks as a model showing the organized, inferential character of usul al-fiqh as a fully formed legal theory rather than a scattered set of interpretive controls.

## Date of composition

Salim Ogut's article on *al-Ihkam* in the TDV *Islam Ansiklopedisi* dates the book to 625 AH (1228 CE), written in the last years of al-Amidi's life. He died in Damascus in 631 AH (1233). The figure of about 1225 sometimes used for the work is earlier than that encyclopedia date. Ogut presents the book as the place where al-Amidi brought together his training in usul, kalam, philosophy, disputation, and logic, and as a synthesis of the usul tradition up to his time with the debates of his own generation. Bernard Weiss treats *al-Ihkam* as the fullest later statement of the theologians' method after al-Juwayni, while still giving al-Ghazali his place in that line.
