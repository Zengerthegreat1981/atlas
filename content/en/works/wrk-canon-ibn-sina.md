# Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb (The Canon of Medicine)

*Al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (*The Canon of Medicine*) by Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Sina (370–428 AH / 980–1037 CE) is the best-known medical encyclopaedia of Islamic civilisation. It remained a teaching reference in Islamic lands and in European universities for many centuries. Ibn Sina composed it during the years of his travels and service at the courts of northern and western Iran, in places such as Gorgan, Rayy, Hamadan and Isfahan, and its completion is conventionally dated to about 1025 CE. Its author was a practising physician before he was a philosopher: he relates in his autobiography that he treated the Samanid prince Nuh ibn Mansur in Bukhara when he was sixteen or seventeen years old, and that the prince then allowed him to enter the palace library. Two aims thus came together in the book: to organise medicine into a logical system in the way Ibn Sina had done for philosophy, and to comprehend what physicians before him had gathered.

## Structure

The book is arranged in five books forming a hierarchical structure from the general to the particular. The first book deals with generalities, including the definition of medicine, the anatomy of the organs, the four humours, the temperaments, the faculties and spirits, the causes of health and disease, and the regulation of bodies. The second book concerns simple drugs and sets out the rules for judging the action of a drug. It contains seven conditions for drug testing, among them that a drug should be tested free of accidental qualities, on a simple rather than a compound disease, that its effect should be seen to be regular or in most cases, and that it should be tested on the human body rather than on animals. Historians of medicine have seen in these rules an early and influential attention to methodical drug testing, though they are not experiments in the modern sense. The third book covers diseases of the organs from head to foot. The fourth book covers general diseases such as fevers, tumours, fractures and poisons. The fifth book concerns compound drugs, or *aqrabadhin* (pharmacopoeia).

## Method

Ibn Sina follows the humoral theory of Galen, but reorders it with the definitions and logical divisions in which he was trained in Aristotelian logic, beginning with definitions and causes before treatment. In treatment he relies on diet first, then drugs, then surgery, and he describes diagnosis by the pulse, urine and the signs of the face. In his discussion of diseases he records clinical details: the distinction between continuous and intermittent fevers, descriptions of pleurisy and of acute inflammation affecting the brain, and a treatment of pulmonary consumption as a communicable illness, kept distinct from other diseases of the chest. The book also describes lovesickness and melancholy as conditions in which psyche and body affect one another, developing a theme already present in Galenic medicine.

## Commentaries and Abridgements

The *Canon* did not remain as it was written; it became a text that commentators took up. Ibn al-Nafis (d. 687 AH / 1288 CE) wrote a summary of the *Canon* and a commentary on its anatomy, in which he described the pulmonary circulation and offered a critique of the anatomy of both Galen and Ibn Sina. Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi (d. 710 AH / 1311 CE) wrote a well-known commentary on it. Ibn Sina himself versified its essence in the *Urjuza fi al-Tibb* (the *Poem on Medicine*). Ibn Rushd, in his *Kulliyyat*, dissented from Ibn Sina on some points of medical doctrine. From these works a school grew up that read medicine in a sustained critical way.

## Transmission to Europe

Gerard of Cremona (d. 1187 CE) translated the book into Latin in Toledo in the twelfth century, and it became the *Canon medicinae*. It was printed many times in Latin from the late fifteenth century. The first Arabic edition was issued in Rome in 1593 by the Medici Press, followed by the Bulaq edition of 1877. It was taught in the universities of Montpellier, Padua, Paris and Louvain until the seventeenth century. Nancy Siraisi, in *Avicenna in Renaissance Italy* (1987), documented the continuation of its teaching in Italian universities after 1500, alongside the criticism that humanist physicians directed at the translation. This position is inseparable from Ibn Sina's connection to the heritage of Galen, which was also the foundation of European medicine.

## Afterlife in Later Centuries

Nathan ha-Me'ati translated the book into Hebrew in Rome in 1279, and its Hebrew copies spread among Jewish physicians in Italy and southern France. In the Indian subcontinent, the Islamic Greek medicine known as Greco-Arabic medicine (*Yunani*) continued to teach the *Canon* in its colleges until the twentieth century, and it remained practised in India and Pakistan alongside modern medicine. This shows that the practical life of the book was longer than its scientific life.

## Limitations

The *Canon* rests on the theory of humours, which has been shown to be false, and so it is read today historically rather than therapeutically. Many passages attributed to it in advertising, such as herbal treatment or diagnosis by the pulse, do not fit modern medicine. The Latin translation also contains distortions in the names of drugs and diseases, which the humanists criticised. It is also an exaggeration to regard the book as the source of all European medicine, since physicians drew on the books of al-Razi as well as on the work of the Salernitan school alongside it.
