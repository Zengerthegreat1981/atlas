# Totemism

Claude Lévi-Strauss's *Totemism* (1962, French: *Le Totémisme Aujourd'hui*) is a focused critical and theoretical work that deconstructs the anthropological concept of totemism and proposes a new understanding of what totemism actually is. Rather than treating totemism as a distinct institution or religious belief system (as earlier anthropologists had done), Lévi-Strauss argues that totemism is fundamentally a system of classification and thought. Totemism uses differences between animal species to represent and think about differences between human social groups. The work is both a critique of earlier anthropological concepts of totemism and a positive theoretical contribution proposing that totemism should be understood as a form of symbolic logic. *Totemism* became influential in establishing the principle that apparently distinct institutions or beliefs may be understood as variations of underlying logical or classificatory systems.

## Publication & Context

Lévi-Strauss published *Totemism* in French in 1962, the same year as *The Savage Mind*, as a companion work focused specifically on totemism. The book was translated into English by Rodney Needham in 1963. The work was stimulated by Lévi-Strauss's critique of earlier anthropological writings on totemism, particularly functionalist accounts that treated totemism as serving social functions (integrating society, organizing kinship groups, etc.). Lévi-Strauss argued that these functionalist explanations misunderstood totemism by treating it as a unified institution when in fact it was a way of thinking and classifying.

The work also drew on his broader intellectual project of structural analysis. He approached totemism not as a historical phenomenon or institution but as a logical structure. The book was addressed partly to correct misunderstandings of totemism in anthropological literature and partly to establish principles for analyzing symbolic and classificatory systems more broadly.

The publication appeared during the height of structuralism's influence. Totemism became a test case for structuralist analysis and for demonstrating that underlying logical structures could be extracted from apparently diverse ethnographic phenomena.

## Main Arguments & Thesis

Lévi-Strauss's central argument is that totemism is not a distinct institution that characterizes certain societies but rather a way of thinking about relationships—specifically, using nature (particularly animals) to think about and classify social relationships. Totemism employs the logical principle that differences between natural species can represent differences between human social groups. An animal species becomes the emblem or totem of a human group, and the differences between animal species (kangaroo differs from eagle, which differs from bear) are used to mark and analyze differences between human groups.

He argues that totemism should not be understood as belief in mystical relationships between humans and animal species, but rather as a form of logic and classification. The totemic system is not expressing hidden spiritual truths but providing a framework for thinking about human social organization and relationships.

Lévi-Strauss distinguishes totemism from other uses of nature symbolism. Totemism specifically uses natural species differences to represent social group differences. He argues that what earlier anthropologists identified as totemism is actually a diverse set of phenomena that may include totemic classification but also other forms of symbolic thought and ritual practice.

He also argues that totemism is not a stage in human intellectual evolution (as some earlier theorists had proposed) but a particular way of organizing symbolic thought that can coexist with other forms of thought. Different societies may employ totemic classification in different domains and to different degrees.

## Key Concepts Introduced

**Totemism as Classification System**: Rather than viewing totemism as a belief system or religious institution, Lévi-Strauss proposes understanding it as a system of classification that uses natural species as concepts for thinking about social relationships.

**Homology Between Nature and Culture**: The principle that the structure of natural categories (differences between animal species) is used to think about cultural categories (differences between human social groups). Nature provides the conceptual tools for thinking about culture.

**Logical Rather Than Functional Explanation**: Lévi-Strauss argues that totemism should be explained through its logical structure rather than through the functions it serves. The system is logical because it provides a way of organizing thought; it does not require functional justification.

**Totemism as Conceptual Bricolage**: Totemism represents a way of constructing meaningful classifications from materials at hand (nature, available symbols, social categories). It is a creative intellectual activity rather than an irrational belief.

**The Dissolution of Totemism as a Category**: Lévi-Strauss's larger argument that "totemism" as a unified anthropological category should be dissolved; what appears to be totemism is actually diverse phenomena involving different logical principles and domains of classification.

## Ethnographic & Theoretical Evidence

Lévi-Strauss's argument is based on analysis of ethnographic accounts of totemism from Australia, North America, and other regions where totemism has been documented. He shows that Australian Aboriginal totemism uses animal emblems to mark and organize human groups. Each clan has a totemic animal (kangaroo, eagle, etc.), and the relationships between animals are used to represent and analyze relationships between clans.

He analyzes North American Native American totemism, showing similar patterns. Totemic emblems mark group membership, and the system provides a way of thinking about group relationships and hierarchies.

He analyzes the logic of these systems. Kangaroo is to eagle as one clan is to another clan; the difference between the animals mirrors and helps organize the difference between the human groups. This is logical and systematic, not irrational belief.

He also critically discusses ethnographic accounts that describe totemism, showing where earlier anthropologists misunderstood or mischaracterized totemic systems. He argues that treating totemism as a unified institution has obscured the diversity of what is called totemism.

His analysis draws on comparative ethnography and on theoretical discussion of how symbolic systems work. He demonstrates that understanding totemism requires analyzing its logical structure, not seeking hidden spiritual meanings or functional explanations.

## Impact on Anthropology

*Totemism* became influential in establishing new approaches to understanding classification systems and symbolic thought. The work contributed to the principle that apparently distinct institutions or beliefs may be understood as variations of underlying logical systems.

The work influenced how anthropologists understand totemism specifically, shifting from viewing it as a religious belief system to understanding it as a form of classification and logical thought. This reframing was important for understanding what totemism actually is and for not imposing Western categories (religion, belief, myth) on Indigenous systems of thought.

The work also influenced broader structuralist approaches to culture and symbolism. The principle that underlying logical structures can be extracted from diverse ethnographic phenomena became central to structuralist analysis across multiple domains.

The critique of earlier anthropological categories and concepts—showing that totemism as unified category was problematic—also influenced subsequent anthropological work that became more cautious about reifying analytical categories and more attentive to how categories are constructed through anthropological discourse.

## Methodology & Approach

Lévi-Strauss employs critical analysis of earlier anthropological literature on totemism, combined with reanalysis of ethnographic examples. He systematically goes through earlier anthropological accounts, showing how they misunderstood or mischaracterized totemism.

His analytical approach is logical and structural. He identifies the logical principles underlying totemic systems—the use of natural species differences to represent social group differences—and demonstrates how this principle is applied across diverse societies.

The work is more theoretical and critical than ethnographically descriptive. Lévi-Strauss works from published ethnographies and earlier anthropological discussions rather than from his own direct fieldwork on totemism. The emphasis is on conceptual analysis rather than ethnographic narrative.

The organization is argumentative and logical, building from critique of earlier work to positive theoretical propositions about what totemism is and how it should be understood.

## Critique & Limitations

Critics have argued that Lévi-Strauss's approach, while providing logical analysis, may obscure the lived experience and meaning of totemism for Indigenous peoples. How do Indigenous peoples themselves understand their totemic systems? What meanings and purposes do these systems serve for them? Logical analysis may reveal structural principles but not capture these dimensions.

Some scholars have questioned whether totemism as a category should be dissolved entirely or whether it remains useful as an analytical concept for understanding certain kinds of symbolic and classificatory systems. While Lévi-Strauss's critique of totemism as unified institution may be valid, the concept may still have analytical utility.

Postcolonial scholars have noted that the concept of totemism itself is a European anthropological creation, imposed on diverse Indigenous systems of thought and relationship to nature. Rather than analyzing the logic of totemism, one might ask how this analytical category itself came to be constructed and what effects it has had.

Indigenous scholars have critiqued anthropological treatment of totemism for extracting abstract logical principles from Indigenous knowledge systems without recognizing the spiritual, ecological, and ethical dimensions that Indigenous peoples themselves understand as integral to these systems.

Some scholars also noted that Lévi-Strauss's analysis works well for certain cases (particularly Australian and North American examples) but may not apply equally to all societies where totemism has been documented. The logical principles he identifies may not universally characterize what anthropologists have called totemism.

## Contemporary Relevance

*Totemism* remains important for understanding how to approach classification systems and symbolic thought in anthropology. The principle that apparently distinct institutions may be understood through underlying logical structures remains influential.

However, contemporary readings are more critical of the structuralist approach and more attentive to postcolonial and Indigenous perspectives. Contemporary work on totemism, Indigenous classification systems, and relationships between humans and nature increasingly centers Indigenous knowledge and understanding rather than imposing analytical categories derived from European thought.

The work remains historically important for understanding structuralism and the development of anthropological theory. Contemporary scholarship builds on the insights about logical structure while deploying more collaborative, reflexive, and decolonial approaches that respect Indigenous systems of knowledge and meaning-making.
