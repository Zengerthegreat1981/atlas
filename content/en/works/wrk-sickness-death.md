# The Sickness unto Death (Sygdommen til Døden)

*The Sickness unto Death* (1849), by Søren Kierkegaard, is a major analysis of despair in the philosophical tradition, and has been less used in psychological writing than might be expected. Published in Copenhagen in 1849 by C. A. Reitzel, it appeared under the pseudonym Anti-Climacus, with Kierkegaard named on the title page only as its publisher, not its author. The pseudonym is not decoration: Kierkegaard used "Climacus" for a position below his own and "Anti-Climacus" for a position above it — a Christian of a strangely high order speaking from a place Kierkegaard did not claim to occupy himself. The book's extreme demands are therefore to be read as a deliberately elevated measure, not as personal confession. It is one of Kierkegaard's later works, published five years after *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844), and its subtitle states its purpose: "A Christian Psychological Exposition for Upbuilding and Awakening".

## The Self as a Relation

The book opens with what became the most famous sentence in the philosophy of the self: the self is a relation that relates itself to itself. The human being is not one side of a synthesis — the infinite and the finite, possibility and necessity, the eternal and the temporal — but the relation between them, turned back upon itself. Despair is that relation going wrong, and it takes exactly two forms: not wanting to be oneself, the despair of weakness; and wanting to be oneself apart from the power that established it, the despair of defiance. The analysis is then developed in two intersecting classifications. Despair is sorted by the elements of the synthesis — the despair of infinitude and of finitude, of possibility and of necessity — and by the degree of consciousness of it, from despair that does not know it is despair, through the despair of weakness, to the despair of defiance. What matters is not the intensity of a feeling but the structure of a misrelated self.

## The Despair That Is Not Felt

The most dangerous form of despair, Kierkegaard argues, is also the most common: the despair its owner does not know he is in. The book reverses the usual scale — the person who does not complain is not necessarily the healthier one, but may be the furthest from himself. This is an early systematic formulation of the idea that a condition can be fully present and entirely unrecognised by the person in it, an idea with a direct bearing on every practice that assumes a person may not see their own situation. It is also what makes the book a phenomenology of despair half a century before clinical psychology existed: it describes not the severity of a symptom but the structure of the misrelatedness from which symptoms arise, and it classifies despair by awareness rather than by intensity.

## The Two Parts

The book is in two parts. The first, "The Sickness unto Death Is Despair", defines despair, shows its universality, and classifies it by the two methods described above. The second, "Despair Is Sin", moves the analysis from psychology to theology: despair *before God* is sin, and sin is a positive position rather than a mere lack — the most intense forms being despair over the forgiveness of sins and then the conscious rejection of Christianity. The move is the book's standing difficulty for the secular reader. The second part defines despair theologically, so whoever takes the psychological analysis alone takes half the argument and leaves behind the premise that binds it. The book does not allow the separation its psychological readers want to make.

## Reception and Influence

The book had limited reach outside Scandinavia until the translations of the twentieth century. In English, Walter Lowrie's translation (Princeton, 1941) first introduced it, and the translation by Howard V. Hong and Edna H. Hong in *Kierkegaard's Writings XIX* (Princeton, 1980) became the standard text; Alastair Hannay added another translation (Penguin, 1989). In theology and philosophy, Paul Tillich drew on Kierkegaard's analysis of anxiety and despair in *The Courage to Be* (1952). In psychology, Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death* (1973) draws on Kierkegaard's account of the self and of despair, and on his own account of the "hero project" that hides mortality. In existential therapy, Alice Holzhey-Kunz's Daseinsanalytic view of the symptom as a form of suffering from existential truths that others avoid also draws on this tradition.

## Why It Is Neglected

The book is the least present of the existential works in psychological writing precisely because it is the most theologically committed. It is harder to secularise without distortion than *The Concept of Anxiety*, and the second part's identification of despair with sin resists the translation into clinical language that readers want. But the book's place in the Atlas is secure: it is a phenomenology of despair that precedes clinical psychology by half a century, describing what no symptom list describes — the structure of a self misrelated to itself — and it remains the root from which the existential understanding of the symptom grows.
