# The Fear of Freedom (1941)

The first major book that **Erich Fromm** wrote in English. It appeared in 1941 in New York from Farrar & Rinehart under the title *Escape from Freedom*, and in Britain in 1942 under the title *The Fear of Freedom* (Routledge & Kegan Paul). It is regarded as one of the founding texts of the attempt to combine psychoanalysis with social-historical analysis in explaining the rise of totalitarian regimes.

## An Echo of the Idea in the Arabic Heritage

The book was preceded by Arabic reflection on the effect of oppression and tyranny on the human soul. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah* (Chapter Six), says that severity toward learners is harmful to them, because oppression constricts the soul and dissipates its vigour, leading to laziness, lying, and deceit; and elsewhere he observes that the vanquished are always inclined to imitate the victor. Among the Arabic texts closest to Fromm's subject is Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi's *Taba'i al-Istibdad wa-Masari' al-Istib'ad* (*The Characteristics of Despotism and the Ends of Enslavement*, Cairo, 1900), which analysed how despotism cultivates in people fear, flattery, and the surrender of the will. There is no evidence that Fromm knew these texts, and they approach similar phenomena in a moral and political language rather than through psychoanalysis, so the link is one of comparison, not influence.

## The Central Thesis

Fromm poses a single question: why do millions of people voluntarily surrender the freedom that they wrested through centuries of struggle? His answer is that the **modern liberation from traditional bonds** (feudalism, the Church, the organic community) left behind, alongside independence, **isolation and existential anxiety** that many could not bear. Instead of achieving "positive freedom" (the realisation of the self through love and spontaneous creative work), many turn to "mechanisms of escape from freedom": **authoritarianism** (submission to an external authority, or domination over others, that is, social masochism and sadism), **destructiveness**, and **automaton conformity** (the dissolving of the individual into the herd and the adoption of a "pseudo-self" dictated by society). This framework is what Fromm applied to explaining the lower-middle-class German support for Nazism. He offers the concept of the **"social character"** (*Gesellschafts-Charakter*): the shared psychic structure that a society forms so that individuals want what the society requires of them.

## Structure of the Work

The book begins with a historical review of the birth of modern individualism from the end of the Middle Ages (the Reformation of Luther and Calvin, and the rise of early capitalism), then moves to a psychoanalytic examination of the three "mechanisms of escape." Within the discussion of the mechanisms of escape it analyzes the **authoritarian character** as a composite of sadistic and masochistic strivings understood in social-psychological rather than sexual terms, applies the framework to the psychology of Nazism, and concludes with a chapter on "freedom and democracy" in which it offers a positive alternative; an appendix develops the concept of character and social process. Fromm draws his tools from Marx (the theory of alienation) and from Freud (the unconscious, the mechanisms of defence), with an explicit critique of the centrality of the sexual instinct in Freud and a partial replacement of it by the socio-economic factor.

## Historical Context of Publication

Fromm wrote the work after emigrating from Germany to the United States following the rise of the Nazis, and after his separation from the Frankfurt Institute for Social Research in 1939; he had worked in its inner circle close to Horkheimer, Löwenthal, and Pollock. Between 1929 and 1931 he led within the Institute a survey of roughly a thousand German workers and employees, which showed that many who declared leftist sympathies had an authoritarian psychic structure; its results were published decades later under the title *Die Arbeiter und Angestellten am Vorabend des Dritten Reiches* (*Workers and Employees on the Eve of the Third Reich*). The book appeared in 1941, in the midst of the Second World War, and the question that moves it is a thoroughly contemporary one: how a modern European society surrendered to an authoritarian leader.

## Reception and Criticism

The book enjoyed wide circulation among educated audiences in America during and after the war, and was regarded as one of the early keys to understanding the psychology of mass fascism. It influenced Adorno and his colleagues in *The Authoritarian Personality* (1950), which developed an experimental scale (the F-Scale) for a notion close to Fromm's authoritarian character. Among the principal criticisms: that his analysis explains complex political-economic phenomena (unemployment, the collapse of the German middle class) through individual psychic mechanisms, and that it assumes a single psychic structure shared by millions of people on the basis of limited clinical evidence. Adorno, in *Revised Psychoanalysis* (written in 1946, published in 1952), attacked the new current in analysis that Fromm represented, because in his view it softened the severity of Freud's theory of instincts. Herbert Marcuse debated with him in the journal *Dissent* in 1955, holding that Fromm's concept of positive freedom reconciles the individual with the existing society. Nevertheless the book remained a frequently cited reference whenever the relation between psychic freedom and the attraction to populism and authoritarianism is discussed.

## Its Place among the Fields

The book stands as a bridge between Freudian psychoanalysis, social critique (the Frankfurt School), and existential themes of isolation and anxiety (which resonate with Kierkegaard and Jaspers, though Fromm's argument is not derived from them). It is among the first serious attempts to explain a collective political phenomenon (totalitarianism) with the tools of psychoanalysis without reducing it to individual illnesses, which makes it a reference point in any discussion of political psychology and the rise of populisms.
