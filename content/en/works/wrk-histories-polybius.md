# Histories of Polybius (40 Books)

A large Greek historical work (40 books, of which the first five survive complete and the rest in fragments), written by the Greek historian **Polybius** (c. 200–118 BCE) during his detention as a political hostage in Rome after the battle of Pydna (168 BCE). It represents the first known attempt to write a genuine **universal history**, bringing together the events of the Greek, Roman, Carthaginian and Hellenistic Eastern worlds within a single causally connected narrative.

## Aim and Method: Pragmatic History

Polybius states that his main aim is to explain **how and why** Rome expanded and subjected most of the known world in roughly 53 years alone (220–167 BCE). He presents what he himself called "pragmatic history" (*pragmatikē historia*): a political and military history aiming at a practical, educational benefit for its readers (statesmen and politicians), through analyzing causes and strategies rather than merely narrating events. Polybius clearly distinguished between the immediate cause of an event and its deeper structural causes, a methodological distinction considered advanced for his time.

## The Theory of Anacyclosis (the Cycle of Political Regimes)

In Book Six, Polybius sets out his famous theory of **anacyclosis** (*anakyklōsis*, "the recurring cycle"), which holds that every political system inevitably passes through a repeated cycle from monarchy to tyranny, then to aristocracy and corrupt oligarchy, then to democracy and mob rule (ochlocracy), before returning to monarchy again. Polybius argues that the **mixed Roman constitution** (which combined elements of monarchy in the consuls, aristocracy in the Senate and democracy in the popular assemblies) is what gave Rome its exceptional stability that prevented this degenerative cycle, and by this he explained its political and military superiority.

## Impact

Polybius's work had a profound influence on later Roman historians, and on the theory of the mixed constitution, which Renaissance and Enlightenment thinkers later revived (such as Montesquieu in his analysis of the separation of powers). It remains a key reference in comparative world history and in analytical political history.

## Limitations

- The work has not survived complete: only Books 1–5 are preserved in full text, while the rest are known through excerpts and later Byzantine abridgments, which limits the possibility of assessing his full method in the lost parts.
- Polybius's personal bias as a Greek hostage in the Roman court (and his personal affinity with the Scipio family) is a subject of debate regarding how far he was impartial in portraying the Roman conquest favorably.
