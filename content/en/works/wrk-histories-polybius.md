# Histories (Polybius)

## Definition

The ***Histories*** (Ancient Greek: Ἱστορίαι, *Historíai*) of **Polybius** (c. 200–c. 118 BCE) is a monumental universal history originally comprising forty books, designed to explain how the Roman Republic achieved undisputed mastery over the Mediterranean world in less than fifty-three years (220–167 BCE). Written in Hellenistic Greek (*koine*), the work developed an analytical method known as "pragmatic history" (*pragmatikē historia*), making it a cornerstone of classical political thought and institutional historiography.

## Historical Context and Composition

A native of Megalopolis in Arcadia, Polybius was a prominent statesman and cavalry commander (*hipparchos*) of the Achaean League. Following the Roman victory over Macedonia at the Battle of Pydna (168 BCE), Polybius was among one thousand prominent Achaeans transported to Rome as political hostages. 

In Rome, his erudition secured the patronage and intimate friendship of the prominent Scipio family, particularly Scipio Aemilianus (Africanus the Younger). Polybius traveled extensively across Italy, Spain, Gaul, and North Africa, witnessing firsthand the siege and sack of Carthage in 146 BCE. While only Books I through V survive intact, Books VI through XL are partially preserved through extensive Byzantine anthologies (such as the *Excerpta Constantiniana*) and citations in later historians.

## Methodological Innovations and Theoretical Concepts

### 1. Pragmatic History (*Pragmatikē Historia*)
Polybius defined history as an empirical training ground for statesmen and military commanders. Rejecting the sensationalism, rhetorical embellishments, and dramatic tragedies characteristic of Hellenistic "tragic historiography" (represented by Phylarchus), he insisted that serious history requires three qualifications:
- Detailed study and collation of documentary archives.
- Geographical autopsy (*autopteia*): personal travel to verify terrain, routes, and harbors.
- Firsthand political and military experience.

### 2. Tripartite Theory of Historical Causation
In Book III, Polybius formulated a rigorous taxonomy of causation, warning historians never to confuse the opening moves of a war with its origins:
- **Underlying Causes (*Aitia*)**: Pre-existing political grievances, mentalities, and strategic tensions that make war likely.
- **Pretexts (*Prophasis*)**: Public justifications and moral pretexts announced by leaders.
- **Immediate Beginning (*Archē*)**: The specific initial spark or military action that inaugurates combat.

### 3. Anacyclosis and the Mixed Constitution
In Book VI, Polybius set forth his influential constitutional theory of **anacyclosis** (*anakyklōsis*), a natural biological cycle of regime degradation: monarchy degenerates into tyranny; tyranny is overthrown by aristocracy, which decays into corrupt oligarchy; oligarchy is replaced by democracy, which deteriorates into mob rule (*ochlocracy*), ultimately requiring a monarch to restore order. Polybius argued that Rome achieved unprecedented stability and imperial resilience because its Republican constitution harmoniously blended elements of all three systems: the monarchic principle (the Consuls), the aristocratic principle (the Senate), and the democratic principle (the Popular Assemblies and Tribunes).

### 4. *Tyche* and Universal Interconnection (*Symplokē*)
Polybius viewed the rise of Rome as a unification (*symplokē*) of regional narratives: before 220 BCE, Mediterranean events were isolated regional struggles; after this point, history became an organically interconnected world system guided by *Tyche* (Fortune) toward a single imperial focus.

## Reception and Legacy

Polybius's analysis of constitutional checks and balances influenced later political thought, including Niccolò Machiavelli (*Discourses on Livy*) and Montesquieu (*The Spirit of the Laws*), and mixed-constitution theory was familiar to eighteenth-century American writers such as John Adams.
