# The Idea of History

A philosophical work by **Robin George Collingwood** that raises **fundamental questions about the nature of historical knowledge**. It was published in 1946, after his death.

## Core Ideas

### Re-enactment of the Past

It is not enough for the historian to know **what happened**; he must **reconstruct the events in his mind**. He must understand **what people were thinking** and **why they did what they did**.

### Re-enactment

Collingwood puts forward his central concept, the "re-enactment of past thought": when the historian studies a political decision taken by an ancient ruler, he does not merely know what that ruler did. He tries to think the very thought that the ruler thought at the moment of decision, relying on the available evidence to reconstruct the logic of the historical agent's internal situation, rather than merely describing its external outcome.

### Internal and External Events

- **External event**: the concrete act (a battle, the signing of a treaty)
- **Internal event**: the ideas, intentions and emotions that led to the event

## Historical Context

Collingwood worked as a professor of metaphysical philosophy at the University of Oxford. He was also a field archaeologist specializing in Roman Britain. This practical background gave him a sensory understanding of reconstructing the past from incomplete material fragments, which later appeared in his philosophical view of historical method. The book was not published during his lifetime. His editor, **T. M. Knox**, gathered Collingwood's lectures and scattered manuscripts after his sudden death in 1943, and published them in 1946 under this title. This means the book is not a unified text written by Collingwood all at once, but an edited compilation of an intellectual project that had not been completed in its final intended form.

## Evidence, Not Testimony

Collingwood distinguishes between **testimony**, meaning what a source explicitly says about itself, and **evidence**, meaning everything a skilful historian can extract from a source even if its author did not intend to say it. He illustrates this with examples from his field archaeology in Roman Britain, where a silent archaeological site can "testify" to historical facts that no one intended to record explicitly. This methodological distinction later influenced the development of modern historical archaeology and comparative archaeological studies, even in the absence of explicit written texts.

## Critique of Historical Positivism

Collingwood launches a sharp critique of what he calls the "scissors-and-paste" or positivist history (Scissors-and-Paste History): the conception of the historian as a passive worker who cuts and pastes the testimonies of sources without any genuine intellectual intervention of his own. Collingwood argues that this conception, inherited partly from Ranke's idealism of absolute objectivity, conceals the truth that every historical writing is necessarily an active process of intellectual construction carried out by the historian's mind, not a neutral, passive transmission.

## Other Major Works by Collingwood

Alongside *The Idea of History*, Collingwood wrote *An Essay on Metaphysics* (1940) and *An Autobiography* (1939), in which he explained the development of his historical philosophy over the course of his career. He linked it to his political stance against the rising fascism of 1930s Europe, arguing that practical philosophy must engage with the issues of its own age rather than isolate itself in abstract theoretical towers.

## Impact and Criticism

The book had a profound influence on contemporary **philosophy of history** and emphasized the **role of understanding and empathy** in historical writing. It inspired later historians and theorists, such as Hayden White, in his analysis of the narrative structure of historiography. Critics, however, have pointed out the vagueness of Collingwood's concept of "re-enactment": it is difficult to draw clear boundaries between genuinely reconstructing the thought of a historical agent and simply projecting the historian's own conceptions onto that agent without his being aware of it.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Collingwood's concept that the historical agent must be understood from within finds a notable methodological echo in Ibn Khaldun's *Muqaddimah*. Six centuries before Collingwood, Ibn Khaldun also stressed that understanding historical events requires understanding the "nature of civilization" ('umran) and the internal social context that produced the event, rather than merely transmitting the report superficially, as the "storytellers" (al-qassasun) whom Ibn Khaldun sharply criticized in his introduction did, for their naivety in accepting reports without examining their social and natural plausibility. This criticism largely parallels Collingwood's critique of "scissors-and-paste history," despite the vast chronological and civilizational gap between the two thinkers. This intellectual convergence may deserve a separate comparative study, which has not yet been carried out in sufficient detail.
