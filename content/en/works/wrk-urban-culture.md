# Urban Culture: Studies of Urban Community

**Urban Culture: Studies of Urban Community** treats the work of the American anthropologist **Richard G. Fox**, above all his book *Urban Anthropology: Cities in Their Cultural Settings*, published in **1977**. Fox's argument was that anthropology had studied cities badly: it had treated them either as overgrown villages or as mere collections of poor neighbourhoods, and it had failed to ask what a city is as a cultural form. His book set out to correct this by arguing that cities must be studied as wholes — as systems of relations that bind neighbourhoods, institutions, classes, and regions together — and that urban culture is not a diluted version of rural culture but a distinctive way of life with its own logic.

Fox wrote at a moment when urban anthropology was finding its identity. Anthropologists had traditionally studied small, bounded communities: villages, tribes, island societies. When they turned to cities, they tended to study the parts they knew how to study — a slum, a migrant community, an ethnic enclave — and to treat the city itself as background. The result, Fox argued, was a fragmented picture: a mosaic of neighbourhood studies that never added up to an understanding of the city. His book was a plea for a holistic urban anthropology, one that would take the city itself as the unit of analysis.

## The City as a Cultural System

Fox's central claim is that a city is not a place but a process. Drawing on the Chicago School of sociology — Robert Park's 1925 essay "The City," Louis Wirth's "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938), and the ecological models of Burgess and his colleagues — Fox argued that urban life is shaped by size, density, and heterogeneity, but that these conditions do not produce a single, uniform urban culture. Instead, different kinds of cities produce different kinds of urban life. He distinguished between the pre-industrial city, the colonial city, the industrial city, and the city of the developing world, and argued that each must be understood in its own historical and political context.

This comparative approach was the book's strength. Fox examined cities across the world — from the ancient cities of Mesopotamia to the colonial cities of India, where his own earlier fieldwork had been conducted, to the industrial cities of Europe and North America — and showed how the same urban processes worked differently in each. In the colonial city, for example, the sharp separation between the European quarter and the "native" town was not an accident but the spatial expression of colonial rule. In the industrial city, the separation between workplace and residence, and between classes, was the spatial expression of capitalism. Urban culture, in this account, is the lived experience of these structures: the ways people make homes, form networks, and create meaning within a city that is also making them.

## Networks, Neighbourhoods, and the Whole

A second theme of the book is the relationship between the part and the whole. Fox argued that neighbourhood studies are valuable but insufficient: a neighbourhood can only be understood in relation to the city that contains it, and the city can only be understood in relation to the region and the world economy that sustain it. He drew on the work of Ulf Hannerz, who had studied a Washington ghetto in the 1960s and later criticised the fragmentary character of urban research, and on the Manchester school's network analysis, to argue that anthropologists should study the webs of relation — kinship, friendship, work, patronage — that connect the neighbourhood to the city and the city to the world beyond.

Fox also insisted that urban anthropology must be historical. Cities are not timeless; they grow, decline, and are remade by political and economic forces. His discussion of Indian cities drew on his knowledge of the colonial period, when British rule reshaped urban settlement, and on the post-independence period, when new industrial towns and administrative capitals were built. The city, for Fox, is a historical product, and its culture is the accumulation of the histories that have made it.

## Reception and Influence

*Urban Anthropology* was widely used as a textbook and helped to establish urban anthropology as a recognised subfield. It was praised for its comparative breadth and for its insistence that the city be studied as a whole, and it was cited in the surveys of the field that followed, notably in Ulf Hannerz's *Exploring the City* (1980), which took up Fox's critique of fragmentation and developed it into a fuller theory of urban life. The book's influence can also be seen in the later turn toward the study of global cities, transnational migration, and urban space — fields in which the question Fox posed, of how the part relates to the whole, remains central.

The book has also been criticised. Some readers found its typology of cities too schematic, and its treatment of individual cities too brief to be convincing. Others noted that it said little about gender, and that its account of urban culture was stronger on structure than on the everyday experience of city life. The Marxist urban sociology of Manuel Castells and David Harvey, published in the same years, offered a more explicitly political account of the city, and the debate between their structural approach and Fox's cultural one shaped the field for a decade. Yet Fox's central insistence — that anthropology must study the city itself, and not only the neighbourhoods within it — has become a basic premise of the subfield.
