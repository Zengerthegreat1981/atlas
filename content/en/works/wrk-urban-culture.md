# Urban Anthropology: Cities in Their Cultural Settings

*Urban Anthropology: Cities in Their Cultural Settings* (Prentice-Hall, 1977) is a book by the American anthropologist Richard G. Fox, known mainly for his work on South Asia. It is an early comparative study of cities from an anthropological point of view, published as a textbook when urban anthropology was emerging as a subfield. Its starting point, expressed in the title, is that cities have to be understood within the cultural, political and economic settings that produced them rather than as one universal type.

## Background: The Study of the City

Anthropologists had traditionally studied small communities such as villages and tribes. When they turned to cities after the Second World War, they often did so through studies of particular neighbourhoods, migrant communities or ethnic groups, an approach later summarized as the anthropology *in* the city rather than of the city. The sociologists of the Chicago School had earlier proposed general accounts of urban life: Robert Park's essay on "The City" (first published in 1915 and revised for a 1925 volume) and Louis Wirth's "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938), which linked urban culture to size, density and heterogeneity. Fox's book belongs to the effort to compare cities historically and cross-culturally and to take account of how types of city differ with their economic and political base.

## Approach

Fox treated the city as a product of history and of the wider society, so that different periods and regions (from early cities to colonial and industrial ones) produce different urban forms and different kinds of urban social life. Neighbourhood studies, in this approach, are valuable but must be related to the larger urban and regional systems of which they are part.

## Reception and Related Works

The book belongs to the period in which urban anthropology became a recognized field, alongside works such as Edwin Eames and Judith Granich Goode's *Anthropology of the City* (1977) and Ulf Hannerz's *Exploring the City* (1980), which gave a more developed account of the field's debates. Contemporaneous Marxist urban theory, such as Manuel Castells's *The Urban Question* (English translation 1977), offered a more explicitly political-economic account of the city, and discussions of the relation between cultural and structural approaches shaped the field in the following years. Setha Low's review "The Anthropology of Cities" (1996) summarizes later developments.

## The five urban types

A chapter-by-chapter outline of the 1977 book is not available in the discussions consulted here. What those discussions do fix is Fox's typology. Walburga Wiesheu, writing in *Cuicuilco* in 1998, reads the book against Gideon Sjoberg's single category of the preindustrial city. Fox, on her account, ties the city to the kind of state it belongs to and distinguishes five types: regal-ritual, administrative, mercantile, colonial and industrial. The first three are different preindustrial forms, not one stage. Where state power is weak and the city depends economically on a wider society, the city's main cultural role is ideological, which is the regal-ritual case. Wiesheu finds the scheme useful because it places cities in a broader social order, and she argues that it still lacks the concepts needed to explain the earliest, pristine growth of cities.
