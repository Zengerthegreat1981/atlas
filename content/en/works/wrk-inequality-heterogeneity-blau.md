# Inequality and Heterogeneity: A Primitive Theory of Social Structure

A later work by Peter Blau (1977) in which he moves from the analysis of individual exchange to formulating a formal structural theory of the effect of the distribution of social attributes in society on patterns of interaction between individuals and groups.

## Historical Context

Peter Blau published this work in 1977, thirteen years after his best-known book *Exchange and Power in Social Life* (1964). Whereas that early work focused on analyzing power and exchange at the level of the direct individual relationship, in this later work Blau moved to an entirely different ambition: building a "formal," quasi-mathematical theory of overall social structure, influenced by Georg Simmel in his early analysis of abstract forms of social interaction, and moving away from the psychological-motivational focus that characterized his first work in favor of a purely demographic-statistical analysis.

## The Central Idea

Blau distinguished two kinds of social differentiation: "Inequality," a hierarchical differentiation on ranked variables (income, education, status) that places individuals in "higher" and "lower" positions relative to one another; and "Heterogeneity," a horizontal differentiation on unranked variables (religion, ethnicity, occupation) that distributes individuals among distinct categories with no hierarchical ordering between them. He argued that the structure of these two kinds of differentiation within a given society, their degrees and their intersection (do the lines of class and ethnic division run parallel or cross-cut?), determines statistically the probabilities of interaction, marriage and friendship between individuals across the boundaries of different categories, independently of any conscious individual motives or personal attitudes toward other categories: the greater the "heterogeneity" and the more the different lines of division cross-cut (rather than run parallel, that is, when class membership does not coincide completely with ethnic or religious membership), the greater the statistical likelihood of interaction between different categories, simply because the individual in daily life faces individuals from other categories in multiple contexts (work, neighborhood, club) that he cannot easily avoid. On this basis Blau developed a number of formal theorems, derived from a few primitive assumptions and open to statistical testing, on the relationship between group size and structural distribution and the probabilities of interaction between categories, offering a quasi-geometric mathematical model of social structure in complete isolation from any specific cultural or historical content.

## Impact and Critique

The book offered an influential formal model of social structure, proposing that the demographic structure of a society, and not only individual attitudes or prejudices, helps explain patterns of integration and isolation among social categories. Blau tested the theory empirically with Joseph Schwartz in *Crosscutting Social Circles* (1984), using data on American metropolitan areas, including rates of intermarriage between groups. The model has been criticised for excessive abstraction that neglects the cultural and historical content of intergroup relations, and for treating associations as determined by opportunity structure. Its influence on quantitative social network analysis and on research on intergroup relations is widely recognised.
