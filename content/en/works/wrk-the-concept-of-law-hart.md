# The Concept of Law

Hart's most famous work, published in 1961, and one of the most influential books in the philosophy of law in the twentieth-century Anglo-American world.

## The Central Thesis

In its early chapters, the book criticizes the **classical Austinian model** (law is a command backed by a sanction, issued by a habitually obeyed political sovereign). It argues that this model fails to explain: (a) **power-conferring rules** that do not oblige but enable individuals to do something (such as making a contract); (b) the continuing validity of laws across a change in the identity of the sovereign; and (c) the difference between "being obliged" (under the threat of a robber) and "having an obligation" under a legal rule.

## The Hartian Alternative

Hart proposes a system of **primary rules** (which impose duties) and three **secondary rules**: the rule of change (how rules are enacted and repealed), the rule of adjudication (who determines that a rule has been broken), and the **rule of recognition**, the most important of the three, which provides a social criterion for the validity of primary rules.

## The Final Chapter: Hard Cases, Law and Morality

The book also discusses the problem of **vagueness in legal language** (the "open texture" of law), and Hart's middle position between extreme formalism and extreme American realism. It also covers his position on the relationship between law and morality. He accepts the existence of a **minimum content of natural law** necessary for the survival of any society, while rejecting the thesis that moral wickedness deprives a rule of its legal character.

## Historical and Intellectual Context

The book was published in 1961, when Hart was professor of jurisprudence at Oxford. At that time the legacy of **John Austin** on the one hand and American Legal Realism on the other dominated Anglo-American legal thought. Hart was influenced by the method of **ordinary language philosophy** that prevailed at Oxford in the 1950s, through the philosopher J.L. Austin and the later Wittgenstein, and he applied its analytic tools to the basic concepts of law rather than relying on prior essentialist definitions. In this sense the book was not merely a theoretical reply to Austin, but an alternative methodological project for understanding legal systems as complex social practices, understood from the inside (the internal point of view) and not from the outside alone.

## The Rule of Recognition and the Internal Point of View

Hart introduces the concept of the **"internal point of view"** as an analytic tool that distinguishes between someone who describes a legal rule from outside as an observer (recording only the regularity of behavior) and someone who accepts it from inside as a critical standard for guiding their own conduct and that of others. In this sense the **rule of recognition** becomes a social phenomenon consisting of the practice of state officials (judges in particular), not merely an abstract logical rule. Its validity derives from its actual acceptance in the practice of judicial authority, not from a higher rule that proves its validity in turn (unlike the concept of the "basic norm" in Hans Kelsen).

## Reception and Critique

Upon its publication the book was regarded as a radical renewal of legal positivism. It has been the reference text around which most debates in analytic philosophy of law have revolved since the 1960s. The most prominent criticism came from **Ronald Dworkin**, Hart's successor in the Oxford chair of jurisprudence, who argued in later essays (most notably "The Model of Rules") that Hart's rule-based model cannot account for the role of **principles** in judicial reasoning. He also argued that judges in **hard cases** do not exercise discretion outside the law, as Hart claims, but discover rights latent within the legal system itself. Hart partly answered this critique in the **Postscript** appended to the second edition after his death in 1992. There he maintained his positivist position, and accepted that a rule of recognition may incorporate moral criteria among its tests of validity (a position now usually called inclusive or soft positivism, in contrast to the exclusive positivism of his student Joseph Raz). Another criticism came from the realist direction, arguing that Hart, despite criticizing formalism, retained a degree of rule-based certainty that does not reflect the actual complexity of judicial practice.

## Lasting Impact

The book formed the cornerstone of what is known as **modern legal positivism**, and it profoundly influenced Hart's direct students such as Joseph Raz and Neil MacCormick. They developed distinct tendencies within the positivist school: Raz is the leading exponent of exclusive positivism, while MacCormick developed an institutional theory of law that engages more with morality and reasoning. The rule of recognition remains a basic analytic tool in the study of comparative constitutional systems, used to understand how legal legitimacy passes through major political transformations such as revolutions and constitutional coups.

## The Open Texture of Law and the Debate with Formalism and Realism

Hart devotes a full chapter to what he calls the "open texture of law." Drawing on ordinary language philosophy, he shows that any legal rule, however precisely worded, necessarily contains a **core of settled meaning**, in which it applies to clear cases without dispute, and a **penumbra of uncertainty**, where the rule becomes subject to competing interpretations. A rule such as "no vehicles in the park" certainly applies to a car, but it raises disagreement about a bicycle or a children's pram. From this starting point Hart takes a middle position between legal formalism (which imagines that every case is settled by mechanical application of the text) and extreme American realism (which denies any rule-based discipline and reduces law to predictions of judicial behavior). He affirms that judicial discretion is a structural necessity in the penumbra, not a defect that complete legislation could remove.

## Law and Morality: A Minimum Content of Natural Law

Hart devotes a section to the possibility of a necessary connection between law and morality. He rejects the strict natural law thesis that a morally iniquitous rule loses its legal character altogether ("lex injusta non est lex"). He considers this a confusion that weakens our ability to describe unjust legal systems (such as Nazism) accurately and to criticize them from outside, rather than denying their legality linguistically. On the other hand, he recognizes what he calls the "minimum content of natural law": obvious truths about human nature (such as the relative weakness of humans, the scarcity of resources, and limited altruism). These require any successful legal system to include minimal rules protecting life and property and enforcing promises, failing which society itself could not survive.
