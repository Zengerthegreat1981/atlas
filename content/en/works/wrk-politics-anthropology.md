# Political Anthropology (Balandier)

*Anthropologie politique* (1967) is a synthesis by the French anthropologist and sociologist Georges Balandier. Its English translation by A. M. Sheridan Smith appeared in 1970 as *Political Anthropology*. The book surveys the comparative study of power and argues that societies once described as simple or unchanging contain political tension, competition, and history.

## Aims

Balandier presents political anthropology as a means of decentring political thought. A theory built mainly on the modern European state cannot say what politics is in general. At the same time, the study of societies without such a state must not conclude that they lack political life. He reviews earlier writers and the British work that followed *African Political Systems* (1940), and discusses competing definitions based on territory, the legitimate use of force, functions, and structures.

## Power and its ambiguity

The book treats power as double. It protects a society against disorder and external threat, yet it rests on and maintains inequality. Authority therefore requires justification, often through the sacred, and remains open to challenge. Balandier examines relations between kinship and political position, showing that lineage organisation can supply a language for rivalry and rank as well as for solidarity. He also discusses how differences of sex, age, clientship, status, and class are connected with the distribution of power.

## Religion and the state

Rulers' ties to ancestors, ritual, and cosmic order are analysed as sources of legitimacy and also as limits on conduct. Practices that appear to oppose power, including some forms of witchcraft accusation or ritual reversal, may express conflict and sometimes contain it. On the state, Balandier avoids a single sharp line between societies with and without it. He considers traditional states of varied forms and cautions against deriving all of them from one cause.

## Change

A chapter on tradition and modernity continues Balandier's earlier work on the "colonial situation", set out in an influential 1951 article and in studies of Central Africa. Colonial administration and independence did not simply replace old institutions with new ones. Existing offices, symbols, and loyalties could be reinterpreted within parties and bureaucracies, while appeals to tradition could serve present aims. This dynamic approach contrasts with analyses that describe a self-balancing system.

## Place in the field

The volume appeared while political anthropology was moving from typologies of structure toward process, as in the work of Max Gluckman and in the 1966 collection edited by Marc Swartz, Victor Turner, and Arthur Tuden. Its breadth made it a widely used introduction. Because it is a synthesis, it moves rapidly across cases and depends on others' ethnography for much of its material. Its examples reflect the African focus and the debates of the 1960s, and later research has given more attention to bureaucracy, global institutions, gender, and the anthropology of contemporary states.
