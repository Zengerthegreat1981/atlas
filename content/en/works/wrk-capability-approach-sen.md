# Capability Approach

## Meaning and Definition

The Capability Approach is a theoretical framework in the philosophy of justice and development. It was developed by the economist and philosopher Amartya Sen. It measures an individual's well-being neither by the amount of resources he owns (such as income), nor by the degree of satisfaction he feels (as in utilitarianism). It measures it by the extent of the actual "capabilities" he possesses to do and be what has real value in his life, such as nutrition, learning, political participation, and health.

## Central Theses

The essential distinction in this approach lies between "resources," "functionings," and "capabilities." Owning a bicycle (a resource) means nothing if the person is paralyzed and cannot use it. The actual functioning (such as actual mobility) depends on the "conversion" of the resource into real action. This conversion varies between individuals: a disabled person needs more resources to achieve the same functioning that an able-bodied person achieves with fewer. A "capability" is the set of possible functionings that the person can choose to achieve. It thus assesses freedom itself, not merely the achieved outcome. A person who chose not to eat while fasting differs from a hungry person who has no other option, even though the outcome of the act is the same. Sen refuses to reduce his approach to a fixed list of essential capabilities, leaving this determination to democratic deliberation in each context. Martha Nussbaum, by contrast, later developed a fixed list of ten central capabilities (including life, bodily health, the senses, imagination and thought, affiliation, and play) as a universal minimum that must be guaranteed.

## Historical Context

The approach emerged in the 1980s as a response by Sen to the traditional development metrics based on gross domestic product alone. Those metrics showed their stark inadequacy when they revealed high-income societies in which the actual well-being of marginalized groups was low. This critique was articulated in his 1979 Tanner Lecture "Equality of What?", in *Commodities and Capabilities* (1985), and in his book *Development as Freedom* (1999). It became an influential theoretical basis for the Human Development Reports published by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), as an alternative to GDP alone as a measure of human progress.

## An Approach from the Objectives of the Shariah

This approach intersects methodologically with the theory of the objectives of the Shariah (*maqasid al-shari'a*), systematized by al-Shatibi (d. 1388 CE) in *al-Muwafaqat*. That theory holds that the purpose of legislation is to preserve five essentials of human life (religion, life, intellect, progeny, and property), not merely to distribute resources. Both approaches go beyond the logic of abstract material wealth to the condition that enables a person to realize the essential purposes of his existence. The sources differ, however. For al-Shatibi the objectives are derived inductively from the Shariah. For Sen the capabilities are derived from a rational reflection on the conditions of human freedom.

## Influence

The approach has profoundly influenced development economics and anti-poverty policy. It has also influenced the philosophy of justice through its critique of distributive theories that are content to measure resources, such as Rawls's theory of justice. Sen argues that equality in resources may conceal deep inequality in the capacity to convert those resources into a good life in practice.

## The Debate Between Sen and Nussbaum

The methodological disagreement between Sen and Nussbaum is one of the most important internal debates in this field. Sen, coming from a cautious liberal economic background that is wary of imposing comprehensive conceptions of the good, rejects fixing a list of capabilities. He fears falling into a kind of intellectual paternalism that dictates to societies what they should value, and he leaves the determination to local democratic deliberation. Nussbaum argues that without a specified list the approach loses its critical force in practice. One cannot condemn the violation of women's rights in a given society if the determination of the "important capabilities" is left to the consensus of that very society, which may itself be the source of the violation. For this reason she proposed her fixed list of ten capabilities (life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination, and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; and control over one's environment) as a non-negotiable universal minimum.

## Practical Applications: The Human Development Index

The theoretical framework was put into practice in the Human Development Index (HDI), published by the United Nations Development Programme since 1990, devised by Mahbub ul Haq with Sen's contribution. The index measures countries' progress by combining indicators of life expectancy, education, and income per capita, rather than relying on gross domestic product alone. The index has revealed stark contradictions, such as countries with high incomes but low human development because of weak education or health. This has demonstrated in practice the value of the approach in exposing what economic figures conceal.

## The Nobel Prize and Its Significance

Amartya Sen was awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1998 in recognition of his contributions to welfare economics and to the theory of famines. In his influential analysis of famines he showed that most major historical famines did not result from an absolute shortage of food. They resulted from a failure of "entitlement," meaning the inability of certain groups to access food that was actually available, due to weak economic or political entitlements (set out in *Poverty and Famines*, 1981). This analysis embodies the essence of the Capability Approach in practice: the problem is not the abundance of resources, but the distribution of the actual ability to access them.
