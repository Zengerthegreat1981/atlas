# Caring

## Context and Background

The American philosopher and educator Nel Noddings published her book *Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education* in 1984. It appeared two years after Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* (1982), and the two works were developed independently. The two books emerged from a shared intellectual context: a critique of male dominance in Western moral philosophy. That philosophy had traditionally centered on justice, rights and abstract universal principles, as in Kant, Rawls and Kohlberg. Noddings argued that another mode of moral thinking had been neglected, one that she saw as historically and culturally linked to women's experience. This mode is rooted in concrete relationships and in responsibility toward a particular other, rather than toward abstract "humanity." Noddings was a philosopher of education and a former school teacher. She drew explicitly on her experience as a mother and a teacher. This explains the concrete, practical character that distinguishes the book from the abstract philosophical literature of its time.

## The Central Thesis: Caring as Relationship, Not Individual Virtue

The book rejects the description of "caring" as an individual virtue possessed by a single person (as when we say "so-and-so is a compassionate person"). Instead, it proposes that caring is a two-party relationship requiring two parties: the "one-caring" (the carer) and the "cared-for." Caring is morally complete only when two essential elements are present. The first is "engrossment," meaning the carer receives the reality of the other as it is, without filtering it through prior theoretical categories or abstract principles. The carer does not ask, "What is the general duty here?" but opens genuinely to the need of this particular person in this particular situation. The second is "motivational displacement," meaning that the carer's motivational energy shifts toward achieving what the cared-for needs, rather than toward satisfying the carer's desire to be seen as a virtuous carer. Most important in Noddings's theory is that complete caring also requires "reception" on the part of the cared-for, that is, their recognition and response to the carer's care. Unlike one-sided benevolence, caring on this account is a relation that is completed when the cared-for recognizes and responds to the care.

## Critique of Principled Ethics: Ethical Caring vs. Natural Caring

Noddings distinguishes between two kinds of caring. "Natural caring" is the spontaneous response to those we love, requiring no conscious effort (such as a mother's care for her infant). "Ethical caring" is the deliberate effort made to maintain our readiness to care even when we do not feel a natural inclination (such as a teacher caring for a difficult student). According to Noddings, ethical caring arises from the "ethical self," which was historically formed through memories of receiving natural caring in childhood. When a person faces a situation in which they feel no natural motive to care, they call upon the "idealized ethical self" that wishes to remain a caring being, and they act on this self-commitment rather than on an abstract external law. This is the decisive difference between her approach and the Kantian ethics that derives obligation from abstract reason: for Noddings, the obligation springs from "I want to remain someone who cares," not from "I am logically required to."

## Educational Application

Noddings devotes a large part of the book to applying her theory to education. She proposes that schools be organized around "centers of care" (for example, caring for self, intimate others, strangers, animals, plants and the physical environment, and ideas) rather than an exclusive focus on abstract academic achievement. She also proposes that teachers be trained in "receptive engrossment" toward the needs of each student, rather than applying uniform, rigid standards. She later developed these ideas in subsequent books, such as *Caring: A Relational Approach to Ethics and Moral Education* (revised edition, 2003) and *The Challenge to Care in Schools* (1992). Through them she became a leading figure in the educational thought known as the "ethic of care" in schooling, which has influenced discussion of teacher education.

## Reception and Criticism

The book was widely welcomed as a foundational text for what is now known as "care ethics," an independent feminist moral theory. It also faced substantial objections. Some feminist critics warned of the danger of "essentializing" care as an innate feminine trait, which could reproduce the traditional roles that burden women with unpaid care work within the family and society. Others objected that the theory, by focusing on close, two-party relationships, struggles to address major questions of justice that concern distant others (such as global poverty or structural injustice), since the moral agent cannot "engross" themselves in the need of each distant individual one by one. A further question concerned the limits of care: must we care for someone who abuses or exploits our care? Noddings's account allows the carer to set limits, since the aim of ethical caring is to sustain caring relations, and she did not present it as unconditional submission.
