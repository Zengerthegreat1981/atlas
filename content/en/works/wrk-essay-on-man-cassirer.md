# An Essay on Man - Ernst Cassirer

## The Central Thesis

Man is not the "rational animal" (*Animal Rationale*) in the direct Aristotelian sense, but the **symbolic animal** (*Animal Symbolicum*): a being that does not deal with reality directly, but through a network of symbolic systems (language, myth, religion, art, history, science) that mediates all its experience and shapes its world. This definition is Cassirer's own formulation, not a borrowing from his Kantian or Marburg teachers. In the Arab philosophical culture, man is defined as "the speaking animal" (*al-hayawan al-natiq*), and speech there combines language and reason (*logos*); this is closer to Cassirer's intuition of linking humanity with language than the literal translation "rational animal." But Arabic logic (following the Isagoge of Porphyry) takes speech as a differentia constituting the essence of man, not as a plural cultural system, so the connection is conceptual rather than historical.

## The Structure of the Work

The book was written while Cassirer was teaching at Yale University, and was written directly in English after his emigration from Nazi Germany. It condenses for an English-speaking non-specialist reader the thinking of his larger three-volume German work, *Philosophy of Symbolic Forms* (*Philosophie der symbolischen Formen*: volume one on language, 1923; volume two on mythical thought, 1925; volume three on the phenomenology of knowledge, 1929), without its heavy technical philosophical apparatus. It consists of two parts. The first, "What Is Man?", presents the crisis of human self-knowledge from the Socratic "know thyself" to the doctrines of life and instinct of the nineteenth century, and then introduces the symbol as "a clue to the nature of man" (chapters on the symbolic system, the human world of space and time, and the origin of language). The second, "Man and Culture," surveys the symbolic forms one after another (myth and religion, language, art, history, science), showing how each builds an internal "logic" of its own for shaping experience, rather than being merely a means of expressing a prior knowledge.

## The Psychological-Biological Argument

In building his argument, Cassirer relies on the theoretical biology of Jakob von Uexküll, who held that each animal species has a "functional circle" (*Funktionskreis*) linking its receptor apparatus to its response apparatus, thereby constructing for each organism its own world (*Umwelt*). Cassirer adds that humans are distinguished by adding a third **symbolic system** between reception and response, so that they live in a "new dimension of reality." He distinguishes between the **sign**, which functions within the organism's physical world as part of it, and the **symbol**, which belongs to the world of human meaning and designates without being part of a chain of stimulus and response. He cites the cases of Laura Bridgman and Helen Keller, both deaf and blind: Helen's realisation that everything has a name was the decisive moment in which she passed from sign to symbol. He also draws on Kurt Goldstein's studies of aphasia, in which patients lost the capacity for abstract thought and classification when they lost the symbolic capacity.

## The Historical Context of Publication

The book was published by Yale University Press in 1944, a year before Cassirer's death (he died in 1945 in New York), after he had left Germany in 1933 and then lived in Oxford and Gothenburg before settling in the United States. The book mentions Benjamin Franklin's definition of man as "a tool-making animal," in order to compare it with his own definition, not to adopt it. Cassirer followed it with *The Myth of the State*, published after his death in 1946.

## Reception and Criticism

The book influenced Susanne Langer, who was not his direct pupil: she read his work, translated *Language and Myth* (1946), and in *Philosophy in a New Key* (1942) developed a philosophy of the symbol close to his project, although her book predates *An Essay on Man*. The book is generally classified as an accessible English extension of the programme of the Marburg Neo-Kantian school: extending Kant's question about the conditions of possibility of scientific knowledge to include the conditions of possibility of meaning in all cultural systems. Later work in cognitive science addresses questions close to Cassirer's: Terrence Deacon's *The Symbolic Species* (1997) treats symbolic reference as what distinguishes human cognition, and Michael Tomasello's *Origins of Human Communication* (2008) traces human uniqueness to shared intentionality. Neither derives from Cassirer (Deacon draws on Peirce's semiotics, for example), and both rely on evidence from primates and children rather than on the philosophical method Cassirer adopted, so the relation is one of thematic kinship and not of documented influence.

## The Limits of the Book

It is objected that *An Essay on Man* is a brief, generalising exposition that presents its thesis without elaborating the arguments found in *Philosophy of Symbolic Forms*, and that Cassirer's concept of "the symbolic" is so broad that it includes every cultural activity, making it hard to identify what is not symbolic. Some have considered it an optimistic humanism that does not explain political myth, which he sought to fill in *The Myth of the State*. The book remains an introduction to a question that is still open: does the symbol mediate every experience, or is there a bodily experience that precedes it?

## The Importance for the Philosophical-Psychological Bridge

The book is the easiest English-language entry into Cassirer's project in the philosophy of the symbol, and a meeting point between critical Kantian philosophy and cognitive psychology through the concept of the symbolic mediation of experience: the idea that man does not perceive "the world as it is" but through semantic systems that shape his perception, intersecting with questions in the psychology of perception and cognition about the representational mediators between stimulus and response.
