# Social Exchange and Power in Network Structures

A research program developed from the late 1970s by the American sociologist Karen S. Cook, first with Richard M. Emerson and later with colleagues including Toshio Yamagishi, which extended classical social exchange theory (Homans, Blau) from isolated dyadic interactions to multi-party exchange networks.

## Definition

This is not a single work but a group of interrelated scientific studies and articles published mainly in prestigious American peer-reviewed journals (especially the *American Sociological Review* and the *American Journal of Sociology*) from the late 1970s onward. Together with related work by other researchers (notably David Willer and colleagues), they formed the research tradition known as network exchange theory.

## Historical Context

Cook built her work on the foundation of the "social exchange" theory laid down by George Homans and developed by Peter Blau, and on the concept of "power-dependence" formulated by Richard Emerson in his influential 1962 article, which ties the power of any party in an exchange relation to the degree of the other party's dependence on it. Cook observed that this classical framework had been developed mainly to analyze an isolated dyadic relation between two parties, whereas most actual social exchanges (economic, organizational and political) take place within complex networks of interconnected multi-party relations. This called for the development of a new analytical framework.

## Main Ideas

The program proposes that an actor's position within the whole network of relations, and not a single dyadic relation considered in isolation, is the basic determinant of bargaining power. An actor is more powerful the more alternative exchange partners he or she has, while potential partners have fewer alternatives; power therefore reflects the dependence of others on the actor. Cook, Emerson and their colleagues ran controlled laboratory experiments on exchange networks of different shapes (for example lines, stars and other small configurations) to test how the benefits of exchange are distributed among positions. In the 1983 study, they found that power was not a simple function of how central a position is in the network, which showed that structural position matters in more subtle ways than early centrality measures suggested. Later work by Cook and Yamagishi (1992) developed a power-dependence formulation that includes an equidependence principle, while Willer and colleagues developed alternative models of network exchange (such as the graph-theoretic power index), and the competing models were tested against one another.

## Impact

This research offered testable predictions about the distribution of power in networks, going beyond the descriptive intuitions of classical exchange theory, and it tied social exchange theory to the broader study of social networks. The experiments are a distinctive feature of the program: participants negotiated exchanges with one another across artificially structured networks for rewards, which allowed the distribution of benefits in each structure to be measured and compared with theoretical predictions. Cook later widened her interest, as a professor at Stanford University, to the study of trust and cooperation in exchange relations, including *Trust in Society* (2001, edited) and *Cooperation without Trust?* (2005, with Russell Hardin and Margaret Levi). The program is also open to criticism: experimental networks are small and stylised, and findings from laboratory settings with student participants may not generalise to complex real-world networks.
