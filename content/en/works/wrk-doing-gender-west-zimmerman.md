# Doing Gender

"Doing Gender" (1987) is an article by Candace West and Don H. Zimmerman, published in *Gender & Society*, that proposed an ethnomethodological and interactionist account of gender as a routine, recurrent accomplishment rather than a fixed trait of individuals.

## The Central Thesis

West and Zimmerman distinguish three concepts. Sex is a classification as female or male based on socially agreed biological criteria, such as genitalia at birth. Sex category is the placement of a person in one of those categories in everyday life, achieved through identificatory displays and not necessarily dependent on the sex criteria. Gender is the activity of managing conduct in light of normative conceptions of attitudes and activities appropriate to one's sex category. On this view gender is something people "do" in interaction, and not something they simply "have". Because members of society are held accountable to these normative conceptions, almost any conduct can be assessed as gendered, so interaction becomes an occasion for reproducing, and sometimes contesting, the gender order.

The authors draw on Harold Garfinkel's case study of "Agnes" in *Studies in Ethnomethodology* (1967), a young person who sought recognition as a woman and had to learn deliberately the everyday practices that others acquire without explicit attention. They use the case to show how membership of a sex category is sustained in ordinary interaction. They also criticise earlier role-theoretic accounts that treat gender as something socialised into individuals, and argue instead that it is an interactional achievement supported by institutions.

## Context

Candace West (University of California, Santa Cruz) and Don H. Zimmerman (University of California, Santa Barbara) were both associated with ethnomethodology and conversation analysis. The article appeared in the first volume of *Gender & Society* (vol. 1, no. 2, 1987, pp. 125-151), at a time when feminist scholarship was debating the distinction between sex and gender.

## Impact

The article became one of the most cited works in the sociology of gender and helped shift attention from fixed gender differences to the interactional production of gender. It is often discussed alongside Judith Butler's account of performativity in *Gender Trouble* (1990), although the two approaches come from different traditions. West and Sarah Fenstermaker extended the framework in "Doing Difference" (1995), arguing that gender, race and class are accomplished together in interaction. West and Zimmerman themselves reflected on the debate in "Accounting for Doing Gender" (2009).

## Critique and Assessment

Critics have argued that the framework leaves the binary categories of "man" and "woman" in place as the standard against which conduct is judged, and so pays too little attention to how the binary itself is produced and resisted. Francine Deutsch (2007) proposed instead studying how interaction can "undo" gender. Others argue that the emphasis on micro-interaction underplays institutional structures such as law and labour markets, and that the account of Agnes has been complicated by later evidence about her case.
