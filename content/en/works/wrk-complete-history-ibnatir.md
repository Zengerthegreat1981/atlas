# The Complete History (al-Kamil fi al-Tarikh) — Ibn al-Athir

*Al-Kāmil fī al-Taʾrīkh* (*The Complete History*) is a monumental universal chronicle authored by the Arab-Islamic historian and scholar **ʿIzz al-Dīn Abū al-Ḥasan ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad al-Jazarī**, known universally as **Ibn al-Athīr** (555–630 AH / 1160–1233 CE). Composed largely in Mosul, where Ibn al-Athīr lived under the Zengid atabegs, the work stands as one of the most comprehensive annalistic histories produced in the medieval Islamic world, narrating human history from the creation of the world to the year 628 AH (1231 CE).

## Structure and Scope

Ibn al-Athīr organized *al-Kāmil* chronologically, utilizing a strict annalistic framework (*ḥawliyyāt*, year-by-year narration):
- **Universal Pre-Islamic History**: Synthesizes the history of the biblical and Qur'anic prophets, ancient empires (Achaemenid and Sasanian Persia, Rome, and Byzantium), and pre-Islamic South and North Arabian tribes.
- **The Classical Caliphate**: Recounts the life of the Prophet Muḥammad, the Rashidun Caliphate, the Umayyad dynasty, and the rise and gradual fragmentation of the Abbasid Caliphate.
- **Regional Dynasties and the Crusading Era**: Documents the emergence of regional dynasties across the Islamic world, devoting extraordinary detail to the Levant (Bilād al-Shām), the Jazīra (Upper Mesopotamia), Egypt, and Iraq during the Crusades and the campaigns of the Zengid and Ayyubid rulers, notably Saladin (*Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn*).
- **The Peripheral Regions**: Distinctively preserves extensive regional histories often marginalized in chronicles centered solely on Baghdad or Cairo, including al-Andalus, the Maghrib under the Almoravids and Almohads, Central Asia, and Transoxiana.

## Methodology and Narrative Synthesis

While Ibn al-Athīr adopted the annalistic model established by Muḥammad ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī (224–310 AH / 839–923 CE) in *Taʾrīkh al-Rusul wa-l-Mulūk*, he introduced critical historiographical innovations. Rather than reproducing repetitive, competing reports separated by exhaustive chains of transmission (*isnād*s) as al-Ṭabarī did, Ibn al-Athīr critically compared his sources, resolved chronological contradictions, and synthesized them into a continuous, elegant narrative. 

For the periods prior to 300 AH, Ibn al-Athīr relied primarily on al-Ṭabarī, augmenting his account with subsequent sources such as Ibn Miskawayh's *Tajārib al-Umam*, al-ʿUtbī's *al-Taʾrīkh al-Yamīnī*, and Abū al-Faraj ibn al-Jawzī's *al-Muntaẓam*. For events from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries (his contemporary era), *al-Kāmil* transitions from a largely derivative compilation into an important contemporary source. Writing with access to officials and eyewitnesses in Mosul, Syria, and Iraq, Ibn al-Athīr provided detailed accounts of battles, sieges, and political diplomacy.

## The Witness to the Mongol Invasions

*Al-Kāmil* is one of the earliest and most influential Arabic chronicles to record the devastating initial Mongol invasions of Khwārazm, Transoxiana, and Khurāsān (616–618 AH / 1219–1221 CE) under Genghis Khan. Ibn al-Athīr's account is famous for its visceral grief and profound historiographical self-awareness: he hesitated for years before writing down the catastrophe, remarking in an evocative passage that he wished his mother had never borne him, or that he had died before having to narrate the catastrophe that befell Islam and the Muslims. His detailed documentation of the fall of Bukhara, Samarkand, and Nishapur remains a primary witness to the psychological and geopolitical shockwave that preceded the sack of Baghdad.

## Reception and Historiographical Legacy

Ibn al-Athīr's chronicle served as an important source for later Islamic encyclopedic historians, including Ibn Kathīr (d. 774 AH / 1373 CE) in *al-Bidāya wa-l-Nihāya*, al-Dhahabī (d. 748 AH / 1348 CE) in *Taʾrīkh al-Islām*, and Ibn Khaldūn (d. 808 AH / 1406 CE) in *Kitāb al-ʿIbar*. In Western scholarship, *al-Kāmil* became the central Arabic textual witness for the Crusades, first critically edited in Europe by Carl Johan Tornberg in fourteen volumes (Leiden, 1851–1876) and later translated in substantial part into English by D. S. Richards. Modern historians have noted a pro-Zengid outlook that may have colored his appraisal of rivals such as Saladin, but his work remains valued for its breadth, compositional balance, and narrative coherence.
