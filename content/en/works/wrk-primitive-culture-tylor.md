# Primitive Culture

A foundational book by Edward Burnett Tylor (1871). Its full title is *Primitive Culture: Researches into the Development of Mythology, Philosophy, Religion, Language, Art, and Custom*. It is one of the first works that established cultural anthropology as a systematic academic discipline.

## Content and Thesis

Tylor opens the book with his famous definition of culture: "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society". This definition treats culture as a comprehensive phenomenon that can be studied scientifically and comparatively, rather than as a random accumulation of habits. The book sets out the theory of **animism** (belief in spiritual beings) as the simplest and earliest religious form, arising from humanity's attempt to explain phenomena such as dreams and death. It also introduces the concept of **survivals**: customs that persist in a society after losing their original function, serving as fossil evidence of earlier evolutionary stages.

## Method

Tylor adopted a broad comparative method that gathered scattered ethnographic reports about peoples around the world (rather than direct field observation), arranging them within a hypothetical evolutionary sequence from "primitive" to "civilised". This method would later be subjected to decisive criticism by Franz Boas and his students.

## Impact

This book is a historical starting point for defining "culture" anthropologically, and Tylor's definition has remained the most frequently quoted in the history of the discipline, despite the later overcoming of its evolutionary framework.

## Historical Context

Tylor wrote this work in the period of Darwin's *Origin of Species* (1859) and Victorian confidence in progress. He sought to treat culture and religion as subjects of law-like development, in an evolutionary framework that drew on ideas of progress as well as on Darwin. The book, together with the works of Herbert Spencer and Lewis Henry Morgan, is counted among the main works of the "classical evolutionary school" of Victorian anthropology, which ranked human societies on a scale from "savagery" through "barbarism" to "civilisation" (a terminology associated especially with Morgan's *Ancient Society*, 1877).

## The Concept of Survivals

Among Tylor's most important methodological tools is the concept of **survivals**: customs, rituals and sayings that persist in a "civilised" society even though they have lost their original practical function, thereby becoming a "fossil" piece of evidence from which earlier evolutionary stages that have left no direct traces can be reconstructed. Tylor used this concept to analyse contemporary European customs (such as some folk rituals and proverbs) as remnants of earlier "primitive" religious or intellectual stages.

## Later Criticism

Tylor's comparative method was subjected to decisive criticism by Franz Boas and his students in the early twentieth century. They rejected the idea of a unilinear evolutionary ordering of cultures and insisted that each culture must be studied in its own particular historical context ("historical particularism" rather than "unilinear evolutionism"). They also criticised Tylor's reliance on scattered and unreliable ethnographic reports rather than systematic direct field observation.

## Its Scientific Legacy

Although it went beyond its evolutionary framework later, Tylor's definition of culture ("that complex whole...") remains the most frequently quoted in the history of cultural anthropology, and this book remains the founding starting point for defining "culture" as an object that can be studied scientifically and comparatively.

## The Theory of Animism in Detail

Tylor explains his theory of animism as the first religious stage in human history. He believed that primitive humans, in trying to explain puzzling phenomena such as dreams, death and illness, arrived at the idea of a "soul" or "spirit" separate from the body that could temporarily detach from it (in dreams) or permanently (at death). He then generalised this conception to include all natural phenomena (the spirits of trees, rivers and atmospheric events), before this belief gradually developed, according to Tylor, into polytheism and then monotheism, in a hypothetical linear evolutionary sequence.

## The Book's Influence on Defining Comparative Religion

Beyond its influence on general anthropology, the book helped establish the early academic field of **comparative religion**. It provided a systematic framework for studying "primitive" religions (as they were then called) in an organised comparative way, instead of describing them as random superstitions that did not deserve serious analysis, even though its unilinear evolutionary framework was later subjected to criticism.

## Tylor's Institutional Position

Tylor became Reader in Anthropology at the University of Oxford in 1884 and its first Professor of Anthropology in 1896, and is therefore one of the founding figures of the discipline in Britain, institutionally as well as through his definition of culture.

## A Renewed Debate

Tylor's definition is still cited as a historical starting point in discussions of how culture should be defined, although his evolutionary framework has long been abandoned.
