# Democracy and Distrust: A Theory of Judicial Review

***Democracy and Distrust: A Theory of Judicial Review*** is a book by **John Hart Ely**, published in 1980 by Harvard University Press. It is regarded as an influential work of American constitutional theory, offering a systematic attempt to resolve the **"counter-majoritarian difficulty"** formulated by Alexander Bickel less than two decades earlier.

## The Inherited Problem: Bickel's Difficulty

Ely begins from the diagnosis offered by **Alexander Bickel** in *The Least Dangerous Branch* (1962): when an unelected supreme court nullifies legislation enacted by the elected representatives of the people, it exercises a power that apparently contradicts the democratic principle of majority rule. Earlier scholars sought to resolve this difficulty through two opposing approaches, both of which Ely rejected: **interpretivism**, which confines the legitimacy of judicial review to applying a clear constitutional text alone (and thus fails to justify important rulings based on general principles such as "due process"); and **noninterpretivism**, which allows judges to derive substantive moral values from outside the constitutional text (thereby reproducing the democratic dilemma in a sharper form, since it gives judges an almost unlimited discretionary power to impose their own particular moral values).

## The Solution: A Procedural Theory That Reinforces Representation

Ely proposes a third alternative: the **"representation-reinforcing" theory of the democratic process**. The legitimate task of judicial review should be confined to **protecting the democratic process itself from malfunction**, not to imposing particular substantive outcomes on society. Ely identifies two main functions of judicial review in this sense: first, **keeping channels of political change open** (such as protecting freedom of expression, voting and the formation of parties, which are essential conditions for correcting errors through the democratic process itself rather than through external judicial intervention); and second, **correcting systematic representational malfunctions** that prevent the interests of certain groups from being represented fairly in the legislative process, especially when these groups are isolated and structurally disliked minorities (*discrete and insular minorities*) who lack sufficient political influence to defend their interests through ordinary electoral channels.

## The Inspiration from the Carolene Products Footnote

Ely explicitly builds his framework on the famous **Footnote Four** in the U.S. Supreme Court decision in *United States v. Carolene Products Co.* (1938), in which Justice Harlan Stone proposed a standard for distinguishing cases that deserve stricter judicial scrutiny of legislation (when it touches fundamental political rights or targets minorities without effective political voice) from those in which the democratically elected legislator's judgment should be respected to a large degree (ordinary economic and regulatory matters). Ely transforms this footnote from a passing remark into a complete constitutional theory.

## His Relation to Bickel and Waldron

The book occupies a middle position between Bickel and later critics of judicial review such as Jeremy Waldron (*Law and Disagreement*, 1999), who questioned the legitimacy of strong judicial review at its foundation. Ely is more reconciled with judicial review than Waldron, but more ambitious than Bickel in offering a specific positive framework to justify it, rather than relying on techniques of avoiding decision (the "passive virtues").

## Impact and Diffusion

The book has been widely cited in American constitutional law literature and has formed a reference framework for analyzing questions of freedom of expression, voting and minority rights. It has influenced generations of constitutional law scholars who have tried to reconcile respect for representative democracy with the protection of certain fundamental rights from the excesses of transient majorities.

## Criticism

Ely's theory has been criticized, notably by Laurence Tribe and Ronald Dworkin, on the grounds that the distinction between "reinforcing process" and "imposing substance" is not as decisive as it appears, since identifying which minorities are "isolated and disliked" enough to deserve special judicial protection, and which rights count as "procedural" in the first place, are themselves substantive value judgments that cannot be avoided merely by calling them "procedural."

## The Author's Professional Context

Ely (1938–2003) wrote this work after a professional experience that combined academia and practice at the highest levels of the American judiciary: he served as a law clerk to Chief Justice Earl Warren at the Supreme Court, and then as a professor of law at Yale, Harvard and Stanford (where he was also dean of the law school), and later at the University of Miami. This dual experience between academic theory and actual judicial practice gave him a particular sensitivity to the gap between abstract philosophical theses about the legitimacy of judicial review and the constraints of day-to-day judicial practice, a sensitivity that is clearly reflected in the book's relatively practical style compared with the abstraction of some contemporary philosophical debates on the same subject.
