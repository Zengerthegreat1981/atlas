# Schooling in Capitalist America

The central book (1976) by Samuel Bowles and Herbert Gintis, which established a Marxist political-economy analysis of the relationship of the American educational system to the reproduction of capitalist class inequality.

## Context

Bowles and Gintis, two radical economists at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, wrote this work in the wake of the influential "Coleman Report" (1966), which examined statistically the factors of American educational inequality and found that school resources themselves (such as per-pupil spending) explained a relatively limited part of differences in achievement compared with family background, which fed a heated debate over whether education is really a neutral "social elevator" or another mechanism for reproducing existing inequality. Bowles and Gintis started from a direct Marxist critique of the liberal discourse of "equality of opportunity" prevailing in American education.

## The Central Thesis: The Correspondence Principle

Bowles and Gintis argued that the American educational system, despite its declared discourse of "equality of opportunity" and neutral individual merit, operates according to the "Correspondence Principle": the everyday social structure of the classroom reproduces itself, the hierarchy between teacher and pupil, the rewarding of discipline, obedience and keeping to the schedule rather than independent creativity or critical thinking, the fragmentation of knowledge into separate tasks assessed individually, resembling the fragmented industrial division of labor, producing the same social and behavioral relations that the individual will later face in the capitalist workplace, regardless of the explicit cognitive content of the school curricula. They also observed that this correspondence varies according to the social class of the school itself: schools in poor working-class neighborhoods stress strict discipline and obedience to orders in preparation for lower executive roles, while schools of the upper middle class and the elite encourage independence and individual initiative in preparation for managerial and leadership roles.

## The Statistical Evidence

They drew on statistical evidence, including their earlier work on IQ, arguing that the association between schooling and economic success owes little to cognitive ability as measured by tests and that class background strongly shapes educational attainment, concluding that education works as a mechanism of ideological legitimation of already existing class inequality (by leading individuals to believe that their success or failure is the product of purely individual merit rather than their inherited class position), not as a neutral social elevator rewarding purely individual merit as the official discourse claims.

## Impact and Later Revision

This book offered an influential economic alternative, parallel to Pierre Bourdieu's cultural-symbolic explanation (cultural capital) and Basil Bernstein's linguistic explanation (class linguistic codes) of educational reproduction, and became a founding reference in the "radical political economy" of education whose influence continued for decades. The work was later criticized for an excessive structural determinism that neglects the actual resistance of pupils and teachers to school standardization (as Paul Willis's ethnographic study *Learning to Labour*, 1977, later showed, on working-class youths' cultural resistance to school), In a 2002 reassessment they defended the core of the correspondence argument in light of later research, while acknowledging that schools are also sites of conflict and that the original account needed to allow for the agency and resistance of pupils.

## The Contradiction Between Formal Democracy and the Capitalist Economy

The authors put forward a broader thesis that education reflects a deeper structural contradiction in liberal capitalist societies between two opposing principles: the democratic principle, which assumes formal political equality among all citizens (one vote per individual), and the capitalist market principle, which necessarily produces sharp hierarchical economic inequality (unequal ownership of the means of production). The school, as the intermediary institution that is supposed to reconcile these two contradictory principles, in fact sides, despite its declared democratic discourse, with reproducing the existing economic hierarchy instead of achieving real social equality.

## Comparison with Bourdieu's Cultural Analysis

Bowles and Gintis's economic-structural analysis intersects with Pierre Bourdieu's cultural-symbolic analysis of educational reproduction (through the concept of cultural capital), as the two arrive at a similar conclusion (the school reproduces class inequality rather than abolishing it) through two different mechanisms: Bowles and Gintis focus on the direct structural correspondence between the classroom system and the work system, while Bourdieu focuses on how the apparently unbiased school system evaluates cultural competences (language, taste, tacit knowledge) that upper-middle-class children already possess from their family environment, without these competences actually being acquired within the school itself.

## Differentiation by School Level

The authors also argued that different levels of schooling correspond to different levels of the occupational hierarchy: lower levels of education stress rule-following, middle levels stress dependability, and higher levels stress internalised norms and self-direction. On this view class differences are reproduced through differentiated kinds of schooling rather than through a single system applied equally to everyone.
