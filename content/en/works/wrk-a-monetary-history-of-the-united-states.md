# A Monetary History of the United States, 1867-1960

In 1963 **Milton Friedman**, in collaboration with the economist **Anna Jacobson Schwartz**, published their monumental book *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867-1960*. It is an encyclopaedic empirical work that documents the development of American monetary policy over nearly a full century, and is regarded as one of the most important works that established the empirical foundation of the monetarist school in macroeconomics.

## A Historical-Statistical Methodology

The authors adopted a methodology combining precise historical documentation of monetary and institutional events (Federal Reserve decisions, banking crises, changes in the money supply) with statistical quantitative analysis of how these variables relate to the path of national income and prices. Their central argument was that changes in the money supply are not merely a by-product reflecting fluctuations in the real economy, but a primary causal driver of these very fluctuations.

## The Central Chapter: Reinterpreting the Great Depression

The book devotes a highly influential central chapter to reinterpreting the Great Depression of the 1930s. It advances an argument that opposes the prevailing Keynesian explanation. The depression was not a structural failure inherent in the mechanisms of the free market, but the direct result of a mistaken monetary policy by the Federal Reserve. They argued that the Federal Reserve, through inaction and policy errors, allowed the money stock to fall by about a third between 1929 and 1933 and failed to act as a lender of last resort during the waves of bank failures. This turned an ordinary recession into a depression exceptional in its severity and duration.

## Empirical Evidence on the Monetary-Cyclical Relationship

The book presents a long series of detailed historical evidence that the major fluctuations in American economic activity over several decades (from the panic of 1893 through the panic of 1907 to the Great Depression) were associated with earlier or simultaneous changes in the money supply. The authors relied on detailed statistical data that they collected themselves from scattered banking and government archival sources. It was a pioneering systematic effort in quantitative monetary history.

## The Direct Theoretical and Political Impact

The book challenged one of the historical arguments used to support Keynesian economics (the supposed structural failure of free-market capitalism in the 1930s), and strengthened the credibility of the rising monetarist school. It was widely cited in academic and political debate over the role of monetary policy versus fiscal policy in managing the macroeconomy. Ben Bernanke, a scholar of the Depression who later became chairman of the Federal Reserve, has described the Friedman-Schwartz account as a major influence on his thinking, and the Fed's aggressive response to the 2008 crisis is often read against that background.

## Later Criticism and Revisions

Friedman and Schwartz's explanation of the Great Depression later faced academic criticism from Keynesian and post-Keynesian economists, who argued that the exclusive focus on monetary policy neglects other factors. Peter Temin (1976) stressed an autonomous fall in spending, and Barry Eichengreen (*Golden Fetters*, 1992) stressed the international gold standard. Nevertheless, many economists accept that monetary contraction played an important part in deepening the crisis, even if they dispute that it was the sole cause.

## An Acknowledgement from the Federal Reserve

In November 2002, at a conference marking Friedman's ninetieth birthday, Ben Bernanke, then a Federal Reserve governor, told Friedman and Schwartz that the Fed had indeed made the mistakes they described in the 1930s and that, thanks to their work, it would not repeat them. The remark, made partly in jest, is often cited as a symbolic acknowledgement by the central bank of the influence of the book.

## A Work Within a Broader Research Project

The book grew out of a long research programme on money and business cycles at the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), which also produced related statistical studies by Friedman and Schwartz. Schwartz was a full co-author, and the data collection drew on many years of work with archival and statistical sources before publication in 1963.
