# Al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya

A reference work of jurisprudence and politics by the Shafi'i judge **Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Muhammad al-Mawardi** (974–1058 CE). He came from Basra and was one of the leading judges in Baghdad during the Abbasid caliphate. It is among the earliest comprehensive systematic treatments of the structure of the Islamic state and its institutions in the Sunni juristic heritage. The title came to name a small genre of juristic writing on the rulings of government (*al-ahkam al-sultaniyya*).

## Structure and Content

The book surveys in twenty chapters the principal institutions of the state and their functions. These include: the contract of the imamate (caliphate), its conditions of validity and its modes of establishment; the vizierate and its two types, the vizierate of delegation (*wizarat al-tafwid*) and the vizierate of execution (*wizarat al-tanfidh*); the emirate of provinces; the judiciary; *mazalim* (examining the grievances of subjects against senior officials); *hisba* (overseeing markets and public morals); the emirate of jihad and the distribution of spoils; *kharaj* and *zakat*; criminal penalties and the regulation of crimes (*hudud* and *jara'im*), and the administration of public lands and of non-Muslim subjects.

## Central Thesis: The Contract of the Imamate

Al-Mawardi details the seven conditions of the imam (caliph), such as justice, knowledge, physical and administrative fitness, and Qurashi descent. He also explains the ways in which the imamate is validly concluded: by the choice of the people of loosing and binding, or by the designation of the previous imam. He discusses necessary cases such as the *imarat al-istila'* (emirate by seizure), in which the caliph recognizes an amir who has seized a province by force without prior delegation, in order to preserve the unity of the community and avoid chaos. This discussion registers the loss of direct Abbasid control to military amirs. In al-Mawardi's lifetime that meant above all the Buyids. The Seljuk leader Tughril Beg entered Baghdad only in 1055, three years before al-Mawardi's death, so the Seljuks belong to the end of his career rather than to the setting in which the book is usually placed.

## Method

Al-Mawardi combined juristic grounding in the Quran, the Sunna and the consensus of jurists with an empirical observation of the administrative practice of his era's state. The book is thus a mixture of **normative ideal jurisprudence** and a **practical descriptive account** of the institutions of the late Abbasid caliphate. It can be read as a historical source for Abbasid administration as much as a normative juristic text.

## Reception

The book became a principal reference for the jurisprudence of governance (*fiqh al-siyasa al-shar'iyya*) in the Shafi'i school. The Hanbali Abu Ya'la al-Farra' (d. 458 AH / 1066 CE) wrote a book of nearly the same title. The two works are close in plan. Scholars disagree about which was written first and about how far either depends on the other. Abu Ya'la's text is a Hanbali statement of the same genre. The book has remained a basic reference in Islamic political jurisprudence, and it is cited in academic debate on classical Islamic political thought.

## Circumstances of Composition

The book was written in Baghdad during the caliphate of al-Qa'im bi-Amr Allah (1031–1075), while effective power in the city still lay with the Shi'i Buyids. Tughril Beg entered Baghdad in 1055, near the end of al-Mawardi's life. Al-Mawardi had served as judge in several cities. In 429 AH / 1037–38 CE he received the title *aqda al-qudat*, an elative meaning "the most judicious of judges," distinct from the ordinary title *qadi al-qudat* (chief judge). Jurists disputed whether that elative was permissible. He also carried out embassies between the caliph and the Buyid amirs, and so stood between the caliphate and the amirs who held power. Alongside the *Ahkam* he wrote *Adab al-Dunya wa-l-Din*, on the ethics of worldly and religious life; *Qawanin al-Wizara wa-Siyasat al-Mulk*, on the vizierate; *Tashil al-Nazar wa-Ta'jil al-Zafar*, on political deliberation; and a *Nasihat al-Muluk* (Counsel for Kings) that is only attributed to him. His *al-Hawi al-Kabir* is a work of Shafi'i positive law. The *Ahkam* belongs with the books of a judge who also wrote on administration.

## Later Developments in Jurisprudence and Politics

Abu Ya'la's book agrees with al-Mawardi in many sections and differs in others. To call it a compilation is to assume a dependence that comparison of the two texts has left open. Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH / 1111 CE), writing under the Seljuk sultans, treated coercive power as necessary to public order and recognized a ruler who held power in fact, while still speaking of the caliph. Ibn Jama'a (d. 733 AH / 1333 CE), in *Tahrir al-Ahkam fi Tadbir Ahl al-Islam*, held that seizure by force can itself contract a valid imamate, so that the community is not left without a ruler. Later jurists continued to rewrite the imamate in this genre.

## Modern Readings

Rashid Rida's *al-Khilafa aw al-Imama al-Uzma* (The Caliphate, or the Supreme Imamate, 1922–23), written before the Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the Ottoman caliphate in March 1924, drew on al-Mawardi in arguing that the caliphate was a religious necessity. After the abolition, Ali Abd al-Raziq argued in *al-Islam wa-Usul al-Hukm* (1925) that the caliphate had been a historical form of government, not an obligation imposed by the religion. H. A. R. Gibb read the *Ahkam* as an effort to bring the existing distribution of power under the legal form of the caliphate. Hanna Mikhail argued that the book was more than an apology for the Abbasid court. Wael Hallaq, in *The Impossible State* (2013), argues that the modern concept of the state does not match the structure of rule as the jurists describe it. Patricia Crone, in *God's Rule* (2004), places al-Mawardi in a wider survey of Islamic political thought and of the relation between the caliphate and the scholars.

## Caveats

The exact date of composition is uncertain; it is generally thought to date from the last decades of al-Mawardi's life (he died in 1058), and c. 1045 is a conventional approximation. The book reflects the reality of an Abbasid caliphate tending toward real political weakness, more than it presents an abstract theory valid for every time and place. Contemporary scholars differ over whether Mawardi's flexibility in accepting the emirate of seizure was a pragmatic concession to reality or a deliberate juristic grounding of the idea of separating religious legitimacy from actual power.
