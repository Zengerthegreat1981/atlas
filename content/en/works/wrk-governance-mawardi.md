# Al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya

A reference work of jurisprudence and politics by the Shafi'i judge **Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Muhammad al-Mawardi** (974–1058 CE). He came from Basra and was one of the leading judges in Baghdad during the Abbasid caliphate. It is among the earliest comprehensive systematic treatments of the structure of the Islamic state and its institutions in the Sunni juristic heritage. Its title became a collective name for the entire field of the "rulings of government" (*al-ahkam al-sultaniyya*).

## Structure and Content

The book surveys in twenty chapters the principal institutions of the state and their functions. These include: the contract of the imamate (caliphate), its conditions of validity and its modes of establishment; the vizierate and its two types, the vizierate of delegation (*wizarat al-tafwid*) and the vizierate of execution (*wizarat al-tanfidh*); the emirate of provinces; the judiciary; *mazalim* (examining the grievances of subjects against senior officials); *hisba* (overseeing markets and public morals); the emirate of jihad and the distribution of spoils; *kharaj* and *zakat*; criminal penalties and the regulation of crimes (*hudud* and *jara'im*), and the administration of public lands and of non-Muslim subjects.

## Central Thesis: The Contract of the Imamate

Al-Mawardi details the seven conditions of the imam (caliph), such as justice, knowledge, physical and administrative fitness, and Qurashi descent. He also explains the ways in which the imamate is validly concluded: by the choice of the people of loosing and binding, or by the designation of the previous imam. He discusses necessary cases such as the *imarat al-istila'* (emirate by seizure), in which the caliph recognizes an amir who has seized a province by force without prior delegation, in order to preserve the unity of the community and avoid chaos. This practical juristic position reflects the actual disintegration of central Abbasid authority in al-Mawardi's time, when the caliph had lost much of his real power to the Buyid and Seljuk amirs.

## Method

Al-Mawardi combined juristic grounding in the Quran, the Sunna and the consensus of jurists with an empirical observation of the administrative practice of his era's state. The book is thus a mixture of **normative ideal jurisprudence** and a **practical descriptive account** of the institutions of the late Abbasid caliphate. It can be read as a historical source for Abbasid administration as much as a normative juristic text.

## Reception

The book became the primary reference for the jurisprudence of governance (*fiqh al-siyasa al-shar'iyya*) in the Shafi'i school. Abu Ya'la al-Farra' the Hanbali later drew on it in a book of nearly the same title (*al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya*), following a parallel Hanbali method. This reflects the standing of the model Mawardi established, as a framework that other juristic schools emulated. The book remained a fundamental reference in Islamic political jurisprudence until the modern age, and it is cited today in academic debate on the origins of classical Islamic political theory.

## Circumstances of Composition

The book was written in Baghdad during the caliphate of al-Qa'im bi-Amr Allah (1031–1075 CE), when the real power of the Abbasids lay in the hands of the Shi'i Buyids, and then of the Seljuks after Tughril Beg entered Baghdad in 1055 CE. Al-Mawardi had served as judge in several cities. He was given the title *aqda al-qudat* (chief judge) in 429 AH/1038 CE, after a dispute over whether this title was permissible, and he carried out embassies between the caliph and the Buyid amirs. His position between the caliphate and the dominant amirs probably made him a witness to the reality he describes. Alongside this, he wrote books on the ethics of worldly rule and religion (*Adab al-Dunya wa-l-Din*), on the laws of the vizierate, on facilitating reflection and hastening victory (*Tashil al-Nazar wa-Ta'jil al-Zafar*), and the attributed *Nasihat al-Muluk* (Counsel for Kings). These works concern the ethics of governance, while his *al-Hawi al-Kabir* pursued pure Shafi'i jurisprudence. This explains why the *Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* is the fruit of the experience of a practising jurist rather than purely that of a political theorist.

## Later Developments in Jurisprudence and Politics

Abu Ya'la al-Farra' (d. 458 AH/1066 CE), the Hanbali, followed Mawardi's model in his book of the same name. He agreed with him on many sections and disagreed on others, and some researchers consider his work closer to compilation. Al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH/1111 CE) accepted the authority of one who seizes control of the land when he possesses power. Ibn Jama'a (d. 733 AH/1333 CE), in *Tahrir al-Ahkam*, built on this acceptance by holding that the imamate of one who seizes power is valid in order to preserve the order of the community. Scholars continued to rewrite the imamate between grounding and justification, until the title itself became a literary genre.

## Modern Readings

Modern Islamic movements re-read the book after the abolition of the Ottoman caliphate in 1924. Rashid Rida, in *al-Khilafa aw al-Imama al-Uzma* (The Caliphate or the Supreme Imamate, 1922–23), drew on Mawardi's heritage to defend the necessity of the caliphate. Ali Abd al-Raziq, in *al-Islam wa-Usul al-Hukm* (1925), answered that the caliphate was not a binding religious system but a historical political experience. Orientalists later at times described the book as a justification of the prevailing reality, as some scholars have observed. Wael Hallaq, in *The Impossible State* (2013), offered a reading holding that the modern concept of the state contradicts the structure of Islamic rule as the jurists describe it. Patricia Crone, in *God's Rule* (2004), places Mawardi within her wider survey of Islamic political thought and of the relation between the caliphate and the scholars.

## Caveats

The exact date of composition is uncertain; it is generally thought to date from the last decades of al-Mawardi's life (he died in 1058), and c. 1045 is a conventional approximation. The book reflects the reality of an Abbasid caliphate tending toward real political weakness, more than it presents an abstract theory valid for every time and place. Contemporary scholars differ over whether Mawardi's flexibility in accepting the emirate of seizure was a pragmatic concession to reality or a deliberate juristic grounding of the idea of separating religious legitimacy from actual power.
