# Institutiones Grammaticae (The Principles of Grammar)

The most comprehensive surviving work of ancient Latin grammar. Priscian wrote it in Constantinople in the early 6th century CE, in eighteen books, methodologically applying to Latin specifically the syntactic framework devised by the Greek grammarian Apollonius Dyscolus.

## Transferring the Greek syntactic method to Latin

Unlike simpler and more pedagogical Latin grammatical works (such as the abridged works of Donatus), Priscian sought to build a comprehensive syntactic description of Latin on the model of the analytical depth Apollonius Dyscolus had reached in Greek. He did not limit himself to classifying the parts of speech and their inflections, but analyzed the syntactic relations between words within the sentence with a theoretical methodology.

## Priscian's theory of the parts of speech

Priscian elaborated the theory of the eight parts of speech inherited from the Greek-Latin tradition (the noun, the verb, the participle, the pronoun, the preposition, the adverb, the conjunction, and the interjection). He analyzed each part according to its specific morphological "accidents" (*accidentia*): gender, number and case for the noun, and tense, mood and number for the verb. He provided a detailed morphological description that surpasses in its comprehensiveness any earlier Latin grammatical work. He also clearly distinguished between the lexical meaning of a word and its grammatical function within the sentence, a methodological distinction that paved the way for later developments in European grammatical theory about the relation between morphology and syntax.

## Structure: from sounds to syntax

The eighteen books are divided into two main parts. The first sixteen books (*Priscianus Maior*) cover phonology and morphology in detail (the inflection of nouns and verbs, and the other parts of speech). The last two books (*Priscianus Minor*) are devoted to syntax, a division that many later grammatical works followed.

## The context of composition in Constantinople

Priscian composed his work at the beginning of the 6th century CE in Constantinople, the capital of the Eastern Roman Empire, where he worked as a teacher of Latin; he composed a panegyric for the Emperor Anastasius I (reigned 491–518). At the time Latin was the official language of administration and the army, while Greek remained the dominant cultural language in the Byzantine East. Priscian's work responded to a pressing practical need to teach Latin in a methodical and precise way to students who were native Greek speakers. This explains its close dependence on Greek grammatical method, especially that of Apollonius Dyscolus, in describing Latin, rather than relying on simpler local Latin grammatical traditions such as the work of Donatus.

## Method of citing literary texts

Priscian followed a careful citation method, drawing thousands of illustrative examples from the works of the great classical Latin writers, above all Vergil, Cicero, Terence and Horace. This makes his work also a rich textual source that preserved fragments of Latin literary works that are lost or survive only incompletely in other sources. This intensive citation method distinguished it from simpler educational grammatical works contemporary with or earlier than it, and made it at once a grammatical reference and a vast anthology of classical Latin literature.

## Long-term impact

This work became the most comprehensive and authoritative Latin grammatical reference throughout the entire European Middle Ages. Medieval grammarians, the Modistae, in the 13th and 14th centuries, used it as a textual basis for building their philosophical interpretations of the structure of language and the theory of "modes of signifying" (*modi significandi*). It continued to be taught and commented on in European universities until the late Middle Ages. Hundreds of medieval manuscripts survive, reflecting the centrality of the work in the European educational curriculum for many centuries.

## Comparison with the contemporary Arabic grammatical tradition

Comparative scholars note that Priscian's work preceded by roughly two centuries the work of Sībawayh (died c. 180 AH / 796 CE). The two traditions remained independent of each other, though both developed systematic accounts of how words combine in sentences according to regular rules. The conceptual tools differed fundamentally between the two schools: Sībawayh relied on the concept of governor and governed (*ʿāmil* and *maʿmūl*), unlike the Greco-Latin syntactic framework, which was based on the parts of speech and the morphological accidents of the individual word first.
