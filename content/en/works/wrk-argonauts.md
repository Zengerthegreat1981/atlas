# Argonauts of the Western Pacific

*Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) is Bronisław Malinowski's ethnography of maritime enterprise and ceremonial exchange in the Massim region off eastern New Guinea, written mainly from the standpoint of the Trobriand Islands. Its full subtitle describes it as an account of native enterprise and adventure in the archipelagoes of Melanesian New Guinea. James Frazer contributed the preface.

## Fieldwork

Malinowski carried out research in New Guinea during the First World War, including two long stays in the Trobriands between 1915 and 1918. The introduction sets out principles that became a charter for modern fieldwork. The ethnographer should have real scientific aims, live among the people and away from other Europeans, and apply systematic methods of collecting evidence. Malinowski asks for records of institutional rules, of the "imponderabilia of actual life", and of statements, narratives, and formulae in the native language. The final goal is to grasp the native's point of view and relation to life.

## The kula

The kula links island communities in a ring through which two kinds of shell valuables travel in opposite directions: red necklaces (*soulava*) clockwise and white armshells (*mwali*) anticlockwise. Men hold the objects for a time and pass them on to partners, acquiring renown through the quality of valuables and the history of their transactions. The exchange is governed by rules of delayed return and is distinguished from the barter of useful goods, *gimwali*, which may take place on the same expeditions.

The book follows an expedition from canoe building and launching through the voyage, reception, and return. Garden work, chiefly authority, gift obligations, myth, and above all magic are woven into the narrative. Magic accompanies technical skill; it does not replace it.

## Argument

Malinowski used the kula to challenge the figure of a "primitive economic man" concerned only to satisfy wants by the most direct means. Large efforts were organised around objects with little practical use and great social value. The institution connects economy, prestige, ritual, and alliance, and participants need not hold a picture of the whole ring that the ethnographer constructs.

## Later research

Marcel Mauss drew on the book in *The Gift* (1925). Restudies, including the contributions in *The Kula* (1983) edited by Jerry Leach and Edmund Leach, showed regional variation, strategies of competition, and links with other forms of exchange that Malinowski treated briefly. Annette Weiner's Trobriand research demonstrated the importance of women's wealth and exchanges. The publication in 1967 of Malinowski's field diaries, which record irritation, illness, and prejudice, complicated the self-portrait in the introduction. The work also gives limited attention to the colonial officials, traders, and missionaries present in the region. It remains central both as ethnography and as a text through which fieldworkers established their authority.
