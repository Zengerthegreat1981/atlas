# The Historian's Craft

A short but profound and influential book written by **Marc Bloch** shortly before his death during the Second World War (published in 1949). The book explains **the nature of the historian's work** and how to **read sources and engage in dialogue with them**.

## Main Ideas

- **Questions matter more than answers**: a good historian asks the past the right questions.
- **Scientific skepticism**: every source must be doubted and examined carefully.
- **Everyday life matters**: history is not only wars and kings, but also ordinary people.

## Impact

The book has become a classic in historical studies and influenced a whole generation of historians to focus on **the right questions and precise methodology**.

## Context of Composition

Marc Bloch wrote this work in exceptional circumstances: during the German occupation of France, after he had been dismissed from his university post under the racial laws of the Vichy government for being Jewish, and while he was active in the secret French Resistance. The book remained unfinished when Bloch was arrested and executed by firing squad at the hands of the Gestapo in June 1944, a few weeks before the liberation of France. His wife and daughter later published the text on the basis of his draft, which was not completely finished, which gives the text a powerful testamentary character: a historian thinking aloud about the meaning of his profession while facing possible death in defense of the values of reason and liberty to which he had devoted his life.

## Expanded Ideas and Method

In the book Bloch rejects the two competing definitions of history that dominated his era: "event-based" narrative history, which merely recounts political facts without deep explanation, and deterministic history, which seeks general, strict "laws" on the model of the natural sciences. Instead he proposes a history of "humanity in time" (*l'homme dans le temps*), which treats the document not as a neutral witness but as a trace that requires active critical interrogation: "Documents speak only when we know how to question them," a phrase that has become one of the most famous maxims of modern philosophy of history. He also defends the necessity of comparative history across civilizations and eras, to reveal the shared and unique features of each social phenomenon, and discusses the problem of the historian's inevitable bias as a child of his own time, calling for critical awareness of this bias rather than an illusion of impossible absolute objectivity.

## Influence

The book, together with Bloch's earlier works and those of his fellow co-founder of the journal *Annales*, Lucien Febvre, established the intellectual foundation of the French Annales school across its successive generations (Fernand Braudel, Jacques Le Goff, Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie), and it has had a worldwide influence on the professional philosophy of history in the twentieth century. It is taught today in historian training programs around the world as the most concise and profound introduction to the question "What is history, and why do we write it?"

## Structure of the Book

The book is organized into chapters that address logically sequential questions: What is history, and why is it worth serious study? How do we observe the past (through its traces rather than direct observation)? How do we critique testimony and distinguish the authentic from the forged? How do we analyze historical causation without falling into a deterministic, single-cause simplification? Bloch's style combines a rare mixture of methodological precision and personal human warmth: he draws examples from his own research on French feudal history and from his personal experience of the two world wars, which gives the abstract theoretical discussion a concreteness and sincerity rare in methodological writing.

## Comparison with Similar Works

The book is usually compared with other influential works of historical methodology, such as Edward Hallett Carr's *What Is History?* (1961). But it predates Carr by more than a decade, and according to many historians of methodology it surpasses him in its exceptional existential sincerity, arising from the tragic circumstances of its composition, and in its deep rootedness in a long, concrete research experience (feudal history and French peasant society), rather than in a purely philosophical reflection on the nature of historical knowledge detached from concrete research practice.

## Reception in the Arab World

The book has been translated into Arabic and has become a key reference in historical methodology teaching in Arab universities. It is usually presented as the most concise and penetrating possible introduction for Arab history students to the questions of modern philosophy of history (the nature of the document, causation, objectivity), without requiring them to engage with the more complex and abstract theoretical debates such as the later postmodernist debates. This gives it a lasting educational influence that extends beyond its original French context to history fields in many academic languages and cultures around the world.

## Conclusion

*The Historian's Craft* remains a unique testimony that combines the intellectual and the existential: a historian thinking deeply about the meaning of his profession and its ethical responsibility toward truth and reason, while personally facing one of the darkest chapters of violence and irrationality in modern European history. This gives his defense of the value of critical historical knowledge a moral weight that goes beyond a technical methodological text, into a human statement about the value of rational understanding of the past in the face of barbarism and tyranny.
