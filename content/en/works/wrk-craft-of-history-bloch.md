# The Historian's Craft

## Definition

***The Historian's Craft*** (original French title: *Apologie pour l'histoire ou Métier d'historien*) is a seminal twentieth-century treatise on historical methodology and epistemology written by the French medievalist and co-founder of the *Annales* school, **Marc Bloch** (1886–1944). Written during the German occupation of France and left unfinished upon Bloch's execution by the Gestapo in June 1944, the book was first edited and published posthumously in 1949 by his close colleague and intellectual collaborator Lucien Febvre. It remains one of the most widely read and influential reflections on the purpose, techniques, and ethics of the historical discipline.

## Circumstances of Composition

Bloch drafted the work between 1941 and 1943 under severe adversity. Affected by the anti-Semitic Vichy statutes, Bloch relocated to Montpellier before joining the clandestine French Resistance (Franc-Tireur) in Lyon. Working in hiding without access to his personal library, he composed the text as a meditation on his craft, prompted, as the opening dedication recalls, by his young son's question about what history is for Following his capture, torture, and execution by firing squad in June 1944, the manuscript was gathered by Lucien Febvre, who published an edition in 1949; a definitive critical edition based on the surviving manuscripts was later produced by Bloch's eldest son, Étienne Bloch, in 1993.

## Methodological Innovations and Core Concepts

### 1. History as "The Science of Men in Time"
Bloch famously rejected both dry chronological chronicle (*histoire événementielle*) and the mechanistic determinism that sought to impose rigid natural-science laws onto human society. He described history as the science of human beings in time (*la science des hommes dans le temps*), emphasizing that historical inquiry must capture living human reality across duration and change.

### 2. Unintentional Traces and Active Interrogation
A central contribution of the work is Bloch's distinction between intentional reports (memoirs, official proclamations, histories) and unintentional traces (*témoins malgré eux*), such as fiscal ledgers, judicial rolls, aerial photographs, and linguistic shifts. Bloch insisted that historical documents do not speak for themselves; they yield meaning only when subjected to rigorous, targeted questions posed by the researcher.

### 3. The Regressive Method
Drawing upon his extensive research into agrarian structures (*French Rural History*), Bloch advocated the "regressive method" (*méthode régressive*): reading historical evidence backwards from the known, well-documented present to illuminate the more obscure origins of the past, thereby preventing anachronistic assumptions.

### 4. Critical Doubt and the Plurality of Causes
Bloch formulated sophisticated guidelines for testing the authenticity of sources, detecting forgery, and evaluating the psychology of false rumors (a theme he had explored during his service in the First World War). Furthermore, he warned against the "idol of origins"—the erroneous belief that finding the beginning of a phenomenon equates to explaining it—and argued for complex, multi-layered causal explanations that integrate economic, social, geographical, and psychological dimensions.

## Legacy and Impact

*The Historian's Craft* has been widely read as a methodological statement of the *Annales* outlook and has influenced later historians. It joins technical source criticism with broad humanistic and comparative inquiry, and remains widely used in historiographical training.
