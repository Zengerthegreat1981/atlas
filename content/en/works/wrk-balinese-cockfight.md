# Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight

"Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight" is an essay by Clifford Geertz, first published in the journal *Daedalus* in 1972 and reprinted as the final chapter of *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973). It is among the best-known demonstrations of interpretive anthropology, treating a popular and officially illegal pastime as something that can be read for what it says about its participants.

## The opening scene

The essay begins with Geertz and his wife arriving in a Balinese village in 1958, where villagers behaved as though the visitors were not there. When police raided a cockfight, the couple fled along with everyone else instead of invoking their status as foreigners. The villagers then received them warmly and with much teasing. The story introduces the setting and also establishes the ethnographer's rapport, a narrative move that later critics examined closely.

## Cocks, bets, and status

Geertz describes the identification of Balinese men with their cocks, the care lavished on the birds, and the association of animality with disorder. Fights use blades tied to the birds' legs and are often over quickly. Betting has two layers: a large, even-money central wager arranged between the owners' sides, and many smaller side bets at varying odds. He argues that matches with high, balanced central bets draw the most intense interest because pride, kin loyalty, village membership, and rivalry are engaged. People generally back birds belonging to their own group.

## Deep play

The phrase "deep play" comes from Jeremy Bentham, who used it for gambling in which the stakes are so high that it is irrational, on utilitarian grounds, to take part. Geertz argues that in the Balinese case money matters partly as a measure of esteem and social standing. No one's rank is actually changed by a result; the fight stages status tensions without resolving them. He calls it "a story they tell themselves about themselves": a dramatisation through which participants experience themes of rivalry, rage, and hierarchy that ordinary etiquette keeps muted. Culture appears as an ensemble of texts that the anthropologist strains to read over the shoulders of those to whom they properly belong.

## Criticism and influence

William Roseberry argued that treating culture as a text separates meanings from the processes that produce them, including colonial rule, markets, and state power. Vincent Crapanzano questioned how Geertz moves from observed conduct to claims about what Balinese experience, since individual voices are largely absent. Others noted that women's economic and social participation receives little attention, and that the mass killings in Indonesia in 1965–66, which reached Bali severely, appear only briefly. The essay remains widely taught as a model of vivid, layered interpretation and as a case for discussing what evidence an interpretation requires.
