# Environmental Ethics

## Context and Background

Holmes Rolston III, the American philosopher and Presbyterian minister, is regarded as one of the founding figures of "environmental ethics" as an independent academic philosophical field. This began with his pioneering essay "Is There an Ecological Ethic?" (1975) and continued with his reference work *Environmental Ethics: Duties to and Values in the Natural World*, published in 1988 by Temple University Press. The latter is considered the first comprehensive systematic treatment of the fundamental philosophical question: does nature itself (animals, plants, and ecosystems) possess intrinsic value independent of its usefulness to humans, or must every environmental value ultimately be reduced to a human interest (anthropocentrism)? The work appeared in the context of the rising Western environmental awareness that followed Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) and the establishment of Earth Day in 1970. At that time, many philosophers felt that traditional ethical tools (Kantian and utilitarian), designed originally for dealing with relations among humans, were unable to encompass the relationship between humans and non-human nature.

## Central Thesis: The Gradation of Value in Nature

Rolston rejects the sharp dichotomy between "intrinsic value" (possessed by the rational human alone) and "instrumental value" (in which all of nature is reduced to usefulness). In its place he proposes a complex, graduated model of value. Individual living things, from plants to animals, possess what he calls "intrinsic value" in the sense that they pursue their own good ("Good of Its Own") regardless of any benefit to humans. A tree "strives" toward light and grows and reproduces according to its own genetic program, and this self-directed striving is the basis for regarding it as an object of moral consideration. However, Rolston goes further than ethics centered on the individual animal (such as Peter Singer's theory of animal liberation). He argues that ecosystems and species also possess collective intrinsic value independent of the individuals that compose them, since the "species" rather than the individual alone is the evolutionary unit that carries a "historical story" of adaptation and genetic innovation deserving respect in itself.

## The Concept of Value-Generating Project and Nature as Narrative

Among Rolston's most important conceptual innovations is the idea that nature is a "project" with an evolutionary history stretching back billions of years, and that this history is itself a source of value. A species threatened with extinction loses not only present individuals, but a unique "narrative line" of adaptation that will never recur if it is cut off. In this way, Rolston introduces a temporal and historical dimension into environmental ethics that is absent from traditional ethical theories, which center on the present moment alone. He also distinguishes between the multiple aspectual values that nature carries (aesthetic, scientific, recreational, religious, and historical) and the deeper "intrinsic value" that is not reducible to any of these roles. In doing so, he seeks to provide a solid philosophical foundation for protecting wilderness even in the absence of any human visitor who enjoys it.

## Reception and Criticism

Rolston's work has received wide acclaim. In 2003 he received the Templeton Prize in recognition of his contribution to linking science, religion, and environmental ethics, and he is widely called a founder of environmental ethics; he taught at Colorado State University. His thesis, however, has faced objections. Some critics, including advocates of radical ecological egalitarianism, find his graded ranking of kinds of value hierarchical. Analytic philosophers have also questioned the vagueness of the concept of the "good of a plant," and whether it really entails an "interest" in the precise moral sense, or is merely a biological metaphor that carries no genuine normative weight. An illustration of how his argument applies: on his view, a rare insect species with no economic or aesthetic use still has value, because it is the product of a long, unrepeatable evolutionary lineage that cannot be restored once it is extinct.

## Relation to Religion and Science

Rolston differs from many environmental philosophers in combining his scientific background (he studied physics and the philosophy of science, and holds a doctorate in theology from the University of Edinburgh) with his work as a Presbyterian minister. This allowed him to formulate a vision that regards biological evolution itself, with its long history of increasing complexity, as carrying a quasi-spiritual significance worthy of reverence, without abandoning scientific precision in describing Darwinian mechanisms. This mixture of science and value has prompted debate over whether an "ought" can be derived from an "is" (Hume's is-ought problem). Rolston has sometimes been accused of jumping from the biological description of organisms' behavior to a normative conclusion about their value without sufficient argumentative justification for this leap. He has argued in reply that values are discovered in natural systems rather than projected onto them.
