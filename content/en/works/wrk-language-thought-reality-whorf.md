# Language, Thought, and Reality

## Definition

A collection of essays by Benjamin Lee Whorf, edited by the psychologist and linguist John B. Carroll and published in 1956 by the Technology Press of MIT (with John Wiley), fifteen years after Whorf's death in 1941. It brings together Whorf's most important writings on linguistic relativity. Whorf was a chemical engineer who worked for the Hartford Fire Insurance Company, and he studied linguistics as an amateur, attending Edward Sapir's courses at Yale, which made him an unusual figure in the history of linguistic anthropology.

## Historical Context

Whorf built on the ideas of his teacher Sapir, including the account of language in *Language* (1921). He studied the Hopi language, an Uto-Aztecan language spoken in Arizona, working with Hopi speakers. His professional work also supplied observations: as a fire-prevention engineer he reported that linguistic labels (such as calling gasoline drums "empty" although they contained explosive vapour) affected how people behaved toward hazards. This professional observation contributed to the formation of his theoretical ideas about language and cognition.

## Key Ideas

The book contains Whorf's core essays on his study of the Hopi language and his claim that the language has no words, grammatical forms or constructions that refer directly to time as an objectified, countable dimension, and that its verbs mark validity and aspect rather than past, present and future tense. From this he concluded that Hopi speakers have a conception of time and space different from that of speakers of English and other European languages. The book also contains the general formulation of the "linguistic relativity principle", often read in a stronger "linguistic determinism" form: that the structure of a language's grammar imposes on its speakers a particular way of dividing and categorizing their cognitive experience of the world, so that language is not merely a neutral instrument for expressing prior ideas but an active factor in shaping thought itself. Whorf uses the term "Standard Average European" for the grammatical habits shared by the major European languages (such as treating time as a quantifiable extension), and argues that these are not universal.

## Major Works and Representatives

Whorf and his teacher Sapir are the figures most associated with the hypothesis of linguistic relativity, which provoked debate lasting decades. Experimental work by Eric Lenneberg and Roger Brown in the 1950s opened empirical testing, and the strong form of the hypothesis was criticized by later writers such as Steven Pinker. More recent experimental work, for example by Lera Boroditsky, John Lucy and others, has found support for "weak" versions (language influencing some aspects of cognition without determining thought).

## Impact and Critique

This book became the principal source circulated for what was later known as the "Sapir-Whorf hypothesis," although Whorf himself did not name it so. It provoked a broad debate lasting decades in linguistics, cognitive psychology and anthropology concerning the relationship between language, thought and cognition. Whorf's description of the Hopi language was later subjected to systematic criticism by the linguist Ekkehart Malotki in *Hopi Time* (1983), who argued, on the basis of his own fieldwork, that Hopi has expressions for units of time, tense-like constructions and other means of referring to time that Whorf had said were absent. This weakened the strong deterministic formulation of the hypothesis without eliminating the broader scientific interest in questions of linguistic relativity.

## Additional Detail on Workplace Observations

In the essays collected by Carroll, Whorf recounts his insurance work. As a fire-prevention inspector he noticed that workers handled drums labelled "empty" carelessly, for instance smoking near them, even though empty drums of gasoline held explosive vapour. He concluded that the linguistic classification ("empty" versus "full") guides actual behavior toward risk regardless of the objective physical reality. This practical observation formed the seed of his broader linguistic theory later on.

## Counting and Quantity Classification

Whorf also analyzes how the expression of countable and uncountable quantities differs between English and Hopi. He argues that English treats "ten days" like "ten men", as a countable plurality of separate units, whereas Hopi treats such durations as successive cycles or numbered returns of a single process ("the eleventh day" rather than "ten days"). He uses this grammatical contrast as further evidence that even our simplest concepts of "counting" and "quantity" are not a universal cosmic logic but a product of the particular structure of our own language.
