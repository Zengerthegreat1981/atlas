# Philosophical Investigations

Ludwig Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) is one of the most important works of twentieth-century philosophy, and one of the strangest. Published two years after Wittgenstein's death, it is not a treatise but a collection of numbered remarks — 693 numbered sections in the first part, plus a second part (later edited as "Philosophy of Psychology: A Fragment") — that circle around a single question: how does language work? The book's answer overturned the picture of language Wittgenstein himself had offered in his earlier *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921), and it reshaped the philosophy of language, the philosophy of mind and, through Peter Winch and others, the foundations of the social sciences.

## Historical Context and Publication

Wittgenstein wrote the *Investigations* over the last two decades of his life, first at Cambridge, where he held the chair of philosophy from 1939 until he resigned it in 1947, and then in Ireland and elsewhere in his final years. He did not publish it in his lifetime, revising the manuscript repeatedly. After his death in 1951, G. E. M. Anscombe and Rush Rhees, two of his literary executors (the third was G. H. von Wright), prepared the text for publication. It appeared in 1953 as a bilingual edition, with the German original and Anscombe's English translation on facing pages. The book's fragmentary form is deliberate: Wittgenstein believed that philosophical problems could not be solved by building a system, but only dissolved by looking carefully at how words are actually used.

## Language-Games and Meaning as Use

The book's central idea is that language is not a single system for naming things but a motley of activities — what Wittgenstein calls "language-games." Giving orders, telling jokes, asking questions, praying, cursing and describing are different games, each with its own rules, and the meaning of a word is its use within a game. In a well-known formulation (§43), for a large class of cases, though not for all, the meaning of a word is its use in the language. This overturns the *Tractatus*, in which Wittgenstein had argued that language pictures facts and that the meaning of a word is the object it stands for. In the *Investigations*, there is no single essence of language to be discovered; there is only a family of overlapping practices, held together by what Wittgenstein calls "family resemblance" rather than by a common definition.

## Rule-Following and the Private Language Argument

Two of the book's most famous arguments concern rules and privacy. The rule-following considerations (§§185–242) ask what it is to follow a rule: no rule can determine its own application, since any rule can be interpreted in indefinitely many ways. What makes an action a case of following a rule is not an inner mental act but a shared practice — a "form of life" in which people are trained to go on in the same way. The private language argument (§§243–315) extends this to the mind: a language that only one person could in principle understand is impossible, because words for sensations get their meaning from public criteria, not from private ostension. Together, these arguments undermine the idea of the mind as a private inner theatre and relocate meaning and understanding in public, social activity — a move with profound implications for sociology.

## Structure and Method

The book has no linear argument. It proceeds by examples, thought experiments and imagined dialogues, often opening with a quotation from Augustine or a simple scenario — a shopkeeper fetching apples, a child learning to count — and then drawing out its consequences. Wittgenstein's method is therapeutic: philosophy, he says, is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language, and its aim is to show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle. The remark that the limits of my language mean the limits of my world belongs to the *Tractatus*, but the *Investigations* is often read as giving it a social turn: the limits of our language are tied to the forms of life we share.

## Reception and Influence

The *Investigations* became a central text for philosophy attentive to ordinary language in Britain and the United States, often discussed alongside the work of Gilbert Ryle and J. L. Austin. Its impact on the social sciences was equally deep. Peter Winch's *The Idea of a Social Science and Its Relation to Philosophy* (1958) drew directly on Wittgenstein to argue that social action must be understood through the concepts of the actors themselves, and that sociology could not be a natural science of behaviour. Ethnomethodology and the sociology of scientific knowledge (for example in the work of David Bloor and Harold Garfinkel's followers) have drawn on Wittgenstein's account of rules and practices, although the extent of the debt is disputed. In sociology, the book underwrites the interpretive tradition's insistence that social order is an accomplishment of shared understanding rather than a mechanical effect of structures.

## An Empirical Case: Rules in Scientific Practice

The *Investigations* contains invented examples, not field research, but its account of rule-following has informed empirical work. A well-documented case is Harry Collins's study of laboratories trying to build a TEA laser, a type of gas laser, reported in an article of 1974 and revisited in *Changing Order* (1985). Collins found that scientists could not reliably reproduce the device from written instructions alone. Those who succeeded had generally had extended personal contact with people who had already built one, and even they could not always say in advance which details mattered. Collins read this through the problem of rules: instructions do not fix their own application, and competence in following them is acquired through participation in a practice. In the same book he examined disputes over the detection of gravitational radiation, arguing that when a result is contested, judging whether an experiment was done competently depends partly on what result it ought to produce. This sociological use goes beyond Wittgenstein's philosophical aims, and Bloor and ethnomethodologists such as Michael Lynch have disagreed about how rule-following should be interpreted.

## Critical Assessments and Limitations

Critics have questioned whether the book's arguments are as decisive as its admirers claim. Saul Kripke's *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) argued that the rule-following considerations lead to a radical scepticism about meaning that Wittgenstein himself did not resolve. Others have charged that the book's emphasis on ordinary use makes it conservative, unable to criticise the forms of life it describes. Still others note that Wittgenstein says almost nothing about power, inequality or history — the very things sociology cares about. Defenders reply that the book's achievement is not a theory but a way of seeing: once language is understood as a social practice, the idea that social life could be explained without reference to meaning becomes untenable.
