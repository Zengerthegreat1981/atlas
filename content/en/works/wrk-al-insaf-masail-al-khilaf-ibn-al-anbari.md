# Al-Insaf fi Masa'il al-Khilaf bayn al-Nahwiyyin al-Basriyyin wa al-Kufiyyin

A reference work by Abu al-Barakat Ibn al-Anbari that documents, in an organized method, about one hundred and twenty (traditionally 121) grammatical questions on which the grammarians of Basra and Kufa disagreed across the history of the classical Arabic grammatical tradition. It is the best-known systematic late synthesis of this dispute.

## The author and historical context

Abu al-Barakat Abd al-Rahman ibn Muhammad al-Anbari (513–577 AH / 1119–1181 CE) was a Baghdadi grammarian and man of letters who lived in the sixth century AH. In his time, the historical disagreement between the Basran and Kufan schools had become a fully formed body of tradition that could be recorded and organized retrospectively, since the living debate between the two schools had ceased for centuries. Ibn al-Anbari undertook a task of synthesis and pedagogy in this context: gathering the most important disputed questions scattered across the works of earlier grammarians into a single organized work that could easily be consulted in teaching circles. Ibn al-Anbari also became known for other works on grammar and literature, including *Asrar al-Arabiyya* and *Lum' al-Adilla fi Usul al-Nahw*, in which he discusses the foundations of grammatical argument in an abstract theoretical manner. His *Lum' al-Adilla* is generally regarded as the first systematic work on the foundations of grammar (*usul al-nahw*) modeled on the principles of jurisprudence.

## Examples of the questions treated

The book deals with central grammatical questions that remained matters of dispute throughout the history of the Arabic grammatical tradition. Among them: why is the imperfect verb (*al-fi'l al-mudari'*) in the nominative: because it occupies the position of a noun (the Basran view) or because it is free of any governing particle (*tajarrud*, the Kufan view)? Do *inna* and its sisters govern both the noun and the predicate, as the Basrans held, or only the noun, as the Kufans held? Further questions concern the permissibility of particular word-order and case phenomena. In each question, Ibn al-Anbari presents the textual evidence (citations from the Qur'an and poetry) and the rational evidence (analogy and cause) on which each side relied.

## Balanced method and the structure of each question

The book presents each disputed question in a nearly fixed structure: first the argument and evidence of the Basran side, then the argument and evidence of the Kufan side, then the objections each side raises against the other and their replies, and finally a critical discussion that attempts to achieve "equity" (*insaf*) between the two sides, as the book's title suggests, rather than an a priori partisan bias toward one school over the other. This repeated structure made the book an effective teaching instrument that allows the student to follow the logic of each position step by step. Despite this apparent balance, scholars have noted that Ibn al-Anbari, being Basran in inclination in many of his judgments, sometimes tended to implicitly favor the Basran opinion through the order in which he presented the arguments or through his concluding comment on the question. This method reveals that the grammatical disagreement was not merely a random matter of taste, but a methodological dispute about the very principles of argument: should one analogize from the rare and scarce sayings of the Arabs, or confine oneself to the common and widespread usage? And should linguistic phenomena be explained by rational logical causes, or is it sufficient to record usage as it was transmitted?

## Its value as a source and its influence

This book is a primary source used by modern scholars in reconstructing the details of the disagreement between the Basran and Kufan schools, although it is a late and partly schematic account, and scholars such as Owens and Versteegh have questioned how far the two "schools" were fixed, coherent bodies. It preserved many arguments that might otherwise have been lost, and it bears witness to the dialectical character that has marked the heritage of Arabic grammar since its inception, in contrast to the sometimes simplified image of a homogeneous and harmonious grammatical tradition. The book remains a standard reference in the study of Arabic grammatical history. Its method of presenting arguments and replies parallels the *khilafiyyat* ("disputed questions") genre of Islamic jurisprudence, in which the opinions and evidence of different schools are set side by side before a preference is given. It is a clear illustration that the tools of argument (analogy, causes) were largely shared between the sciences of language and of law in that period.
