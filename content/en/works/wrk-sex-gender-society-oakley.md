# Sex, Gender and Society

An influential early work by Ann Oakley (1972), in which she set out for a wide readership the conceptual distinction between biological "sex" and socially acquired "gender," which became a foundation of the sociology of gender.

## Context

Oakley, the British sociologist, wrote this early work at the height of the second feminist wave in the early 1970s. She drew on the concept of "gender," which had been used a few years earlier in an entirely different medical-psychological context (the psychologist John Money in the 1950s and the psychiatrist Robert Stoller in *Sex and Gender*, 1968, in studies of intersex and gender-identity cases). But she was among the first to carry the term over and develop it systematically as a general sociological tool applicable to society as a whole and not only to exceptional medical cases.

## The Central Thesis: The Distinction between Sex and Gender

Oakley distinguished with decisive methodological clarity between "sex," the biological and physiological differences between males and females (chromosomes, genital organs, hormones, the capacity for pregnancy and childbirth), which are relatively fixed and uniform across cultures, and "gender," the social roles, traits and behavioral expectations associated with masculinity and femininity (such as the distribution of tasks, expected psychological traits like aggressiveness in men or gentleness in women, clothing, ways of speaking). The latter differ widely historically and culturally across societies despite the relative constancy of the biological differences. She argued that much of what is considered "natural" in the behavior of men and women (occupational roles, psychological traits, the division of housework) is in fact a social construct (gender) falsely justified by reference to biological differences (sex) that do not actually explain it sufficiently.

## Comparative Anthropological Evidence

Oakley drew on broad comparative anthropological evidence, especially Margaret Mead's *Sex and Temperament in Three Primitive Societies* (1935), which reported wide variation in the temperaments attributed to each sex among three New Guinea peoples. In Mead's account the Arapesh expected both sexes to be gentle and nurturing, the Mundugumor expected both to be aggressive, and among the Tchambuli the expectations of men and women were reversed relative to Western ones. Oakley used this to argue that gender roles vary widely across cultures, which undermines the claim that the gender roles prevailing in the West are the inevitable product of biology alone. Mead's findings have since been criticised on methodological grounds, so this evidence is best read as illustrative rather than conclusive.

## Impact

This distinction between sex and gender became a basic conceptual tool in feminist sociology and gender studies worldwide. It directly paved the way for Oakley's later analysis of housework in her book *The Sociology of Housework* (1974) as a socially gendered construct and not a "natural" feminine duty. It also deeply influenced later feminist theories (including Judith Butler's later critique of the fixity of the separation between sex and gender itself in the theory of gender performativity, which argued that even biological "sex" itself is partly a discursive construct and not a purely neutral biological given). The distinction itself became a standard element of teaching in the sociology of gender.

## Critique of the Prevailing Biologism

In this work Oakley criticised the biologically determinist accounts of sex roles then current, which explained the division of gender roles by supposedly innate male dominance and female nurturance. She considered that these explanations project contemporary Western cultural patterns onto human "nature," whereas comparative evidence shows that such patterns vary culturally and historically. Similar arguments were later revived in sociobiology (from the 1970s) and evolutionary psychology, and feminist critics have continued to draw on this line of critique.

## The Distinction as a Tool of Political Liberation

Oakley considered that the conceptual distinction between sex and gender is not merely a neutral academic analytical tool but a directly practical tool of political liberation. If women's traditional roles (such as confinement to housework) are the product of a "gender" social construct and not of a "sexual" biological determinism, then they are open to actual political and social change, unlike the case where these roles are biologically rooted in a fixed way that does not admit change. This made the distinction a central theoretical weapon in the hands of the feminist movement in confronting the arguments of opponents who used "biological nature" to justify the continuation of existing gender inequality.

## Oakley and the Feminist Method in Research

Later in her career Oakley developed a parallel methodological interest, most famously in her essay "Interviewing Women: A Contradiction in Terms" (1981), which criticised the conventional "neutral" interview style that imposes strict distance between researcher and respondent. In its place she advocated a more reciprocal feminist method that acknowledges the personal dimension of the research relationship (drawing on her research on motherhood and childbirth). She held that the claim of complete researcher neutrality sometimes conceals an implicit power relation between a researcher and the subject of research, which deserves explicit acknowledgment instead of methodological denial.
