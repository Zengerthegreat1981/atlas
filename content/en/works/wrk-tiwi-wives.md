# Tiwi Wives: A Study of the Women of Melville Island

*Tiwi Wives: A Study of the Women of Melville Island, North Australia* (University of Washington Press, 1971) is the ethnography by the American anthropologist Jane C. Goodale of the lives of Tiwi women. It complements the earlier general ethnography of the Tiwi by C. W. M. Hart and Arnold R. Pilling (*The Tiwi of North Australia*, 1960), which concentrated on men and on the marriage system. Goodale's fieldwork on Melville Island took place in the mid-1950s, when the Tiwi were already living under the influence of the Catholic mission and the Australian administration.

## Context

The Tiwi, an Aboriginal people of Melville and Bathurst Islands off the coast of northern Australia, were known to anthropology for their marriage system, in which girls were promised in marriage before birth or in infancy and older men could hold many wives (the system is described in the entry on Hart and Pilling's study). Because the earlier literature was written largely from the men's side, Goodale's book looks at the same system from the position of women, treating women as people with their own work, interests and relationships rather than as objects passed between men.

## Contribution

Goodale describes the life course of Tiwi women: betrothal, marriage, widowhood and remarriage, and their roles as wives, mothers and daughters. She gives particular attention to the economic role of women, whose foraging provided a large part of the daily food supply, and to women's ritual and social life, including participation in funeral (*pukamani*) ceremonies. Her account shows that women took part in the negotiation of marriages and other relationships, and that the formal rules of the marriage system left room for women's choices and manoeuvres. The book is often cited, together with other studies by women anthropologists of the 1950s and 1960s, as an early example of ethnography that treats women's activities as central.

## Reception and Critique

*Tiwi Wives* is read as a corrective to male-centred ethnography of Aboriginal Australia and is widely used in discussions of gender in Aboriginal societies. As with Hart and Pilling's study, critics point out that the "traditional" society reconstructed in the ethnography had already been shaped by colonial contact, mission influence and administration, so that descriptions combine remembered custom with changing practice.
