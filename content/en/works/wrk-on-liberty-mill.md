# On Liberty by John Stuart Mill

## The Central Thesis

The sole end that justifies society or the state in interfering coercively with the freedom of action of an individual, even for his own good, is **to prevent harm to others**, which has come to be known as the "Harm Principle". Mill says that the only power that can rightfully be exercised over any member of a civilized community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. He explicitly rejects **coercive paternalism**: an individual may not be compelled to do or forbidden from doing something on the ground that it is better for him, for his health, happiness and wisdom in his private affairs are his alone, and society may advise him, persuade him and avoid him, but not compel him.

Mill does not build this principle on the idea of "natural rights" but on utility in its widest sense, that is, "utility in the largest sense, grounded on the permanent interests of man as a progressive being". Liberty for him is a condition of the advancement of knowledge, the diversity of tastes and the flourishing of personality, and not an abstract end.

## Structure of the Work

The book consists of five chapters. The first is introductory, setting out the principle and showing that the modern danger to liberty has shifted from the despotic ruler to society itself, so that the **tyranny of the majority** appears, an expression Mill borrowed from Tocqueville: public opinion and custom impose stifling restraints on individuality in the name of public morality without any need for law. The second chapter concerns freedom of thought and discussion, in which he presents four arguments: the suppressed opinion may be true; it may contain a part of the truth; even if the prevailing opinion is wholly true, if it is not contested it becomes a dead dogma; and the very force of its meaning is thereby lost. He cites the execution of Socrates and the crucifixion of Christ as examples of the persecution of a truth by a society that thinks itself virtuous.

The third chapter concerns **individuality** as an element of well-being, and calls for varied "experiments in living", drawing on Wilhelm von Humboldt's idea of the free development of human powers, from whom comes the quotation with which he prefaced the book. The fourth chapter concerns the limits of the authority of society over the individual, where he distinguishes what concerns the individual alone from what affects others. The fifth chapter concerns applications: freedom of trade, the sale of poisons, intoxicants, marriage and compulsory education, and the question of the Mormons and polygamy.

Mill excepts from the principle children and minors, and also excepts the societies he calls "backward", and holds that enlightened despotism is a legitimate means of governing them so long as its end is their improvement, an exception that has remained the object of sharp criticism because Mill worked for the East India Company for thirty-five years (1823–1858).

## Historical Context of Publication

The book was published in 1859 in London, and Mill dedicated it to his wife **Harriet Taylor Mill**, who died in Avignon in November 1858, months before its publication, and he ascribed to her a major contribution to the formation of his ideas. He mentions in the *Autobiography* that the seed of the book was a short essay he wrote in 1854 and that the two decided to expand and revise it together. His own "mental crisis" of 1826, described in the *Autobiography*, had led him to value individuality, feeling, and the cultivation of the self, themes that run through the third chapter of the book. The book came in the context of anxiety over the Victorian society's tendency to moral control, and over the expansion of the franchise and the debate on the limits of the authority of public opinion in the mid-nineteenth century. Mill had by then ended his work at the Company after Parliament dissolved it following the 1857 revolt in India.

## Reception and Critique

The book became the founding reference of the liberal current in modern political thought, and has remained the source of citation in debates on freedom of expression. Among its earliest critics was James Fitzjames Stephen in *Liberty, Equality, Fraternity* (1873), who held that moral coercion is necessary for the cohesion of society, and the debate was renewed in the twentieth century between Lord Devlin in *The Enforcement of Morals* (1965) and H. L. A. Hart in *Law, Liberty, and Morality* (1963). Isaiah Berlin in "Two Concepts of Liberty" said that Mill is the best representative of the tendency of negative liberty, while John Gray in *Mill on Liberty: A Defence* (1983) made the principle spring from an indirect utilitarian theory, and Gertrude Himmelfarb read the book in a conservative reading that separates the early from the later Mill.

An objection recurs in the criticism to the vagueness of the dividing line between what concerns the individual and what harms others, since hardly any personal act is free of indirect effect on others, and it is observed that Mill treats the balance between liberty and utility without a strict rule.

## The Arab Reader

The thinkers of the Nahda and of Arab liberalism received the book as a reference on intellectual and political freedom. Albert Hourani in *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age* (1962) treats the influence of the English utilitarians, among them Mill, on Ahmad Lutfi al-Sayyid, who called for individual freedom as a means of reforming Egyptian society.
