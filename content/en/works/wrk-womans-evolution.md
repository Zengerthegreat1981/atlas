# Woman's Evolution: From Matriarchal Clan to Patriarchal Family

*Woman's Evolution: From Matriarchal Clan to Patriarchal Family* (Pathfinder Press, 1975) is a book by the American Marxist feminist writer Evelyn Reed (1905-1979), a member of the Socialist Workers Party. It argues that early human society was organized around the matriarchal clan, and that the later patriarchal family and the subordination of women were products of the rise of private property and class society. The book is a work of political and theoretical argument rather than fieldwork, and it holds an unusual place in the literature: it was influential in socialist and feminist circles but has not been accepted by most professional anthropologists.

## The Argument

Reed built on the nineteenth-century evolutionary tradition of Lewis Henry Morgan (*Ancient Society*, 1877), J. J. Bachofen (*Das Mutterrecht*, 1861) and Friedrich Engels (*The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State*, 1884), together with Robert Briffault's *The Mothers* (1927). Like Engels, she held that group or clan organization through the mother preceded the patriarchal family, and that women's historic defeat was linked to the emergence of private property. She also argued against biological explanations of women's status and in favour of a social and historical one, a position she defended in earlier essays such as *Problems of Women's Liberation* (1969) and *Is Biology Woman's Destiny?* (1971).

Reed gave particular weight to women's role in the early development of culture, including food production and the care of children, and to the importance of kinship organized through women. She drew on the ethnographic literature of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, including work on totemism and taboo, to reinterpret these institutions in terms of the clan.

## Reception and Critique

The book had an audience among feminists and socialists in the 1970s, a period when many were interested in the history of women's status. In academic anthropology its central thesis, that there was a stage of universal matriarchal clan society, has been widely rejected. Critics note that the evolutionary schemes of the nineteenth century on which it depends have been abandoned, that cross-cultural evidence does not show a universal matriarchal stage, and that matrilineal descent should not be confused with female rule. The anthropologist Eleanor Leacock, who also worked in the Marxist tradition of Engels, took a different route: in *Myths of Male Dominance* (1981) she argued from ethnographic and historical evidence, especially on the Montagnais-Naskapi, that women's status in many egalitarian societies was relatively high and was undermined by colonialism and class formation, without claiming a universal matriarchy.
