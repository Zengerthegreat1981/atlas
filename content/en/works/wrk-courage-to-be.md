# The Courage to Be

*The Courage to Be* (1952), by the German-American theologian Paul Tillich, offers an influential classification of the anxieties that later existential psychology took up, though it has been less used in the clinic than later schemes. The book grew out of the Terry Lectures Tillich delivered at Yale University in 1950 and was published by Yale University Press in 1952. Its thesis is that courage is an ontological act: the self-affirmation of being in the face of the threat of nonbeing. Courage is not the absence of anxiety but action in spite of it — accepting oneself although one is unacceptable, and acting although one is uncertain.

## Three Kinds of Anxiety

Tillich refuses to confine existential anxiety to the fear of death. The true object of anxiety, he argues, is the permanent threat of nonbeing, and that threat takes three distinct forms, each dominant in a different historical age. The first is the anxiety of fate and death: the threat to being itself, to one's existence and its continuance, which dominated the ancient world. The second is the anxiety of emptiness and meaninglessness: the threat to spiritual meaning, the loss of any ultimate concern that makes life worth living. The third is the anxiety of guilt and condemnation: the threat to moral self-affirmation, the sense of having failed what one ought to be. Each form of anxiety has a corresponding courage that meets it. Tillich's historical claim is that the modern age is dominated by the second anxiety — meaninglessness, not death — and this shifts the centre of gravity away from where Irvin Yalom later placed it with his four ultimate concerns. The book also draws a distinction that became foundational for existential psychology: existential anxiety, which belongs to the structure of being human, must be separated from neurotic anxiety, which is a pathological avoidance of it, and from fear, which always has a definite object.

## The Structure of the Book

The book is short and tightly built, in six chapters: "Being and Courage", "Being, Nonbeing, and Anxiety", "Pathological Anxiety, Vitality, and Courage", "Courage and Participation", "Courage and Individualisation", and "Courage and Transcendence". Tillich begins with a history of courage from Plato's *Laches* through Thomas Aquinas and Spinoza, concluding that courage is not one virtue among others but "self-affirmation in the face of nonbeing". He then distinguishes two principal forms of courage. The "courage to be as a part" finds security in dissolving into the group, and appears in modern collectivism and totalitarian movements; the "courage to be as oneself" is embodied in existentialism from Kierkegaard and Nietzsche to Sartre. Each, taken alone, becomes dangerous: the first loses the self, the second loses the world and falls into emptiness. The final chapter proposes what Tillich calls absolute faith: the courage to accept that one is accepted although unacceptable, an idea he takes from Luther's theology of justification by faith and had preached in his sermon "You Are Accepted", published in *The Shaking of the Foundations* (1948).

## The God above God

The book's most controversial move comes in its closing pages. Tillich argues that the courage to be draws its ultimate root from what he calls the "God above God" — a phrase that belongs to this book in particular. When the traditional image of a personal God collapses under doubt and meaninglessness, faith can still hold to the "Ground of Being" that remains even when God in the familiar sense is absent. This is not atheism, but it is a deliberate step beyond theism as usually understood, and it is the point at which Tillich's theology most unsettled his critics. His correlational method is on display throughout: the question comes from philosophy and the answer from revelation, but the answer is never offered as a proof of the question. The analysis of anxiety stands on its own; the theological conclusion is a response to it, not a deduction from it.

## Influence on Psychotherapy

Tillich's influence on existential psychotherapy was direct and personal. Rollo May knew Tillich in New York and drew on Tillich's distinction between existential and neurotic anxiety in *The Meaning of Anxiety* (1950) and *Man's Search for Himself* (1953). Tillich's account of neurotic anxiety as the self's flight from existential anxiety — narrowing its horizon to gain a false security — became a working idea for existential therapists, and Yalom later made it one of the starting points of *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980). Tillich's threefold classification of anxiety is less often used than Yalom's four concerns, although it predates them, and the book itself offers no therapeutic technique. It is a philosophical and theological analysis, and its value for the clinician lies in the precision of its distinctions rather than in any procedure it prescribes.

## Criticism and Limits

Conservative theologians attacked Tillich for dissolving the personal God into "being itself", a move some read as close to atheism. Philosophers criticised the vagueness of turning nonbeing into an ontological category that functions as a general explanatory term. The book's engagement with Freud is superficial, and its threefold classification is presented in a reflective rather than empirical style that admits no systematic testing. In the Arab world Tillich is little known compared with Kierkegaard and Sartre. Its lasting place is as an early systematic treatment of the anxieties that existential therapy later drew on — and as a reminder that the classification most therapists now use is not the only one.
