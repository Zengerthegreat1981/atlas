# The Consolation of Philosophy (De Consolatione Philosophiae)

The last thing Boethius wrote, **in prison around 523–524 CE, condemned for treason under the Gothic king Theodoric and awaiting an execution that was in fact carried out**. The text is a dialogue between the prisoner and **Lady Philosophy** (Philosophia), who appears to him in his cell and treats his despair.

## The Central Thesis

That the misery felt by the condemned man does not result from his loss of something he truly possessed, but from **an error in judging what is good**: wealth, rank, power, fame, and pleasure are all "false goods" bare of stability, and philosophy had been in his hand and then slipped away when his fortunes turned. The remedy is therefore not to change the facts (the sentence stands) but **to reframe the good**: the highest good is one, not many, and it is God, and happiness (beatitudo) is participation in him, which no external force can take from you. Hence the **wheel of Fortune** (rota Fortunae): Fortune is not a treacherous enemy but is by nature revolving, and whoever complains of its turning has **misunderstood its condition**, not suffered an injustice.

## The Structure of the Work: Prosimetrum

**The structure is part of the argument, not an ornament.** The text is a **prosimetrum**: prose and verse in alternation, five books, interspersed with about thirty-nine poems in various meters. The prose carries the dialectical argument, and the verse produces what Lady Philosophy herself calls **the milder medicine** that prepares the patient before the stronger medicine. That is, the book **treats in two stages, affective and then argumentative**, and moves from healing by consolation to healing by logic:

- **Book 1**: the diagnosis: the patient's forgetting of his identity, and the presentation of his complaint.
- **Book 2**: the nature of Fortune and her wheel, and the falseness of external goods.
- **Book 3**: the highest good and the simplicity of happiness, and the reduction of partial goods to one.
- **Book 4**: the problem of evil and punishment, and evil as privation and impotence, not a power.
- **Book 5**: providence, divine foreknowledge, and freedom, and its resolution by holding that God is **outside time** and sees everything in a single eternal present, so that his knowledge does not compel action.

## The History and Transmission of the Text

The book **was not published by its author**, who died shortly after, so there is no sense in speaking of a "first edition": its history is the history of **manuscripts** that spread enormously in the Western Middle Ages until it became one of the most copied, commented on, and taught secular texts. The reference today is the **critical edition**: the text of **Ludwig Bieler** in the *Corpus Christianorum Series Latina* volume 94 (1957), and the text of **Claudio Moreschini** in the Teubner edition (2000).

## Reception and Translation

- An Old English translation traditionally attributed to **Alfred the Great** in the late ninth century; the attribution is a matter of modern dispute.
- **Geoffrey Chaucer** rendered it into Middle English as *Boece* in the late fourteenth century.
- **Queen Elizabeth I** translated it in 1593.
- In philosophy: his formulation of eternity as total presence rather than temporal extension became the reference that scholastic theology discussed on the question of divine knowledge and freedom.

## The Famous Limit

Boethius **does not mention Christ or any explicit scriptural text in the book**, although his theological tractates (*Opuscula Sacra*) are unmistakably Christian in doctrine. This generated an old debate about its place: is it a purely Platonic philosophical text in the language of a believer, or a Christian book that chose consolation by reason alone? The question **has not been settled**.

## The Opening Scene and the Image of Lady Philosophy

The book begins with an imprisoned poet writing elegiac verses when a majestic woman appears to him whose stature exceeds the human, and on her garment is the Greek letter Π at its bottom and Θ at its top with a ladder between them, an allusion to practical (praktike) and theoretical (theoretike) philosophy. She drives from around his bed the "Muses of poetry" who feed his illness instead of curing it, and says that his illness is forgetfulness of his truth, not merely grief over what he lost. The book made of this scene a basis for the conception of healing by dialogue: the medicine is not given until an examination of the patient has been made, and the argument is held back until he becomes ready to receive it.

## Two Definitions Inherited by the Middle Ages

The book is famous for two definitions that became part of the vocabulary of scholastic philosophy. The first is the definition of eternity as the complete, simultaneous possession of endless life, in Book Five, which Thomas Aquinas adopted in analyzing God's relation to time. The second is the definition of the person that appears in Boethius's theological tractate against Eutyches and Nestorius, "an individual substance of a rational nature," which is the origin of the common scholastic definition of the person. The question of foreknowledge and freedom in Book Five holds a central place: Boethius holds that God does not know events "before" they occur but encompasses them in an eternal present, and hence his knowledge does not impose necessity on events, just as my seeing a man walking does not force him to walk.

## Literary and Intellectual Influence

No other text of late antiquity left a mark of such depth on medieval literature. Dante placed Boethius in the sphere of the Sun in the "Paradiso" (Canto X), Chaucer built parts of "Troilus and Criseyde" and "The Knight's Tale" on the "Consolation," and monasteries circulated Latin commentaries on it from the Carolingian period. Because the book mixes prose with verse in a pattern known as Menippean satire, it became a model for later works combining seriousness and mockery. In Arabic literature it is not established that the text was translated into Arabic in the Middle Ages, but its subject is close to the Arabic tradition of philosophical consolation: "The Device for Dispelling Sorrows" by al-Kindi, "The Spiritual Physic" by al-Razi, and "Refinement of Character" by Miskawayh, all of which treat grief through rational management and contemplation of the vicissitudes of fortune. This is a comparison between distant texts, with no evidence of direct transmission.

## Limits of the Book and Its Contested Readings

Some scholars criticize the book's reliance on Platonic and Stoic premises without reference to an explicit Christian authority, while others see its silence about Christ as a deliberate style for addressing a philosophical reader. It is observed that its image of "Fortune" may seem a prescription for resignation, while others regard it as a preliminary condition for inner freedom. As for its argument that evil is nothing, it meets the usual objection: how can the prisoner's pain not be real? Some scholars hold that the book is closer to a deliberate philosophical exercise in consolation than to an autobiography.
