# Al-Hidaya Sharh Bidayat al-Mubtadi

A central reference work of the Hanafi school by the jurist Burhan al-Din al-Marghinani (d. 593 AH / 1197 CE). It is one of the most widely circulated, commented upon and taught Hanafi legal books, and its influence extended from Transoxiana to the Indian subcontinent and the Ottoman state.

## The Origin of the Book: From Text to Commentary

According to the tradition, Marghinani first composed a concise text entitled *Bidayat al-Mubtadi*, which combined two earlier Hanafi texts, *Mukhtasar al-Quduri* and *al-Jami al-Saghir* of Muhammad ibn al-Hasan al-Shaybani. He then commented on this text himself, first in a very long commentary entitled *Kifayat al-Muntaha*, and then in the much more concise *Al-Hidaya*, which clarifies its wording and proofs without excessive length. This twofold process (composing the text and then commenting on it himself) reflects Marghinani's methodological awareness of the need for an integrated instructional text that combines the brevity of memorization with clarity of understanding at the same time.

## Structure and Method

*Al-Hidaya* covers all the chapters of Hanafi jurisprudence, from acts of worship to the hudud and jinayat. It adopts a restrained style of presentation that states the ruling adopted in the school with a brief reference to its proof. It occasionally touches on the disagreement of the three Hanafi imams where this has practical importance, without entering the lengthy usuli discussions found in al-Sarakhsi's *Al-Mabsut*. This precise balance between concision and clarity is what made the book an ideal teaching text for beginning and intermediate students alike.

## The Succession of Commentaries

*Al-Hidaya* produced a rich commentary tradition that lasted for centuries. Its most prominent links include *Fath al-Qadir* by Kamal al-Din Ibn al-Humam, which added deep hadith and usuli scholarship; *Al-Inaya Sharh al-Hidaya* by Akmal al-Din al-Babarti; and *Al-Binaya Sharh al-Hidaya* by Badr al-Din al-Aini, which added extensive comparisons with the other schools. This cumulative commentary tradition makes *Al-Hidaya* the nucleus of a complete jurisprudential library that took shape around it across generations, similar to what happened with other central legal texts in different Islamic schools.

## Its Transfer to the British Colonial Context

One notable chapter in the book's history is its transfer to a legal context entirely different from that of its original composition. The British orientalist Charles Hamilton published an English version of *Al-Hidaya* in 1791 under the title *The Hedaya*, made from a Persian translation of the Arabic text commissioned under Warren Hastings, for use by the judges of the British East India Company in applying what became known as "Anglo-Muhammadan law" in the courts of British India. This transfer represents an early and striking case of turning a classical legal text into "applied law" in a colonial bureaucratic setting radically different from the original context of jurisprudential teaching for which it was written.

## Its Contemporary Standing

*Al-Hidaya* is still taught today in the religious institutes and universities that follow the Hanafi school, especially in South Asia, Turkey and Central Asia. It is a standard text in the later stages of traditional Hanafi study. It also remains a basic academic reference in orientalist and historical studies of the development of Hanafi jurisprudence and its transmission across different geographical and political contexts.

## Its Place Among Other Hanafi Texts

*Al-Hidaya* occupies a distinctive position among the many Hanafi texts composed over the centuries, from *Mukhtasar al-Quduri* to later texts such as al-Nasafi's *Al-Kanz* and Ibrahim al-Halabi's *Al-Multaqa* in the Ottoman period. Scholars distinguish between the functions of these texts. Some were formulated for pure memorization without argument, while others combined brevity with sufficient argument, as Marghinani did. This helps explain why *Al-Hidaya* remained widely used despite the existence of other texts that are more concise or more recent. The book strikes a skilful balance between the beginner's need for brevity and the need to understand the basis of a ruling rather than merely memorize it.

## Its Importance for Nineteenth-Century Orientalist Studies

Beyond Hamilton's translation, *Al-Hidaya* was a principal source for a number of European orientalists who studied Islamic jurisprudence in the nineteenth century, treating it as a representative model of the logic of "Islamic law" as they then understood it. These early orientalist readings were later subjected to academic criticism for reducing the internal complexity of Hanafi jurisprudence to a single text, detached from the rich commentary and interpretive context that surrounded it in the centuries after its composition.

## Its Use in Contemporary Teaching in South Asia and Central Asia

*Al-Hidaya* remains present in the contemporary curricula of Hanafi jurisprudence in Pakistan, India, Bangladesh, Afghanistan and Central Asia. It is a standard text in the traditional seminary curriculum (the Dars-i Nizami tradition in South Asia). Completing its study marks a student's arrival at an intermediate-to-advanced stage of jurisprudential formation before moving on to deeper commentaries such as *Fath al-Qadir*. This confirms its continued living presence in traditional religious education, more than eight centuries after its composition.

## Date of Composition

The traditional biographical and bibliographical literature reports that Marghinani began *Al-Hidaya* in Dhu al-Qa'da 573 AH (1178 CE) and worked on it for about thirteen years. This report is repeated, among others, by the Ottoman bibliographer Katib Chelebi (Hajji Khalifa) in *Kashf al-Zunun* and the thirteen years of work are also mentioned by the Indian Hanafi scholar Abd al-Hayy al-Laknawi in his introduction to the *Hidaya*. Counting thirteen years from the starting date places completion at about 586 AH, around 1190 CE, several years before the author's death in 593 AH (1197 CE). The completion year is therefore a calculation from the traditional report, and it should be treated as approximate. The same literature adds pious accounts of the author's habits during these years, including continuous fasting, which belong to the reception of the book and its reputation more than to its datable history. The long period of work fits the sequence described above, in which Marghinani first wrote the base text and an extended commentary and then produced the more concise commentary that became the standard one.
