# Genealogies of Religion: Discipline and Reasons of Power in Christianity and Islam

## Definition

Talal Asad's major work, published in 1993 by Johns Hopkins University Press under the title *Genealogies of Religion: Discipline and Reasons of Power in Christianity and Islam*. In it Asad extends his critique of anthropological analytical concepts to the concept of "religion" itself. Asad's earlier research, which included fieldwork among the Kababish Arabs of northern Sudan, concerned power and authority in a nomadic society.

## Historical Context

The book appeared in a context of broader anthropological questioning (of which Edward Said's *Orientalism*, 1978, is a prominent example) of how modern Western analytical concepts (religion, culture, society) were built as neutral universal categories, while in fact they are products of a specific European history that is later projected onto other societies without scrutiny. Asad drew on Michel Foucault's genealogical method as a tool for dismantling the history of these concepts.

## Main Ideas

Asad argues that the concept of "religion" as a universal category, unified and comparable across all cultures, is not a neutral category. It is the product of a specific European Christian, post-Reformation history, shaped through modern processes of separating the "religious," the "secular," and the "political." These processes were not necessarily present in the same way in other religious traditions, such as Islam. The classical comparative anthropology of religion, from Edward Tylor to Clifford Geertz himself, presupposes this category. Asad criticizes Geertz's definition of religion as a system of symbols, set out in "Religion as a Cultural System" (1966).

Asad uses tools from Foucault (power, discourse, and bodily discipline) to analyze how particular religious practices (such as medieval Christian monastic discipline and ritual, and the limits of religious criticism in Muslim societies) are produced within specific historical contexts of power relations, bodily discipline, and institutional discipline, rather than as expressions of a fixed religious "essence" outside history. His earlier essay "The Idea of an Anthropology of Islam" (1986) similarly treats Islam as a discursive tradition, in which orthodoxy is a relationship of power rather than a fixed standard defined in advance.

## Key Works and Representatives

The book is part of Asad's broader project, which he continued in *Formations of the Secular* (2003). His work on discipline and embodied virtue influenced Saba Mahmood, whose *Politics of Piety* (2005) studies the women's mosque movement in Egypt. In the book Asad also directly engages Clifford Geertz, critiquing his definition in "Religion as a Cultural System."

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The book's discussions of Islam are mainly in its later chapters, which consider the limits of religious criticism in the Middle East and the controversy over Salman Rushdie's *The Satanic Verses*, as well as the relation between Islamic traditions and European secular categories. The related argument that colonial and modernist reforms transformed Islamic law and ethics, so that the change was more than administrative, is developed in *Formations of the Secular* (2003).

## Impact and Criticism

The book deeply influenced critical anthropology of religion and academic Islamic studies. It pushed the field to question basic analytical categories (religion, secularism, modernity) rather than accepting their universality. It received criticism from researchers who felt that its Foucauldian genealogical focus sometimes makes it difficult to speak of genuine doctrinal continuity within religious traditions. Other critics saw its approach as an essential tool for freeing the study of Islam from essentialist Orientalist frameworks.

## A Further Detail on Christian Monasticism

Asad analyses medieval Christian monastic discipline, including the Benedictine Rule, in his chapters on ritual, discipline and humility. He shows how religious virtue (such as obedience and humility) was built through daily disciplined bodily and behavioral training (repeated prayer rituals, rules of silence), rather than acquired merely by embracing an inner belief. Later work, notably Saba Mahmood's study of piety in Egypt, has extended this attention to embodied discipline in Islamic settings.

## Critique of Geertz's Definition

In his opening chapter Asad critiques Clifford Geertz's definition of religion as a system of symbols. He argues that this definition implicitly assumes a modern, privatized Christian understanding of religion (centred on belief and inner meaning), which does not necessarily apply to other religious traditions in which religion is connected more directly with bodily, legal, and authoritative practices (such as Sharia) than with a private inner belief. The critique has been widely influential in the anthropology of religion.

## Asad's Personal Background

Asad's method was influenced by his unique personal path. He was born in 1932 in Medina to an Austrian-born father of Jewish background who converted to Islam (Muhammad Asad, translator of the Qur'an and author of *The Road to Mecca*), and he grew up in India and Pakistan and was educated in Britain. This biography is often mentioned in discussions of his work, although his arguments stand on their own scholarly grounds.
