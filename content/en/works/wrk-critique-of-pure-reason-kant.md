# Critique of Pure Reason by Immanuel Kant

The book that opens the **critical philosophy**, published in Riga in **1781** (edition A) and then in a revised second edition in **1787** (edition B). Its central question is one: **how are synthetic a priori judgments possible?** That is, how can reason know something necessary and universal about the world that experience alone does not give — as in mathematics and in the principles of natural science.

## The Central Thesis

Kant's answer is the **Copernican revolution**: knowledge does not revolve around things, but things — **as appearances** — revolve around the conditions of our knowledge. Space and time are a priori forms of intuition, not properties of things in themselves, and the categories are a priori forms of the understanding by which sensory data are organized. From this come the **limits** of reason: the understanding knows only the **appearance** (Erscheinung), while the **thing in itself** (Ding an sich) is thought but not known. If reason tries to go beyond experience to God, the soul and the world as objects of knowledge, it falls into a **transcendental illusion**, not an incidental error. The result is that **metaphysics is not a theoretical science** — and that this very restriction is what opens room for the practical use of reason.

## Structure of the Work

Two great divisions: the **Transcendental Doctrine of Elements** and then the **Transcendental Doctrine of Method**. The detail of the first is the well-known structure of the book:

1. **The Transcendental Aesthetic** — space and time as the two forms of sensibility.
2. **The Transcendental Analytic** — the table of **categories**; then the **transcendental deduction** of the categories (proof of their right to apply to all possible experience); then the **System of Principles** (schematism and principles), which includes the distinction between phenomena and noumena.
3. **The Transcendental Dialectic** — the dismantling of three metaphysical systems: the **paralogisms of rational psychology** concerning the soul, the four **antinomies** concerning the world (finite/infinite, simple/composite, freedom/determinism, necessary being/none), and the **ideals of rational theology**, where the proofs for the existence of God are dismantled.

## Historical Context of Publication

The two editions are **not a revision but a substantive difference**, which is why academic custom has settled on **double A/B pagination** in every citation. The most prominent differences: the **complete rewriting of the transcendental deduction** in B, and the **change in the place of the "Refutation of Idealism"** (Widerlegung des Idealismus), which was inserted in B in reply to the charge that Kant's critique is an idealism in the manner of Berkeley — the charge launched by the famous Garve/Feder review in 1782. Between them Kant wrote *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics* (1783), a simplified presentation of the same thesis.

## Reception and Critique

- **German Idealism**: the "thing in itself" became the first object of objection — Jacobi said that one cannot enter the system without it and cannot remain in it with it, and from this objection Fichte, Schelling and Hegel set out to go beyond it.
- **Logical positivism**: rejected the category of the "synthetic a priori" altogether, and reduced necessity to analyticity and convention.
- **Non-Euclidean geometries and relativity**: weakened the claim that the space of a priori intuition is necessarily Euclidean.
- **The twentieth century**: analytic readings restored value to the deduction as an argument about the conditions of the possibility of experience (Strawson in *The Bounds of Sense*, 1966), and Kantianism became a ground for contemporary philosophies of mind and perception.

## Deduction and Schematism: The Argument for Possible Knowledge

The hardest part of the book is concentrated in the "transcendental deduction," in which Kant asks about the right of the categories to apply to objects, that is, why appearances conform to concepts we did not derive from experience. His argument in edition B is that every representation that can become known to me must be accompanied by the "I think," and that the unity of this self-consciousness is achieved only by the synthesis of the manifold in an object, and the synthesis proceeds according to categories. Hence the categories are the conditions of the unity of consciousness itself, so if experience is possible for a single consciousness it is bound by them. Then comes "schematism," the temporal intermediary that allows the application of the pure concept to sensible intuition: the category of causality, for example, is conceived in time as a necessary succession. The "three analogies of experience" include a detailing of the principles of the permanence of substance, temporal succession according to the law of causality, and simultaneity in reciprocity, and include an answer to Hume's doubt about causal necessity by transforming it from a psychological habit into a condition for the possibility of objective experience.

## The Dialectic: The Antinomies

Kant holds that reason, when it seeks the absolute, falls into contradiction with itself within four antinomies in each of which both the proposition and its contradictory can be proved. The first concerns the beginning of the world and its finitude in time and space, the second the simplicity of substances and divisibility, the third causality by freedom versus natural determinism, and the fourth the existence of a necessary being. Kant resolves the first two by saying that the world of appearance is not a given complete totality, and resolves the last two by the possibility that both propositions are true if one is ascribed to appearance and the other to the thing in itself. These solutions prepare the ground for the domain of practical reason, freeing the concept of freedom from theoretical contradiction without proving its existence. In the critique of the proofs for the existence of God, Kant rejects the ontological argument by saying that existence is not a real predicate, and holds that the cosmological and teleological arguments depend on the ontological argument implicitly.

## Method and the Appendix on Method

The last division adopts a classification of reason between disciplined use and regulative use: the three ideas, soul, world and God, give no knowledge, but work as regulative principles that direct inquiry toward a systematic unity in knowledge, so that we suppose as if nature were designed with a purpose in order to search for the coherence of its laws. In the chapter "The Architectonic of Pure Reason" Kant explains the idea of the system, and in "The History of Pure Reason" its position relative to dogmatism, empiricism and skepticism. The book is characterized by dense technical language and newly coined terms, and for this reason it did not meet with quick acceptance, which led him to re-express his ideas in the "Prolegomena" (1783).

## Limits of the Argument

The deduction was criticized as presupposing what it wants to prove, and as not explaining the transition from the unity of consciousness to the application of particular categories. Some commentators held that the "thing in itself" is used to explain the affection of sensibility although causality is a category that does not apply to it, the objection that Jacobi raised long ago. Other discussions revolve around how valid the theory of space and time remains after non-Euclidean geometries, and around the interpretation of schematism as an act of the productive imagination, on which Heidegger relied in his reading of Kant. Contemporary answers differ between those who read Kant epistemically and those who read him metaphysically.
