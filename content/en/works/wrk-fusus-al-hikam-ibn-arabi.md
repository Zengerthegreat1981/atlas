# The Bezels of Wisdom (Fusus al-Hikam) by Muhyi al-Din Ibn Arabi

A brief, extremely condensed text, one of the most famous and most controversial works of theoretical Sufism. It was written by **Muhyi al-Din Ibn Arabi** (560–638 AH / 1165–1240 CE) in Damascus late in his life, after he had composed much of the "Meccan Revelations", and among his students it is regarded as the quintessence of his doctrine.

## The Central Thesis

That the One Real discloses itself in all the ranks of being, and that each prophet is a manifestation of one particular divine name, so his "wisdom" is the face of the Real that was disclosed in him and not in others. Hence the prophethoods are not a repetition of one content but a variation on a self-disclosure that never repeats, and therefore each prophetic "word" bears its own stamp for him: the divine for Adam, the breath-like for Seth, the glorifying for Noah, the holy for Idris, the true for Isaac, the sublime for Ishmael, the luminous for Joseph, and so on to the singular for Muhammad. Two related themes branch from this: **imagination** as an intermediate world between meaning and sense and not a mere subjective illusion, and the **perfect human** as the mirror by which the Real knows Himself and by which the world appears, as the eye sees itself in the mirror. Beneath all this lies a conception of both transcendence and immanence: whoever restricts the Real to transcendence alone or to immanence alone is ignorant, and the gnostic combines the two aspects (as in the bezel of Noah).

## The Structure of the Work

Twenty-seven chapters (bezels), each with a prophet and a wisdom attributed to him: it opens with the bezel of Adam ("the divine wisdom") and closes with the bezel of Muhammad ("the singular wisdom"). Some chapters are devoted to prophets seldom mentioned in the books of the theologians, such as Khalid ibn Sinan, Luqman, and Uzayr. The title itself rests on a metaphor: the bezel is the stone of the ring, and the prophet is a ring in which a meaning is engraved. The chapters are very short, and their language is allusive and implicit, which has made the book **practically unreadable except with a commentary** since the seventh century AH. In the introduction the author sets out the question of the seal of the saints who draws from the niche of the seal of the prophets, a question that later led to great controversy.

## The History of the Text and Its Transmission

In the introduction to the book Ibn Arabi states that he received it in a **vision in Damascus in 627 AH / 1229 CE** from the Prophet by handing over, and that he did nothing but convey it. This is a formula of reception, not of authorship, and it has itself become a subject of later discussion.

The text was studied and transmitted through a chain of commentaries and not through mere copies:
- **Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi** (d. 673 AH), Ibn Arabi's stepson and closest student, who is the key to the scholastic reading of the book.
- **Mu'ayyad al-Din al-Jandi**, author of the first full, expansive commentary.
- **Abd al-Razzaq al-Kashani** (d. about 730 AH) and **Dawud al-Qaysari** (d. 751 AH): al-Qaysari's commentary is the approved study text in the seminaries and Sufi schools after him, and its introduction contains an expansive grounding of the terminology.
- Among the commentaries of the Ottomans and later scholars are the commentary of Abd al-Ghani al-Nabulusi (d. 1143 AH) and the Persian commentary of Abd al-Rahman Jami, *Naqd al-Nusus fi Sharh Naqsh al-Fusus*.

As for modern printing, the best-known approved edition is the edition of **Abu al-Ala Afifi** (Cairo, 1946) in two volumes: the text and then the notes.

## Reception and Criticism

The book is one of the most divisive texts of the Islamic heritage. On one side the whole **Akbarian School** was founded on it, east and west, and it entered teaching curricula in Iran, Anatolia, and India. On the other side it was **the focus of a continuous theological and juristic attack**, the most famous being Ibn Taymiyya's attack on the claim of unity of being, and al-Biqa'i's book *Tanbih al-Ghabi ila Takfir Ibn Arabi*, and the dispute continued for centuries among those who declared him an unbeliever, those who interpreted him, and those who defended him. The most disputed passages are his statement on the faith of Pharaoh in the bezel of Moses, on the worshippers of the calf in the bezel of Aaron, and on the "seal of the saints".

Besides the disagreement over content, a disagreement was raised over **authenticity of attribution**: is the book as a whole by Ibn Arabi, or were additions introduced into it? Some defenders of the Shaykh went so far as to deny the attribution of particular passages to him in order to soften the sharpness of the objection. The two questions, attribution and theological judgment, remain matters of scholarly dispute. It should be noted that the phrase "unity of being" does not appear literally in the *Fusus*, but is a later designation that came onto the tongues of the school's students and its opponents, and for that reason some contemporary scholars, such as William Chittick, prefer to describe his doctrine as the self-disclosure of the One Being in countless forms.

## Modern Readings

In modern scholarship Abu al-Ala Afifi produced the edition of the text and the study "The Mystical Philosophy of Muhyid Din-Ibnul Arabi" in English (1939), and Henry Corbin wrote his reading of the creative imagination in the Sufism of Ibn Arabi (1958), making imagination the key to the doctrine. Titus Burckhardt translated the *Fusus* into French (1955) and R. W. J. Austin translated it into English under the title "The Bezels of Wisdom" (1980). In Arabic, Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd presented "The Philosophy of Interpretation: A Study of Quranic Interpretation in Muhyi al-Din Ibn Arabi" (1983), reading Ibn Arabi as the holder of a theory of interpretation.

## Language and Editions

The work was composed in Arabic, so Arabic readers read the original text directly. For decades, the standard reference edition was that of Abu al-Ala Afifi (Cairo, 1946), based on several Cairo and Istanbul manuscripts. Later scholarship has noted that Afifi did not use some of the earliest manuscripts, notably copies associated with Sadr al-Din al-Qunawi, and later editions have collated these earlier witnesses. The work is traditionally read alongside its classical commentaries.

## Influence on Psychology and Contemporary Thought

Three of his concepts fall directly on the ground of psychology, though in a language not its own: the **self** (nafs) and its ranks, **imagination** as an image-forming faculty with its own objectivity, and the **perfect human** as a model of human completeness. These concepts are often invoked in contemporary Arabic discourse on "Islamic psychology", and a return to the text is what allows one to know what is in it and what is not.
