# African Political Systems

*African Political Systems* (1940) is a volume edited by Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard and published by Oxford University Press for the International Institute of African Languages and Cultures. With a preface by A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, it became a foundation of British political anthropology.

## Contents

The editors' introduction is followed by eight studies. Max Gluckman wrote on the Zulu kingdom, I. Schapera on the Ngwato of Bechuanaland, K. Oberg on the kingdom of Ankole, Audrey Richards on the Bemba, S. F. Nadel on the Kede of Nupe, Günter Wagner on the Bantu of Kavirondo, Fortes on the Tallensi, and Evans-Pritchard on the Nuer. Most authors had done field research in the societies they described.

## The classification

The introduction distinguishes two principal types among the cases. In one, there is centralised authority, an administrative apparatus, and judicial institutions, and differences of wealth, privilege, and status correspond to the distribution of power. The Zulu, Ngwato, Bemba, Banyankole, and Kede represent this group. In the other, these features are absent; the examples are the Logoli of Kavirondo, the Tallensi, and the Nuer. There, political relations are organised through segmentary lineage structures in which groups of like order balance one another. The editors also mention very small societies in which political and kinship relations largely coincide.

## Arguments

The central lesson is that the absence of a state does not mean the absence of political order. Territory, lineage, ritual office, and the regulation of feud may all perform political functions. The introduction connects the stability of authority with checks, ritual obligations, and the symbolic values attached to land, ancestors, and kingship. Radcliffe-Brown's preface defines political organisation in terms of the maintenance of order within a territorial framework through the organised use, or possibility of use, of force.

## Colonial setting

The societies were under European administration when studied, and several contributors expected their work to be useful to officials. The editors nevertheless declared that political philosophy had not helped them understand the societies and set out a comparative, scientific aim. The chapters differ in how much they discuss colonial change; Gluckman, for example, treats the position of Zulu authorities under white rule. Critics later argued that the volume reconstructed systems as if conquest and the colonial state stood outside them.

## Criticism and influence

M. G. Smith questioned the clarity of the distinction between political and lineage organisation. Others showed that two types could not capture age sets, village councils, secret associations, or mixed forms; the volume *Tribes Without Rulers* (1958), edited by John Middleton and David Tait, subdivided the uncentralised cases. Lineage theory was also criticised, notably by Adam Kuper, for presenting an abstract model as the working structure of actual groups. Historical research has revised the description of several kingdoms. The book remains a starting point for comparing authority without taking the European state as the sole model.
