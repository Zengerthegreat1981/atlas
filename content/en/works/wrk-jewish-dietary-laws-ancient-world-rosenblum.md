# The Jewish Dietary Laws in the Ancient World

Jordan Rosenblum's book, published in 2016 by Cambridge University Press, is a historical study of the development of Jewish dietary laws (*kashrut*) from the Hebrew Bible to classical Rabbinic literature (the Mishnah and the Talmud). Rosenblum is a professor of classical Judaism at the University of Wisconsin-Madison.

## Definition

The book deals with the system of *kashrut* (the laws of Jewishly permitted food), which determines the animals permitted for eating (ruminants with cloven hooves) and those forbidden (such as the pig), the lawful methods of slaughter (*shechita*), and the strict separation between meat and dairy products. It traces how these laws developed from their concise biblical textual origins (the books of Leviticus and Deuteronomy) to their extended elaboration in later Rabbinic literature.

## Thesis

Rosenblum argues that Jewish dietary laws were not a fixed system from the start. They developed gradually over centuries of Rabbinic interpretation until they became a comprehensive mechanism for regulating the Jewish body and delineating the boundaries of the community in the face of the surrounding Hellenistic and Roman cultures. This corresponds, without literal identity, to similar theses about the function of dietary laws in building religious otherness in general. However, Rosenblum confines himself to the Jewish tradition alone, with greater textual depth, rather than comparing the three Abrahamic religions.

## Closing Note

The book contributes to showing how food rules can go beyond their direct practical function (health, hygiene) to become a complete symbolic language of identity, belonging, and collective distinction. This is a central idea in the field of food and religion studies more broadly.

## Academic Reception

The book is used in university courses on Jewish studies and on food and religion, and complements earlier works on kashrut that were largely limited to descriptive legal exposition.

## Textual Examples Highlighted by the Book

Rosenblum cites detailed Talmudic debates on the rules separating meat and milk. These derive from the biblical injunction "You shall not boil a kid in its mother's milk," which appears three times in the Torah and which the Rabbis interpreted through gradual extension of the ruling. He also discusses the conditions of lawful slaughter (*shechita*), which is governed by detailed technical rules set out in the Talmud.

## Rosenblum's Academic Background

Jordan Rosenblum is known for his specialization in classical Rabbinic Judaism, and he has earlier work on food and Jewish identity (such as *Food and Identity in Early Rabbinic Judaism*, 2010). This book is a direct extension of a consistent research trajectory on the relationship between food, religion, and collective Jewish identity across history.

## Method

Rosenblum combines close philological analysis of the Mishnah and Talmud texts with theories from cultural anthropology about the function of food in building social identity. He engages anthropological work such as Mary Douglas's on the symbolic classification of food and impurity. He applies this theoretical framework to specific Jewish texts, rather than confining himself to a descriptive account of the history of the laws alone.

## Historical Context

Rosenblum traces how food laws transformed from relatively concise biblical rules (chiefly in Leviticus and Deuteronomy) into a detailed legal system in the Mishnah and Talmud. This development took place as Jews lived in the Hellenistic and Roman world, amid cultural environments with very different dietary and religious customs.

## Key Ideas

Rosenblum shows how food laws in Rabbinic literature turned from merely practical dietary rules into a comprehensive symbolic language of religious identity. Abstaining from non-kosher food was no longer only a legal obligation. It became a daily, repeated expression of belonging to the Jewish community in the face of a pagan (and later Christian) environment that practiced radically different food rituals, and sometimes sacrificial rituals to pagan gods, whose sacrificial meat Jews were forbidden to share in eating.

## The Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The book focuses on the Jewish tradition and does not treat Islam at length. Readers may note structural similarities between *kashrut* and the later Islamic *halal* and *haram* system: both forbid pork and both require a prescribed method of slaughter (Jewish *shechita* and Islamic *dhabiha*). Comparative treatments of the legal construction of food boundaries across Jewish, Christian and Islamic law can be found in Freidenreich's *Foreigners and Their Food*.
