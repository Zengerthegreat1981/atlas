# Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz

## Definition

*Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz* ("The Proofs of Inimitability") is a principal work of ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī (died 471 AH / 1078 CE; some sources give 474 AH). In it he set out his theory of *naẓm* (composition, syntactic arrangement). He argued that the rhetorical inimitability of the Qur'an lies not in its individual words taken alone, but in the grammatical way they are composed, so that the arrangement of words answers to the arrangement of meanings. Together with *Asrār al-Balāgha* (The Secrets of Eloquence), the book is a main source for the later systematic teaching of Arabic rhetoric. The relative order of the two books is uncertain.

## Historical Context

Al-Jurjani wrote this work within a long theological and linguistic debate about the nature of the Qur'an's inimitability (iʿjāz). The debate had occupied scholars of theology and rhetoric since the third century AH, from al-Jahiz to al-Rummani, al-Khattabi and al-Baqillani. They addressed the aspects of Qur'anic inimitability from several angles: the doctrine of *ṣarfa* (divine diversion from imitation), verbal eloquence, and the report of the unseen. In the fifth century AH, al-Jurjani produced a more coherent theoretical project with greater methodological rigor. He sought to go beyond merely listing scattered rhetorical merits, aiming instead at a comprehensive theory that explains the basis of eloquence and rhetoric in language as a whole, not in the Qur'an alone.

## Main Ideas and Method

### The Central Thesis: Composition, Not the Isolated Word

The book refutes a view common in its time, that the eloquence of speech resides in the quality of the individual words themselves, such as their pleasant sound or their strangeness. It replaces this with a radical thesis: a word has no rhetorical value when isolated from its grammatical context. Eloquence arises from the **grammatical relations** that link a word to its companions in the structure. These include the arrangement of fronting and postposing, and the choice of a conjunction, a circumstantial, or a specification. Each grammatical choice produces a fine semantic difference that cannot be achieved by another word in the same place.

### The Qur'an's Inimitability: Rhetorical, Not Purely Linguistic

Al-Jurjani applied this theory to explain the inimitability of the Qur'an, that is, the impossibility of producing its like, in precise rhetorical terms. The challenge lies not in the strangeness of its vocabulary, since its words are familiar Arabic, but in the exactness of its grammatical composition. Each word stands in the precise position required by the intended meaning, so any attempt to change an arrangement or a particle fails to produce the same semantic effect. In this way al-Jurjani transformed the question of inimitability from a theological-kalam question into a rhetorical-linguistic question open to rigorous systematic analysis. He relied on the analysis of numerous Qur'anic and poetic examples to show the effect of changing the position of a single word or a single particle on the overall meaning of the construction.

## Key Works and Figures

*Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz* complements al-Jurjani's other book, *Asrār al-Balāgha*, which he devoted to analyzing figurative imagery such as metaphor, simile and metonymy. *Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz* focused on the topics of the science of meanings (ʿilm al-maʿānī), such as fronting and postposing, ellipsis and mention, separation and connection, and definiteness and indefiniteness. Later, al-Sakkaki relied on this heritage in his book *Miftāḥ al-ʿUlūm* (The Key to the Sciences). He organized al-Jurjani's ideas into a simplified pedagogical structure, which later became the standard form taught in Arabic rhetoric schools. Many modern researchers hold, however, that al-Sakkaki's systematization stripped al-Jurjani's original ideas of part of their philosophical and analytical depth.

## Impact and Criticism

The book strongly shaped the later science of meanings (*ʿilm al-maʿānī*). It has remained a basic reference for Arabic stylistics and for discussions of Qur'anic inimitability. Modern interest in *naẓm* owes much to Muḥammad ʿAbduh (d. 1905), who taught the book; Rashīd Riḍā published an edition that records notes from that teaching. Later writers, including Amīn al-Khūlī and Tammām Ḥassān, compared al-Jurjānī's claim that meaning depends on syntactic relations with ideas in modern structural linguistics. Others have warned against treating that comparison as a historical identity between two distant projects.

## The Arab-Islamic Heritage Connection

The theory of *naẓm* was developed inside the Arabic tradition, in response to the theological question of the Qur'an's inimitability. There is no good evidence that al-Jurjānī borrowed the theory from a non-Arabic source. The book remains a standard reference in studies of language and revelation and in modern Arabic stylistics that link rhetorical effect to syntactic structure rather than to ornamental wording alone.
