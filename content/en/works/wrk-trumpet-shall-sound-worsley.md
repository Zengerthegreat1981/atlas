# The Trumpet Shall Sound: A Study of "Cargo" Cults in Melanesia

A reference book by the British sociologist Peter Worsley, published in 1957 by MacGibbon & Kee in London (a second, revised edition appeared in 1968 from the American publisher Schocken). The book gathers and analyses many documented cases of prophetic religious movements that arose in Melanesia, promising followers the arrival of a "cargo" of Western goods through ritual or spiritual means.

## The Central Thesis

Worsley reads these movements, from an explicitly Marxist perspective, as proto-political forms of protest against economic inequality and colonial domination, expressed in millenarian religious form in the absence of available secular political channels for colonised Melanesian societies. The book locates cargo cults within their social, economic and historical colonial context, rather than treating them as isolated psychological or cultural curiosities.

## Examples of Cases

Worsley surveys many cases from the late nineteenth century to the 1950s. One of the best known is the John Frum movement on the island of Tanna in what is now Vanuatu, in which followers looked for the return of a figure who would bring abundant goods and end European domination; similar movements elsewhere in Melanesia, including Papua New Guinea, announced a coming reversal of relations between colonisers and colonised.

## The Marxist Reading

Worsley rejects the stereotyped colonial reading of these movements as "mass madness" or a naive misunderstanding of Western technology. He proposes instead that they were a response to real conditions of economic and political deprivation under colonial rule (loss of land, labour for low wages, exclusion from the goods enjoyed by Europeans), carrying under a religious cover a protest about inequality that preceded organised secular politics in the region.

## Impact and Critique

The book became an influential reference, and later interpretations of cargo cults are commonly compared with it. Kenelm Burridge (*New Heaven, New Earth*, 1969), among others, emphasised the local moral and cosmological dimensions of the movements rather than economic grievance alone, and later anthropologists have questioned the explanatory weight of the Marxist reading while still treating its historical-social framework as an unavoidable point of reference.

## The Term "Cargo Cult"

The term "cargo cult" arose in colonial usage and has been widely criticised as condescending and as lumping together very different movements. Many scholars have debated whether it remains appropriate as an analytical category.

## Worsley's Academic Background

Peter Worsley (1924–2013) was a British sociologist, politically on the left, who taught at several universities, including the University of Manchester, where he became professor of sociology.

## Later Applications of the Model

The idea of linking millenarian religious expression to concrete colonial deprivation has been used by historians and sociologists to compare movements outside Melanesia, such as the Ghost Dance in North America and prophetic movements in colonial Africa.
