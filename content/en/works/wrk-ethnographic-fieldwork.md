# Ethnographic Fieldwork: Anthropological Locations

*Anthropological Locations: Boundaries and Grounds of a Field Science* (1997), edited by Akhil Gupta and James Ferguson, is the landmark reassessment of ethnographic fieldwork to emerge from anthropology's reflexive turn. Its subject is the discipline's most taken-for-granted idea: "the field." Gupta and Ferguson argue that the field is not a place anthropologists go but a construction they make — a way of dividing the world into bounded cultures that fieldwork then claims to discover. The volume asks what fieldwork can still mean once that construction is exposed.

The book grew out of a conference held in the mid-1990s, and its contributors include Henrika Kuklick, James Clifford, and Gupta and Ferguson themselves. Published by the University of California Press, it belongs to a cluster of 1990s works — alongside Gupta and Ferguson's companion volume *Culture, Power, Place* (1997) — that rethought anthropology's spatial imagination.

## Thesis and Structure

The editors' opening essay, "Discipline and Practice: 'The Field' as Site, Method, and Location in Anthropology," sets out the argument. Classical fieldwork, from Malinowski onward, rested on a spatial fiction: that the world is divided into discrete cultures, each occupying its own place, and that the anthropologist travels to one such place, stays there, and brings its culture home. This model made fieldwork possible, but it also smuggled in assumptions — that cultures are local, bounded, and homogeneous, and that the anthropologist arrives from an outside that is somehow not itself a place.

The chapters that follow test that fiction against cases and histories that refuse to fit it. Henrika Kuklick ("After Ishmael: The Fieldwork Tradition and Its Future") traces the history of the fieldwork ideal itself, showing how the image of the lone ethnographer in a remote village was assembled in the early twentieth century and how it served the discipline's professional ambitions. James Clifford ("Spatial Practices: Fieldwork, Travel, and the Disciplining of Anthropology") reflects on fieldwork as a form of travel and encounter rather than simple dwelling. Other contributions look at settings such as refugee communities and urban or non-traditional sites, where there is no bounded village to settle in. The editors' introduction similarly challenges the assumption that the proper field is a remote, bounded place, noting the informal hierarchy that ranks distant sites above "home."

## Method and Argument

The volume's method is critical history and ethnographic reflection rather than new fieldwork. Its contributors read the discipline's own practices — how field sites are chosen, how "rapport" is narrated, how the field is distinguished from "home" — as evidence about anthropology's unspoken theories. The argument is not that fieldwork should be abandoned; it is that fieldwork's authority depends on a spatial ideology that no longer describes the world anthropologists study, a world of migration, media, and transnational connection.

Two moves carry the argument. The first is to historicise the field: to show that the village, the tribe, and the culture area were professional devices as much as empirical discoveries, tied to colonial administration and to anthropology's need for a distinctive method. The second is to pluralise the field: to show that anthropology already happens in refugee camps, clinics, studios, and cities, and that recognising these as legitimate fields changes what counts as ethnographic knowledge.

## Reception and Influence

*Anthropological Locations* became a standard text in graduate training on method, and its discussion of "the field" as site, method, and location entered the discipline's vocabulary. Its influence is visible in the rise of multi-sited ethnography — George Marcus's proposal, developed in the same years, that fieldwork should follow people, things, and stories across sites rather than settling in one — and in the growth of anthropology "at home," in institutions, and in digital worlds.

The book has also drawn criticism. Some reviewers noted that it says little about archaeology, linguistics, or biological anthropology, and that its case studies cluster in cultural anthropology. Others argued that dissolving the field risks dissolving what makes anthropology distinctive — the sustained, embodied engagement with other people's lives. Gupta and Ferguson anticipated the objection: their point was never that place does not matter, but that place is made, contested, and political, and that fieldwork begins by asking how.

A generation later, the volume's questions have only sharpened. Fieldwork now happens over video calls, in online communities, and across borders that researchers cannot cross. *Anthropological Locations* remains the book that taught anthropology to ask, before setting out, what the field is — and who gets to draw its boundaries.
