# The Rites of Passage

*Les rites de passage* (1909) by Arnold van Gennep is a comparative study of ceremonies that accompany movement from one place, status, or period to another. An English translation by Monika B. Vizedom and Gabrielle L. Caffee appeared in 1960 as *The Rites of Passage*. Its lasting contribution is the observation that very different ceremonies often share an ordered pattern.

## The scheme

Van Gennep distinguishes rites of separation, transition rites, and rites of incorporation. He also calls them preliminal, liminal, and postliminal, from the Latin word for threshold. The first detach a person or group from a previous condition; the second mark an interval between conditions; the third admit them to a new one. The three are not equally developed in every ceremony. Separation tends to be prominent in funerals and incorporation in marriages, while transition may be elaborated in pregnancy, betrothal, or initiation, sometimes so fully that it contains its own sequence of three.

## Thresholds and territory

The analysis begins with spatial passage. Crossing a frontier, entering a village, or stepping over the doorway of a house may require ritual action. Van Gennep treats such physical movements as models for, and often components of, social transitions. Later chapters consider the reception of strangers, pregnancy and childbirth, childhood, initiation, betrothal and marriage, funerals, and other occasions, including ceremonies tied to the calendar and the seasons.

## Initiation and social age

Van Gennep distinguishes physiological puberty from social puberty. Initiation rites need not coincide with bodily maturation, so they cannot be explained by it alone. Removal of marks or parts of the body, special dress, instruction, seclusion, and shared meals gain their meaning from their position in a sequence. This insistence on reading a rite within the whole ceremony, rather than collecting similar customs detached from context, set his method apart from much comparative writing of the period.

## Evidence and limits

The book is based on published ethnographic, folkloric, and historical material from many societies, of uneven quality; it is not a report of the author's fieldwork. Examples are often brief. The three-part model is a tool for comparison and can be forced onto events whose sequence is contested, incomplete, or understood differently by participants. It says relatively little about conflict or about people who fail or refuse to complete a passage.

## Reception

The work received limited recognition within the Durkheimian school, whose members, including Marcel Mauss, reviewed it critically. Its wider influence in English-language anthropology followed the 1960 translation. Max Gluckman discussed it in essays on ritual and social relations. Victor Turner expanded the middle phase into a theory of liminality and communitas, in which persons in transition stand outside ordinary rank and may form intense bonds. The vocabulary has since been used for pilgrimage, education, military training, illness, and migration, with varying attention to the original comparative argument.
