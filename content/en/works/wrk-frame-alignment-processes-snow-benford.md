# Frame Alignment Processes, Micromobilization, and Movement Participation

"Frame Alignment Processes, Micromobilization, and Movement Participation" (1986) is an article by David A. Snow, E. Burke Rochford Jr., Steven K. Worden and Robert D. Benford, published in the *American Sociological Review*. It brought the concept of the interpretive frame, taken from Erving Goffman, into the study of social movements and helped found the framing perspective.

## Historical Context

In the early 1980s the sociology of social movements was dominated by resource mobilization theory, which stressed organisation, resources and leadership, and by political process theory, which stressed political opportunities. The authors argued that both underplayed how potential participants come to interpret events as grievances that can be addressed collectively, and why they decide to take part. They adapted the notion of the "frame" from Goffman's *Frame Analysis* (1974), the schemata of interpretation that allow people to locate, perceive, identify and label occurrences.

## The Central Thesis

The authors argued that participation in a movement depends on a linkage between the interpretive orientations of individuals and those of the movement, which they called frame alignment. They identified four alignment processes. Frame bridging links two or more ideologically congruent but structurally unconnected frames about a particular issue, for example by connecting a movement to sympathetic individuals who are not yet organised. Frame amplification clarifies and invigorates an interpretive frame, typically by emphasising certain values or beliefs. Frame extension widens the boundaries of a movement's primary frame to take in issues or interests that are important to potential adherents but not originally part of its agenda. Frame transformation changes old understandings, or creates new ones, when the movement's frames do not resonate with prevailing ones.

## Impact and Critique

The article founded what became the framing perspective, which complements resource mobilization and political process approaches by attending to meaning construction. Snow and Benford developed the idea in "Ideology, Frame Resonance, and Participant Mobilization" (1988), which distinguished diagnostic, prognostic and motivational framing tasks, and Benford and Snow reviewed the field in 2000. The perspective has been applied to a wide range of movements, including counter-movements and the political right.

Critics have argued that frames are difficult to measure precisely, that framing research has tended to explain success by the quality of frames, which risks circularity, and that it has sometimes focused on the rhetoric of movement leaders and paid too little attention to audiences and structural conditions.
