# Phenomenology of Perception (Phénoménologie de la perception) - Merleau-Ponty

## Central Thesis

The principal work of **Maurice Merleau-Ponty** (1908–1961), and the text of his principal doctoral thesis, defended at the Sorbonne in 1945 (his secondary thesis was *The Structure of Behavior* (*La Structure du comportement*, 1942)). His central thesis: **perception is not a rational judgment upon raw sensory data, nor a mere mechanical response to physical stimuli**, but an original **bodily** engagement that precedes all thinking about the world. The original agent of perception is neither the disembodied "consciousness" of the Cartesian sense nor the mechanical "body" of physiology, but the **lived body** (le corps propre): an entity that knows the world through its movement and its positioning within it before reflecting on it. Hence his double rejection of two competing explanations prevalent at the time: **behaviorism and experimental physiology** (which reduce perception to a reflex mechanism) and **rationalist intellectualism** (which reduces it to a purely mental judgment or synthesis). Both overlook the intermediate layer: lived experience as it is actually lived, prior to any theorizing about it.

## Structure of the Work

A long methodological introduction (on the "phenomenon," and on the critique of both descriptive psychology and intellectualism) is followed by three major parts:
1. **The body** (Le corps): treats the body as an object of mechanical physiology, the experience of the body and classical psychology, the spatiality and motility of the body, and the body as expression, speech, and sexual body.
2. **The perceived world** (Le monde perçu): sensation, space, the thing and the natural world, the other and the human world.
3. **Being-for-itself and being-in-the-world** (Être-pour-soi et être-au-monde): the cogito, temporality, and freedom.

## Clinical Cases in the Text

Merleau-Ponty built his argument on cases from clinical neurology and Gestalt psychology. The most famous is the case of **Schneider**, a German soldier who was wounded in the head by shrapnel during World War I and was studied by Adhémar Gelb and Kurt Goldstein. Schneider could perform "concrete" movements that are habitual, such as scratching the place where he was stung or taking a handkerchief from his pocket, but was unable to perform "abstract" movements, such as pointing to a part of his body when asked to do so without a practical context. Merleau-Ponty explained this by a disturbance in the **intentional arc** (arc intentionnel) that connects the body to its practical world, rather than in sensation or thought. He also studied the **phantom limb**, and concluded that it cannot be explained by physiology alone (cutting the nerves) or by psychology alone (memory): the amputee continues to live in a world with a body still oriented toward the tasks it once performed. He also spoke of bodily skills, such as typing on a keyboard or the blind person's cane, which become an extension of the body.

## The Historical Context of Publication

The book was published in 1945 by **Gallimard** (Paris), in the same year Merleau-Ponty helped found the journal *Les Temps modernes* with Sartre and Simone de Beauvoir. The book is the product of his double formation: his study with **Edmund Husserl** (he examined Husserl's unpublished manuscripts in the Leuven archive in 1939) and his parallel reading of **Heidegger**, of Gestalt psychology, and of the clinical neurology of his time. Merleau-Ponty did not republish the book with substantial revisions before his sudden death in 1961.

## Comparative Roots in Arab Philosophy

Medieval Arab philosophy offers a picture close to the idea of "bodily knowledge that precedes thought." In the story of *Ḥayy ibn Yaqẓān* by Ibn Tufayl (12th century), a human being grows up on an island without instruction and builds his knowledge step by step, from sensory perception and experimentation with his body and hands, and then toward rational contemplation. Lenn Goodman translated this story into English (1972; University of Chicago Press edition, 2009). There is no evidence that Merleau-Ponty knew this text, and he does not assume the ascending rational stages that Ibn Tufayl presupposes, so the connection is comparative and interpretive. Ibn al-Haytham's analysis of visual perception as a composite judgment is also relevant to the topic.

## Reception and Critique

The book was received with immediate seriousness in French philosophical circles. **Sartre** wrote about it admiringly, and the two collaborated for years before their intellectual and political relationship deteriorated, and the disagreements became public after Merleau-Ponty published *The Adventures of the Dialectic* (*Les Aventures de la dialectique*) in 1955, in which he criticized Sartre's Marxism. The book reached the Anglophone world through Colin Smith's translation in 1962, and it later influenced **phenomenological psychology** (for example Amedeo Giorgi at Duquesne) and the movement of **embodied cognition**, notably Varela, Thompson, and Rosch's *The Embodied Mind* (MIT Press, 1991) and Alva Noë's *Action in Perception* (2004). Medard Boss's existential psychiatry draws mainly on Heidegger, and contemporary research on phantom limbs (for example by Ramachandran) is not derived from Merleau-Ponty.

## Limits of the Book

Iris Marion Young, in *Throwing Like a Girl*, argued that the "body" in Merleau-Ponty is implicitly a neutral, male body, and does not represent the experience of women who learn to restrict their movement in space; Judith Butler also wrote a critique of his account of the sexual body ("Sexual Ideology and Phenomenological Description," 1989). It is held against him that his descriptive character makes it difficult to translate his claims into empirical hypotheses, and that his reliance on a few clinical cases (Schneider) is generalized too widely. This tendency pushed Merleau-Ponty himself, in his later work (*The Visible and the Invisible*, left unfinished), toward the concept of "flesh" (la chair) as an alternative to the concept of the lived body.

## The Importance for the Philosophical-Psychological Bridge

The book offers a major philosophical alternative to the Cartesian dualism (mind/body) with which contemporary psychology still wrestles implicitly: the idea that perception, knowledge, and identity are **embodied**, not abstract, and that the body is not a tool used by a separate "I" but a existential condition of all experience. This philosophical foundation stands behind contemporary currents in somatic therapy, embodied cognitive psychology, and cognitive neuroscience, even if Merleau-Ponty was not always their direct technical source but often their philosophical background.
