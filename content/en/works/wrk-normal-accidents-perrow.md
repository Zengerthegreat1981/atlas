# Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies

Charles Perrow's pivotal book, published by Basic Books in 1984 (with an expanded edition from Princeton University Press in 1999), inspired by his analysis of the 1979 Three Mile Island nuclear accident, in which he put forward his founding theory that some technological disasters are structurally inevitable in certain complex systems.

## Context

Perrow, a sociologist of organizations, studied the 1979 accident at the Three Mile Island nuclear plant in Pennsylvania, the most serious accident at a commercial nuclear plant in United States history, in connection with the presidential commission that investigated it. He noted that official accounts placed heavy emphasis on individual "human error" by the plant's workers and operators, whereas he saw the problem as structural: the design of the system itself produced conditions in which it is impossible for any human operator, however competent and well trained, to understand quickly enough what is actually happening inside the system during the crisis.

## The Central Thesis

Perrow argues that technological-organizational systems that combine two structural characteristics, "complex interactions" (that is, nonlinear and invisible interactions among the system's different components that are hard to anticipate) and "tight coupling" (that is, close, rapid interdependence among parts that prevents any timely corrective human intervention or the halting of the sequence of events), are prone to "normal" catastrophic accidents that are statistically inevitable given enough time, regardless of the quality of training and of the precautionary procedures adopted. The book applies this framework to several high-risk systems, among them nuclear power plants, aviation, chemical plants, dams, marine transport, and space missions.

## The Structure of the Book and the Risk Matrix

The book begins with a detailed analysis of the Three Mile Island accident as a founding case study, showing how a failure involving a relief valve combined with other small failures and ambiguous instrument readings, so that the operators did not form a correct picture of the state of the reactor for hours. It then extends the theoretical framework (complex interaction × tight coupling) into a two-dimensional classificatory matrix that places different technological systems according to their degree of exposure to this pattern of inevitable accidents, comparing systems that are complex but loosely coupled, such as universities (less exposed because the slowness of the linkages allows errors to be corrected), with systems that are both complex and tightly coupled, such as nuclear power plants (the most exposed to normal accidents because of the speed with which error spreads and the system's lack of clear visibility).

## Engineering Detail of the Three Mile Island Accident

The accident began early on 28 March 1979 in Unit 2, a pressurized water reactor designed by Babcock and Wilcox. In such a plant, water in the primary circuit carries heat from the core to steam generators, where it boils water in a separate secondary circuit. The main feedwater supply to the steam generators stopped, apparently in connection with maintenance on the system that purified it, and the turbine and then the reactor shut down automatically. Auxiliary pumps started, but valves on their lines had been left closed. Pressure in the primary circuit rose and a pilot-operated relief valve on the pressurizer opened as designed, then failed to close. A control-room light showed only that the signal to close had been sent. Coolant escaped for more than two hours. Because the pressurizer level indicator suggested that the system held too much water, operators sharply reduced emergency injection, and later stopped the main coolant pumps, which were vibrating. The core became partly uncovered and a large part of it melted. Reaction of fuel cladding with steam produced hydrogen. The containment building largely held, and off-site radiation releases were small.

## Impact and Critique

The book became a founding reference in the sociology of risk and technological disasters, stimulating a continuing theoretical debate with the competing "High Reliability Theory" school developed by researchers such as Todd La Porte and Karlene Roberts, which argued that careful organizational and cultural design can achieve high safety even in complex, tightly coupled systems (such as nuclear aircraft carriers), in contrast to Perrow's structural pessimism. The book deeply influenced safety engineering, the design of complex systems, and the regulatory policies of high-risk industries around the world, and it was cited heavily again after later disasters such as the Challenger shuttle explosion (1986), the Chernobyl disaster (1986), and the Fukushima accident (2011).

## Later Applications and Widening Scope

Later researchers extended the application of Perrow's framework to systems beyond purely industrial technology, such as complex, tightly coupled global financial systems (where some analysts have discussed the 2008 financial crisis in these terms), digital systems and large electrical power grids. Perrow added new material in the 1999 Princeton edition, including a postscript on the Y2K computer problem, and later turned to catastrophic risk more broadly in *The Next Catastrophe* (2007).

## The Limits of Predictive Capacity

Perrow argued that his theory carries an additional pessimistic epistemic implication: since accidents resulting from complex interaction arise from unexpected combinations of small simultaneous failures, they inherently resist complete advance prediction however good the safety systems designed, since it is impossible to design a system that calculates in advance all theoretically possible combinations of multiple simultaneous failures in an extremely complex system, a point that made the optimism of some safety engineers about the possibility of the "complete elimination" of major accidents unrealistic in his view.
