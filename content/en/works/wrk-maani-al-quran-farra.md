# Ma'ani al-Qur'an (al-Farra')

A reference work by al-Farra', one of the oldest Arabic works combining the interpretation of the Qur'an with systematic grammatical analysis of its verses. It represents the grammatical method of the Kufa school in dealing with the Qur'anic text.

## Interpretive-Grammatical Method

Al-Farra' interprets the Qur'anic verses one by one. He draws on the variety of widely transmitted Qur'anic readings (qirā'āt), cites pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry, and applies the grammatical rules of the Kufan school. Throughout, he explains the possible modes of inflection and their divergent semantic implications. This method combines linguistic precision with interpretive interest.

## Historical Context

Yahya ibn Ziyad al-Farra' (144–207 AH) was of Kufan origin. He studied under al-Kisa'i, and was one of the leading figures of the Kufa grammatical school. He served as tutor to the sons of the Abbasid caliph al-Ma'mun, and he died on his way to Mecca. His book *Ma'ani al-Qur'an* is one of the oldest complete works of this kind that have come down to us, combining interpretation and grammar. Alongside it stands a contemporary work of similar content, Abu ʿUbayda Maʿmar ibn al-Muthanna's *Majāz al-Qur'ān*. The two methods differed, however. Abu ʿUbayda leaned toward explaining Qur'anic terms through direct poetic citation, while al-Farra' leaned toward analyzing the probable grammatical modes of inflection in greater depth. His analysis started from the principles of the Kufan school, which was known for its flexibility in accepting analogy based on rare evidence, compared with the more conservative Basra school.

## Characteristics of al-Farra's Method

Al-Farra's method is open to the multiple widely transmitted Qur'anic readings. He uses their diversity as a tool for interpreting meaning and for preferring one inflectional mode over another. This is often linked to the Kufans' reputation for accepting evidence from a wider range of Arab speech than the Basrans did. Al-Farra' also used grammatical terms particular to his school that differ from Basran terminology. For example, he used terms of his own for some categories (such as *sarf* for what Basrans called the circumstantial accusative or other cases) that differ from the terminology of Sibawayh in *al-Kitab*. This makes his book an essential source for studying the terminological and methodological differences between the two great grammatical schools of the Arab heritage.

## Impact on the Science of Qur'anic I'rab

This book paved the way for the later development of an independent branch of Qur'anic studies known as *iʿrāb al-Qurʾān* (grammatical parsing of the Qur'an). That branch analyzes the possible grammatical modes of each verse and their effect on the interpretive meaning. The book influenced later exegetes who used grammatical analysis as a basic interpretive tool, such as al-Zajjaj in *Ma'ani al-Qur'an wa I'rabuhu* and al-Nahhas in *I'rab al-Qur'an*. It also had an indirect influence on al-Tabari, who drew on al-Farra's linguistic material in his comprehensive commentary.

## The Position of al-Farra' Among the Kufan Scholars

Al-Farra' is the most mature link in a series of great Kufan grammarians that began with Abu Ja'far al-Ru'asi and continued with al-Kisa'i. The bibliographical tradition (for example Ibn al-Nadim's *al-Fihrist*) attributes to him other works, including *al-Ḥudūd* (on grammatical definitions), *al-Mudhakkar wa-al-Mu'annath* and *al-Maqṣūr wa-al-Mamdūd*. Al-Farra' combined grammar, lexicography, Qur'anic readings and jurisprudence. This range of knowledge is clearly reflected in *Ma'ani al-Qur'an*, which is not limited to the purely grammatical dimension but blends several disciplines that were intertwined in that early period of the codification of Arab and Islamic sciences.

## Comparison with the Basran Method

The contrast between al-Farra's Kufan method and that of his Basran contemporaries becomes clear when compared with the works of Sibawayh, al-Mazini, and later al-Mubarrad. The Basrans are traditionally said to have adopted a more restrictive approach to grammatical analogy, preferring to exclude rare or anomalous evidence. Al-Farra' and his school showed greater flexibility in accepting the interpretation of such evidence within multiple explanatory frameworks. The disagreement was methodologically deeper than a mere difference in terminology, since it reflects two conceptions of the linguistic rule itself. Is the rule a strict logical system from which the anomalous is excluded, or a reflection of a diverse actual usage whose plurality should be accommodated? This disagreement remained a living grammatical debate for centuries in the Arab heritage. Later scholars combined the two methods, or preferred one depending on the question at hand. This shows that the disagreement was not absolute hostility so much as a productive methodological diversity within a single system of Arab grammar. It ultimately yielded a grammatical heritage richer and more varied than it would have been had it been confined to a single school.
