# Capital: The Critique of Political Economy

*Capital: A Critique of Political Economy* is Karl Marx's life work and the founding text of Marxist sociology and economics. Its first volume appeared in German in Hamburg in 1867; the second and third volumes were edited from Marx's manuscripts by Friedrich Engels and published after Marx's death, in 1885 and 1894. Marx worked on the project for more than two decades, in exile in London, in the reading room of the British Museum, drawing on the factory inspectors' reports, parliamentary Blue Books, and the classical political economy of Adam Smith and David Ricardo — and then turning that economy against itself.

## The Argument: The Commodity and Surplus Value

The book opens not with capital but with the commodity, the elementary cell of capitalist wealth. A commodity has a use-value and an exchange-value, and what makes different commodities exchangeable, Marx argues, is the socially necessary labour time embodied in them. From this labour theory of value he derives his central mechanism: surplus value. The worker sells not his labour but his labour-power, and is paid its value — the cost of reproducing it. But in the working day he produces more value than that cost, and the excess is appropriated by the capitalist. Profit, rent, and interest are all forms of this surplus value. The working day therefore becomes a field of struggle: capital presses to lengthen it, workers organise to shorten it, and the Factory Acts that Marx documents are the record of that struggle. Around this core Marx builds the analysis of the fetishism of commodities — the way social relations between people take the fantastic form of relations between things — and of accumulation: capital must constantly expand, concentrating wealth at one pole and misery at the other, and producing its own gravediggers in an organised working class.

## Structure and Method

Volume I follows a strict logical ascent. Parts I and II analyse the commodity, money, and the transformation of money into capital. Parts III and IV derive surplus value, first absolute — by lengthening the working day — then relative, by raising productivity through machinery and the division of labour. Later parts treat wages, accumulation, and the so-called primitive accumulation: the enclosures, colonial plunder, and state violence through which a propertyless working class was historically created. Marx's method is a distinctive blend of dialectical argument, historical narrative, and empirical documentation. The chapter on the working day, for instance, cites factory inspectors' reports and the case of Mary Anne Walkley, a London milliner who died in 1863 after working very long hours. The Blue Books supply the evidence of child labour, sixteen-hour days, and industrial disease; the dialectic supplies the movement from abstract categories to concrete social forms. He calls this the critique of political economy rather than a new economics: he accepts Ricardo's categories in order to show their inner contradictions. Volumes II and III, edited by Engels, extend the analysis to the circulation of capital and to the division of surplus value into profit, interest, and rent, ending with the tendency of the rate of profit to fall — the most contested prediction in the book.

## Reception and Influence

*Capital* was little read in Marx's lifetime — the first German edition sold slowly — but after his death it became the central text of the socialist movement, translated into Russian, French, and English, and read as both science and scripture. In sociology it supplied the class theory that shaped Weber's response, Durkheim's contrast, and the whole tradition of conflict theory. Its influence runs through the Frankfurt School, dependency theory, world-systems analysis, and contemporary studies of globalisation, financialisation, and precarious labour. David Harvey's lectures and companion volumes have made the first volume teachable again to new generations, and Michael Heinrich's introduction has clarified the architecture of all three volumes. The book's predictions have been fiercely debated: the immiseration thesis, the falling rate of profit, and the inevitability of crisis have all been challenged by later evidence and by Marxists themselves, and the labour theory of value remains the most disputed foundation in economics.

## Criticism and Continuing Relevance

Feminist critics have argued that Marx's analysis of labour excludes the unpaid domestic labour on which the reproduction of labour-power depends — a gap taken up by social reproduction theory. Postcolonial critics note that the account of primitive accumulation, powerful as it is, treats colonialism as a prehistory rather than a continuing structure of capitalism. Ecological critics have asked whether a theory built on the expansion of productive forces can address a world of finite resources, though others find in Marx's remarks on the metabolic rift an early ecological insight. Contemporary sociologists return to the book for its analysis of commodification — of care, of data, of attention — and for its insistence that economic categories are social relations in disguise. Its ability to sustain both reverent and hostile readings, and to generate new research programmes a century and a half after publication, is the measure of its standing: a work that is simultaneously a historical document of nineteenth-century industrial England and a living framework for understanding twenty-first-century capitalism.
