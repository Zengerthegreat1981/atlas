# Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience

## The central thesis

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi (Harper & Row, 1990) defines "flow" as the state of complete engagement that arises when the **level of challenge in an activity matches the level of the agent's skill**: challenge above skill generates anxiety, and skill above challenge generates boredom, and in the narrow range between them self-awareness fades and the perception of time is distorted. The activity that is practiced for its own sake and not for an external return is what he calls the **autotelic activity**, and the broader thesis is that the quality of life is a function of how attention is allocated and not of external circumstances.

## Structure of the work

Ten chapters progress from a theory of consciousness and attention (consciousness is a limited resource, and disorder in it is "psychic entropy") to enumerating the eight components of optimal experience: clarity of goal, immediate feedback, balance of challenge and skill, concentration of attention, a sense of control, loss of self-awareness, altered sense of time, and intrinsic motivation. The chapters then move to the domains of flow: the body, thought, work, solitude and relationships, then confronting adversity and the "autotelic self," and conclude with meaning as the unification of a single life theme.

## Historical context of publication

The book is a popularization of a research program preceding it by fifteen years. It began with *Beyond Boredom and Anxiety* (1975), based on interviews with climbers, dancers, chess players and other enthusiasts, and then rested on the **Experience Sampling Method**: pagers randomly alert participants during the day and they record what they are doing and their state at that moment. This method, whose psychometric properties Csikszentmihalyi and Reed Larson reviewed in 1987, is the actual empirical base of the book. From this base came the result of Csikszentmihalyi and Judith LeFevre (1989): participants reported flow at work more than in leisure time, and yet said they would prefer not to work, the "paradox of work."

## Reception and criticism

The concept spread beyond psychology to game design, education and sport psychology, and became one of the pillars of positive psychology in the 1990s. But its measurement remained disputed from three directions: (1) reliance on self-report and on retrospective reconstruction of the state, with no independent behavioral or physiological indicator; (2) the construction of measurement instruments separate from the original method, such as the Flow State Scale of **Susan Jackson and Herbert Marsh** (1996); (3) the weak predictive power of the challenge/skill "channel" model itself: **Moneta and Csikszentmihalyi** (1996) retested the model and found that the level of skill contributes more than the balance alone assumes, and then **Stefan Engeser and Falko Rheinberg** (2008) found that the balance of challenge and skill predicts flow only partially, and that it is conditioned on the importance of the activity to the agent.

## Why it is in the atlas

Because it is the primary source of the concept of **flow** as it appears in the two concept entries in the atlas and the experience entry, and because it is a model of a mass-audience work behind which stands a real empirical base (the experience sampling method) while its standard model remains contested.
