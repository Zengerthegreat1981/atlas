# Realist Social Theory: The Morphogenetic Approach

Margaret Archer's reference work (1995), in which she put forward "analytical dualism" as a solution to the structure/agency dilemma, a direct reply to Anthony Giddens's structuration theory.

## Context

Archer, influenced by Roy Bhaskar's philosophy of "critical realism," wrote this work as a sustained critique of the "structuration theory" developed by Anthony Giddens, a critique she had begun in her 1982 article "Morphogenesis versus Structuration." That theory argued that structure and agency are a "duality," inseparable analytically even at a single moment, since every structure includes the possibility of the agent's action and every action reproduces the structure at the same moment. Archer held that this complete merging makes it methodologically impossible to study empirically the causal relation between structure and agent, since one dissolves into the other conceptually before any empirical research.

## The Central Thesis: Analytical Dualism

Archer argued that social structure temporally precedes any particular individual action (the individual is born into a social structure that already exists before him), but it does not determine that action. Rather, it constrains or enables human agency according to his position within it, and agency in turn reproduces or transforms the structure through a temporally later cycle, in what she called "morphogenesis" (when action produces change in the structure) or "morphostasis" (when action reproduces the existing structure without essential change). This framework required a strict analytical separation (not an absolute ontological one) between two temporally successive moments, not simultaneous ones. First comes the prior structural condition ("T1"), which prepares the context of action. Then comes the subsequent agential interaction ("T2-T3"), which responds to this condition in multiple possible, non-predetermined ways. Then comes the later structural elaboration ("T4") resulting from this action, in a continuous cycle that can be traced empirically more clearly than in Giddens's merged model.

## Emergent Properties and Causal Powers

Archer relied on the critical realist principle that social and cultural structures have "emergent properties," that is, properties that cannot be reduced to the sum of the individuals who compose them (as methodological individualism does) nor to a mere automatic reflection of the structure onto individuals (as excessive structuralism does). Rather, these structures possess causal powers of their own that affect actors without being reduced to them or abolishing their relative freedom to respond variously to these powers.

## Impact and Critique

This work offered one of the most methodologically rigorous solutions to the structure/agency dilemma in contemporary sociology, and it influenced the sociology of education and studies of reflexive identity, especially after Archer later developed in *Structure, Agency and the Internal Conversation* (2003) the concept of the "Internal Conversation." This explains how the individual's inner self-reflection (silent thinking with oneself) mediates between structural conditions and actual action, specifying distinct modes of reflexivity (communicative, autonomous, meta, fractured) that explain individual variation in how different persons respond to the same structural condition. The work was criticized by Anthony King and others, who held that the strict separation between structure and action reintroduces a dualism instead of overcoming it, and by other researchers who demanded more concrete empirical clarification of how this relatively abstract framework can be applied to actual field research.

## Applications in the Sociology of Education

Archer applied her morphogenetic framework in particular detail to analyzing the historical development of national education systems in her early work *Social Origins of Educational Systems* (1979). She showed how interactions among competing interest groups (the church, the state, economic elites) produce, over successive cycles of time, fundamentally distinct national educational structures (centralized in France, for example, and relatively decentralized in England). This was regarded as an early and convincing empirical application demonstrating the validity of her abstract theoretical framework on concrete historical material, chronologically preceding her full theoretical formulation of analytical dualism in this very book.

## Collective Agency and Individual Agency

Archer also distinguished between "Primary Agents," ordinary individuals who are affected by the structure without conscious collective organization, and "Corporate Agents," organized groups conscious of their shared interests (such as unions and parties), able to voice explicit demands and to try to reshape the structure deliberately. She held that the distinction between these two levels of agency is necessary for understanding how some structural conditions turn into actual change while others remain fixed despite ordinary individuals' individual dissatisfaction with them in the absence of effective collective organization.
