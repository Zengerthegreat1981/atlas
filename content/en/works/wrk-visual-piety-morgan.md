# Visual Piety: A History and Theory of Popular Religious Images

David Morgan's early major book in the field of material religion studies, published in 1998 by the University of California Press. It traces the history of popular religious images in American Protestantism, particularly the widely circulated printed images of Christ produced in the twentieth century. It also proposes a theoretical framework for analyzing "looking" itself as a religious practice: how the viewing of a religious image is socially and ritually organized so that it produces an experience of piety, rather than a mere passive reception of a visual representation.

## The Example of Warner Sallman

Morgan devotes an extended analysis to the painting *Head of Christ* (1940) by the artist Warner Sallman. It is one of the most widely printed and distributed religious images in American history (reproduced in enormous numbers). He shows how the image moved beyond being a mere wall decoration to become the subject of actual devotional practices. It is hung in children's rooms so that the child grows accustomed to "looking" at the face of Christ every day, and it is used in Sunday schools as an educational tool that shapes the new generation's visual conception of the identity of Christ itself. In this way the image sometimes precedes the text in shaping the actual popular religious conception.

## The Theoretical Framework: Looking as Practice, Not Reception

Morgan draws on visual culture studies and reception theory to propose that "religious seeing" is a socially acquired process rather than an innate one. The believer learns through religious upbringing how to "look correctly" at a sacred image: where to direct the gaze, what feelings the image should evoke, and how to connect the act of seeing the image with the act of prayer or meditation. This makes the religious image a site of actual ritual action, not a neutral medium transmitting a ready-made theological meaning from elsewhere.

## Impact

The book contributed to the field of "material religion studies," which partly shifted the focus of comparative religious studies from belief and the written text toward objects, images and everyday bodily practices. It prepared the ground for Morgan's later and broader work *The Sacred Gaze* (2005), which extended the theoretical framework itself beyond the American Christian context into a comparison among multiple religious visual traditions around the world.

## Archival Sources

Morgan drew on sources that were unconventional for academic religious studies at the time, including the records of commercial religious publishers and the circulation of images such as Sallman's. Such materials had been little used in art history (which usually prefers "high," unique works) and in religious studies (which prefers official theological texts).

## The Concept of "Popular Visual Religion"

Morgan distinguishes between official religion, recorded in sermons and church doctrines, and the "popular visual religion" that the ordinary believer actually lives in his daily home, through simple, inexpensive religious images hung on walls. He suggests that this popular level of visual piety is no less important in shaping the actual religious consciousness of the majority than any complex official theological preaching, and may even exert a more direct daily influence.

## Class Reception of the Religious Image

Morgan shows that the consumption of cheap printed images of Christ has historically been associated with a specific class audience from the American Protestant middle and working classes. Some of the higher religious and artistic elites, by contrast, viewed them with aesthetic disdain as vulgar religious "kitsch," lacking theological or artistic depth. Morgan analyzes this taste hierarchy as part of the broader pattern of how modern American society organizes its relationship to popular religiosity in general.

## Morgan's Academic Career

David Morgan is a professor of religious studies at Duke University. He was a founding editor of the journal *Material Religion* (2005), a forum for research in the field this book helped to shape.
