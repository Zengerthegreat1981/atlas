# The Analects (Lunyu)

## The Central Thesis

The "Analects" does not put forward a single systematic thesis but is presented as a collection of scattered sayings and dialogues revolving around one central idea: **virtue is acquired through the daily practice of ritual (禮 Lǐ) and relationships, not through abstract theoretical contemplation**. The complete person (君子 jūnzǐ, the "noble person" or "true gentleman" — a moral title, not a class lineage for Confucius) attains benevolent humaneness (仁 Rén) through continual training in "reciprocity" (恕 shù): not imposing on others what you would not wish imposed on yourself (15.24). There is no conclusive answer in contemporary scholarship as to whether Confucius himself wrote anything; the most probable view is that the text was composed by his students and their students, his sayings gathered generations after his death.

## Structure of the Work

It consists of 20 chapters (篇 piān) and **about 500 short passages** (章 zhāng) — a sentence, an incident, or a brief dialogue, without a coherent thematic order or argumentative sequence among the passages. The construction is narrative-compilatory rather than methodical: each passage is a self-standing unit, which makes "reading the book" closer to browsing a collection of maxims than to following an argument. Chapters 1–15 are usually considered the oldest layer of the compilation, while chapters 16–20 (especially 16 and 20) bear stylistic features that some scholars take to indicate a later addition. Among its most prominent themes: ritual (禮), benevolent humaneness (仁), self-directed learning (學 xué) as a condition of virtue rather than the acquisition of information, and governing by virtue (政 zhèng) rather than by coercive force ("Governing by virtue is like the North Star, which stays in its place while all the stars revolve around it," 2.1).

## Historical Context of Publication — The History of Compilation and Transmission

There is no sense here in a "first edition": the text accumulated gradually between the death of Confucius (c. 479 BCE) and the end of the Warring States period (221 BCE), through successive circles of students who recorded and transmitted his sayings orally and then in writing. In the Han period there were distinct versions (the "Lu Lunyu" and the "Qi Lunyu", along with a so-called Old Text version). The received text descends from the Lu version as combined with elements of the others by Zhang Yu and later edited by **Zheng Xuan** (鄭玄, 2nd century CE); the commentary of He Yan (3rd century CE) and that of **Zhu Xi** (朱熹, 12th century CE) became standard. Zhu Xi grouped the *Analects* among the "Four Books" (四書 sìshū), which later became the basis of the imperial examination curriculum — and this inclusion, many centuries after the original compilation, is what established its central standing in official Confucian education, not its first spread.

## Reception and Critique

Since the Han dynasty (2nd century BCE – 2nd century CE) it became an important classical text studied beside the "Five Classics" (五經 wǔjīng), then in the 12th century CE, under Zhu Xi's commentary, one of the "Four Books" that every Chinese student aspiring to imperial office memorized for centuries. It was translated into Latin through the Jesuit missions in the 17th century (notably in *Confucius Sinarum Philosophus*, 1687), and into English by **James Legge** in the 19th century, becoming the best-known entry for the Western reader into Confucian thought. In the 20th century it was sharply criticized during the May Fourth Movement (1919) as a symbol of "backward" heritage, then gradually rehabilitated in recent decades within the currents of "New Confucianism," which try to reconcile it with political modernity and liberalism.

## Modern Translations

The standard English translations are those of Legge, followed by Arthur Waley (1938) and D. C. Lau (1979), with more recent versions by Edward Slingerland (2003) and others.

## Why It Is Mentioned in the Atlas

It is the most widely accepted foundational text for understanding Confucius through his direct (or directly attributed) sayings, and an indispensable reference for any file on early Confucianism. But it does not represent "Confucian thought" in its entirety: it largely ignores the later debate about human nature (good for Mencius, evil for Xunzi) that formed the second phase of the school's development. It is read today in Chinese, Korean and Japanese universities as a basic introductory text in "Chinese philosophy."
