# Patterns of Culture

*Patterns of Culture* (Boston: Houghton Mifflin, 1934) is a book by the American anthropologist Ruth Benedict (1887-1948). Written for a broad readership, it argues that each culture selects and emphasises only some of the range of human possibilities and tends to organise them into a more or less coherent configuration.

## Author and Context

Benedict studied anthropology at Columbia University under Franz Boas, received her doctorate in 1923 and taught in his department. Boasian anthropology opposed evolutionary rankings of societies and biological explanations of cultural difference, at a time when racial theories and immigration restriction were prominent in the United States. Benedict was also a poet, and a close colleague of Margaret Mead. Boas contributed an introduction to the book.

## The Argument

Benedict compares culture to personality: it is not a mere collection of traits but a more or less consistent pattern of thought and action. Borrowed customs are reshaped to fit the dominant purposes of the receiving culture. To describe such integration she drew on ideas from Gestalt psychology and German historical thought, and took from Friedrich Nietzsche the contrast between "Apollonian" and "Dionysian" orientations.

## Three Cases

The Zuni, one of the Pueblo peoples of New Mexico, among whom Benedict had done fieldwork, are presented as Apollonian: they value moderation, ceremony and cooperation and distrust individual excess.

The people of Dobu, an island off eastern New Guinea, are described chiefly on the basis of Reo Fortune's *Sorcerers of Dobu* (1932). Benedict portrays a culture marked by suspicion, rivalry and fear of sorcery.

The Kwakiutl (Kwakwaka'wakw) of the Northwest Coast, known largely through Boas's research, are presented as Dionysian, with an intense pursuit of status expressed in the potlatch, where property was distributed or destroyed in competition for prestige.

## Culture and the Individual

Benedict argues that most people are shaped to fit the pattern of their culture, while some find their temperament at odds with it. What counts as abnormal in one society may be accepted or honoured in another. From this she draws a case for tolerance and for the comparative study of one's own customs as one possibility among many, a position closely associated with cultural relativism.

## Reception and Influence

The book became one of the most widely read works of anthropology and was translated into many languages. It helped popularise the anthropological concept of culture and contributed to the "culture and personality" school. Benedict later applied a configurational approach to Japan in *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946).

## Criticism

Critics argue that Benedict's portraits exaggerate coherence and play down internal variation, conflict and historical change, including the effects of colonial rule. The Chinese anthropologist Li An-che, writing on Zuni in 1937, questioned aspects of the picture of Pueblo life, and later researchers disputed her characterisations of the other cases, which in two instances rest mainly on the work of others. Psychological labels for whole societies have been judged impressionistic, and relativism raises the question of how practices may be criticised.
