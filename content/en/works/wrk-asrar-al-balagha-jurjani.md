# Asrar al-Balagha (The Secrets of Eloquence)

A major work of Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani, read together with his *Dala'il al-I'jaz*. The relative order of the two books is not securely known. *Dala'il al-I'jaz* is concerned mainly with grammatical composition (*naẓm*, later absorbed into the science of meanings), while *Asrar al-Balagha* elaborates figurative expression (simile, metaphor and metonymy). That material later became a core of the "science of figurative expression" (*ʿilm al-bayān*) as organized by al-Sakkaki.

## Context of Composition and al-Jurjani's Life

Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani lived in the fifth century AH (eleventh century CE) in the city of Jurjan in northern Iran, and died in 471 AH / 1078 CE (some sources give 474 AH). He grew up in an intellectual environment devoted to grammar, rhetoric and Quranic studies. He wrote *Asrar al-Balagha* in the context of a broad debate in his age on the rhetorical inimitability of the Quran: does inimitability lie in the individual words themselves, or in the manner in which they are arranged and composed? His entire theoretical project, including this book, was a response to those who claimed that the eloquence of the Quran lies in the choice of individual words apart from their arrangement. He insisted instead that the true source of eloquence lies in the precise syntactic and semantic relations among words.

## The Analysis of Simile: From the Sensory to the Intellectual

Al-Jurjani devoted a large section of the book to a detailed analysis of simile, distinguishing its types according to the nature of the point of resemblance (*wajh al-shabah*): whether it is an apparent sensory quality or a hidden intellectual one that requires reflection. He explained how the rhetorical value of a simile varies according to the "strangeness" of the point of resemblance and the subtlety of its discovery, rather than merely its presence.

## Metaphor as a Claim, Not a Simple Trope

Al-Jurjani offered an original analysis of metaphor. It is not merely the substitution of one word for another on the basis of a relation of similarity. Rather it is an implicit **claim** that the thing metaphorically described is in fact the thing it is described as (for example, saying "I saw a lion" of a brave man, meaning the claim that he is a real lion, as hyperbole). This "claim" is the source of the metaphor's rhetorical power, not merely the fact that it is a simile with the particle omitted.

## Metonymy and Allusion

Alongside simile and metaphor, al-Jurjani treats metonymy (*kinaya*) and allusion (*taʿrid*) with careful attention in *Asrar al-Balagha*. He distinguishes metonymy from explicit metaphor by noting that metonymy permits the literal meaning to be intended alongside the figurative meaning without contradiction (as in the saying "so-and-so has a long sword-strap," a metonymy for tallness of stature, while his sword-strap may indeed be long), whereas in metaphor the literal meaning cannot be intended. He distinguished degrees of metonymy according to the clarity of the transition from the concomitant to the implied attribute, from clear close metonymy to remote metonymy that requires deep reflection to grasp its intent. This distinction later enriched the classifications of metonymy by rhetoricians.

## The Method of Taste and the Psychological Analysis of Reception

The style of al-Jurjani in this book is distinguished by its reliance on a fine psychological analysis of the process of literary reception. He does not merely classify rhetorical images formally. He constantly asks: why is the soul stirred by one particular rhetorical image more than another? What gives some metaphors a power of effect that surpasses a direct simile with the same meaning? This method led him to the idea that rhetorical pleasure springs from a mental effort that the reader makes in discovering the hidden relationship between the two terms of the image. The greater this controlled effort (without excess reaching the point of impenetrable obscurity), the greater the aesthetic pleasure of the text. This idea has been compared with modern discussions of the relationship between cognitive effort and aesthetic enjoyment.

## Relationship to *Dala'il al-I'jaz*

*Asrar al-Balagha* and *Dala'il al-I'jaz* form a single, integrated theoretical project. In *Dala'il al-I'jaz*, al-Jurjani addressed the question of *nazm*, namely how words are arranged grammatically according to the requirements of grammatical meanings (such as fronting, postponement, ellipsis and mention), thereby laying the foundations for what would later be known as the science of meanings. In *Asrar al-Balagha* he focused on the individual figurative image itself, apart from the broader syntactic context of the sentence. Many modern scholars have argued that the two books should be read together as a single theoretical project: one explains the condition of *when* an expression is eloquent at the level of the sentence, and the other the condition of *how* the individual image within that sentence is aesthetically effective.

## Impact

Together with *Dala'il al-I'jaz*, this book formed the theoretical basis on which the science of figurative expression was later built by al-Sakkaki in the *Miftah al-Ulum* and by al-Qazwini in the *Talkhis al-Miftah*. Its detailed analyses of metaphor, simile and metonymy have remained a basic reference in Arabic rhetorical studies up to the modern era. Modern literary-critical scholarship, for example Kamal Abu-Deeb's structuralist study of his theory of imagery, has re-read al-Jurjani with the tools of modern critical theory.
