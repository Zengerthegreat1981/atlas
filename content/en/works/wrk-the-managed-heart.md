# The Managed Heart

## Historical Context and Author

Arlie Russell Hochschild published *The Managed Heart: Commercialization of Human Feeling* in 1983, when service work was overtaking manufacturing as the centre of the American economy. Airlines, banks, hospitals, and call centres employed millions whose main product was an interaction, and whose employers treated the worker's feelings as part of the job. Hochschild, a sociologist at the University of California, Berkeley, had been thinking about emotion since her 1979 essay "Emotion Work, Feeling Rules, and Social Structure." The book appeared alongside the early feminist sociology of women's work and the new sociology of emotions, giving both fields their central concept: emotional labor. Hochschild (born 1940) grew up the daughter of a diplomat, watching her mother host gatherings where the family's standing depended on managed feeling — charm, deference, warmth performed on schedule. Trained at Berkeley, she worked in the tradition of C. Wright Mills and Erving Goffman, combining Mills's attention to biography and structure with Goffman's analysis of self-presentation.

## Core Argumentative Structure

The book's thesis is that in service work, managing feeling becomes part of the job and is sold for a wage. Hochschild distinguishes emotion work — the private management of feeling, as when we try to feel cheerful at a party — from emotional labor, the same work done as a job requirement under employers' feeling rules. The argument proceeds through two contrasting cases. Delta Air Lines flight attendants, mostly women, were trained to produce warmth, reassurance, and sexualised friendliness: the smile was part of the product. Bill collectors, mostly men, were trained to produce the opposite — controlled menace, the suggestion of consequences without open threats. In both cases feelings were commodified, at the cost of estrangement: workers lost touch with their own emotions, unsure whether what they felt was theirs or the company's.

## Method and Key Concepts

Hochschild's method is qualitative: interviews with attendants and collectors, training sessions, company manuals, and workplace observation. The book's evidence is the workers' own accounts — forced smiles, suppressed anger at abusive passengers, going home unable to feel anything at all. She also draws on her theory of feeling rules: the norms telling us what we should feel, in what situations, and how intensely. Emotional labor is the book's signature concept, now used across sociology, management studies, psychology, and everyday language. Surface acting is displaying a feeling one does not have — the forced smile — while deep acting is working on oneself to actually feel what the job requires. Feeling rules define appropriate emotion in a situation, and the managed heart names the consequence: emotive dissonance, a split between felt and displayed emotion that can lead to burnout and a loss of the capacity to feel at all.

## Evidence and Findings

Delta's training taught attendants to imagine the cabin as their living room and passengers as guests, a deep-acting technique that made warmth feel authentic — and harder to leave behind after the shift. Attendants reported suppressing fear during turbulence and anger at harassment, and described the exhaustion of sustaining friendliness for hours. Bill collectors learned to calibrate threat: too little and debts went unpaid, too much and the debtor complained. They too reported costs — the difficulty of turning off suspicion and aggression at home. Hochschild also documents the gendered division of this labor: warmth jobs went to women, menace jobs to men, and pay and prestige followed the same pattern. The evidence is small-scale but vivid, and later research has largely supported its broad claims.

## Influence and Continuing Relevance

*The Managed Heart* founded the sociology of emotional labor and reshaped the sociology of work. Its concept travelled into organisational studies, nursing, teaching, and customer service, and is now a standard term in management textbooks. After Hochschild, feelings could no longer be treated as private or biological, but had to be analysed as socially structured and economically exploited. Feminist sociology took up its account of how women's feeling work is devalued and naturalised — treated as something women simply do, not skilled labor. The book is more relevant now than when written: the smile is now a rating, a review, a metric. Later research has applied the concept to customer-facing work monitored by digital systems and to care work, and public discussion of burnout in nursing and teaching has drawn on the vocabulary Hochschild introduced.

## Critical Assessments and Limitations

Critics question whether two cases support so general a theory, and whether surface and deep acting are as distinct as Hochschild presents them. Some argue workers are not passive victims of feeling rules but resist, reinterpret, and play with them. Others note the account of estrangement owes much to Marx, and ask whether the alienation described is specific to emotional labor or a feature of wage labor in general. The gender analysis, pioneering in 1983, has been extended by scholars showing how race and class shape feeling rules too. Scholars also debate the concept's boundaries: does emotional labor include unpaid work in families? Hochschild herself reserves emotional labor for paid work and emotion work for private life, but popular usage has blurred the line — a sign of the concept's success and a problem for its precision.
