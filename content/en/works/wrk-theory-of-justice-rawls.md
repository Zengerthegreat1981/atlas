# A Theory of Justice

The reference work of John Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, was published in 1971 by Harvard University Press. It is widely regarded as **one of the most influential works of political philosophy of the twentieth century**. It revived the tradition of the social contract after decades in which utilitarianism had dominated Anglo-Saxon moral and political philosophy.

## Justice as a Prior Purposive Principle: A Comparison with al-Shatibi

Rawls's project shares with purposive Islamic jurisprudence the view that **justice** is a guiding value for any legitimate institutional design, not an incidental outcome of a balance of power. The foundations, however, differ radically. Rawls builds his principles of justice from a hypothetical contractual thought experiment that rests on neither revelation nor text. Al-Shatibi derives the objectives of the Shariah inductively from the revealed texts themselves. The first is rationalist and purely contractual; the second is inductive and textual. Yet both regard the protection of the interests of the weak as a central criterion of the legitimacy of a system. The contrast can be seen between the jurisprudential principle of removing hardship (*raf' al-haraj*) and Rawls's difference principle.

## The Thought Experiment: The Original Position and the Veil of Ignorance

Rawls proposes an intellectual procedure for choosing fair principles of justice. He imagines rational individuals who gather in the **Original Position** behind a **Veil of Ignorance**. None of them knows his future place in society: whether he will be rich or poor, belong to a minority or a majority, or be healthy or disabled. Under this ignorance of their own particular interests, Rawls argues that the individuals would choose principles of justice that are **structurally neutral**, favoring no particular group. This is because each might find himself in any social position once the veil is lifted.

## The Principles of Justice

Rawls derives two principles, arranged in lexical order (the first takes priority over the second). The second principle has two parts, fair equality of opportunity (which takes priority) and the difference principle. The first is the **principle of equal liberty**: each person has an equal right to the most extensive system of basic liberties compatible with a similar liberty for others. The second is the **difference principle**: social and economic inequalities are justified only if they are to the greatest benefit of the **least advantaged** members of society, not to the benefit of the majority or the statistical average, as in utilitarianism.

## Critique of Utilitarianism

Rawls directs a central critique at utilitarianism, which dominated British moral philosophy. He argues that by seeking to maximize the overall utility of society, utilitarianism is **willing to sacrifice the rights of a minority** if that sacrifice increases the total sum of utility. This contradicts our basic moral intuition that every individual has a dignity and rights that may not be sacrificed merely for the sake of collective utility calculations.

## Reception and Criticism

The book sparked a revolution in political philosophy and restored the standing of the social contract tradition. It faced sharp criticism from several directions. Robert Nozick, in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), objected that the difference principle justifies continual redistribution that violates the right to property legitimately acquired. Communitarians, such as Michael Sandel, objected that the image of the self behind the veil of ignorance presupposes an abstract subject detached from its organic social attachments, which is not realistic. Amartya Sen and others offered a critique from the standpoint of capabilities. They argued that focusing on the distribution of "primary goods" overlooks the differences among individuals in their ability to convert these goods into actual well-being.

## Later Development: From "Comprehensive Theory" to "Political Liberalism"

In response to the communitarian critique, Rawls later refined his position in *Political Liberalism* (1993). He retreated from the claim that his theory rests on a comprehensive metaphysical conception of the human self. He proposed instead that the principles of justice could attain an **overlapping consensus** among adherents of different comprehensive doctrines, whether religious or secular. Each of them would endorse the same basic political principles for his own internal reasons, without any of them adopting the doctrine of the others.

## Priority of Liberty and the Just Savings Principle

Rawls adds an important condition to the difference principle: the **priority of liberty**. This means that no basic liberty (such as freedom of expression or of conscience) may be sacrificed in exchange for economic gains, even if those gains would benefit the least advantaged. This lexical ordering sharply distinguishes Rawls from any utilitarian calculation that would allow liberty to be traded for material welfare. The book also discusses the **just savings principle** between generations. It obliges each generation to leave to later generations a share of resources and just institutions. In this way, Rawls extends the scope of the original contract to include justice between generations, not only among members of a single generation.

## Its Place in the Revival of the Social Contract Tradition

The book restored the standing of a philosophical tradition (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Kant) that had receded in favor of utilitarian dominance throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. It offered a contemporary form that replaces a story of the origin of society (as in the classical contract theories) with an explicitly hypothetical thought experiment (the Original Position). This made the book a governing methodological model even for its critics, such as Nozick, who adopted the same contractual form in order to draw conclusions opposite to Rawls's.
