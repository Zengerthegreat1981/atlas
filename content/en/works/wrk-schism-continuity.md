# Schism and Continuity in an African Society

*Schism and Continuity in an African Society: A Study of Ndembu Village Life* (1957) is Victor Turner's monograph on the Ndembu, in what was then Northern Rhodesia and is now Zambia. Based on fieldwork in the early 1950s under the auspices of the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute, it analyses how villages form, quarrel, divide, and reproduce themselves. It introduced the concept of social drama.

## The problem

Ndembu villages were small and mobile. Turner identified a tension between matrilineal descent, which determined succession and inheritance, and virilocal marriage, by which wives moved to live with their husbands. A man's heirs were his sister's sons, yet his own children grew up with him, while women's children linked the village to other places. Hunting values, men's attachment to their matrilineal kin, and competition for the headmanship added further strain. Marriages were fragile, and individuals could shift residence in pursuit of advantage or safety.

## Social drama

To study these tensions in action, Turner followed a sequence of disputes in one village over many years, with particular attention to an ambitious man named Sandombu. Each drama is analysed in four phases: a breach of a regular norm; a mounting crisis in which wider cleavages appear; redressive action, which may be arbitration, legal procedure, or ritual; and either reintegration or recognition of an irreparable schism. Accusations of sorcery or witchcraft expressed rivalry and could justify separation.

The method shows that rules do not by themselves determine outcomes. People appeal selectively to principles of kinship and residence, and the same conflict resurfaces in later episodes. Genealogies and village censuses accompany the narratives, so that individual actions can be set against statistical patterns.

## Ritual and continuity

Turner argued that cults of affliction, performed for people troubled by ancestral spirits, drew participants from many villages and affirmed ties and values that crossed local divisions. A village might split while the wider moral community persisted. This interest in ritual developed into his later studies of symbols, collected in *The Forest of Symbols* (1967), and of ritual process.

## Setting and influence

The book exemplifies the Manchester approach associated with Max Gluckman, who contributed to its intellectual framework, in its use of extended cases and its attention to conflict. Its explanation still tends to find that conflict ultimately renews a social order, and critics have asked how far that assumption directs the interpretation. Colonial taxation, cash earning, and labour migration appear in the account, but the central analysis concerns village structure. Readers also note that the dramas are reconstructed by the ethnographer, whose sources and relationships shape which version of a quarrel is told. The concept of social drama was later applied by Turner and others to politics, religious history, and theatre.
