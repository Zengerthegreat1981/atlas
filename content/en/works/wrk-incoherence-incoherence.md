# Tahafut al-Tahafut (The Incoherence of the Incoherence)

## Definition

**The Incoherence of the Incoherence** (*Tahafut al-Tahafut*) is a philosophical book written by the Andalusian philosopher **Ibn Rushd** (Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad, 1126–1198 CE) around 1180 CE. It is a direct and detailed refutation of **Abu Hamid al-Ghazali**'s book *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahafut al-Falasifa*, 1095 CE). In that book al-Ghazali criticised the Muslim Peripatetic philosophers, especially al-Farabi and Ibn Sina, on twenty issues, charging them with unbelief on three and with heresy (*bid'a*) on the rest. The three charged with unbelief were the claim of the eternity of the world, the denial of God's knowledge of particulars, and the denial of bodily resurrection.

## Structure and Method

The book follows a **passage-by-passage** structure. Ibn Rushd quotes al-Ghazali's text verbatim and then refutes it issue by issue, so that the book becomes an implicit three-way dialogue between al-Ghazali, Ibn Sina (whom al-Ghazali was essentially answering), and Ibn Rushd himself. Ibn Rushd often criticizes Ibn Sina's own formulations, holding that al-Ghazali's critique sometimes hit Ibn Sina's distortion of Aristotle more than it hit philosophy itself. Ibn Rushd's defence turns on three major issues. The first is the **eternity of the world**, in which he distinguished between "creation from absolute non-being" and "perpetual origination," which does not entail a temporal beginning. The second is **God's knowledge of particulars**, formulated in a way that preserves God's universal and comprehensive knowledge without human-style detail. The third is the **relation between reason and revelation**, which he treated at greater length in his accompanying treatise *Fasl al-Maqal* (The Decisive Treatise). There he affirmed that demonstrative reason and revealed law do not contradict one another when each is understood correctly.

## Reception and Influence

In the Islamic world, the book failed to halt the decline of the influence of Peripatetic philosophy before the Ash'ari theological school, which remained the most influential until the modern age. The book itself had a more limited reach in Latin Europe, where it was translated into Hebrew in the fourteenth century and later into Latin as *Destructio destructionum*, but Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle, translated into Latin from the thirteenth century, gave him wide standing there. He became known in the Latin West by the title **"the Commentator"** on Aristotle, and his work founded an independent philosophical current known as **Latin Averroism**, in the universities of Paris and Padua. It provoked a wide ecclesiastical controversy, such as the condemnations of Paris in 1270 and 1277, over the eternity of the world and the unity of the intellect. Its influence continued until the European Renaissance.

## Caveats

Despite its defensive aim, Ibn Rushd was not a pure defender of every position of Ibn Sina. The book is in essence an independent philosophical project that seeks to recover the **authentic Aristotle**, not one overlaid with Neoplatonic interpretations as Ibn Sina did. This makes it more precise to read it as a "defence of philosophy" than as a "defence of Ibn Sina specifically."
