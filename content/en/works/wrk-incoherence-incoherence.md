# Tahafut al-Tahafut (The Incoherence of the Incoherence)

## Definition

*Tahafut al-Tahafut* (*The Incoherence of the Incoherence*) is a philosophical reply by the Andalusian jurist and philosopher Ibn Rushd (Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Ahmad, 520–595 AH / 1126–1198 CE), written around 1180. It answers Abu Hamid al-Ghazali's *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (*The Incoherence of the Philosophers*), composed in Baghdad around 1095. Al-Ghazali set out twenty objections to the Muslim Peripatetics, above all al-Farabi and Ibn Sina. He held that three of their doctrines were unbelief: the eternity of the world, the claim that God does not know particulars, and the denial of bodily resurrection. He classed the other seventeen as innovation (*bid'a*).

## Structure and method

Ibn Rushd quotes al-Ghazali and answers him discussion by discussion, so that the book sets al-Ghazali's charges beside the positions of Ibn Sina and beside Ibn Rushd's own reading of Aristotle. He often treats a charge as striking a formulation of Ibn Sina rather than an argument he is willing to attribute to Aristotle.

On the eternity of the world, he argues that an eternal effect can still be a created effect, because it depends at every moment on an eternal cause. A beginning in time out of absolute non-existence is, on this account, a different claim from the philosophers' claim. On God's knowledge, he rejects the formula he assigns to Ibn Sina, that God knows particulars only in a universal way. Divine knowledge, as he describes it, is not human knowledge of a universal or of a particular. It is the cause of the things known. On bodily resurrection his reply is more guarded. He holds that the philosophers affirm a return after death, and that the disagreement concerns how the revealed descriptions of that return are to be understood.

The relation of demonstration to the revealed law is argued more fully in *Fasl al-Maqal* (*The Decisive Treatise*) and in *al-Kashf 'an Manahij al-Adilla*, both from the same period of his work. *Fasl al-Maqal* maintains that demonstrative inquiry is permitted by the law, and that a sound demonstration and a sound reading of revelation agree.

## Reception

In much of the Sunni world the book did not take the place of al-Ghazali's *Tahafut* as the familiar account of the philosophers. Ash'ari kalam remained the usual scholastic theology in many regions, and *Tahafut al-Tahafut* circulated less widely than al-Ghazali's book. Philosophical writing continued in the Islamic East in other genres, including commentary on Ibn Sina.

In Latin Europe Ibn Rushd's standing came first from his commentaries on Aristotle, translated from the thirteenth century, which earned him the scholastic title "the Commentator." Teaching drawn from those commentaries, including the eternity of the world and the unity of the intellect, was condemned at Paris in 1270 and again in 1277. A current later called Latin Averroism developed at Paris and, over a longer period, at Padua. This book reached Latin and Hebrew later. Kalonymos ben Kalonymos translated it into Latin in 1328. That version, *Destructio destructionum*, was printed at Venice in 1497 with Agostino Nifo's commentary and was reprinted in the sixteenth century. A second Latin translation of the 1520s was made from an earlier Hebrew translation. The Latin career of this text belongs to the fourteenth century and the Renaissance, and it is separate from the thirteenth-century condemnations.

## Caveats

The book defends philosophical demonstration, and it repeatedly corrects Ibn Sina where Ibn Rushd held that he had departed from Aristotle. A long-standing reading describes that correction as a recovery of Aristotle. How far Ibn Rushd's Aristotle is his own reconstruction has to be weighed discussion by discussion. The placing of the book around 1180 is a scholarly estimate.
