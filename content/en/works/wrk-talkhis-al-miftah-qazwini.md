# Talkhis al-Miftah

A systematic summary of the rhetorical section of *Miftah al-Ulum* (The Key to the Sciences) by Abu Ya'qub al-Sakkaki (d. 626 AH / 1229). Al-Khatib al-Qazwini composed it in the eighth century AH (the fourteenth century CE). It became a standard teaching text of Arabic rhetoric (*balagha*).

## Method of Summarization

Al-Qazwini reduced al-Sakkaki's lengthy exposition to a concentrated formulation suitable for memorization and instruction. He preserved the basic tripartite structure (*ma'ani*, *bayan*, *badi'*) and the essential definitions, but omitted the heavy logical and philosophical digressions that characterized al-Sakkaki's original style.

## A Network of Commentaries and Glosses

The book's extreme brevity created a constant need for explanatory commentaries. Numerous influential Arabic commentaries appeared over the centuries (most notably Sa'd al-Din al-Taftazani's *al-Mutawwal* and *al-Mukhtasar*), until an integrated instructional system of text, commentaries, and glosses formed around it, which remained the backbone of the teaching of traditional Arabic rhetoric.

## The Author and the Context of Composition

Al-Khatib al-Qazwini, Jalal al-Din Muhammad ibn Abd al-Rahman (666–739 AH / 1268–1338), was preacher (*khatib*) at the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, the source of his title, and later chief judge in Damascus and then in Egypt under the Mamluks. Beside the *Talkhis* he wrote *al-Idah fi Ulum al-Balagha* (The Clarification of the Sciences of Rhetoric), which explains and expands the summary: a short text for memorization and a longer one for further study.

## The Tripartite Scientific Structure

The book's content is distributed across three rhetorical sciences. Al-Sakkaki's *Miftah al-Ulum* systematized *ma'ani* and *bayan* (with *badi'* treated more briefly as an appendix), and al-Qazwini gave the three-part division its standard form: the science of *ma'ani* (the conformity of speech to the requirements of the situation, including the topics of concision, elaboration, restriction, and the separation and connection of clauses), the science of *bayan* (the ways of expressing a single meaning in multiple forms, such as simile, metaphor, and metonymy), and the science of *badi'* (the verbal and semantic embellishments that enhance speech, such as paronomasia and antithesis). Through this short, widely copied book, that three-part division became the usual framework of later Arabic rhetorical teaching.

## Its Relation to the Work of Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani

The theoretical framework that al-Qazwini summarized from al-Sakkaki is rooted in the foundational theory of *nazm* (syntactic arrangement) developed by Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani in the fifth century AH (in his two books *Dala'il al-I'jaz* and *Asrar al-Balagha*). That theory tied the eloquence of speech to the grammatical relations of composition among words rather than to the beauty of individual words in isolation. Al-Sakkaki's work and al-Qazwini's summary therefore constitute a later pedagogical systematization of this deep Jurjanian theoretical foundation, which made it easier to teach and circulate, without adding a radical theoretical innovation comparable in depth to the original Jurjani.

## Impact

The book and its commentaries long served as the standard rhetoric text in traditional Arabic and Islamic learning, and they continue to be studied in traditional curricula.

## Continued Circulation in Contemporary Arab Education

*Talkhis al-Miftah* and its commentaries (especially al-Taftazani's *al-Mutawwal* and *al-Mukhtasar*) have long been studied in traditional institutions such as al-Azhar, and in some modern faculties of Sharia and Arabic language, as the traditional gateway to the study of the sciences of *ma'ani*, *bayan*, and *badi'* before moving on to the direct study of applied rhetorical texts (such as the rhetorical inimitability of the Quran). The text has therefore remained in use for centuries in that curriculum.

## Al-Qazwini as a Judge and His Relation to the Mamluk Context

Al-Khatib al-Qazwini served as chief judge in Damascus and then in Egypt under the Mamluk sultanate, a high official post that reflects his broad scholarly standing, which was not limited to rhetoric alone but extended to jurisprudence and religious judicature. This was typical of many scholars of his era who combined religious, linguistic, and rhetorical disciplines in a single integrated scholarly persona, reflecting a traditional Islamic educational system that did not draw the strict boundaries between these sciences that modern academic specialization would assume.

## Printed Editions and Modern Critical Editions

Numerous printed editions of *Talkhis al-Miftah* have appeared from Arabic presses since the late nineteenth century, usually printed with the margin occupied by al-Taftazani's commentary *al-Mukhtasar* to facilitate the simultaneous study of the text and its standard commentary. More recent academic critical editions with greater textual precision have since been published, although the widest reliance in traditional study curricula remains on the familiar older editions accompanied by the glosses of successive scholars over centuries of continuous teaching.

## Its Comparative Standing Among Its Sources

*Talkhis al-Miftah* represents an intermediate link between three levels of the Arabic rhetorical text: the original, deeply theoretical and stylistically extended work of Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani; the more logically abstract and terminologically complex organization of al-Sakkaki; and the pedagogical concision of al-Qazwini, which was made accessible for broad instructional circulation. It was the success of this last concise version, rather than its original theoretical depth, that secured its wide circulation and practical survival in traditional teaching curricula, far beyond the circulation of its deeper theoretical sources.
