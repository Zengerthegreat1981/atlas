# On the Genealogy of Morals by Friedrich Nietzsche

## The central thesis

The book is not a logical analysis of the concepts of good and evil, but a **historical-genealogical** tracing of their origin: where did our moral judgments come from, who formulated them, and in whose interest? Nietzsche distinguishes between "master morality", in which "good" means nobility, strength and fullness, and "slave morality", in which these values are inverted through **ressentiment**: the weak, unable to take actual revenge, invent an imaginary revenge by turning the master's strength into "evil" and their own impotence into "good" (humility, mercy). This inversion is what Nietzsche calls the "slave revolt in morality". The book is thus a thesis about method as much as about content: understanding a moral concept requires uncovering the history of its emergence, not analyzing its logical structure.

## The structure of the work

The full title is "On the Genealogy of Morals: A Polemic" (Eine Streitschrift), consisting of three essays independent in subject but sequential in argument, preceded by a preface saying that "moral scientists" never asked about the value of values themselves.

1. **"Good and Evil" / "Good and Bad"**: the emergence of master morality and slave morality and the inversion of values through ressentiment. Nietzsche works here on linguistic derivation to show that "good" first designated the noble, that "bad" designated the common, and that Judaism and then Christianity reversed the equation. The famous image in this essay is that of lambs and birds of prey: the lambs hold the birds of prey responsible for being birds of prey.
2. **"Guilt, Bad Conscience, and the Like"**: he derives the concept of "guilt" (Schuld) from the material concept of "debt" (Schulden) in the relation of creditor and debtor, not from a metaphysical origin. He presents the human being as "the animal that makes promises", and holds that memory was made with pain. And "bad conscience" is the result of the repression of aggressive instincts when they were prevented from external discharge in organized society and turned back upon the self.
3. **"What Is the Meaning of Ascetic Ideals?"**: he analyzes the spread of the ascetic ideal, in the figure of the priest, the philosopher and the scholar, as an attempt to give suffering a meaning, not to be rid of it, and concludes that man "would rather will nothingness than not will". In it Nietzsche presents the idea of perspectivism, that is, that there is no knowledge except from a perspective, and that science itself is a more recent form of the ascetic ideal because it retains the belief in truth.

## The historical context of publication

Nietzsche wrote the book in the summer of 1887 and it was published in Leipzig by Naumann in the same year, supplementing and elaborating the theses of *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886), and he described it in *Ecce Homo* as "three decisive preliminary studies by a psychologist for a revaluation of all values". In its preface he was replying to Paul Rée's book *The Origin of the Moral Sensations* (1877) and to the "English psychologists" who reduced morality to utility, and he presented a counter-genealogy that brings force, power and ressentiment into the explanation of its emergence. It has no direct connection with the eternal recurrence or the Superman, which belong to *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*; the *Genealogy* is an analytical-historical prose text, not a literary-symbolic discourse.

## Editions

The standard critical edition of the German text is the Colli–Montinari edition (*Kritische Gesamtausgabe*, from 1967, and the paperback *Kritische Studienausgabe*, 1980, in which the *Genealogy* appears in volume 5). A widely used English translation is Walter Kaufmann's (1967).

## Reception and influence

The book became a reference in historical moral philosophy and in the study of guilt and conscience. It is usually paired with Freud's analysis of the superego and the sense of guilt in *Civilization and Its Discontents*, although Freud himself said that he avoided reading Nietzsche, so the connection here is the work of commentators, not an acknowledgment by the theory's author. Michel Foucault drew from the book his genealogical method in his essay "Nietzsche, Genealogy, History" (1971) and in his study of systems of knowledge and power. Gilles Deleuze wrote *Nietzsche and Philosophy* (1962) and made ressentiment the axis of his reading, and Brian Leiter in *Nietzsche on Morality* (2002) presented a naturalistic reading that separates genealogical critique from evaluative critique.

## The Arab reader

The Arab reader first knew Nietzsche through literature and criticism: Abd al-Rahman Badawi published his book *Nietzsche* in Cairo in 1939, one of the first serious Arabic introductions to his philosophy.

## Limits and critique of the work

Historians fault Nietzsche for weak historical and philological accuracy: his derivation of "good" from nobility is open to linguistic dispute, the image of masters and slaves is a generalization without sufficient historical evidence, and his linking of guilt to debt is exaggerated according to anthropologists. Critics note that Nietzsche relies on what he criticizes, namely the values of truthfulness and intellectual integrity that are the offspring of the ascetic ideal, and that he sometimes uses a language of contempt that readers find troubling, and that the distortion of his manuscripts after his death by his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche remained for decades a source of misreading of him. His deepest argument, that values have a history and that appeal to their "nature" is not enough, remains a point of agreement more than the details of his genealogy.
