# The Precariat: The New Dangerous Class

A book by the British economist **Guy Standing**, published in 2011 by Bloomsbury Academic, which introduces the concept of the **"precariat"**, a word coined from "precarious" and "proletariat", as a new social class emerging from the neoliberal globalization and the flexibility of labor markets since the 1980s.

## Central Thesis

Standing argues that the neoliberal transformations of the global labor market (temporary work, part-time contracts, the dismantling of trade unions and the decline of social protection) have produced a growing class of millions of people who lack the **seven forms of labour security** that characterized the industrial working class of the twentieth century: labour market security, employment security, job security, work security (health and safety), skill reproduction security, income security and representation security. In this sense the precariat is not simply the poor or the unemployed, but a group living with unstable work and income, lacking a stable professional identity and the institutional belonging that the industrial working class once provided.

## Why a "Dangerous" Class?

The subtitle describes this class as "dangerous" because, according to Standing, its rapid growth produces a condition of **social instability**: the precariat is internally divided (between migrants and citizens, between educated unemployed and unskilled workers), which makes it vulnerable to extremist political mobilization that directs its anger toward the weakest groups (such as migrants) rather than toward the real source of its insecurity within the structure of the economy itself. The absence of traditional political representation for this group (since neither the old industrial unions nor the traditional parties represent it), in Standing's view, makes it susceptible to attraction toward right-wing or left-wing populism alike.

## Political Proposals

In the book's conclusion, Standing calls for redesigning distributive policies so that the demands of the precariat stand at the heart of any progressive strategy. Among his most prominent practical proposals is the **universal basic income**, as a mechanism for separating material security from the condition of stable waged employment. Standing developed this proposal further in his later work, being one of the founders of the Basic Income Earth Network (BIEN).

## Reception and Impact

The book received wide attention in public debate about work and globalization. The term "precariat" became widely used in sociology, economic anthropology and labor studies, and it became associated with discussions after the 2008 financial crisis about the future of the middle class.

## Limitations

The book has been criticized from several angles. Some have considered the definition of the "precariat" too loose to be a social class in the precise Marxist sense (a class sharing a specific relation of production), since it groups together categories that differ greatly in income, education and the actual degree of precariousness. It has also been objected that the thesis is generalized mainly from the context of Western Europe and North America to the whole world.

## Author and Context

Guy Standing is a British economist who worked for many years at the International Labour Organization in Geneva on questions of economic security and labor markets, and later became a professorial research associate at the School of Oriental and African Studies (SOAS), University of London. His book followed many years of work on labour-market flexibility and economic security, and his objection to the idea that deregulating the labor market creates stable jobs. It was preceded by French and Italian literature on precariousness, including Pierre Bourdieu's "La précarité est aujourd'hui partout" (translated as "Job Insecurity Is Everywhere Now"), published in *Contre-feux* (1998). Brett Neilson and Ned Rossiter discussed the concept's history in "Precarity as a Political Concept, or, Fordism as Exception" (*Theory, Culture and Society*, 2008).

## Standing's Class Structure

Standing classifies contemporary society into seven groups: the elite, which owns wealth and power; the stable salaried employees with privileges; the holders of portable skills who work on short contracts without institutional belonging; the traditional manual workers, whose numbers are declining; the precariat; the permanent unemployed; and finally the marginalized, who live outside the system. He holds that the precariat is distinguished by three features: unstable relations of production, distributive relations based on a cash wage without benefits, and an ambiguous relationship with the state, because its members gradually lose the rights of citizenship. In *A Precariat Charter* (2014) and later writing he identifies three factions within it: the atavists, who look back to a lost past of stable industrial work; the nostalgics, who lack a sense of home or belonging (migrants, for example); and the progressives, who formulate new demands for security and freedom.

## Unaccounted Labour

The book highlights that the precariat spends much time on unpaid activities, such as searching for work, filling out forms, renewing documents and acquiring skills, which he calls "work-for-labour". He argues that the state places on the individual the burden of proving their eligibility for assistance, so that the poor are turned into unpaid employees serving the administration. He also discusses indebtedness, noting that many members of the precariat borrow for study and housing.

## Connection to the Arab Region

In *Revolution without Revolutionaries: Making Sense of the Arab Spring* (Stanford University Press, 2017), Asef Bayat discusses categories of young people, including educated youth, with unstable incomes and informal work, categories that overlap with what Standing calls the precariat, although Bayat's framework differs from Standing's. The concept carries particular significance in countries where informal work and labor migration are widespread, and where precarity coexists with sponsorship systems that tie the right of residence to the employer.

## Additional Critique

Jan Breman argued in "A Bogus Concept?" (*New Left Review*, 2013) that the book replaces the history of informal labour in the South, where precarity has long been the norm, with an image of a supposed golden age of stable industrial work in the North. Other critics have said that the proposal of a basic income ignores its cost and its effect on wages and public services.
