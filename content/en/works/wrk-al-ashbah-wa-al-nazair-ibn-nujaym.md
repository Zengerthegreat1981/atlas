# Al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir

A reference work on **legal maxims** (*qawa'id fiqhiyya*) by the Egyptian Hanafi jurist **Zayn al-Din ibn Ibrahim Ibn Nujaym** (d. 970 AH / 1563 CE). It is among the most important works of this genre in the Hanafi school. It bears the same title as an earlier Shafi'i work by Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti. Ibn Nujaym's book is generally understood to follow the genre and in part the arrangement of the Shafi'i works of this name (those of Taj al-Din al-Subki and al-Suyuti), but it is an independent work built on Hanafi branch rulings.

## General Structure

Ibn Nujaym organizes his book around a limited number of **overarching juristic maxims** (among the best known: "Actions are judged by their intentions," "Certainty is not removed by doubt," "Hardship brings facilitation," "Harm must be removed," and "Custom is authoritative"). Under each he presents dozens of Hanafi branch rulings that fall under it or are excepted from it. He adds sections on the differences between outwardly similar questions and on juristic puzzles (*alghaz*).

## The Theoretical Function of the Maxims

From the standpoint of legal theory, the maxims in this book do not function as decisive derivation rules that necessarily produce a single ruling. They function as organizing tools that reveal the internal coherence among thousands of scattered juristic branches across the different chapters of jurisprudence, reducing them to a limited number of shared principles. This makes the book closer to a project of "abstracting general principles from accumulated juristic practice" (inductively, from the bottom up) than to imposing them in advance (deductively, from the top down).

## Comparison with Its Counterparts in Other Schools

Ibn Nujaym was not alone in this effort. He was preceded by Jalal al-Din al-Suyuti the Shafi'i (d. 911 AH), whose work bears the same title, *al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir*. The Maliki school also had similar efforts, though less systematically arranged, and so did the Hanbali school. This near-simultaneous emergence of the literature of "legal maxims" across the four schools in the later centuries reflects a shared need among jurists of that era to organize a centuries-old accumulation of branch jurisprudence into a form that is easier to absorb and teach. Some contemporary scholars compare this to the movement of "codifying general principles" in other legal traditions, when a large body of precedents or branches accumulates to the point of requiring organized abstraction.

## The Nature of the Juristic Maxim and Its Limits

As is customary in this literature, Ibn Nujaym is careful to note that most of these overarching maxims are not universally valid without exception. In other words, they are generally applicable rather than strictly universal in the rigorous logical sense. After each maxim he therefore lists a number of "exceptions" (*mustathnayat*) that depart from the maxim for a particular reason. This structure, a general maxim followed by a list of exceptions, is sometimes compared in modern comparative legal studies with the structure of "rule and exception" in other legal traditions. The essential difference is that Ibn Nujaym's exceptions derive from an accumulation of historical juristic branches that preceded the formulation of the maxim itself, not from a later exceptional legislation.

## Its Impact on the Majalla

This book is among the most important textual sources drawn upon by the drafters of the Ottoman **Majallat al-Ahkam al-Adliyya** when they formulated its introductory part, which sets out ninety-nine overarching juristic maxims before entering into the substantive details. Ibn Nujaym's formulations were in several cases carried closely into a modern binding legal text, a clear example of the transfer of a classical juristic literature into a contemporary legislative form.

## Juristic Puzzles and Pedagogical Function

The book also contains less well-known sections beyond the part on general maxims, including a section on juristic puzzles (*alghaz*: questions phrased in a way that seems counterintuitive in their ruling and explained by mentioning their hidden rationale). Such sections reflect a pedagogical and training dimension of the book. They can be used to train students of jurisprudence to distinguish precisely between similar cases, not merely to recite rulings. This dimension shows that the book was not addressed only to the practicing jurist needing a quick reference. It was also a pedagogical tool in forming the juristic faculty of students. Thus the book combines a practical reference function with a formative educational function at the same time. This combination generally distinguishes the classical genre of juristic texts and commentaries from modern legal codes, which mostly serve only the first function.
