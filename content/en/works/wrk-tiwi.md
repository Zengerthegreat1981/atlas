# The Tiwi of North Australia

***The Tiwi of North Australia***, by **C. W. M. Hart** and **Arnold R. Pilling**, published in **1960**, is the classic ethnography of the Tiwi people of Melville and Bathurst Islands, off the north coast of Australia. It is famous for a single fact: Tiwi society was organised around a marriage system in which old men held a near-monopoly of wives, and young men could only obtain a wife by waiting, sometimes for decades, for an old man to die. The book used this striking arrangement to pose a general question — how does a society organise the distribution of its most valued resource, women in marriage, and what does that distribution do to politics, kinship, and everyday life?

Hart had first studied the Tiwi in the late 1920s, publishing his early findings in 1930, and he returned to the field in the 1950s with Pilling. The Tiwi, an Aboriginal people numbering around a thousand at the time of the study, lived by hunting, fishing, and gathering in a tropical island environment. They had no agriculture, no hereditary chiefs, and no centralised political organisation. What they had was a complex social life, organised through kinship, marriage, and a rich ceremonial life of song, dance, and mortuary ritual.

## Marriage and the Monopoly of Wives

The heart of the book is its account of Tiwi marriage. A Tiwi girl was betrothed at birth, often before she was born, to a man who might be forty or fifty years her senior. A successful old man might have several wives, and the most prominent men had as many as twenty or more. Since women were the most valuable resource in Tiwi life — they provided food, labour, and children, and a wife's kin were a man's allies — the old men's control of women was also control of wealth and power. Young men, by contrast, had no wives at all. They could obtain one only in three ways: by being given a wife by an older man who owed them a favour, by inheriting a widow when her husband died, or by abducting a woman and surviving the consequences.

Hart and Pilling argued that this system, strange as it looks, was stable and orderly. The old men's monopoly was not maintained by force alone but by a web of obligations: a young man who wanted a wife had to cultivate the favour of old men, work for them, and defer to them, and in doing so he was drawn into the political life of the community. Marriage was therefore the mechanism through which political authority was exercised. A man became powerful not by commanding an army or inheriting a title but by accumulating wives, and with wives came sons-in-law, allies, and dependants. The system also produced a distinctive emotional world: young men lived in a state of prolonged frustration and anticipation, and much of Tiwi social life — its songs, its quarrels, its ceremonies — turned on the politics of who would marry whom.

## Kinship, Politics, and Ceremony

The book shows how this marriage system was embedded in a wider kinship order. Tiwi kinship was classificatory: a person addressed a whole range of relatives by the same term, and the obligations attached to the term applied to all of them. The crucial relationship was between a man and his wife's mother's brother, who was also his father-in-law. This created a dense network of mutual obligation in which every marriage renewed an alliance between families.

Politics in Tiwi society was personal and competitive. There were no offices and no formal councils; influence belonged to men who could mobilise support, and support came through wives and sons-in-law. Disputes were settled by public discussion and by song duels in which grievances were aired in verse, and occasionally by violence.

Ceremonial life centred on the mortuary rites, the elaborate funeral ceremonies known as *pukamani*, in which the dead were honoured with carved poles, painted bodies, and days of dancing and singing. Hart and Pilling showed that these ceremonies were not only religious events but also political ones: occasions on which alliances were displayed, debts were settled, and the standing of the living was publicly assessed.

## Reception and Influence

*The Tiwi of North Australia* became one of the most widely read ethnographies in the discipline and a staple of undergraduate teaching. Its account of gerontocratic polygyny — rule by old men through the control of wives — entered textbooks as a classic case of how marriage systems shape political life, and it was widely discussed in debates about the evolution of marriage, the origins of inequality, and the relationship between kinship and politics. Jane Goodale's later study, *Tiwi Wives* (1971), complemented the book by examining the system from the women's point of view, showing that Tiwi women were not passive objects of exchange but active participants who used their own marriages, divorces, and alliances to pursue their interests.

Later scholars have qualified the book's picture in several ways. Some have argued that Hart and Pilling exaggerated the rigidity of the marriage system and underestimated the extent of individual negotiation, elopement, and resistance. Others have noted that the Tiwi they described were already living under the influence of the Catholic mission and the Australian administration, and that the "traditional" society of the book was in part a reconstruction. These criticisms have not displaced the book's central achievement: a clear, detailed, and analytically sharp account of a society organised around principles very different from those of the ethnographers' own world, and a demonstration that such a society has its own rigorous logic.
