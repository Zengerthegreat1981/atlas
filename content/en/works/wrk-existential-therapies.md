# Existential Therapies

*Existential Therapies* (2003), by the British psychologist Mick Cooper, is the book that made the plural in its title normal, and in doing so changed how the field is taught. Its central claim is that there is no single existential therapy. What travels under that name is a family of approaches that grew up in different countries, languages, and clinical traditions, and that disagree with one another on the most basic questions: what freedom means, what the therapist should do, and whether therapy should aim at meaning at all. Cooper's project is to survey this quarrelsome family in a single comparative language, so that a student can see the differences clearly instead of mistaking one current for the whole school. The first edition was published by Sage in London in 2003; a substantially revised second edition appeared in 2017.

## The Shared Philosophical Ground

Cooper begins with what the approaches share before turning to what divides them. The common ground is philosophical rather than technical. Existence precedes essence: a person is defined by what they do, not by a fixed nature. The human being is a being-in-the-world, not an isolated subject looking out at objects. Freedom is real but bounded — people choose within givens they did not choose, such as death, the body, and history — and anxiety is a condition of life rather than merely a symptom to be removed. From these commitments follow certain habits of practice: description and exploration are preferred to causal explanation and diagnosis, and the therapeutic relationship is treated as an encounter between two people rather than as a technique applied by an expert to a patient. But Cooper insists that these shared commitments are thin. They do not add up to a method, and the moment they are put into practice, the approaches diverge.

## The Five Approaches

The book's core is a chapter on each of the main approaches. Daseinsanalysis, the earliest current, descends from Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss and reads distress as a constriction of the patient's world — a narrowing of what can be experienced, felt, and done. Meaning-centred therapies, following Viktor Frankl's logotherapy, make the search for meaning the explicit goal of treatment, and hold that meaning can be found even in suffering. Existential-humanistic therapy, the American current associated with Rollo May, James Bugental, and Irvin Yalom, combines existential themes with humanistic psychology's emphasis on the lived encounter; Yalom's four ultimate concerns — death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness — are its best-known formulation. R. D. Laing's work is treated as a current of its own: an existential reading of psychosis that insists the patient's speech is intelligible once it is heard in the context of their life and family. Finally, existential-phenomenological therapy, the British school associated with Emmy van Deurzen and Ernesto Spinelli, is the most descriptive and least directive of the five, bracketing interpretation and theory in favour of a disciplined attention to the client's experience as it presents itself. Cooper shows that these approaches disagree in practice as well as in theory: the British phenomenologists, wary of confrontation and interpretation, work very differently from Yalom, who uses dynamic and interpretive interventions, and Frankl's directive search for meaning sits uneasily beside those who refuse to steer a client towards any particular meaning.

## Structure and Method

The second edition keeps the first edition's architecture — an introduction, a chapter on the shared philosophical grounding, a chapter on each approach, and a closing chapter on evidence and challenges — but updates it in two notable ways. It adds a fictional case study, Siân, whose presentation is described in the introduction and revisited in each chapter, so that the reader can see how the same client would be understood and worked with differently by each approach. It also adds a wholly new final chapter in which Cooper revisits the questions with which he ended the first edition, including the challenge of evidence and the field's relationship to mainstream psychology.

## Evidence

One of the book's most discussed contributions is its treatment of research evidence. Cooper has followed this question beyond the book itself, and the text reports the meta-analysis he conducted with Joel Vos and Mick Craig, published in 2015, which found that existential therapies show moderate positive effects compared with no treatment, with stronger effects for people with cancer and chronic illness — while warning that the quality of the underlying studies is limited. The book is frank that existential therapy still lacks the base of randomised controlled trials that cognitive-behavioural therapy can point to, and that this remains the field's most persistent vulnerability. The discussion is even-handed: Cooper neither dismisses the demand for evidence nor pretends that the evidence already settles the question. The debate belongs to the field as a whole, but the book is where most students first meet it in a form they can use.

## Reception and Limits

*Existential Therapies* became a standard text in existential training programmes in Britain and beyond, and is routinely recommended as the single best point of entry to the field before specialising in any one current. Its influence has been to entrench a pluralistic frame: instead of arguing about which current is the true existential therapy, practitioners are encouraged to treat the approaches as complementary ways of working, to be chosen according to the client's needs. The book's limits follow from its virtues as a survey. It advances no new theory of its own, and each approach gets a single chapter that can flatten the complexity of its original texts. Its comparative language tends to smooth over the sharpest disagreements between schools, and its perspective remains predominantly British and English-language.
