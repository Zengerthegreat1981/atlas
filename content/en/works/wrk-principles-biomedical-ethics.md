# Principles of Biomedical Ethics

A reference work by **Tom Beauchamp** and **James Childress**, first published in 1979 and reissued in successive editions (the eighth edition appeared in 2019). It became one of the most widely used texts in biomedical ethics courses around the world, and a practical tool used in drafting institutional medical ethics committee guidelines and clinical research protocols.

## Historical Context: From the Tuskegee Scandal to the Belmont Report

The book came after a wave of ethical scandals in American medical research, the most prominent of which was the Tuskegee syphilis study (1932–1972). In that study, several hundred Black men with syphilis were left untreated and uninformed for decades, in order to observe the progression of the disease. The scandal led to the formation of a national commission, which issued the **Belmont Report** (1979). The report established three basic principles for research ethics involving human subjects: respect for persons, beneficence, and justice. Beauchamp, who had worked with the National Commission, and Childress published their book in the same year. It sets out four principles (separating beneficence from non-maleficence) and applies them to clinical medicine in general, not to research alone.

## The Four Principles

The framework known as **principlism** rests on four mid-level principles that are not arranged in a fixed hierarchy. Instead they are balanced according to the context of each case: **respect for autonomy**, meaning the patient's right to make treatment decisions with knowledge and free consent, without paternalistic guardianship; **beneficence**, meaning the physician's commitment to act for the patient's actual benefit; **non-maleficence**, meaning the duty to avoid harm, traditionally associated with the maxim *primum non nocere* ("first, do no harm"), which is often attributed to Hippocratic medicine but does not appear in the Oath itself; and **justice**, meaning the fair distribution of scarce health resources and equal treatment of patients. The authors hold that these four principles constitute a "common morality" that people with different philosophical and religious backgrounds can agree upon, without needing to settle the debate between the major moral theories (Kantian, utilitarian, and virtue ethics).

## Presence in Islamic Thought: Maqasid al-Shari'a and Medicine

The four-principle framework shows a structural parallel with the *maqasid al-shari'a* as articulated by al-Shatibi. Preservation of life is counted among the five necessities that the legal rulings aim to protect, which provides a maqasid-based foundation for the principles of beneficence and non-maleficence in Islamic medicine. Contemporary jurists, relying on established legal maxims such as "harm is to be removed" and "the greater harm is removed by the lesser harm," have also developed detailed rulings on questions of organ transplantation, the withdrawal of treatment, and the care of the dying. The concept of individual autonomy in its Western liberal sense has remained a subject of more nuanced discussion in Islamic jurisprudence, where the patient's autonomy is usually weighed against the responsibility of the family and the community for the preservation of life, rather than being regarded as an absolute individual right separate from this network of reciprocal obligations.

## The Principal Critique

The framework has been criticized from several directions. Proponents of the ethics of care (such as Annette Baier and Virginia Held) regarded it as overly abstract and as neglecting the concrete personal relationships between physician and patient. Proponents of virtue ethics (such as Edmund Pellegrino) held that it fails to address the moral character of the physician as the basis of sound practice, rather than relying on abstract rules. Other critics questioned the assumption of a "common morality" that transcends cultures, arguing that the four principles carry an implicit bias toward American individual liberalism that does not necessarily suit other cultural contexts.

## Institutional Impact

Despite this criticism, the framework has become the working common language of medical ethics committees and institutional review boards (IRBs) around the world. It is also the basis for training physicians to resolve ethical dilemmas in daily practice, from decisions to withdraw treatment to informed consent protocols in clinical trials.

## The Mechanism of Balancing and Specification

The authors explain that the four principles are not applied as absolute rules in which some take precedence over others in a hierarchical order. They are applied through two complementary analytic processes: **balancing** the competing principles in the concrete case (for example, weighing the autonomy of a patient who refuses life-saving treatment against the duty of beneficence toward him), and **specification**, which turns an abstract principle into a more detailed practical rule suited to a particular context (for example, specifying the principle of autonomy in a rule concerning the age of medical consent for minors). In later editions the authors emphasized that this model is not a rigidly deductive system but a flexible "framework" that develops gradually through interaction with new issues. They draw on the method of "reflective equilibrium" developed by John Rawls in general moral philosophy.

## Contemporary Issues: End of Life and Health Justice

The framework is tested in practice on difficult issues such as debates over mercy killing and assisted suicide, where the principle of respect for the autonomy of a patient who wishes to end his suffering collides with the principle of non-maleficence, which is sometimes understood as an absolute prohibition on participating in the ending of a human life. It is also tested in questions of allocating scarce health resources, such as the priority of distributing mechanical ventilators during pandemics, where the principle of justice imposes distributive criteria (such as priority by severity of condition or by social impact) that may conflict with the principle of treating each patient as an individual rather than as a number in a collective calculation.
