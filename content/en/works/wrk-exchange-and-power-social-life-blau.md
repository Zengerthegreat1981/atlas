# Exchange and Power in Social Life

*Exchange and Power in Social Life* (1964) is Peter M. Blau's main theoretical work. It extends social exchange theory from face-to-face interaction, as in George Homans's work, to an account of power, organisations and social structure.

## Historical Context

Peter Blau (1918-2002), an American sociologist born in Vienna, wrote the book while at the University of Chicago and later taught at Columbia. It builds on George Homans's *Social Behavior: Its Elementary Forms* (1961), which grounded exchange theory in behavioural psychology. Blau accepted exchange as a basic social process but argued that complex structures, such as organisations and class systems, have emergent properties that cannot be derived from elementary exchange alone, and he sought to link micro processes with macro structures.

## The Central Idea

Blau distinguishes social exchange, which involves unspecified obligations, trust and gratitude, from economic exchange, in which terms are specified in advance. Power arises from imbalanced exchange: a person who needs a resource from another and cannot reciprocate is dependent. Blau lists alternatives for such a person: supply something the other values in return, obtain the resource elsewhere, take it by coercion, or do without. If none is available, the person must comply with the other's wishes, and the resulting power relation can become established through repeated exchange.

Blau then considers how power becomes legitimate authority when it is socially approved by subordinates and how it can provoke opposition. He examines the emergence of differentiated structures of status and organisation from these processes, and discusses the dynamics of group integration, competition and the relation between micro and macro levels.

## Impact and Criticism

The book influenced the sociology of organisations and later network approaches to power and exchange, notably Richard Emerson's power-dependence theory (1962) and the network exchange theory developed by Karen Cook and others. Blau later turned to a more formal structural theory in *Inequality and Heterogeneity* (1977), in which the distribution of populations across social positions shapes the probability of association.

Critics have argued that the exchange framework applies an economic model to social life and neglects norms, values and symbolic meaning, and that it struggles to explain how macro structures arise from micro exchanges. Blau himself acknowledged the limits of reductionism by stressing emergent properties of social structures.
