# Existential Psychotherapy: The Four Ultimate Concerns

*Existential Psychotherapy* (1980) by Irvin D. Yalom, published by Basic Books in New York, is the most famous book in the existential school and the one that most gives the impression that the school is a single thing. Yalom, professor of psychiatry at Stanford and already known for *The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy* (1970), set out to organise an unruly European tradition into a teachable clinical framework. The result became the standard textbook of American existential therapy — and, for many readers, the whole of existential therapy.

## The Thesis: Four Ultimate Concerns

Yalom's thesis is a fourfold classification of the "ultimate concerns" that every human being faces by virtue of existing: death, freedom and responsibility, existential isolation, and meaninglessness. Psychological anxiety, at its root, springs from the person's encounter — conscious or repressed — with one of these four givens, rather than from sexual conflict or early relationships alone, as in classical psychoanalysis. The dynamic conflict in Yalom's model is therefore a conflict between the person's awareness of one of these facts and his defences against that awareness. Each of the four concerns generates its own characteristic defences: against death, the beliefs in one's own specialness and in an ultimate rescuer; against freedom, the evasion of responsibility and the search for an external structure; against isolation, the attempt to merge with another; against meaninglessness, compulsive activity or the adoption of ready-made meanings. The fourfold map is the book's lasting contribution, and it is also the feature most criticised, as will be seen below.

## Structure: Four Parts, Three Levels Each

The book is divided into four parts, one per concern, and each part mixes three levels: a philosophical exposition of the idea's origins, clinical cases from Yalom's own practice, and a review of whatever empirical research was available. The section on death treats the link between death anxiety and symptoms, describes death terror as the repressed origin behind many disorders, distinguishes death as a future event from death as a presence in life, and argues that an encounter with death can be an awakening experience that provokes a change of life. The section on freedom and responsibility treats the absence of an external structure for existence, responsibility for choosing oneself, and the will as wish, decision, and action. The section on isolation distinguishes interpersonal isolation, intrapersonal isolation, and the basic existential isolation that no love can bridge. The section on meaninglessness takes up the question of the meaning of life through Frankl and Camus, and proposes engagement as the remedy for meaninglessness. Running through all four parts is Yalom's account of the therapeutic relationship: the therapist is a fellow traveller, not an expert applying a technique, who shares the same existential facts and faces them alongside the patient.

## Intellectual Sources, Declared and Undeclared

The book is an American academic synthesis, not a direct continuation of any European school. Its four concerns are close to the "boundary situations" of Karl Jaspers; its analysis of death is indebted to Heidegger; its account of defences against death terror draws on Otto Rank and Ernest Becker's *The Denial of Death* (1973); and its arguments about meaninglessness invoke Epicurus. It therefore differs from Frankl's logotherapy and from the European existential analysis of Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss. Concepts from Yalom's later works — *Love's Executioner* (1989) or *Staring at the Sun* (2008) — should not be attributed to this book. No authorised Arabic translation of the book is known, although some of Yalom's novels have been translated; those should not be mistaken for translations of this textbook.

## Reception and Criticism

Many readers count the book the most teachable reference in existential therapy, praising its organisation, its personal honesty, and its breaking of a professional silence around subjects — above all death — that had been taboo in the consulting room. The British existential school, including Emmy van Deurzen and Ernesto Spinelli, objected that Yalom reduces existentialism to four "givens" presented in a quasi-dynamic model, and neglects the phenomenological description that distinguished the European tradition. Methodologically, the book has been criticised for repeatedly moving from convincing description to causal claim without settling the question, for the difficulty of saying what could falsify its theses, and for relying on a narrow clinical sample drawn from the author's own practice. A further criticism is that it presents American existential therapy — one current among several — as though it were existential therapy as such, a mistake especially common in Arabic reading. Read as Yalom himself suggests it be read — as an excellent phenomenological map of what people do with the conditions of their existence, not as a proven causal theory of the origins of symptoms — the book keeps its value and its honesty.
