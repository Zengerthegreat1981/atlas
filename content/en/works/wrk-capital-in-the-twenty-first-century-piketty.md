# Capital in the Twenty-First Century

The French economist **Thomas Piketty** published his book *Le Capital au XXIe siècle* in 2013; the English translation, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*, appeared in 2014. It is a vast work based on a research project that extended over more than a decade, collecting historical tax data from several countries. The book returned the global debate on the concentration of wealth to the forefront of contemporary political economy, after decades in which the topic had received relatively little attention in mainstream economics.

## A Uniquely Broad Data Project

Piketty built his book on a huge database, which he compiled with colleagues, most prominently Anthony Atkinson, from tax records in France, Britain, the United States and other countries. Some of these records extend for more than a full century. This made it possible to compare the evolution of the share of the highest income and wealth brackets in national income over long historical periods. Data of such temporal depth is rarely available.

## Refuting Kuznets's Optimistic Hypothesis

The book opens with a direct challenge to the "Kuznets curve" hypothesis, which had long been influential. That hypothesis holds that inequality rises temporarily in the early stages of industrialisation, then falls automatically as economic growth matures. Piketty shows, through his long-run data, that the decline seen in Europe and the United States in the mid-twentieth century was not the product of inevitable natural growth laws. It was the result of exceptional, non-recurring shocks: the two world wars, the Great Depression, and high post-war taxation. Inequality, he argues, has risen strongly again since the 1980s, and in the case of wealth in Europe it is moving back towards the levels of the Belle Époque at the end of the nineteenth century.

## The Law of r > g

The book offers a central explanatory formulation of the concentration of wealth. When the rate of return on capital (r) exceeds the growth rate of the economy as a whole (g) over a long period, which Piketty regards as the historical normal pattern rather than an exception, wealth tends to become concentrated in the hands of those who already own capital, growing faster than general income. Over time this produces societies in which wealth is inherited more than it is earned through work and individual effort. Piketty calls this a return to "patrimonial capitalism," resembling Europe before the First World War.

## A Radical Policy Recommendation: A Global Tax on Wealth

Piketty reaches an ambitious policy proposal: a coordinated global progressive tax on wealth, rather than on income alone. He argues that individual national taxes collide with tax competition between states, which pushes wealth to migrate to tax havens. This proposal has faced wide scepticism from other economists about its political feasibility in the absence of broad international coordination, which is difficult to achieve realistically in the foreseeable future.

## A Methodological Critique of the Central Thesis

The book faced technical criticism from economists (for example Matthew Rognlie) who questioned the assumed elasticity of substitution between capital and labour on which the predicted rise in the capital share depends. They argued that the theoretical conclusion does not follow from the data alone without additional modelling assumptions that are open to debate. Other critics questioned the measurement of wealth and the role of housing. These disagreements remain open among specialists.

## An Exceptional Public and Academic Reception

The book achieved an unusual commercial success for a large technical economic work relying mainly on lengthy statistical tables. It topped bestseller lists in several countries and provoked wide public debate that went beyond academic circles into political and journalistic discourse on the global concentration of wealth. This made it one of the most influential economics books of the second decade of the twenty-first century.

## An Unusual Literary Style for an Economic Work

The book combines precise statistical analysis with literary citations from nineteenth-century novels, especially those of Honoré de Balzac and Jane Austen. It uses their literary depiction of a world in which social destiny depends on inheritance more than on work as a vivid illustration of what contemporary societies might come to resemble if the current trend of wealth concentration continues without policy correction. This style helped make the book readable for a wider audience than specialist economists alone.

## Influence on the Design of Later Databases

The research project behind the book led to the World Wealth and Income Database, later renamed the World Inequality Database, which is open to researchers and later expanded to cover many additional countries beyond the original focus on Europe and North America. It also supported later studies using similar methods, including one on inequality in the Middle East.

## A Comparison with Marx's Title

The book bears a title that deliberately recalls Karl Marx's famous work *Capital*, although Piketty differs fundamentally from Marx in method and conclusion. Piketty relies on long-run statistical evidence rather than on Marx's theoretical deduction. He explicitly rejects the inevitability of capitalist collapse that some readings of Marx imply. In its place he offers a description of a distributive pattern that is politically reversible through reformist tax instruments, rather than through a revolutionary structural transformation of the economic system itself.
