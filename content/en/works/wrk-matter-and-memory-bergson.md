# Matter and Memory (Matière et Mémoire): Bergson

*Matière et Mémoire: Essai sur la relation du corps à l'esprit*. Bergson's second major book, published in Paris in 1896, carries an explicit subtitle: an essay on the relation of the body to the mind.

## An Arabic Parallel in the Psychology of Memory

In *al-Shifa'* (the Book of the Soul), Ibn Sina presents the theory of the inner powers, which place retention and memory in the back ventricle of the brain, and distinguishes between **retention** (*hifz*) and **recollection** (*tadhakkur*), that is, between storing meanings and actually recalling them. At the same time he holds that universal rational meanings are not stored in a bodily organ but emanate upon the soul from the Active Intellect. This argument resembles, from a distance, Bergson's idea that the brain does not store pure memory. Yet Ibn Sina holds that imaginative forms and partial memories are preserved in a bodily faculty, which runs counter to Bergson's view. Fazlur Rahman set out this difference in *Avicenna's Psychology* (Oxford University Press, 1952). The link between the two thinkers is an interpretive comparison; there is no evidence that Bergson knew Ibn Sina on this subject.

## The Central Thesis

The book offers a solution to the problem of the interaction of mind and body by dividing **memory** into two distinct kinds rather than one: **habit memory** (*mémoire-habitude*), bodily, mechanical, and repeated, stored as movement in the nervous system and recalled through repetition, like skills; and **pure memory** or **recollection** (*mémoire pure* or *souvenir*), spiritual, unique, and non-repeated, which recalls a moment of the past as a complete image rather than as a motor mechanism. The body, and especially the brain, is not a repository where these memories are stored but an **instrument of selection**: it chooses from the flood of spiritual memories what fits the present action and inhibits what is not useful. Brain damage therefore disables the mechanism of recall but does not erase the memory itself. Bergson holds that the past survives in itself, and that present perception is formed by mixing sensation with memory, so there is no pure perception without memory.

## Structure of the Work

Four chapters: (1) on the selection of images for representation, where matter is a "collection of images" and perception is a selection from them according to possible action; (2) on the recognition of images, which distinguishes the two memories and their relation to the brain; (3) on the survival of images, which contains the theory of the "cone," representing the superposition of memories in levels that widen from the apex of the effective present to the base of pure past; (4) on the determination and fixation of images: perception, matter, the soul, and the body, and a critique of the mechanist and finalist accounts of matter.

## Historical Context of Publication

The book came seven years after his first major work, *Essai sur les données immédiates de la conscience* (1889, translated into English as *Time and Free Will* in 1910), and in the midst of a sharp French and European debate over the place of neurophysiology (especially aphasia) in the philosophy of mind. Bergson discussed the research of Broca, Wernicke, and Charcot, and the law of regression of Théodule Ribot in *Les maladies de la mémoire* (1881), which holds that recent memories fade before old ones in disease. He used it as an argument that the matter concerns retrieval rather than erasure of memory. The book was translated into English by N. M. Paul and W. S. Palmer (Allen & Unwin, 1911).

## Reception and Criticism

The book was warmly received in French philosophical circles, and its influence went beyond them. **William James** admired Bergson's work and saw in it a kinship with his own radical empiricism; he devoted a lecture to Bergson in *A Pluralistic Universe* (1909). Scholars have compared his analysis of duration and time with Husserl's phenomenology of time, though Husserl's own account is independent and critical of Bergson in some respects. Gilles Deleuze reread it in *Bergsonism* (1966) as a philosophy of difference and becoming. It also became a foundation for critiques of neural reductionism, which reduce memory to a single physical location in the brain.

## Scientific Assessment

His distinction between habit memory and recollection parallels later distinctions in neuropsychology between procedural and declarative memory. It is paralleled by the case of patient H. M., who had parts of his medial temporal lobes removed, as described by Scoville and Milner in 1957: he could no longer form new explicit memories, yet later studies (Milner and Corkin) showed that he could still learn motor skills. His stronger claim, that the brain does not store memory, is contradicted by **engram** research: Liu, Ramirez, and Tonegawa showed in *Nature* in 2012 that activating specific cells in the hippocampus of mice recalls a fear memory. Bergson is therefore read today as a philosophical meditation on the nature of remembering and its relation to time and action, rather than as a neural theory that can be believed literally.

## Its Importance for the Philosophical-Psychological Bridge

The book offers, from within philosophy and decades before the rise of cognitive psychology, a distinction between procedural memory (habit) and recollective memory (the image), and the hypothesis that "the brain is an instrument of selection, not a repository" bears directly on the contemporary debate about the localization of memories in the brain.
