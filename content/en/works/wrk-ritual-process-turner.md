# The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure

A book by the British anthropologist Victor Turner, published in 1969 by Aldine (Chicago). It is the text of his Lewis Henry Morgan Lectures, which he delivered at the University of Rochester in 1966. It is one of the most influential books of symbolic anthropology in the twentieth century. In it Turner formulated the concepts of **liminality** and **communitas**, whose influence extended beyond anthropology into religious, theatrical, and cultural studies in general.

## Core Content

Turner started from Arnold van Gennep's three-phase model of **rites of passage**: separation from the previous social position, then the phase of the **threshold**, or **liminality** (from the Latin *limen*, meaning threshold), and then reaggregation into a new social position. Turner focused his analysis on the middle phase, liminality, drawing on his long fieldwork among the Ndembu of Zambia. He described it as a "betwixt and between" state in which the individual has left their previous social status but has not yet entered the next one. Their status is marked by ambiguity, seclusion, testing, and sometimes the blurring of sexual and symbolic identity.

In this liminal state, Turner argued, a social pattern emerges that is opposed to the everyday hierarchical structure of society, which he called **communitas**: a direct, equal human relationship among individuals who together submit to the authority of ritual elders, temporarily transcending the usual class and status distinctions. Turner extended this detailed ethnographic analysis (of Ndembu rituals) into a general theory of the cyclical tension between **structure** (the everyday hierarchical social system) and **anti-structure** (the liminal state and communitas). In the book he discusses phenomena such as millenarian movements, the early Franciscans and other monastic and mendicant movements, and the hippies and Beats, and he later extended the approach to pilgrimage. He considered the pursuit of communitas to be a widespread human possibility.

## Impact and Legacy

The terms "liminality" and "communitas" have become standard analytical tools in the study of ritual, religion, theatre, and performing arts across disciplines that go beyond anthropology alone. They influenced performance theory (Richard Schechner) and pilgrimage studies (Turner himself later developed this line with his wife Edith Turner in *Image and Pilgrimage in Christian Culture*, 1978). The model has also been applied to festivals and social protest movements.

## Limits

Turner's generalization of the concepts of liminality and communitas beyond their original ethnographic context (the specific Ndembu rituals) has been criticized for sometimes sliding into a loose generalization applied to very distant phenomena (from a tribal rite of passage to a modern political revolution) without sufficient distinction of the essential differences between them. Later critics also observed that the image of an equal "communitas" may conceal ongoing actual power relations even within the liminal moment itself.

## Fieldwork: The Ndembu and Their Rituals

Turner based his argument on fieldwork he conducted among the Ndembu in northwestern Zambia in the early 1950s, and on analysis of many rituals, including the Mukanda ritual for boys' circumcision and the Isoma ritual of affliction concerned with women's reproductive troubles. Among his decisive examples in *The Ritual Process* is the ritual for installing a Ndembu chief. The candidate is stripped of his garments, covered with worn cloth, belittled, and rebuked for his alleged sins by ordinary men, transforming him from an ordinary person into a bearer of authority. Turner wanted to show that the one who is expected to hold higher authority first goes through an experience that strips him of his status and makes him like everyone else, so that ritual humility is a condition of authority. The book treats this under the theme of humility and hierarchy.

## Modes of Communitas

In the chapter "Communitas: Model and Process," Turner distinguished three modes: **spontaneous existential communitas**, which arises in moments of direct fusion; **normative communitas**, which is organized into institutions that try to fix this fusion in rules and customs; and **ideological communitas**, which becomes a utopian program taking the form of a doctrine. He cited diverse cases bringing together Saint Francis of Assisi, dervishes, Buddhist monks, hippie movements, and the Beat generation, along with the ideas of Martin Buber on the relationship between I and Thou. He argued that religious communities always try to turn a spontaneous glow into a structure, and in doing so lose part of it.

## Hajj and Ihram

Later studies have applied Turner's model to the hajj; the book itself does not treat it. The Muslim pilgrim strips off stitched clothing and wears two white cloths (an *izar* and a *rida*), and is commanded to abstain from obscenity, wickedness, and disputing during the pilgrimage: "there shall be no obscenity, no wickedness, and no quarrelling during the pilgrimage" (Qur'an 2:197). Millions of people from different nations perform the rites in one place, so that class and linguistic differences become a temporary equality. Turner's model has been used to describe this scene, but it has been questioned in later work on Muslim travel, including discussion in the volume edited by Eickelman and Piscatori, *Muslim Travellers: Pilgrimage, Migration, and the Religious Imagination* (University of California Press, 1990). Critics note that the hajj also combines hierarchy, national competition, and official organization, and that the pilgrim's experience is shaped by their expectations and religious education, not by the rite of passage alone. This debate shows that Turner described one phase of a complex journey rather than the whole journey.

## The Liminoid and Post-1969 Developments

In essays of the 1970s, collected in part in *From Ritual to Theatre* (Performing Arts Journal Publications, 1982), Turner added the concept of the **liminoid** to distinguish between the obligatory rituals of traditional societies and the voluntary activities of industrial societies, such as art, play, and theatre, which allow threshold-like moments without collective obligation. In this way he answered the objection that his theory did not apply to modern societies.

## Critique of the Theory

Studies of Christian pilgrimage, such as Eade and Sallnow's *Contesting the Sacred* (Routledge, 1991), argued that pilgrimage is an arena of conflict between competing groups, not a pure equality. Other critics noted that Turner took the concept of the threshold from van Gennep but transformed it into a general feature, so that what had been part of a sequence became the focus of the analysis.
