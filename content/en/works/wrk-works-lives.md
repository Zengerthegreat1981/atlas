# Works and Lives: The Anthropologist as Author

*Works and Lives: The Anthropologist as Author* (1988) is Clifford Geertz's most sustained reflection on ethnographic writing. In four linked essays, Geertz argues that anthropology is fundamentally an act of authorship: ethnographers do not merely report other cultures, they compose them, and the persuasiveness of an ethnography depends as much on how it is written as on what was observed. The book helped launch the literary turn in anthropology and remains the classic statement of the discipline's textual self-consciousness.

Geertz's question is disarmingly simple: how do anthropologists convince readers that they have really "been there"? His answer is that ethnographic authority is a literary achievement. Fieldwork gives the anthropologist something to write about, but it is writing — voice, structure, metaphor, the management of evidence — that turns field notes into a credible account of another way of life. Anthropology, in this view, is closer to the essay and the novel than to the laboratory report, and its practitioners should be judged as authors as well as observers.

## Structure and Argument

The book grew out of the Harry Camp Lectures delivered at Stanford University in 1983, and its four chapters each take one classic ethnographer as a case study in authorial style. Geertz examines Claude Lévi-Strauss, E. E. Evans-Pritchard, Bronislaw Malinowski, and Ruth Benedict, treating each as a distinct literary type. Lévi-Strauss is the mythographer, building vast architectures of meaning from fragmentary myths; Evans-Pritchard is the plainspoken empiricist, whose flat, unadorned prose performs scientific detachment; Malinowski is the confessional diarist and romantic, whose vivid first-person presence makes the Trobriand Islands feel intimately known; Benedict is the poet of cultural patterns, whose lyrical prose turns whole societies into coherent aesthetic wholes.

The point of these portraits is not biography but method. Each writer, Geertz shows, persuades by different means, and each means carries different assumptions about what a culture is and how it can be known. Lévi-Strauss's structures exist because his sentences build them; Evans-Pritchard's Nuer exist as reasonable actors because his prose makes them sound reasonable; Malinowski's Trobrianders are vivid because his narrator is vivid. The ethnographer is always present in the text, whether acknowledged or not, and the choice of how to be present is a theoretical choice.

This argument is sharpened in the book's framing chapters, where Geertz distinguishes between "author-saturated" and "author-evacuated" texts. Early ethnography often hid the author behind impersonal description; the confessional and experimental writing of the 1970s and 1980s put the author back in the frame. Geertz insists that neither strategy escapes the problem: evacuating the author is itself a rhetorical move, a way of claiming objectivity by erasing the observer. There is no writing without a writer, and anthropology's task is to be honest about that rather than to pretend otherwise.

## Method and Style

Geertz's own method in the book is close reading. He treats ethnographies as texts, attending to their metaphors, their narrative structures, and their characteristic tropes with the care a literary critic gives to a novel. His famous phrase "thick description," developed in *The Interpretation of Cultures* (1973), underlies the approach: what matters is not the bare record of behaviour but the layered web of meaning in which behaviour is embedded, and rendering that web is an interpretive, writerly act.

The book's style performs its argument. Geertz writes with an essayistic elegance — aphoristic, allusive, occasionally barbed — that makes the case for anthropology as literature by example. His portrait of Malinowski, for instance, turns on the tension between the published ethnographies and the posthumously published diary, in which Malinowski recorded boredom, desire, and contempt for his subjects. Rather than treating the diary as a scandal that invalidates the ethnography, Geertz treats it as evidence of the distance between the experiencing self and the authoring self — the gap that all ethnographic writing must somehow bridge.

## Reception and Influence

*Works and Lives* appeared at the height of anthropology's reflexive moment, alongside James Clifford and George Marcus's *Writing Culture* (1986), and it quickly became a touchstone of that movement. It was praised for its wit and erudition and for giving the discipline a vocabulary — authorial voice, textual strategy, "being there" — with which to discuss its own writing. It also drew criticism from anthropologists who felt that Geertz's literary emphasis underplayed questions of power, politics, and the colonial conditions under which ethnographies were produced. Feminist and postcolonial critics noted that all four of Geertz's chosen authors were figures of the colonial era, and that the book says little about the people written about.

Even so, the book's influence has been durable. It reshaped how ethnographies are taught, making questions of voice, genre, and representation central to graduate training. It legitimated experimental forms — the dialogue, the memoir, the multi-vocal text — that would have seemed unprofessional a generation earlier. And it gave anthropology a lasting image of itself: a discipline whose central product is neither data nor theory alone but the written account, the work in which a life of fieldwork becomes a book.

Geertz's closing insistence is modest but consequential. Anthropologists cannot escape authorship, and they should stop trying. The way forward is not more rigorous concealment of the author but more self-aware writing: ethnography that knows it is made, and that takes responsibility for how it is made. That is the standard *Works and Lives* set, and it is one the discipline is still working to meet.
