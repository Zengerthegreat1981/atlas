# Meditations on First Philosophy (Descartes)

## The Central Thesis

Descartes proposes a project to rebuild the whole of human knowledge on a certain foundation that admits no doubt, by means of a methodological tool, "radical methodical doubt": supposing the existence of a "malicious demon" able to deceive him in everything he takes to be knowledge, including mathematical truths, the existence of the external world, and his own body. From this universal doubt emerges the one certainty that cannot be refuted: the act of doubting itself entails the existence of a doubting subject, which Descartes expresses in the Second Meditation as "I am, I exist" (*ego sum, ego existo*); the famous formula *cogito, ergo sum* ("I think, therefore I am") belongs rather to the *Discourse on Method* (1637, in French as *je pense, donc je suis*) and the *Principles of Philosophy* (1644, in Latin). From this solid point he rebuilds the certainty of God's existence (as a perfect being who does not deceive), then the certainty of the existence of the external world, relying on God's truthfulness as the guarantor of the validity of his clear and distinct perception.

## Structure of the Work

It consists of six meditations: the first on doubt and its reasons; the second on the nature of the human mind and that it is more certain than the body (the cogito); the third on the existence of God through an argument based on the idea of perfection present in the mind; the fourth on truth and error and how they are consistent with God's perfection; the fifth on the essence of material things and a proof of God's existence once more (the ontological argument); the sixth on the real distinction between soul and body and on the existence of the external world. The work was published accompanied by a set of "Objections" raised against it by philosophers and theologians contemporary with him, among them Antoine Arnauld, Thomas Hobbes, and Pierre Gassendi, and the "Replies" that Descartes wrote to them. This exchange is not a marginal appendix but an integral part of the structure of the work as Descartes himself published it, since it clarifies the points of obscurity and objection raised by his contemporaries immediately upon the book's appearance.

## Historical Context of Publication

The work was published in Latin in 1641 (the language of science and philosophy in Europe at the time, not French), after Descartes had sent it before publication to a number of scholars and theologians asking for comments, which are the objections attached to the edition. The work came in the wake of the crisis of Galileo's trial (1633), which led Descartes to caution in presenting his cosmological ideas, so he chose to formulate his philosophical project in a personal, meditative form closer to a mental exercise directed at the reader than to a direct polemical thesis.

## Reception and Criticism

From its appearance the work provoked sharp philosophical and theological controversy documented by the objections and replies attached to it: Gassendi objected to the ontological argument and described it as proving no more than that the idea of perfection logically includes existence, not that a perfect being actually exists; and Hobbes doubted the real distinction between soul and body from a materialist perspective. The "Cartesian circle" (using clear and distinct certainty to prove God's existence, then using God's truthfulness to guarantee clear and distinct certainty) has remained a subject of renewed philosophical discussion to this day.

## The Two Editions and the Title

The first edition appeared in Paris in 1641 with a title in which the author promises a proof of the existence of God and the immortality of the soul. When critics saw that the book does not prove immortality, but is confined to showing the distinctness of the soul from the body, which is the condition of its possibility, he changed the title in the second edition, published in Amsterdam in 1642, so that it became a proof of the existence of God and of the distinction of the human soul from the body. In this edition the Seventh Objections, written by Father Bourdin, were added. The book was translated into French by the Duke of Luynes in 1647, and Descartes himself reviewed the translation and made changes to it, so the French version became part of the sources of the text.

## The Objections and Replies

The objections number seven sets: the first from Father Caterus, who raised the question of the ontological argument; the second from Mersenne and his group; the third from Hobbes, who held that thinking may be a bodily motion; the fourth from Antoine Arnauld, who raised the Cartesian circle and asked about the question of the Eucharist; the fifth from Gassendi, who held that existence is not a perfection; the sixth from a group of philosophers and theologians; and the seventh from Bourdin. Among the objections most influential in later history is the question of interaction between two disparate substances, which Princess Elisabeth pressed in her later correspondence with Descartes, leading him to write *The Passions of the Soul*.

## The Main Arguments in Detail

In the third meditation Descartes presents an argument holding that the idea of an infinite perfect being cannot have as its source a finite subject, so its cause must be a being that has as much perfection as the idea represents. In the fifth he revives the argument from the idea of perfection that includes existence. The sixth meditation presents the argument of real distinction: since I clearly perceive that I am a thinking, non-extended being, and perceive body as extended, non-thinking, God is able to separate the two, so they are two distinct substances. He then admits that sensation teaches me that I am united with my body in an intimate union, and that I am not lodged in it like a pilot in his ship.

## The Arabic Reading and Comparison

It has been common in Arabic studies to present Descartes as the founder of modern philosophy, and comparisons have been drawn between his methodical doubt and al-Ghazali's doubt in *Al-Munqidh min al-Dalal*, and between the cogito and the "floating man" argument that Avicenna offered, comparisons between philosophers who arrive at the certainty of the self through reflection. But scholars caution against considering these a historical influence, for al-Ghazali did not build on doubt a foundation for a new science, and Avicenna uses the thought experiment to establish the substantiality of the soul, not to found a comprehensive certainty.

## Limits of the Project

- The Cartesian circle: he establishes the truth of clear perception by God, and establishes God by clear perception, and commentators have tried to resolve it by distinguishing between transient certainty and firm certainty, without consensus.
- Dualism: it does not explain how an extended substance and a thinking substance interact, and the problem of interaction has remained afflicted ever since.
- The cogito establishes the existence of the act of thinking and does not necessarily establish a substantial self that bears it, which Lichtenberg, Hume, Kant, and others criticized.
