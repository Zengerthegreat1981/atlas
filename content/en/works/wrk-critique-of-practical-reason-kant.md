# Critique of Practical Reason by Immanuel Kant

## The central thesis

The book is the full critical formulation of Kantian ethics, after it had been, in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), a preliminary presentation that was not critical in the sense of the transcendental method. Here Kant restates the **Categorical Imperative** (first presented in the *Groundwork*) as the fundamental law of pure practical reason, the sole moral law that binds pure practical reason by itself and not as a means to an end outside it, and infers from it the three postulates of practical reason: freedom of the will, immortality of the soul, and the existence of God. These postulates are not theoretical theorems that theoretical reason could prove (the first Critique, 1781/1787, had already blocked this road) but **necessary conditions of the possibility of moral action itself**: the will cannot legislate a universal law for itself unless it is free, and the pursuit of the "highest good" (the union of virtue with happiness) is not coherent without assuming an immortality that permits the completion of virtue and a God who guarantees this union. Faith here is "practical faith" and not knowledge.

## Structure of the work

It is divided into a preface and then the **Doctrine of Elements**, which is the larger part and branches into the **Analytic of Pure Practical Reason** (in which he establishes the supreme principle of morality and the concept of "good and evil" as the objects of practical reason, and the topic of the "incentive", that is, respect for the moral law as the sole moral incentive) and the **Dialectic of Pure Practical Reason** (in which he presents the problem of the "highest good" and infers from it the three postulates). The book ends with a brief **Doctrine of Method**, which investigates how the principles of practical reason are instilled in actual moral education.

## Historical context of publication

The book appeared in 1788 and is **the second of the great critical trilogy**, between the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, second edition 1787) and the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790). Kant wrote it in response to the need for an independent critique of practical reason after the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) raised questions about how to justify the moral law critically and not merely formulate it. It precedes the *Metaphysics of Morals* (1797), which later elaborated the theory of virtue and the theory of right in application of these foundations; the theses of the latter must not be confused with this book.

## Reception and criticism

It formed the basis on which the later traditions of deontological ethics were built, but the concept of the postulates in particular was subject to early criticism: Kant was accused of bringing back through the back door (practical reason) what he himself had closed at the front door (theoretical reason), namely the metaphysical proof of the existence of God and the immortal soul. Schopenhauer was among the harshest critics, arguing in *On the Basis of Morality* (1840) that Kant's imperative form of duty is a disguised theological morality of command and obedience. On the other hand, the problem of freedom and determinism that the book raises (is the will free in a sense compatible with the order of natural causality, or does it transcend it) has remained a living focus in the philosophy of mind and contemporary psychology on free will.

## From the Groundwork to the second Critique

The book's title was not "Critique of Pure Practical Reason" as might be expected, and Kant explained the reason in the preface: what is required is not a critique of pure practical reason in itself, because this reason has in itself the law that does not err, but to show that pure practical reason exists, and to critique empirical practical reason which claims that the passions alone determine the will. This is a methodological difference from the first Critique: there Kant limits transcendent reason, and here he defends that pure reason can be practical by itself.

## The moral law and the fact of reason

The supreme principle in section seven states that one ought to act so that the maxim of one's will can always serve as a universal law. Since a deduction on the pattern of the first Critique is no longer possible, Kant declares that the moral law is a "fact of reason" (Faktum der Vernunft), that is, an immediate datum in moral consciousness that is not derived from anything else, from which we infer freedom as the condition of the law's existence, while we know freedom through the law. This is what the commentators called a reversal of the order of inference: freedom is the "ground of being" of the law, and the law is the "ground of knowing" of freedom. He distinguishes between the incentive based on respect and the motives of inclination, and says that respect is the only feeling that results from reason.

## The highest good and the dialectic of practical reason

It appears in the Dialectic that the moral will is directed to a universal object, the highest good, that is, the agreement of virtue and happiness, and that joining them is not achieved by the laws of nature alone, so a paradox arises that Kant called the antinomy of practical reason: virtue does not produce happiness, and the pursuit of happiness does not produce virtue. Kant resolves it with the postulates: the immortality of the soul because complete holiness requires infinite progress, and the existence of God because the union of virtue with happiness needs a cause of nature that accords with morality. This "rational faith" differs from knowledge, because it does not increase theoretical knowledge but justifies hope. In the "primacy of practical reason" Kant affirms that the interest of practical reason takes precedence over the theoretical when they conflict.

## Objections and later transformations

Criticism went in several directions. Hegel faulted Kantian ethics as formal, saying what ought to be without content that determines particular duties, and made this part of his critique of the "moral view" in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* and the *Elements of the Philosophy of Right*. Schiller replied that Kantian ethics places duty and inclination in enmity, and proposed the idea of the "beautiful soul" in which the two are in harmony. Schopenhauer held that morality rests on compassion and not on law. In the twentieth century John Rawls in *A Theory of Justice* (1971) reformulated the Kantian idea of autonomy and universal law in a political framework, and Christine Korsgaard offered a contemporary reading of the sources of normativity in Kant. Some scholars hold that the validity of the "postulates" is today less important than the idea of the autonomous will.

## Freedom between philosophy and the sciences

Kant's idea that freedom is not known theoretically but assumed practically remains present in the neuroscience discussion of free will, where some philosophers point out that the results of brain experiments do not touch freedom in the Kantian sense because, according to Kant, it is not observed in appearances. In contrast, determinists regard this solution as abandoning the explanation of action in the natural world. In the Arabic literature the book appears in comparisons of Kantian ethics with the ethics of the will in Islamic thought and the question of rational good and evil, a comparison that requires caution because Kant makes the law self-given by reason and not from the revealed law, whereas the kalam debate revolves around the place of reason relative to revealed law.
