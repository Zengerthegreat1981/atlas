# Hawaiian Kinship Systems

This entry concerns Hawaiian kinship as it figures in anthropological theory. The term "Hawaiian" names one of the classic types of kinship terminology, and the Hawaiian Islands, like the rest of Polynesia, have been an important comparative case for debates about descent, rank and flexible group membership. The entry does not summarize a single monograph.

## The Hawaiian Terminology

Lewis Henry Morgan used Hawaiian data, collected through correspondence with missionaries, in *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family* (1871). He took the Hawaiian terms as the example of what he called the Malayan system, in which relatives are classified chiefly by generation. In what later became known as the "Hawaiian" or generational type, the mother's and father's siblings are called by the same terms as the parents (with gender distinguished), and cousins are called by sibling terms. Anthropologists have since used the Hawaiian type as one of the standard types of cousin terminology, alongside Eskimo, Iroquois, Omaha, Crow and Sudanese.

Morgan interpreted such terminologies as survivals of earlier marriage customs, an evolutionary reading that later anthropologists rejected. Later work treated terminologies as part of living social systems rather than as fossils of past practice.

## Polynesian Descent and Group Membership

Polynesian societies are often described as having cognatic (non-unilineal) descent, in which people may trace membership through either parent and may have claims in more than one group. Raymond Firth's study of Tikopia (*We, the Tikopia*, 1936) is the classic description of kinship, rank and ritual in a Polynesian community, and later comparative work, notably Marshall Sahlins's *Social Stratification in Polynesia* (1958), related variations in descent and rank to ecology and political organization. Models of this kind, such as the "ramage" and the "conical clan" (groups ranked by genealogical closeness to a senior line), were developed from Polynesian data. Hawaii, with its strongly ranked chiefly system and land divisions administered under chiefs, is usually placed at the stratified end of the Polynesian range.

## The 'Ohana

In Hawaiian usage the *ʻohana* is the extended family, including relatives by birth, marriage and adoption. The practice of *hānai*, in which a child is raised by someone other than the birth parents (often grandparents or other relatives), is a well-known feature of Hawaiian family life, discussed in the work of Mary Kawena Pukui and colleagues in *Nānā i ke Kumu* (1972). Hawaiian-language and community-based scholarship stresses that kinship is expressed through care, obligation and shared genealogy as well as through terminology.

## Critique and Contemporary Perspectives

The idea that terminology reveals the logic of a kinship system has been criticized since the mid-twentieth century. Critics point out that terms are used in many contexts and may reflect practice poorly. Colonial transformations, including missionization, depopulation, land tenure reform and the overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1893, also changed Hawaiian family and land relations, so that sources from different periods describe different conditions. Contemporary Hawaiian scholars and practitioners emphasize their own genealogical and cultural knowledge in discussing kinship.
