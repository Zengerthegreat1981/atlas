# Kinship Systems of Polynesia

F. Allan Hanson's work on Hawaiian and Polynesian kinship (1970s) provides crucial analysis of how kinship systems in the Pacific Islands organize society, property, and social relationships in distinctive ways. Hawaiian kinship, while varying across Polynesian cultures, shares characteristics that distinguish it from other kinship systems analyzed in anthropology. The Hawaiian system is ambilineal (allowing descent through either parent), emphasizes generation as an organizing principle, and shows patterns of bilateral grouping. Hanson's analysis demonstrates that Hawaiian kinship is neither patrilineal nor matrilineal in the exclusive sense but allows considerable flexibility in tracing descent and group membership. The work contributes to understanding how kinship systems respond to particular ecological and social circumstances and shows the diversity of kinship organization beyond the patrilineal-matrilineal dichotomy that dominated earlier kinship theory.

## Publication & Context

Hanson's work on Hawaiian kinship appeared in the 1970s as part of broader anthropological reconsideration of kinship diversity and critique of the patrilineal-matrilineal dichotomy that had dominated kinship theory. Earlier work on Polynesian kinship by Raymond Firth (*We, the Tikopia*, 1936) and others had documented the variability in Pacific kinship systems, but systematic comparative analysis was lacking. Hanson's work synthesized earlier ethnography and proposed new analytical frameworks for understanding ambilineal and bilateral kinship systems.

The work appeared when Polynesian anthropology was becoming more sophisticated and when scholars were questioning the universality of descent-based kinship categorization. Hanson's emphasis on Hawaiian kinship as a system of ambilineality—allowing bilateral descent and flexible group membership—offered alternatives to the descent-based models that had dominated kinship theory.

The work also appeared in a context of growing Hawaiian political consciousness and cultural revitalization, though anthropological work had not yet fully shifted toward centering Hawaiian perspectives on their own kinship systems.

## Main Arguments & Thesis

Hanson's central argument is that Hawaiian kinship is fundamentally ambilineal—kinship is traced through both parents, and group membership can be established through either parent. This differs from patrilineal systems (where kinship and group membership are traced exclusively through fathers) or matrilineal systems (traced exclusively through mothers). In ambilineal systems, individuals have greater flexibility in choosing which kinship group to affiliate with and which lineage to claim descent from.

The Hawaiian system also emphasizes generational principles—kinship categories are organized around generational differences as much as around descent. Close collateral relatives (aunts, uncles, cousins) are often classified with parents and siblings, emphasizing generation over lineal distance.

Hanson argues that this system is well-adapted to Hawaiian ecological and social circumstances. The islands' productive capacity can support larger, more amorphous kinship groupings. Flexible group membership allows populations to adjust to resource constraints and to incorporate outsiders. The emphasis on generation creates unity across broad kinship categories.

He also argues that Hawaiian kinship cannot be adequately described through the descent-based models (patrilineal/matrilineal) that dominated kinship theory. These models assume exclusive, unambiguous descent and clear corporate groups. Hawaiian kinship is more flexible and less structured around corporate descent groups.

## Key Concepts Introduced

**Ambilineality**: The principle that kinship and group membership can be traced through either parent, without exclusive preference for one. This concept challenged the assumption that kinship systems must be patrilineal or matrilineal.

**Bilateral Descent**: The principle that kinship relationships are recognized on both mother's and father's sides. Hawaiian kinship recognizes bilateral relationships, though not in the corporate group formation that bilateral systems like the American kinship system emphasize.

**Generational Organization**: The principle that kinship categories are organized around generation as a primary organizing principle. Relatives of the same generation are often classified together regardless of lineal distance.

**Amorphous Kinship Groups**: Groups that are not clearly bounded or corporate but more flexible and contextual in membership. Hawaiian kinship groups show this characteristic rather than the clear corporate descent groups of patrilineal or matrilineal systems.

## Ethnographic & Theoretical Evidence

Hanson's argument is based on analysis of Hawaiian kinship terminology and descriptions of how kinship groups operate. He analyzes the Hawaiian kinship terms, showing how they classify relatives and how the classification emphasizes generation. He demonstrates that what appears in kinship terminology as undifferentiated relatives of the same generation reflects the ambilineal, generationally-organized nature of Hawaiian kinship.

He describes how Hawaiian kinship groups operate—how membership is determined, how inheritance occurs, how property is managed. He shows that Hawaiian kinship groups are less corporate and bounded than patrilineal or matrilineal descent groups, and that individuals have greater flexibility in group affiliation.

He provides comparison with other Polynesian kinship systems, showing both similarities (ambilineality, generational organization) and variations. This comparative approach shows that Hawaiian kinship is part of a broader Polynesian pattern while having distinctive features.

He also contextualizes Hawaiian kinship within Hawaiian social organization more broadly, showing how kinship relates to the chiefly system, land tenure, and religious beliefs.

## Impact on Anthropology

Hanson's work contributed to the reconsideration of kinship diversity and challenged the patrilineal-matrilineal dichotomy that had dominated kinship theory. The recognition of ambilineal and bilateral kinship systems as valid and widespread alternatives influenced kinship anthropology to become more comparative and less biased toward patrilineal systems (which had been overrepresented in ethnography and theory).

The work influenced Pacific anthropology by demonstrating the distinctive characteristics of Polynesian kinship systems and encouraging more systematic analysis of regional patterns. Subsequent scholars of Pacific kinship drew on Hanson's frameworks and built upon his analysis.

The emphasis on flexibility and situational kinship group affiliation influenced anthropological understanding of how kinship operates in practice. Rather than assuming kinship groups are fixed corporate entities, scholars came to recognize that group membership can be contextual and negotiable.

## Methodology & Approach

Hanson employed comparative ethnographic analysis, drawing on published ethnographies of Hawaiian and other Polynesian societies. He analyzed kinship terminology, ethnographic descriptions of kinship groups, and property and inheritance patterns. His analytical approach was structured-comparative—identifying logical principles underlying apparent diversity.

The work moved from analysis of Hawaiian kinship terminology and organization to comparison with other Polynesian and non-Polynesian systems, extracting general principles while noting distinctive features.

## Critique & Limitations

Critics have noted that while Hawaiian kinship shows ambilineal characteristics, Hanson's analysis may overstate the flexibility and understate how historical colonization has transformed Hawaiian kinship. Pre-contact Hawaiian kinship may have had clearer descent organization that was disrupted by colonization. The flexible, amorphous kinship Hanson describes may reflect post-contact conditions.

Postcolonial scholars have noted that anthropological analysis of Hawaiian kinship occurred within colonial contexts where Hawaiians had limited political power and Hawaiian sovereignty had been undermined. Anthropological knowledge production participated in colonial frameworks.

Hawaiian scholars have critiqued anthropological representations of Hawaiian kinship that do not center Hawaiian perspectives and understandings. How do Hawaiians themselves understand their kinship systems? What have Hawaiian oral traditions and modern Hawaiian scholarship said about kinship? These perspectives are incompletely represented in Hanson's analysis.

Some scholars have also questioned whether the emphasis on ambilineality adequately captures the gendered dimensions of Hawaiian kinship and the distinctive roles of Hawaiian women in kinship and political systems.

## Contemporary Relevance

Hanson's work remains important for understanding kinship diversity and for providing analysis of Pacific kinship systems. The concepts of ambilineality and generational organization remain useful for kinship analysis.

However, contemporary scholarship increasingly centers Hawaiian perspectives and attends to colonial contexts. Contemporary work on Hawaiian kinship emphasizes Hawaiian cultural knowledge, recognizes the impacts of colonialism on kinship systems, and seeks to support Hawaiian sovereignty and cultural continuity. Anthropological work builds on foundational scholarship like Hanson's while deploying more collaborative and decolonial approaches.
