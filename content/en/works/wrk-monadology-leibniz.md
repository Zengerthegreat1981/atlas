# The Monadology by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

## The Central Thesis

*La Monadologie* is a condensed metaphysical system in about ninety numbered paragraphs, resting on the claim that everything that exists is composed of simple, indivisible substances that Leibniz called **monads**. Each monad is **windowless** — it neither receives direct causal influence from outside itself nor exerts it — and yet the universe appears coherent and ordered as if the monads interacted. Leibniz resolves this apparent contradiction with the hypothesis of **pre-established harmony**: God set from eternity the states of all monads so that they agree with one another perfectly without direct causal intervention — an alternative solution to the problem of the interaction of the two substances (mind and body) that parallels Malebranche's occasionalism in its function (explaining agreement without direct causation) but by a completely different mechanism: Malebranche invokes continual divine intervention at every event, whereas Leibniz invokes a single eternal setting sufficient forever. To this is attached his inseparable theological-metaphysical thesis: that this world, among all the logically possible worlds that God conceived before creation, is **"the best of all possible worlds"** because it realizes the maximum possible variety with the simplest possible laws.

## The Structure of the Work

A short, condensed text divided into 90 consecutively numbered paragraphs (not chapters), beginning with the definition of the monad and its simplicity, moving on to the principle of the identity of indiscernibles (no two monads are identical), then perception (Perception) and appetition (Appetition) as intrinsic attributes of every monad, then the dominant central monad in every living being (the soul), and ending with pre-established harmony and the best of all possible worlds as the theological crowning of the system.

## Historical Context of Publication

Leibniz wrote the text in 1714, two years before his death, in French in Vienna, where he stayed between 1712 and 1714, as a summary of his final metaphysical position, and its addressee is not known with certainty. He gave it no title, so the name "Monadology" was supplied by later editors. **Leibniz did not publish it in his lifetime** — it remained a manuscript circulated only among his acquaintances. It was first published **in a German translation in 1720** (titled *Lehrsätze über die Monadologie*), and then **in the original French text for the first time in 1840** in Erdmann's complete edition of Leibniz's works — a gap of more than a century and a half between writing and publication in its original language, and a gap that is essential, not an incidental detail, in the history of the text's reception.

## Reception and Critique

The text was later received as the clearest condensed summary of Leibniz's systematic philosophy, and became the standard entry to it in university curricula despite its posthumous appearance and the late publication of the original. Continuing philosophical criticism has been directed at the concept of pre-established harmony as a solution that burdens God with explaining every observed harmony in the universe, and against the thesis of "the best of all possible worlds" in particular there is a famous satirical attack (the character Pangloss in Voltaire's *Candide*, 1759) that accused it of justifying real evil and suffering by a metaphysical optimism unsupported empirically.

## The Best-Known Paragraphs and the Course of the Argument

The text begins by defining the monad as a simple substance entering into composites, that is, without parts. It states that a composite exists only if simples exist, and that the simple has no extension, shape or divisibility. In paragraph seven it declares that monads have no windows, so nothing enters them and nothing leaves them. Paragraph seventeen is known as the mill argument: if we imagined a machine that thinks, and enlarged it until we could enter it as we enter a mill, we would find in it only parts pushing one another, and we would find nothing that explains perception, so perception is not explained by material machines. Then it distinguishes between the perception enjoyed by every monad and conscious perception, which it calls apperception and which is peculiar to rational souls. It rests on two principles: contradiction and sufficient reason, that is, nothing happens without a reason that explains why it is so and not otherwise. From the principle of sufficient reason it moves to the existence of God as the reason for the possible world.

## Monads, Souls and Bodies

Leibniz holds that every monad reflects the whole universe from its own perspective, so the world is a mirror of multiple perspectives. Monads differ in the clarity of their perception: some are bare, whose perception does not exceed obscurity, some are animal souls with memory, and some are rational spirits that know necessary truths and know God. Spirits are called citizens of the "City of God", and at the close of the text the kingdom of nature is in harmony with the kingdom of grace, so he makes the moral end crown the metaphysical system. The body in this picture is not an independent extended substance but an organized phenomenon resulting from the aggregation of monads.

## Publication and Sources

The text was translated into German by Heinrich Köhler and published in 1720 under the title "Principles of the Monadology", then a Latin summary of it was published in the journal *Acta Eruditorum* in 1721. The first publication of the French original came in Erdmann's edition in 1840. It is complemented by another short text the philosopher wrote in the same period, the "Principles of Nature and Grace", and the two are often read together.

## Comparisons with Islamic Thought

Scholars are accustomed to compare Leibniz's monads with the indivisible part (al-jawhar al-fard) of the Ash'arite theologians, for both posit simple units from which bodies are built. But the difference is fundamental: the indivisible part in kalam is a limited material part that God creates and renews the creation of at every instant, whereas the monad is a spiritual unit with an inner striving and perception. Shlomo Pines studied Islamic atomism and linked some of its ideas to the history of philosophical atomism without proving an influence on Leibniz.

## Limits of the System

- The principle of pre-established harmony explains the agreement among monads, but it makes real agency and freedom problematic.
- The impossibility of explaining the origin of space and extension from non-spatial units has led some readers to see the Monadology as an idealism that denies the reality of matter.
- The metaphysical optimism of the best of all possible worlds collides with the problem of evil, which Leibniz tried at length to address in the *Theodicy*.
