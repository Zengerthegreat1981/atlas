# The Sound Pattern of English

## Definition

*The Sound Pattern of English* (often abbreviated SPE) is a reference work by Noam Chomsky and Morris Halle, published in 1968. It established the classical framework of generative phonology and dominated theoretical phonological research for decades. Together with *Syntactic Structures* (1957) and *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), it is one of the pillars of the generative revolution in American linguistics.

## Historical Context

The book came after Chomsky had succeeded in transforming the field of syntax with his generative-transformational approach. He worked with his colleague Morris Halle, a Latvian-American phonologist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), to apply a similar logic to phonology: to build an explicit formal system of rules explaining how a speaker converts an abstract mental representation of a word into actual pronunciation, just as generative grammar explained how a surface structure is derived from a deep structure. The book built on the system of distinctive features that Roman Jakobson and Morris Halle had proposed in the 1950s, but reformulated it within an entirely new generative derivational framework.

## Central Ideas and Method

The book offers a comprehensive analysis of the sound system of English by positing a basic underlying phonological representation for each word, built from a bundle of binary distinctive features (such as [±voiced], [±nasal], [±high]). From this, the surface forms actually pronounced are derived by applying a sequence of ordered phonological "rewrite rules," each applied to the output of the previous rule. This is an explicit derivational model that parallels the methodology of transformational generative grammar in syntax. Among its best-known analytical examples is the treatment of vowel alternations in English (such as the vowel of *divine* versus *divinity*), which they explained by positing a shared underlying vowel from which the two surface forms are derived through stress-sensitive rules together with the Vowel Shift rule, with stress assignment itself governed by ordered rules. The book also included a chapter on markedness and the naturalness of phonological systems.

## Major Works and Representatives

The book is the principal work of Chomsky and Halle on phonology. It is usually read alongside Halle's other contributions to the theory of distinctive features (with Jakobson and Fant in the 1950s), and alongside later works that developed the derivational framework, such as John Goldsmith's autosegmental phonology (1976) and the metrical phonology of Liberman and Prince (1977) in the 1970s and 1980s, which attempted to address shortcomings of the original SPE model without abandoning its derivational core.

## Impact and Criticism

Despite its long dominance of the field, the book's model faced growing criticism later (including from Alan Prince and Paul Smolensky in the early 1990s), on account of the complexity of the sequential ordering mechanisms of rules, the multiplicity of ad hoc rules needed to explain certain patterns, and the difficulty of explaining cross-linguistic phonological patterns in a unified and elegant way. This led to the emergence of "optimality theory" as a radical alternative, based on the interaction of competing, violable constraints rather than strict sequential derivation. This alternative theory became highly influential in theoretical phonology from the 1990s onward.

## Legacy

The book remains a fundamental reference point in the history of theoretical phonology, and a source of basic terms and concepts (such as binary distinctive features and the concept of the derivational phonological rule) that are still used even in later frameworks that criticized its sequential derivational mechanism. It is still widely read as a historical entry point to generative phonology.

## Theoretical Appendix and Underlying Philosophy

The book's concluding chapter discussed broader philosophical questions about the nature of "simplicity" as a criterion for evaluating competing phonological theories, and about the relationship between formal phonological rules and the psychological reality of these rules in the mind of the actual speaker. These questions remained central to theoretical debate within generative phonology and morphology for decades afterward. Elsewhere the book discussed the nature of distinctive features themselves, including whether all features are purely binary; this question was revisited in later feature-geometric and autosegmental work.

## The Book's Place in the Careers of Its Authors

The book appeared at the height of the collaboration between Chomsky and Halle at MIT, where together they established a linguistics program that became one of the most important centers of linguistic research worldwide. The book was the culmination of a decade of research collaboration between them that extended from the 1950s. Halle himself was among the first to apply the concepts of distinctive features to the analysis of Russian and English phonology before joining Chomsky in formulating the complete generative framework presented in this book.
