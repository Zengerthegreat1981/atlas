# A Political Economy of the Middle East

The economist Alan Richards and the political scientist John Waterbury published this book in 1990 (with later revised editions, including 1996 and 2008). It became one of the most widely used university textbooks for teaching the comparative political economy of the Middle East, combining economic analysis with a political science framework that explains the relationship between the state and social classes in the region.

## The central analytical framework: state, class and development

The authors build their analysis around three interacting axes: the nature of the state (its relative strength, its degree of autonomy from society, and its sources of financing); the structure of social classes (especially the role of peasants, the emerging industrial bourgeoisie and the urban working class); and the pattern of economic development pursued (import-substituting industrialization, the oil-rentier model, or trade liberalization). They argue that understanding the trajectory of any Arab economy requires tracing the dynamic interaction among these three axes together, rather than studying any one of them in isolation from the others.

## Patterns among Middle Eastern economies

In broad terms, the book distinguishes between oil-rich economies (the Gulf states), resource-poor or labour-abundant economies that depend heavily on remittances, aid and other external transfers linked to regional oil income (such as Egypt and Jordan), and economies with a larger base in domestic agriculture and industry (such as Tunisia and Morocco). This grouping helps account for differences in the structure of the state, the sources of its income and the patterns of its class alliances; it builds on the rentier-state literature (see Beblawi and Luciani, eds., *The Rentier State*).

## A critique of import-substitution policies in the Arab region

The book devotes extended chapters to analysing the experience of import-substituting industrialization that most non-oil Arab states adopted after independence (Nasser's Egypt as a prominent example). The authors show how these policies initially produced tangible local industrial growth, but gradually led to the swelling of an inefficient public sector and the draining of state resources through broad commodity subsidies, which contributed to the fiscal and balance-of-payments crises that led to programmes of economic opening and structural adjustment from the late 1970s and 1980s.

## The Arab "social contract" and its gradual unravelling

The book develops the concept of the "social contract" that prevailed in the Arab region after independence: an implicit undertaking by the state to provide guaranteed public employment, broad commodity subsidies and free services (education and health) in exchange for popular acceptance of limited political participation. The contract came under strain as its financing became harder to sustain, contributing to social tensions and, later, to political protest.

## A comparative methodology across multiple cases

The authors adopt a comparative approach that studies multiple cases (including Egypt, Turkey, Iran, the Maghreb states and the Gulf states) side by side, which allows the reader to see the similarities and differences in Middle Eastern development trajectories rather than relying on a single isolated case. This breadth has made the book a widely used teaching reference.

## Updates across successive editions

Later editions updated the empirical material to reflect developments such as the wave of privatization and economic reform in the 1990s, while retaining the basic analytical framework of state, class and development.

## Its academic standing

More than three decades after its first edition, the book remains a standard reference in courses on the political economy of the Middle East and an entry point to the relationship between state, economy and society in the region.

## Relation to economic-history surveys

The book is often read alongside economic-history surveys such as Roger Owen and Şevket Pamuk, *A History of Middle East Economies in the Twentieth Century* (1998). The Richards and Waterbury volume gives more weight to political-science tools (theories of the state and class alliances), while the historical survey offers longer-run historical depth, so the two are complementary.

## Its treatment of the diversity of Arab political systems

The book pays particular attention to explaining the wide diversity of Arab political systems (military republics, hereditary monarchies, and oil-rentier systems) from an economic-political perspective. It relates the stability and legitimacy of each type of regime to its principal sources of income and its pattern of class alliances, which helps explain why different regimes responded differently to economic reform and popular protest.
