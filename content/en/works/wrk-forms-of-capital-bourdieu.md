# The Forms of Capital

"The Forms of Capital" is an essay by Pierre Bourdieu, first published in German in 1983 as "Ökonomisches Kapital, kulturelles Kapital, soziales Kapital" and in English in 1986, translated by Richard Nice, in *Handbook of Theory and Research for the Sociology of Education*, edited by John G. Richardson. It is the best-known compact statement of Bourdieu's account of capital.

## The Central Thesis

Bourdieu argues that the social world cannot be understood if capital is limited to the economic form that economic theory recognises. He distinguishes three fundamental forms. Economic capital is directly convertible into money and may be institutionalised as property rights. Cultural capital exists in three states: embodied, in the lasting dispositions of mind and body; objectified, in cultural goods such as books and instruments; and institutionalised, in academic qualifications. Social capital is the aggregate of the actual or potential resources linked to possession of a durable network of more or less institutionalised relationships of mutual acquaintance and recognition. He also refers to symbolic capital, the form that any capital takes when it is perceived and recognised as legitimate.

Bourdieu stresses that the forms can be converted into one another, though conversion requires work and time and involves loss. Economic capital is the root of the others, but each form has its own logic of transmission. Cultural capital in its embodied state depends on investment of time, beginning in the family, and the essay argues that its transmission is among the best hidden forms of hereditary transmission of capital. Because such capital appears to be natural talent or merit and not inherited advantage, it contributes to the reproduction of social inequality while seeming to be disinterested.

## Context and Critique of Economics

Bourdieu develops the argument partly against human capital theory, associated with economists such as Gary Becker, which in his view treated investment in education as a purely economic calculation and ignored the unequal social conditions of such investment. The essay builds on his earlier work with Jean-Claude Passeron on education and cultural reproduction, and on *Distinction* (1979).

## Impact and Criticism

The essay is one of the most cited texts in sociology and a standard reference for the concepts of cultural and social capital, which spread into education research, political science and management studies. James Coleman's "Social Capital in the Creation of Human Capital" (1988) and Robert Putnam's work developed different conceptions of social capital, and Alejandro Portes (1998) reviewed the concept's uses and limits. Critics argue that Bourdieu's conception stresses reproduction at the expense of agency and change, that the notion of "capital" can be stretched to cover too much, and that his account is based on French society.
