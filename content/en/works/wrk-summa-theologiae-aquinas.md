# Summa Theologiae by Thomas Aquinas

## The Central Thesis

A comprehensive systematic synthesis of Christian theology and Aristotelian philosophy, claiming that the truths reached by natural reason (such as the existence of God, inferred by cosmological proofs) and the truths reached only by revelation (such as the Trinity) do not conflict but form a single graded cognitive system: reason prepares the way for faith and does not contradict it. The best-known application of this method is the "Five Ways" (quinque viae) for proving the existence of God in the First Part, question 2, article 3 (among them the way of the first mover and the way of the efficient cause).

## The Structure of the Work

Three great parts, divided in turn into "questions" (Quaestiones) and then "articles" (Articuli), each article in a fixed dialectical form: objections (objectiones), then a counter-argument (sed contra), then Aquinas's answer (respondeo), then a reply to each objection. The First Part: God, creation, the angels and man. The Second Part is divided into two parts (the first general: the last end, the virtues and law; the second particular: the virtues and vices in detail). The Third Part: the Incarnation and the sacraments, and it stopped incomplete at the sacrament of penance. A "Supplement" (Supplementum) was later appended to it, which Aquinas did not write himself but was assembled by his pupil Reginald of Piperno from Aquinas's earlier writings (his commentary on the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard) to fill the gap.

## The Context: Intermittent Composition, Never Completed

It was written in stages in three cities: Rome (begun around 1265), then Paris (the late 1260s and early 1270s), then Naples, where composition stopped in December 1273. Sources contemporary with him related that after a spiritual experience during Mass he stopped writing and said that everything he had written was "like straw" compared with what he had seen. He died months later (March 1274) without resuming the work. The work was therefore not "published" in the modern sense of publication, but was copied and circulated in manuscript, and for this reason there is no single publication year that can properly be assigned to it.

## Reception and Criticism

Its teaching was imposed as a standard in the Dominican schools after its author's death, but it was not free of contemporary condemnations: some of its theses fell within the Paris condemnations of 1277 (Bishop Étienne Tempier of Paris), which targeted several Aristotelian currents, although they did not condemn Aquinas by name. Aquinas was canonized in 1323 and declared a Doctor of the Church in 1567, and is traditionally known as the "Angelic Doctor" (Doctor Angelicus); Pope Leo XIII's encyclical *Aeterni Patris* (1879) commended his philosophy as a model for Catholic thought. The standard critical edition is the Leonine edition (Leonina), whose commission Pope Leo XIII established after his encyclical in 1879, and whose first volumes appeared in 1882.

## Relation to Islamic and Jewish Philosophy

The work is of particular interest in the history of Islamic philosophy, because Aquinas read Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd, al-Ghazali, and Maimonides closely, as the sections below show.

## Philosophical Importance

A founding text for the relation of reason to faith in medieval philosophy, and a source of psychological-moral classifications (the powers of the soul, the affects/passions, the virtues and vices) that influenced Western moral philosophy and philosophy of the soul for centuries before the appearance of modern psychology, and a reference still present in contemporary philosophy of religion.

## The Five Ways and Their Link to the Arabic Heritage

In the second question of the First Part, Thomas presents five ways: from motion to a first mover, from the efficient cause to a first cause, from the possible and the necessary to a being necessary through itself, from degrees of perfection to a supreme perfect being, and from the governance of non-rational things toward ends to a rational governor. The third way connects with Ibn Sina's distinction between the possible in itself, the necessary through another, and the necessary through itself, and goes back in the Islamic tradition to the proofs of the philosophers and theologians who described God as the Necessary Existent. Thomas differs from the theologians in that he does not rely on the world's having a temporal beginning, and says in question 46 that the world's having a beginning is an article of faith that reason does not demonstrate. This places him near Maimonides, who held the same in the *Guide of the Perplexed*, and far from al-Ghazali, who made temporal origination the basis of the argument.

## Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd on Questions of Soul and Intellect

In the First Part there appear questions that Thomas took from a dialogue with the Muslim philosophers. In question 76 he rejects the view ascribed to Ibn Rushd that the possible intellect is one for all human beings, arguing that it is the individual human being who understands, otherwise individual responsibility and reward and punishment have no meaning. From Ibn Sina he takes the distinction of essence from existence in creatures, and goes beyond him by making existence the act of God the Creator, not an accident attaching to the essence. Thomas mentions al-Ghazali by the name Algazel when he presents the philosophers' views, relying on the Latin translation of the *Maqasid al-Falasifa*, and the influence of Maimonides shows in the question of the names of God and how He is described by what is beyond description, where Thomas determines that words are said of God by analogy, neither in the same sense nor in a wholly different sense.

## Ethics and Law

The first part of the Second Part occupies an ethical project based on happiness, holding that every human act is directed to an end, and that the last end is the contemplation of God. He then presents the theory of law in questions 90 and following: the eternal law, which is God's reason in governing the universe; the natural law, which is the rational being's participation in the eternal law, whose first principle is that the good is to be done and pursued and evil avoided; then human law, derived from the natural; and finally the revealed divine law. This theory is the basis of the natural law tradition in Western thought, and scholars compare it with the Mu'tazila's discussion of rational moral assessment of good and bad, but Thomas ties reason to the eternal law and not to the independence of reason from revealed law.

## Criticism and Limits

Duns Scotus and Ockham criticized the Thomists for tying the good to the divine intellect in a way that limits the freedom of the will, Luther criticized Thomas's reliance on Aristotle, and Kant criticized the proofs of God's existence. In modern analysis Anthony Kenny, in *The Five Ways* (1969), held that the arguments rest on Aristotelian physical principles that are no longer acceptable. Feminist criticism points to Thomas's statement in question 92 that the female is a defective male according to Aristotelian biology, an influence of the science of his time that is hard to separate from the structure of his theory of the human being. Contemporary moral criticism holds that natural law moves quickly from facts to norms. On the other side, scholars such as Eleonore Stump and Robert Pasnau defend the coherence of his analysis of soul and action and regard it as better than later theories.
