# On Being Sane in Insane Places

**Author:** David Rosenhan
**Year published:** 1973 (in *Science*)
**Original language:** English

An experimental study that provoked controversy, published by the American psychologist David Rosenhan in *Science* in 1973. It tested the ability of psychiatrists and psychiatric hospitals to distinguish between the "mentally sane" and the "insane" within psychiatric institutions. It became a standard citation in critiques of psychiatric diagnosis and of the public hospital.

## The Question of the Boundary Between Reason and Madness in the Arab Heritage

Rosenhan's question was preceded by long-standing interest in the blurred boundary between the sensible and the mad. In the Arab-Islamic heritage, Abu al-Qasim al-Nisaburi, in the fifth century AH, wrote *'Uqala' al-Majanin* ("The Wise Madmen"), gathering reports about people who were considered mad but who were men of wisdom or who uttered remarks of great reason. Michael Dols treated this literature in his study of the madman in medieval Islamic society, showing that the judgment of madness was usually a social one, linked to strange behavior, poverty and ill suspicion, and that the bimaristans sometimes received people whose illness was unknown. This does not mean that al-Nisaburi anticipated Rosenhan in method, since he was a collector of reports rather than an experimenter. But the two texts meet in the idea that the labeling of madness may obscure the truth about a person.

## Experimental Design

Eight "sane" persons (three women and five men: a graduate student in psychology, three psychologists, a pediatrician, a psychiatrist, a painter and a housewife; Rosenhan himself was one of them) presented themselves for admission to the psychiatric wards of twelve different psychiatric hospitals in five states on the two coasts. They claimed to hear unclear voices (auditory hallucinations) as their only entry symptom, saying that the voice spoke words such as "empty," "hollow" and "thud." Otherwise they concealed their names and occupations and answered the remaining questions truthfully. Once admitted, the "pseudopatients" immediately stopped claiming any symptoms, behaved completely normally, and recorded their observations about the stay secretly.

## Main Findings

All eight "pseudopatients" were admitted. All but one were diagnosed with schizophrenia (one was diagnosed with "manic-depressive psychosis"), and they were discharged with a diagnosis of "schizophrenia in remission" rather than as having recovered. That is, the medical staff never detected any of them as an impostor during their stays, which ranged between 7 and 52 days and averaged about nineteen days, although some real patients on the wards voiced suspicion that they were not genuinely ill. Rosenhan concluded that the institutional context itself compels every ordinary behavior of the patient (such as taking notes) to be read as an additional symptom once an initial label has been attached. He called this phenomenon the "stickiness of psychiatric labels." He recorded that a nurse wrote in a patient's file that the patient "engages in writing behavior." For a subset of the admissions he reported that daily contact with psychiatrists, psychologists, residents and physicians averaged 6.8 minutes (range about 3.9 to 25.1). That figure does not include nurses and attendants. He also reported that staff often failed to answer patients' questions, and he called the pattern "depersonalization." The pseudopatients hid the pills they were given rather than swallowing them, and they found that some real patients did the same.

### The Second Experiment

Rosenhan added a second experiment at a teaching and research hospital whose staff had heard of the first results and doubted them. He informed them that one or more pseudopatients would attempt admission within three months, and asked the staff to rate each new patient by the likelihood that they were a pseudopatient. Among 193 patients admitted during that period, 41 were judged with high confidence to be pseudopatients by at least one staff member, and 23 by at least one psychiatrist. Rosenhan later revealed, however, that he had not sent any pseudopatient at all. He concluded that error in discrimination occurs in both directions, and that the institution has no reliable criterion.

## Impact and Reception

The study caused a wide shock in the American psychiatric establishment. It was regarded as influential evidence of the fragility of the psychiatric diagnostic criteria then in use (before the adoption of the third diagnostic manual, DSM-III, in 1980), and it helped push toward more specific diagnostic criteria that were more amenable to clinical reproducibility. It aligned with Erving Goffman's critique of the total institution in *Asylums* (1961) and Thomas Scheff's labeling theory, and with the anti-psychiatry current led by Thomas Szasz and R. D. Laing. Deinstitutionalization in the United States was already under way after the Community Mental Health Act of 1963; the paper was used by critics of large hospitals, but it did not start that policy. Susannah Cahalan's *The Great Pretender* (2019) reopened the question of accuracy. She reported that she could securely identify only some of the pseudopatients and that Rosenhan's preserved notes did not always match the published article. Those findings are themselves disputed in detail. They are enough to treat unnamed case material in the 1973 paper as incompletely documented. Rosenhan did not publish the eight histories as hospital charts, which is why this entry does not reconstruct them.

## Limits and Criticism

The study has been subject to methodological criticisms since its publication: the small sample size, the absence of a control group, and reliance on an unverified, independently undocumented self-narrative. In 1975 Robert Spitzer wrote a sharp critique arguing that the doctors acted reasonably in diagnosing a patient who complained of hallucinations, that the phrase "schizophrenia in remission" meant the staff observed the disappearance of symptoms, and that an analogy with a patient who feigns vomiting blood would not show that internal medicine is invalid. Rosenhan replied the same year that diagnosis is influenced by its context, but did not offer an alternative criterion. Presenting the matter as a failure to detect a pseudopatient also overlooks that doctors generally trust a patient who requests treatment, and that psychiatric disorders have no laboratory test that verifies a claim, which limits the value of the comparison with organic diseases. This entry is educational and not clinical advice. The study remains important as an argument for the role of context and labeling, rather than as conclusive experimental evidence that psychiatric diagnosis is invalid.
