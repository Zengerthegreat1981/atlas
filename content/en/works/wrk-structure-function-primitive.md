# Structure and Function in Primitive Society

*Structure and Function in Primitive Society: Essays and Addresses* (1952) collects papers by A. R. Radcliffe-Brown written over several decades. With a foreword by E. E. Evans-Pritchard and Fred Eggan, it is the most convenient statement of his version of social anthropology.

## Contents

The introduction sets out the concepts of process, structure, and function. The essays include "The Mother's Brother in South Africa" (1924), two papers on joking relationships, a study of patrilineal and matrilineal succession, an essay on the study of kinship systems, "The Sociological Theory of Totemism", "Taboo", "Religion and Society", "On the Concept of Function in Social Science", "On Social Structure", and discussions of social sanctions and primitive law.

## Structure and function

Social structure consists of the network of actually existing relations among persons occupying institutionally defined positions. Radcliffe-Brown distinguishes it from structural form, the relatively constant pattern that the observer describes as individuals come and go. The function of a recurrent activity is the part it plays in the social life as a whole and therefore the contribution it makes to maintaining structural continuity. He compares society with an organism while noting limits to the analogy. The aim is a comparative natural science of society that seeks general propositions, distinct from conjectural history that reconstructs origins without adequate evidence.

## Analysing kinship and ritual

The essay on the mother's brother rejects the explanation of a sister's son's privileges as a survival of an earlier matrilineal stage. It relates them to a patterned set of attitudes within kinship: familiarity toward the mother's side contrasts with respect owed to the father's. The papers on joking relationships interpret licensed disrespect as a way of managing relations that combine attachment and separation. Principles such as the unity of the sibling group and of the lineage account for terminologies and behaviour. In the work on ritual, rites express and sustain sentiments on which social cohesion depends; Radcliffe-Brown argues that rite may create anxiety as well as relieve it, in contrast with Malinowski's account of magic.

The essays also show how the method works on particular puzzles. A custom is first placed among related customs, then compared with similar arrangements elsewhere, and finally explained by a general principle about relations between persons or groups. In the study of totemism, for example, Radcliffe-Brown asks why natural species acquire ritual value and links that value to the segmentation of society and to the importance of those species in social life. In the discussion of sanctions and law he distinguishes diffuse approval and disapproval from organised penalties, and treats law as social control through the systematic application of force by a politically organised society. This definition, taken partly from jurisprudence, implies that some societies have no law in the strict sense, a conclusion that later anthropologists of dispute settlement contested.

## Intellectual antecedents

The influence of Émile Durkheim is clear in the treatment of social facts, solidarity, and religion. The emphasis differs from Malinowski's functionalism, which referred institutions more directly to human needs.

## Assessment

The essays offered precise questions and encouraged systematic comparison, shaping British and, through his Chicago teaching, some American anthropology. Critics argued that explaining a practice by its contribution to continuity does not show why it arose and can make social order appear presupposed. Conflict, change, individual strategy, and colonial history receive limited analysis. Evans-Pritchard later rejected the natural-science model in favour of closer ties with history, and Edmund Leach and others questioned typological comparison. The word "primitive" in the title reflects the usage of the period and implies a ranking that anthropologists now reject.
