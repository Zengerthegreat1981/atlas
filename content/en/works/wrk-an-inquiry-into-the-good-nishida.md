# An Inquiry into the Good by Kitarō Nishida (Zen no Kenkyū)

*An Inquiry into the Good* (善の研究, Zen no Kenkyū) is the first book of the Japanese philosopher Kitarō Nishida (1870–1945), published in Tokyo in 1911 by Kōdōkan. The word "Zen" in the title means "the good" (善), not Buddhist "Zen" (禅), although the pronunciation of the two words is similar in Japanese, which confused many foreign readers; the book is a philosophical inquiry into reality, ethics and religion, even though its author was a practitioner of Zen meditation.

## The Central Thesis

The thesis of the book is that **"pure experience" (junsui keiken, 純粋経験)** is the original reality and the first cognitive unity, and that it is **logically and temporally prior to the division into subject and object**. Everyday perception, which separates the "knowing subject" from the "perceived thing," is for Nishida a later and derived construction, not the starting point. Pure experience is closer to the moment of complete absorption in action or perception before reflective thinking intervenes and splits it into two poles: like one who climbs a mountain or plays a tune and does not feel himself separate from his act. From this primary unity Nishida derives a broader metaphysical position that seeks to overcome the subject-object duality inherited from modern Western philosophy.

## Structure of the Work

The book is divided into four parts: pure experience, reality, the good, and religion.

1. **Pure experience**: he defines the concept, then treats what is supposed to derive from it: thought, will and intellectual intuition, and shows that each is a form of the primary unity. Thinking for him is a pure experience in which images are unified, and will is the transition within experience to a more complete state.
2. **Reality**: he builds on pure experience a theory of being: all reality is pure experience developing by itself through the unification and differentiation of opposites, and nature and spirit are two faces of one reality.
3. **The good**: an ethical theory that defines the good as the realization of the self in its unity with total reality, not conformity to an external rule nor the pursuit of pleasure. The good act is that which fully realizes personality, and the highest good is the union between self and cosmos.
4. **Religion**: a religious treatment that approaches God as the basis of that unity, not as a transcendent being separate from the world, and he holds that religion is the self's need to unite with the deepest reality.

The order is deliberately ascending: each part presupposes what precedes it. Nishida stated in the preface of the book that he wrote the second and third parts first, then added the first as an introduction and the fourth as a conclusion, so the book in origin is successive stages and not a single plan laid down from the beginning.

## The Good and Personality

In the third part Nishida distinguishes among ethical theories that make the good obedience to an authority (authoritarian ethics), or the attainment of pleasure, or the application of abstract reason, and holds that they all err in making the good something external to the self. The good for him is the **realization of personality**, that is, that the self reach the widest possible expression of its inner unity, and the complete personality is that which unites with total reality until its act becomes the act of reality itself. In this way ethics is tied to religion, because union with the deepest reality is what religion calls union with God, and for this reason the book moves from psychology to ethics to religion in a natural progression.

## Historical Context of Publication

Nishida wrote the book while teaching at the Fourth Higher School in Kanazawa, having practiced Zen meditation and read Western philosophy deeply, William James in particular, and the idea of "pure experience" was taken from James's term but in a different sense: James means a stream of consciousness prior to the distinction between thought and thing, whereas Nishida makes it the whole ontological foundation. In 1909 he moved to the Peers' School (Gakushuin) in Tokyo, and in 1910 to Kyoto University; the book appeared in January 1911, coming as an attempt at synthesis between two heritages: Zen Buddhist meditation on one side, and the idealist and psychological methods of European philosophy on the other.

## Reception and Critique

The book became widely read among Japanese youth, and it is the point around which the Kyoto School later gathered (Nishitani, Tanabe and others). Criticism was directed at it, sometimes from within the Kyoto School itself, that the concept of "pure experience" is loose and hard to distinguish analytically from other forms of unmediated consciousness, and that its theory of the good tends to make ethics an individual sentiment within the total unity, weakening the relation between persons within it. Nishida himself later reformulated his project with concepts such as "absolute nothingness" and "place" (basho), so the 1911 framework is a first stage and not a final formulation.

## Translations

The existence of a published Arabic translation of this work has not been established. The best-known English translation is that of **Masao Abe and Christopher Ives** (Yale University Press, 1990).
