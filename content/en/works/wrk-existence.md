# Existence: A New Dimension in Psychiatry and Psychology

*Existence: A New Dimension in Psychiatry and Psychology* (1958) was a major vehicle for carrying European existential psychiatry into English. It is an edited volume, published in New York by Basic Books in 1958 under the editorship of Rollo May, Ernest Angel, and Henri F. Ellenberger — and its importance lies not in original content but in the act of transmission itself. The volume presents to an American reader a current that had existed in Europe for decades: the *Daseinsanalyse* of Ludwig Binswanger, the phenomenological psychopathology of Eugène Minkowski, Viktor von Gebsattel, Erwin Straus, and others. It is a composite volume, not a continuous argument by a single author, and nothing written by one editor should be attributed to the others.

## The Implicit Claim

The volume's implicit argument is that American psychiatry, then dominated by Freudian psychoanalysis and behaviourism, lacked a theory of being-in-the-world as a unit of analysis that precedes the classification of symptoms. Its philosophical reference is the phenomenology of Husserl, the philosophy of Heidegger in *Being and Time* (1927), and the existentialism of Kierkegaard: the human being is not to be described as a closed psychic apparatus but as a being who opens a world and lives in it. The book puts to work Binswanger's concepts of the three modes of world — the *Umwelt*, the world around; the *Mitwelt*, the world shared with others; and the *Eigenwelt*, the world of the self — concepts May later adopted and introduced into the American literature. May's own framing in his introductory essays identifies the themes on which the existential direction converges: nonbeing, the threat of annihilation that gives existence its sharpness; anxiety as a basic experience rather than a symptom to be removed; commitment and decision; and the fundamental consideration of the patient's lived experience before any interpretation. The direction, May argues, does not want to replace psychoanalysis and behaviourism with a new theory, but to ask the question those sciences leave out: who is the human being they study? That is why the book seems more philosophical than technical, and why the editors presented it as a "new dimension" rather than a "new school".

## The Structure of the Volume

The volume consists of a theoretical introduction followed by translated selections. May contributes two essays: the first on the origins of the existential movement in psychology and its significance, tracing its roots through Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Husserl, Heidegger, and Sartre; the second on the contributions of existential psychotherapy. Together they draw the boundary between the existential direction and the schools of psychoanalysis and behaviourism. Ellenberger contributes "A Clinical Introduction to Psychiatric Phenomenology and Existential Analysis", which presents the European origins and their clinical application. Then come translations of case studies, the most famous being Binswanger's "The Case of Ellen West", the study of a patient whose eating disorder reached its extreme and who killed herself in 1921 after leaving the sanatorium; Binswanger reads her illness as a way of being, not merely as a set of symptoms. The volume also includes studies by Minkowski on schizophrenic depression, von Gebsattel on the world of the compulsive patient, Roland Kuhn on an attempted murder, and Straus. The selection reflects the editors' judgement of what mattered to an American reader — and it left Medard Boss's school, among others, less present than it deserved.

## Historical Context

The book appeared in 1958, at a time when the works of Binswanger and Minkowski were simply not available in English at all. It preceded the founding of the Association for Humanistic Psychology in 1961 and helped prepare the intellectual ground for what was called the third force, after psychoanalysis and behaviourism. May followed it with *Existential Psychology* (1961), and Ellenberger later published *The Discovery of the Unconscious* (1970), which mapped the history of dynamic psychiatry and pointed to its existential roots.

## Reception and Influence

The volume was received as the principal gateway through which European existential analysis entered English, and it influenced American existential therapists. Herbert Spiegelberg's *Phenomenology in Psychology and Psychiatry* (1972) later provided the historical frame that places these works in context. Critics objected that the volume sometimes simplifies in comparison with the complexity of the German and French texts, and that the translation loses some of the precision of Heideggerian terminology — *Dasein*, for example, is rendered in more than one way. Critics of existential analysis also noted that the case studies, such as Ellen West, rest on interpretations that are difficult to test and give the physician no alternative diagnostic tools.

## Limits and Standing Today

The volume offers no complete therapeutic method: it is a theoretical introduction and a display of cases, and it was later completed by works such as Yalom's *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980), which formulated a clearer therapeutic frame. Its value today is as a historical document — the record of the moment a tradition changed language. In the Arabic-speaking world, existential philosophy was introduced largely through Abd al-Rahman Badawi, in *Existential Time* (1943) and *Studies in Existential Philosophy* (1961). As a document, *Existence* remains a key source for anyone who wants to see the moment of transfer.
