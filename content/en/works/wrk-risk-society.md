# Risk Society

*Risk Society: Towards a New Modernity* (1986), by the German sociologist Ulrich Beck, is one of the most influential diagnoses of late modernity. Published in German as *Risikogesellschaft: Auf dem Weg in eine andere Moderne* — the English translation appeared in 1992 — the book argues that modern societies have entered a new phase in which the central problem is no longer the distribution of wealth but the distribution of risks. Industrial modernity, Beck contends, now produces hazards of its own making — nuclear accidents, chemical pollution, climate change, financial crises — that escape the control of the institutions created to manage them.

## Historical Context and Publication

Beck wrote *Risk Society* in West Germany in the early 1980s, a society marked by fierce public conflict over nuclear power, acid rain, chemical accidents, and the environmental costs of industrial growth. Beck was professor of sociology at the University of Bamberg at the time (he later moved to Munich), and his argument grew out of the West German environmental movement and the broader crisis of confidence in technological progress. The book was published in 1986, the year of the Chernobyl disaster, which lent it great resonance. Its English translation in 1992, together with Beck's collaboration with Anthony Giddens and Scott Lash, carried the argument into Anglophone sociology, where it became a defining statement of the theory of reflexive modernisation.

## Thesis and Core Argument

Beck's central claim is that modernity has become reflexive: it now confronts the unintended consequences of its own success. In the first, industrial phase of modernity, the dominant conflict concerned the distribution of goods — wealth, income, and security — and institutions such as the nation-state, the welfare state, and science seemed capable of managing progress. In the risk society, by contrast, the dominant conflicts concern "bads" produced by modernisation itself. These manufactured risks differ from the natural dangers of earlier eras: they are global in scope, invisible to the senses, incalculable in their effects, and irreversible. Because the institutions of industrial society, including science itself, both produce these risks and claim to manage them, they suffer a crisis of legitimacy. Beck's term for this condition, developed especially in his subsequent work, is "organised irresponsibility": everyone is involved, but no one can be held accountable.

## Structure and Method

The book moves from diagnosis to social structure to politics. It opens with the logic of risk distribution, showing how risks follow a different social logic from wealth and how they undermine the insurance principle on which industrial society relied. It then turns to individualisation, arguing that as class, family, and occupational identities weaken, individuals are forced to manage their own biographies — their careers, relationships, and health — under conditions they did not choose. The final part develops the politics of the risk society: the rise of subpolitics, in which decisions about technology and the environment are increasingly made outside parliaments, in laboratories, corporations, and social movements. Beck's method is theoretical and essayistic rather than based on new empirical research: he synthesises evidence from environmental disasters, scientific controversies, and social change to build a sweeping interpretation of an epoch.

## Key Concepts

*Risk Society* and Beck's closely related writings introduced a set of concepts now widely used in sociology. "Manufactured risks" are dangers created by human activity rather than by nature. "Reflexive modernisation" names the process by which modernity turns back on itself, questioning its own foundations. "Individualisation" describes the release of individuals from traditional bonds and their compulsion to construct their own lives. "Subpolitics" refers to political action outside formal institutions, from citizens' initiatives to scientific dissent. "Organised irresponsibility" captures the diffusion of accountability for systemic hazards.

## Reception and Influence

The book was widely acclaimed and translated into many languages, and it established Beck as one of the leading sociologists of his generation. It shaped research on environmental sociology, the sociology of risk, science and technology studies, and globalisation, and it provided a framework for understanding events from the BSE crisis to the Fukushima disaster and the climate emergency. Beck developed the argument further in *World Risk Society* (1999), *The Cosmopolitan Vision* (2006; German 2004) and *World at Risk* (2009), which extended the analysis to global risks such as terrorism, climate change and financial crisis.

## Critical Assessments

Critics have questioned the book on several fronts. Some argue that Beck exaggerates the novelty of manufactured risk, since industrial hazards such as mining disasters and urban pollution are as old as industrialisation itself. Others, notably in the sociology of risk, dispute his account of public perception: empirical studies suggest that lay people do not experience risk in the abstract, global terms Beck describes, and that class and inequality still shape exposure to hazards far more than his slogan about democratic smog implies. Critical assessments, such as Anthony Elliott's of 2002, have pressed Beck on the coherence of his account of reflexivity and individualisation and on how risks are actually governed in institutions. Feminist and postcolonial critics have noted that the book generalises from a Western European experience and pays little attention to how risk is distributed along lines of race, gender, and global inequality. Beck responded to some of these criticisms in later work, giving greater weight to global inequality and to the politics of risk definition.

## Continuing Relevance

*Risk Society* has only gained relevance since its publication. Climate change is the paradigmatic manufactured risk: global, invisible in its causes, unequally distributed in its effects, and resistant to national solutions. The COVID-19 pandemic and financial crises are often analysed as fitting Beck's account of hazards produced by the very systems meant to deliver security, although this application is interpretive. Its lasting achievement is to have shown that the central sociological question of our time is not only who gets what, but who is exposed to what — and who decides.
