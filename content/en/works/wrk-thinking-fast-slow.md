# Thinking, Fast and Slow

## The central thesis

Human cognition is run by two systems distinct in speed and mental cost: **System 1** (fast, intuitive, automatic, emotional, without conscious effort) and **System 2** (slow, analytical, mentally costly, conscious). System 1 dominates most of our daily decisions, while the human being thinks that his "conscious thinking" (System 2) is the actual decision-maker; often its real role is to **justify** retroactively what System 1 produced, not to generate it. The labels "System 1" and "System 2" come from the dual-process literature (Stanovich and West, 2000), and Kahneman adopted and popularized them. This division is an explanatory, pedagogical metaphor and not an exact physiological anatomy of two regions of the brain, and Kahneman himself drew attention to this.

## Structure of the work

The book is a narrative synthesis of decades of a joint research program with **Amos Tversky**, not a single new study. It is organized in five parts: (1) Systems 1 and 2, (2) heuristics and biases, including **anchoring** and the **availability heuristic**, (3) overconfidence, (4) choices and Prospect Theory, (5) the "two selves": the experiencing self versus the remembering self, and the effect of this on the definition of happiness.

## Historical context of publication

It was published in 2011 by Farrar, Straus and Giroux, nine years after Kahneman received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (2002) for his research program with Tversky (who died in 1996, before the prize). The book is **not an original research work** but a popular compilation and simplification of two research programs that preceded it by decades: **heuristics and biases** (Tversky & Kahneman, *Science*, 1974) and **Prospect Theory** (Kahneman & Tversky, *Econometrica*, 1979). The basic technical terms, anchoring, availability, loss aversion, predate the book by decades; the book's genuine contribution is the narrative synthesis and new formulations such as **"What you see is all there is"** (WYSIATI) and the **experiencing/remembering self**.

## Reception and criticism

It was widely received, both popularly and academically, and was regarded as a popular cornerstone of "behavioral economics" and later of "nudge" applications in the work of Thaler and Sunstein. But the critical reception includes three essential points that are not to be omitted:

- **The replication crisis**: the chapter on "priming" relied on studies (including Bargh's experiment on words related to aging and walking slowly, 1996) that did not withstand later replication attempts. Kahneman warned social priming researchers in 2012 of a looming "train wreck" and acknowledged publicly in 2017 that he had placed too much faith in underpowered studies, and the book should not be presented without mentioning this.
- **Gerd Gigerenzer's methodological objection** since the 1990s: much of what is classified as "bias" is an effect of how the question is framed (percentages versus natural frequencies) and not a fixed cognitive defect, and fast heuristics are effective adaptive tools in their environments, not deficiencies. The debate between the two schools has not been settled.
- **Decline in the effect sizes of nudge applications** in public policy from what early readings of the book had promised, with part of this explained by publication bias.

## Why it is mentioned in the Atlas

A central reference in popularizing the idea of "bounded rationality" and the limits of human cognition outside academic circles, and it is the best-known entry point to the heuristics and biases program in the Atlas, although the origin of that research program precedes the book by decades.
