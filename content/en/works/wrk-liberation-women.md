# The Liberation of Women

*The Liberation of Women* is a book by the Egyptian reformer and judge Qasim Amin (1863–1908), published in Cairo in 1899. It is the best-known Arabic text on the woman question at the beginning of the modern era. The book provoked a broad journalistic and intellectual controversy upon publication, because it combined a defense of women's education with a call to review the veil, divorce and polygamy. This was in an age when the British occupation of Egypt, which had begun in 1882, placed social reform under suspicion of political motives.

## Background: From Tahtawi to Abduh

The discussion of women did not begin with Qasim Amin. Rifa'a al-Tahtawi (d. 1873) called in *al-Murshid al-Amin li-al-Banat wa-al-Banin* (1872) for teaching girls reading, writing and arithmetic, arguing that knowledge does not compromise chastity. The first government school for girls in Egypt was established in 1873. Muhammad Abduh, the leading figure of Islamic reform, addressed polygamy and divorce in his fatwas and articles, and said that the condition of justice between wives makes polygamy in practice forbidden or restricted. Amin belonged to Abduh's circle, and some of his contemporaries attributed to Abduh a share of credit for the book. The studies do not settle the extent of this role. The book remains attributed to Amin's pen, while acknowledging Abduh's influence on his intellectual environment.

## The Book's Argument

Amin builds his argument on the claim that the progress of the nation depends on the progress of its women, and that an ignorant woman cannot raise free children. He calls for educating girls at least at the primary level, for restricting divorce under the supervision of a judge, for prohibiting polygamy except in necessity, and for allowing spouses to choose each other before the marriage contract. On the veil, he distinguishes between covering the body, which the Sharia permits, and the face veil (*niqab*) and the seclusion of women. He cites juristic opinions holding that the face and the hands are not *'awra* (parts of the body that must be covered), and he regards seclusion as a recent custom rather than an obligation. He compares the condition of the Egyptian woman with that of the European woman and makes Western civilization the measure of progress. This distinguishes his discourse from that of Abduh, which was more firmly anchored in the jurisprudential reference.

## Controversy and Responses

The book met a torrent of responses. Talat Harb published *Tarbiyat al-Mar'a wa-al-Hijab* (1899) and *Fasl al-Khitab fi al-Mar'a wa-al-Hijab* (1901). Muhammad Farid Wajdi wrote *al-Mar'a al-Muslima* (1901). The leader Mustafa Kamil replied in the newspaper *al-Liwa'*, and other newspapers criticized his discourse. Amin issued *al-Mar'a al-Jadida* (The New Woman, 1900), defending his thesis and expanding his citation of European social scientists. Women's responses came later with Malak Hifni Nasif (*Bahithat al-Badiya*), who called for women's education and rights but expressed reservations about imitating the West. This controversy revealed that the disagreement was not only about education, but about who had the authority to speak about women.

## Critical Readings

Leila Ahmed, in *Women and Gender in Islam* (1992), argued that Amin's discourse borrowed the language of colonialism. Lord Cromer, in *Modern Egypt* (1908), attacked the veil as evidence of Muslim backwardness, so Amin's arguments intersected with the occupier's discourse despite differences in intention. Beth Baron (1994) and Margot Badran (1995) showed that the Egyptian women's press preceded the book, and that women did not wait for men to liberate them. Juan Cole, in his study "Feminism, Class, and Islam in Turn-of-the-Century Egypt" (1981), offered a reading that connects the book to the emerging urban middle class and its interests. Other scholars argue that these readings underestimate the book's role in opening public debate.

## Caveats

Amin's book is a reformist rather than a revolutionary work. It makes the woman a qualified mother and wife before making her an independent individual, keeps her primary mission in the home and in upbringing, and calls for a legal veil in place of complete unveiling. Later feminists criticized these limits. It is also uncertain how much he drew on European sources, a question the text alone does not settle. The book's practical effect on the education of girls and on personal status laws came slowly, and became visible only after decades, with the founding of the Egyptian University in 1908 and the Egyptian Feminist Union in 1923.
