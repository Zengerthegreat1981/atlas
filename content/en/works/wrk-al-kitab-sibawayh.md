# Al-Kitāb (The Book)

## Definition

"Al-Kitāb" is the first comprehensive and systematic Arabic grammatical work to survive in full. It was composed by ʿAmr ibn ʿUthmān ibn Qanbar, known as Sibawayh (died c. 180 AH / 796 CE), in Basra in the second century AH. Sibawayh did not name his work. Its unique standing led later grammarians simply to call it "al-Kitāb" ("The Book"), a designation reserved for this work above all other grammatical treatises. It is said to have been called the "Qur'an of grammar," reflecting its authority among practitioners of the discipline.

## Historical Context

Sibawayh grew up in Basra, one of the two principal centres of early Arabic linguistic study alongside Kufa. He studied under al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad al-Farāhīdī, to whom the tradition attributes Arabic prosody (ʿarūḍ) and the lexicon *al-ʿAyn* (also associated with al-Layth ibn al-Muẓaffar). Al-Khalīl deeply influenced Sibawayh's method and supplied many of the opinions the book includes, attributed to him explicitly ("Qāla al-Khalīl," "al-Khalīl said"). The book was composed at a time when several factors converged: the expansion of Islamic conquests and the entry of many non-Arabs into Arabic, which raised fears of the "corruption of the tongue"; the need to fix the reading of the Holy Qur'an; and the emergence of a movement to collect linguistic evidence from pre-Islamic poetry and the speech of eloquent Bedouins before it disappeared.

## Main Ideas and Method

The book covers the chapters of grammar, morphology and Arabic phonology, using a rigorously inductive method. It gathers evidence from the Qur'an, pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry, and the speech of reliable eloquent Bedouins, and then draws general rules from that evidence. Sibawayh developed a precise methodological distinction between degrees of grammatical acceptability: *mustaqīm ḥasan* (correct and acceptable), *mustaqīm qabīḥ* (correct but stylistically disfavoured), and *muḥāl* (rejected both grammatically and logically). The distinction is sometimes compared with modern notions of grammaticality versus acceptability, though the two frameworks differ. He also established the concept of the grammatical *ʿāmil* (governing agent): every case ending in a sentence is attributed to an "agent" that requires it (a verb, a particle or a noun). This concept became the basis of later Arabic grammatical theory. Later editors divided the continuous text into several hundred chapters (ʿAbd al-Salām Hārūn's edition numbers them throughout). It begins with the basic categories of speech and moves through syntax (the governed cases, the verb and its arguments, the particles and their effects) to morphology (nominal and verbal patterns), and it concludes with chapters on assimilation and the speech sounds (points of articulation and their qualities). Those final chapters are the fullest early phonetic description of Arabic that survives intact. The introduction to *al-ʿAyn*, traditionally linked to al-Khalīl, also treats points of articulation.

## Major Works and Figures

Al-Kitāb is a unique text, unrivalled in comprehensiveness among the works of its generation. No grammar of comparable breadth by a contemporary or predecessor survives. Reports mention earlier Basran books, including works ascribed to ʿĪsā ibn ʿUmar, but they are lost. He drew on evidence from major poets of the pre-Islamic and early Islamic periods, and he transmitted the opinions of his teacher al-Khalīl ibn Aḥmad and of the Basran grammarians ʿĪsā ibn ʿUmar and Yūnus ibn Ḥabīb. He occasionally records disagreements with Kufan grammarians without engaging in the doctrinal controversy that became famous between the two schools later on.

## Influence and Criticism

Al-Kitāb became the essential reference for every later Arabic grammarian. Many scholars over the centuries commented on it, abridged it or glossed it. The most prominent commentators include Abū Saʿīd al-Sīrāfī and ʿAlī ibn ʿĪsā al-Rummānī; al-Mubarrad also engaged critically with it. Some readers have found its terse, archaic prose and its arrangement difficult by modern standards, and it requires supplementary explanation for full comprehension. This has not diminished its unrivalled reference status in the history of Arabic linguistic study, and it remains the subject of in-depth study in modern Arabic linguistics and in research on the history of linguistics worldwide.

## Connection to the Classical Arabic-Islamic Heritage

Al-Kitāb is the foundational pillar of the entire Arabic-Islamic grammatical heritage. It arose from the need to fix the reading of the Holy Qur'an and to preserve the Arabic tongue. It was closely tied to the sciences of Qur'anic exegesis, Hadith and jurisprudence, which relied on its grammatical precision to understand religious texts correctly. It thus became the cornerstone on which the art of Arabic grammar was built as an authentic Islamic science.

## Additional Methodological Significance

The book is also a unique linguistic document, since it is the oldest source to record the pronunciation of Classical Arabic and its ancient tribal dialects in a methodical way. Sibawayh recorded dialectal differences between Arab tribes (such as Tamīm and the Ḥijāz) in matters of inflection, morphology and pronunciation. This makes it an important source for modern scholars of ancient Arabic dialects and of the history of the Arabic language before the standardisation of Classical Arabic. Sibawayh also used precise grammatical terms that remained in use in later Arabic grammar, such as *fāʿil* (subject), *mafʿūl bihi* (direct object), *ḥāl* (circumstantial accusative), *tamyīz* (specification), *mubtada'* (topic) and *khabar* (predicate). He established a systematic classification of verbs, nouns and particles based on integrated morphological and grammatical criteria.

## The Death and the Famous Debate

Traditional sources report that Sibawayh went to Baghdad, where he took part in a famous debate with the Kufan grammarian al-Kisāʾī, known as the "Zunbūriyya question." According to these accounts, Sibawayh was judged to have lost because of the bias of the audience; he then returned to Persia, where he died young. Modern scholars doubt the historicity of parts of this story. He did not witness the wide dissemination of his book, which gained its full reference status only after his death, through his students and commentators.
