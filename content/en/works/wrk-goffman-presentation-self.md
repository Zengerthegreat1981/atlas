# The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life

Erving Goffman's *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* — first published in Scotland in 1956, in the United States in 1959 — is one of the most widely read books in the history of sociology. Its claim is that everyday social life is a performance. When people meet, they present a version of themselves — a front — and manage the impressions others form of them, much as actors manage an audience. Goffman does not mean that people are insincere; he means that social interaction has a structure, and that structure can be described with the precision of a stage manager's handbook. Front stage and back stage, teams, audiences and impression management: these terms entered sociology from this book and never left. 

## Historical Context and Publication

The book grew out of Goffman's doctoral fieldwork on the island of Unst in the Shetlands, where he lived in 1949–1951 studying a small crofting and fishing community, and out of his dissertation at the University of Chicago, "Communication Conduct in an Island Community" (1953). It was first published by the University of Edinburgh's Social Sciences Research Centre in 1956, and in a revised American edition by Doubleday in 1959. The timing matters. Post-war sociology was dominated by grand theory — Parsons's systems, Merton's functionalism — and by survey research. Goffman offered something else entirely: close, almost literary observation of face-to-face interaction, informed by symbolic interactionism and by the Chicago School's ethnographic tradition. 

## Author's Biographical Situation

Goffman was born in 1922 in Mannville, Alberta, to Ukrainian Jewish immigrant parents, and grew up in Canada before studying sociology at the University of Toronto and the University of Chicago. His outsider's biography is often invoked to explain his angle of vision: an immigrant's son, a Canadian in America, a Jew in a largely Protestant discipline, he watched social life as someone who had to learn its rules rather than inheriting them. At Chicago he studied under Everett Hughes, whose ethnographic sociology of occupations and institutions shaped Goffman's method. He taught at the University of California, Berkeley, and later at the University of Pennsylvania, where he held the Benjamin Franklin Chair. 

## Core Argumentative Structure

The book's governing metaphor is the theatre, and Goffman develops it systematically. When a person appears before others, they present a performance, using a front — setting, appearance, and manner — to define the situation and to guide the audience's response. The performance takes place front stage, where the audience is present; back stage, the performer can relax, drop the front, and prepare. Performers work in teams, cooperating to sustain a shared definition of the situation, and they engage in impression management — the techniques, from tact to deception, by which a performance is kept going. His point is not that life is fake, but that the self is a product of interaction: the person we take someone to be is the effect of a successfully sustained performance.

## Key Concepts and Theoretical Framework

The book's vocabulary is its lasting contribution. The front is the expressive equipment a performer uses — the setting, the appearance, the manner. Front stage is the region where the performance is given; back stage is where it is prepared and where the performer can be off character. A team is a group of performers who cooperate to stage a single routine, as a family does for guests. Impression management is the work of controlling the audience's view of the performance, including the defensive practices that protect it and the protective practices by which audiences tactfully overlook flaws. Closely related are the notions of face, the positive social value a person claims for themselves in an encounter, and face-work, the work of maintaining it, which Goffman developed mainly in his essay "On Face-Work" (1955), later reprinted in *Interaction Ritual* (1967). 

## Evidence and Influence

The book's evidence is the hotel on Unst and the countless small encounters Goffman collects. In the hotel, the staff maintained a front of deference and competence for guests while back stage — in the kitchen, in the staff quarters — they mocked the guests and dropped the performance entirely. The boundary between the two regions was policed with real anxiety, because a guest who wandered back stage would see the performance for what it was. The book created the microsociology of interaction as a field. It established that face-to-face encounters have a structure worth theorising, and it gave sociologists a language for describing it. 

## Continuing Relevance and Critical Assessments

The book's relevance has only grown, because the world it describes has expanded. Goffman wrote about hotel staff and dinner parties; his readers now apply his concepts to job interviews on video calls, to dating apps, to the curated self of Instagram, and to the performance of professionalism in remote work. At the same time, the book's limits have become clearer. Feminist critics have argued that Goffman's performers are curiously ungendered, and that the labour of impression management falls unevenly on women and on marginalised people, for whom a failed performance carries higher costs. The book also says little about power: who gets to define the situation, and who must accept someone else's definition, is a question Goffman leaves mostly unasked.
