# Dreaming in the World's Religions: A Comparative History

A book published in 2008 by New York University Press, Kelly Bulkeley's broad attempt to survey the role of dreams in the major religious traditions in comparative perspective.

## Historical Context

The book grew out of a long research trajectory for Bulkeley, an American scholar of religion and psychology who founded the Sleep and Dream Database (SDDb), a digital archive of dream reports, and who has used such material for quantitative comparisons alongside the qualitative reading of traditional texts.

## Main Ideas and Method

The book surveys the major religious traditions, including Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism and Buddhism, together with indigenous and ancient traditions. It treats dreaming not as a phenomenon explained by a single universal key (as some Freudian or Jungian readings tend to suggest), but as an experience that is shaped culturally and religiously and put to diverse uses: prophetic revelation (as in the dreams of biblical and Qur'anic figures), communication with ancestors or spirits (as in many indigenous traditions), medical diagnosis (as in the incubation practices of ancient Greek healing cults), and spiritual practice (as in Tibetan Buddhist dream yoga).

## Comparative Approach

The book combines the reading of traditional religious sources with attention to recurring patterns across traditions. Later, more specialised studies deepen single traditions beyond what a comprehensive survey allows, such as Iain Edgar's work on dreaming in Islam; Serenity Young's *Dreaming in the Lotus* (1999) is an earlier study of Buddhist dream narrative and practice.

## Impact and Critique

The book helped consolidate the comparative study of dreaming within religious studies, a field in which psychoanalytic frameworks (Freud and Jung) had long been the main reference points. Some critics note that the breadth of the comparison, across many traditions and long periods, necessarily imposes generalisations that a close study of a single case might qualify.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Islamic dream beliefs (*ru'ya*), including Qur'anic references, prophetic hadith about true dreams and the later tradition of dream interpretation associated with the name of Ibn Sirin, belong to the wider comparison, although Edgar's specialised study treats this material in far greater depth.
