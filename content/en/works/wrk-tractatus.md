# Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus

The only book Wittgenstein published in his lifetime. It was written between 1914 and 1918 during his service in the Austro-Hungarian army, completed in 1918, and its final form reached Russell from the Italian prisoner-of-war camp at Cassino in 1919.

## The central thesis

A meaningful proposition is a logical picture (logisches Bild) of reality: it corresponds in its structure to what it depicts, and for this reason it can be true or false. What cannot be pictured in this way, that is, logic itself, ethics, meaning and the metaphysical subject, cannot be said but "shows itself" (zeigt sich). Proposition 7, the shortest in the book: "What we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence." And the propositions of the book itself are described at its end as nonsensical (unsinnig) and as a ladder to be thrown away after one has climbed up it.

## The structure of the work

Seven main propositions numbered from 1 to 7, each branching by decimal numbering that indicates logical weight: 2.1 is a comment on 2, and 2.11 a comment on 2.1. Only proposition 7 carries no branching.

- 1: The world is the totality of facts, not of things.
- 2: What is the case, the fact, is the existence of states of affairs (Sachverhalte), which are combinations of objects (Gegenstände).
- 3: The logical picture of the facts is the thought (Gedanke).
- 4: The thought is the significant proposition (Satz).
- 5: A proposition is a truth-function of elementary propositions.
- 6: The general form of a truth-function: [p̄, ξ̄, N(ξ̄)].
- 7: What cannot be said must be passed over in silence.

## The making of the book in the trenches and notebooks

The text was not born complete; it arose from notebooks in which Wittgenstein wrote from 1914, later known as the "Notebooks 1914-1916". The background was his discussions with Frege in Jena and with Russell in Cambridge since 1911, and then his isolation in Norway, where he built a hut near Skjolden and dictated notes to Moore (later published as *Notes Dictated to G. E. Moore in Norway*). Von Wright relates that the idea of the picture theory occurred to him in 1914 while at the front, when he read of a court case in which miniature models of cars were used to represent an accident, and he saw that a model represents a fact when its elements and structure correspond to the elements of the fact. He completed the manuscript in the summer of 1918 during leave, then was taken prisoner in November, and sent it from the Cassino camp to Russell. In 1919 he wrote to the publisher Ludwig von Ficker that the book has two parts, the part written and the part not written, and that the second is the important one, meaning ethics. The book is dedicated to the memory of his friend David Pinsent.

## The historical context of publication

Several publishers rejected the manuscript, so it appeared first in German under the title *Logisch-Philosophische Abhandlung* in the journal *Annalen der Naturphilosophie*, edited by Wilhelm Ostwald, in 1921, in a text about which Wittgenstein complained that it was printed without his having checked the proofs. In 1922 the bilingual edition (German-English) was published by Kegan Paul in London, in the translation of Charles Kay Ogden and with the Latin title suggested by George Edward Moore, together with an introduction by Bertrand Russell that was a practical condition of the publisher's acceptance. Wittgenstein objected in his correspondence to that introduction and regarded it as a distortion of his intent. The second English translation, by David Pears and Brian McGuinness, appeared in 1961.

## Reception and critique

Frank Ramsey reviewed the book in the journal *Mind* in 1923 and was its first serious technical reader in English. The Vienna Circle read it line by line in its sessions between 1926 and 1927 under the leadership of Moritz Schlick, and Friedrich Waismann recorded the minutes of his meetings with Wittgenstein; and Rudolf Carnap built on the book in his distinction between scientific propositions and metaphysics. But the Circle's reading of it as a verificationist text (the meaning of a proposition = its method of verification) is what Wittgenstein himself rejected, since for him ethics and the transcendent are unsayable, not nonsense to be eliminated. The sharpest critique directed at the book came from its author: the *Philosophical Investigations* (1953) demolishes the picture theory and the idea of simple names and replaces them with language-games, and Wittgenstein states in its preface that the errors of his first book are grave.

## The Arabic reading

The book became known in Arabic through the generation that learned logical positivism, foremost among them Zaki Najib Mahmud, who presented positivist philosophy to the Egyptian reader in *al-Mantiq al-Wad'i* (Positive Logic) (1951) and then in *Khurafat al-Mitafiziqa* (The Myth of Metaphysics) (1953), so that the book came to be read by them as a tool for the critique of metaphysics. An Arabic translation of the *Tractatus* appeared under the title *Risala Mantiqiyya Falsafiyya*, translated by Azmi Islam with an introduction by Zaki Najib Mahmud, from the Anglo-Egyptian Bookshop in Cairo in 1968. Some Arab scholars have compared the conclusion of the book with mystical experience, setting Wittgensteinian silence against the idea of what cannot be said in the experience of taste (*dhawq*), a reading that needs caution because Wittgenstein is not speaking of a knowledge given by taste but of the limit of language.

## Limits

The interpretive dispute between the reading of an "objective logical atomism" (the world in itself is atomic) and a "methodological" reading (it is the analysis that stops at simples) has not been settled, and connected with it is the dispute of the "resolute" reading over how seriously the book's description of its own propositions as nonsensical is to be taken. Other criticisms are that examples of the simple objects on which the analysis rests are not given in the book, so the concept of the "simple object" remains an assumption; that the picture theory does not explain how a thought agrees with a fact that has not yet occurred; and that the ethics which the book says cannot be said is said in the book itself indirectly.
