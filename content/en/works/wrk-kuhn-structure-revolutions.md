# The Structure of Scientific Revolutions

## The Central Thesis

Science does not advance by continuous accumulation or by a single logical refutation, but by the alternation of two phases: **normal science**, which is puzzle-solving within an accepted framework that is not itself tested; then **revolution**, which is the replacement of one framework by another. The driver of the transition is **anomaly**: a stubborn observation that the framework cannot absorb, and when anomalies accumulate **crisis** sets in, and only then are alternatives put forward. The heavier consequence is that two successive frameworks are **incommensurable**: their vocabularies cannot be translated into a neutral language, so there is no external arbiter that decides between them apart from the scientific community.

## The Structure of the Work

Thirteen sections advance in a single cycle: from "A Role for History" and the route to normal science, to the nature of normal science and puzzles; then the discovery of anomaly and crisis and the response to it; then the nature of revolutions and their invisibility in **textbooks**, which rewrite the history of the field after every revolution; then the resolution of revolutions and progress through them. In the **second edition (1970)** a **"Postscript—1969"** was appended, in which Kuhn elaborated what he had condensed: he distinguished the paradigm as **disciplinary matrix** (laws, values and commitments) from the paradigm as **shared exemplar**, stressed the learning of science through solved examples, and softened the formulation of incommensurability in response to his readers.

## Historical Context of Publication

It was published by the **University of Chicago Press in 1962** in the series *International Encyclopedia of Unified Science* — that is, in a project that arose from the ambition of logical positivism to unify the sciences, so the book that shook that ambition was published within it. The book came from Kuhn's work as a **historian of science**, not a philosopher by training: he began as a physicist at Harvard and taught the history of science, so the material was historical and the conclusion philosophical; his earlier historical study *The Copernican Revolution* (1957) already anticipated some of the book's themes. Then came the **second edition of 1970** with the postscript, and the **fiftieth-anniversary edition of 2012** with an introduction by **Ian Hacking**.

## Reception and Critique

The organized confrontation took place at the **Bedford College symposium in London, 1965**, and its proceedings were published, edited by Imre Lakatos and Alan Musgrave, in *Criticism and the Growth of Knowledge* (1970). In it:

- **Karl Popper**: in his essay "Normal Science and Its Dangers", Popper treated Kuhn's normal science as a danger to science, the normal scientist being in his view one who has been badly taught; and the criterion remains exposure to falsification. (Falsifiability is Popper's criterion in *Logik der Forschung*, 1934, not a formulation of Kuhn's.)
- **Margaret Masterman** ("The Nature of a Paradigm"): extracted from the 1962 text about **twenty-one** distinct — some incompatible — uses of the word "paradigm", the best-known linguistic criticism directed at the book, which drove Kuhn to the distinctions of the postscript.
- **Imre Lakatos**: as an alternative to the "conversion of the community" he put forward **scientific research programmes**, to make evaluation between frameworks rational, not sociological.
- **Paul Feyerabend**: agreed with Kuhn on the breakdown of methodological rules in actual history, then went on in *Against Method* (1975) to a conclusion further than Kuhn himself accepted.

Kuhn repeatedly denied that the book is a call for **relativism** or for the claim that scientific change is irrational, which is the interpretation on which the "strong programme" in the sociology of knowledge was later built (David Bloor, *Knowledge and Social Imagery*, 1976).

## The Arabic Translation and Its Reading

The book was rendered into Arabic as "Bunyat al-Thawrat al-Ilmiyya" by Shawqi Jalal, and appeared in the "Alam al-Ma'rifa" series from the National Council for Culture, Arts and Letters in Kuwait in 1992. This translation, with the wide circulation of the series, made the terms "normal science", "scientific revolution" and "paradigm" current in Arabic writing on the philosophy of science and in educational and social discussion. Arab translators differ in rendering the word "paradigm" among "al-namudhaj al-irshadi", "al-mithal" and "al-itar al-marji'i", and this divergence reflects the difficulty of the term itself that Masterman's critique enumerated.

## Puzzles and the Gestalt Switch

Kuhn calls the activity of the scientist in the phase of normal science "puzzle-solving", and likens it to solving crossword puzzles: a puzzle has in principle a guaranteed solution, the rules are known in advance, and failure is attributed to the scientist, not to the framework. This explains the scientific community's continued defense of its framework despite anomalies, so the framework is rejected only when a rival candidate exists that promises to solve the stubborn puzzles. When the transition occurs Kuhn describes it as a gestalt switch, like one who moves from seeing the picture as a duck to seeing it as a rabbit, and he cites examples from Lavoisier's chemistry, astronomy between Ptolemy and Copernicus, and the physics of Newton and Einstein, and says that scientists on the two sides of a revolution "live in different worlds". The phrase carries its heaviest epistemological significance: what counts as an observation changes with the framework, so observation is theory-laden.

## Limits and Critique of the Thesis

- Overgeneralization from examples in physics and astronomy, and a question about whether it applies to sciences in which competing frameworks always coexist, such as psychology and the social sciences; Kuhn himself acknowledged that these sciences may be in a "pre-paradigm" phase.
- Vagueness of the criterion of progress: if there is no neutral language between the two frameworks, in what sense is the later better than the earlier? Kuhn answered in the book's conclusion with the new framework's capacity to solve more puzzles and its higher predictive precision, but he rejected the idea of progress toward "truth".
- Actual history is more gradual than the sharp revolutions the book depicts, as historians of science after him pointed out.
- Confusion between description and prescription: does the book describe the practice of scientists or lay down what it ought to be? This is a confusion that fed the relativist readings that Kuhn rejected.

## Influence in the Human Sciences

The book's vocabulary leaked beyond the philosophy of science into psychology, education, economics and management, until "paradigm shift" became a common phrase, often in a loose sense that does not match Kuhn's intention. In the history of psychology Kuhn's scheme is used to read the succession of behaviorism and cognitivism, and to show that neighboring schools may speak in vocabularies not translatable into one another, and that the change of diagnostic classification frameworks cannot be reduced to an accumulation of new discoveries.
