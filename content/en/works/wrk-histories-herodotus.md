# Histories (Historíai)

**The greatest work of ancient historiography** and the first large prose composition in Greek history, written by **Herodotus** of Halicarnassus (c. 484–425 BCE), in roughly 440–430 BCE. It is an encyclopedia of **inquiry** (*historíē*, the root of the word "history" itself, meaning "investigation" or "research") and narration, covering the **Greco-Persian Wars** and their broad civilizational, geographical and ethnic background.

## Structure and Scope

The work is traditionally divided (by a later Alexandrian division that does not go back to Herodotus himself) into nine books, each named after one of the Muses. The first four books cover the culture, history and structure of the Persian Empire and the peoples it subjugated (Egypt, Babylon, Lydia, the Scythians), while the last five books focus on the events of the Persian Wars themselves (490–479 BCE), from the battle of Marathon to the battles of Salamis and Plataea. Herodotus organizes his material through **logoi** (*logoi*): extended narrative digressions (such as the "Egyptian logos" or the "Scythian logos") that intertwine with the main narrative line through genealogical, geographical or thematic connections.

## Method: Inquiry and Narration Together

Herodotus combines two functions: recording what he **witnessed himself** or what was reported to him by reliable witnesses, and presenting **multiple, sometimes conflicting accounts** of the same event without always deciding which is correct, leaving the final judgment to the reader. This feature set him apart from later, more decisive historians (such as Thucydides). He also mixes a **rational** explanation of events with reference to the **divine/fateful** factor (such as the idea of human arrogance, *hubris*, which calls down divine punishment). This blend distinguishes his style from the later purely pragmatic history.

## Importance: The Birth of History as a Field of Knowledge

Herodotus established the concept of **"history as organized inquiry,"** which distinguishes the documented event from the poetic myth of Homer, giving historiography an intellectual standing independent of the epic poetry that preceded it (the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* of Homer). This led Cicero to later give him the title "father of history" (*pater historiae*), a title still in use today despite the criticism it has drawn from more rigorously precise contemporaries (such as Thucydides, who implicitly criticized his method).

## Critical Reception

From antiquity Herodotus was accused of "loving stories" (as Plutarch described him in his polemical essay "On the Malice of Herodotus") and of exaggerating marvelous accounts. But modern archaeological and historical scholarship has shown the accuracy of many of his geographical and ethnographic observations about Egypt, Persia and the Scythians, and has thereby restored academic recognition to his method.

## Impact

The work is the foundation stone of all later Western historiography. It influenced the development of ethnography and the exploration of cultural diversity as a legitimate historical subject, and, as the first long surviving prose narrative of classical Greek literature, it has also had a literary impact.
