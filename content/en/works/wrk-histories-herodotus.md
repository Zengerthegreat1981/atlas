# Histories (Herodotus)

## Definition

The ***Histories*** (Ancient Greek: Ἱστορίαι, *Historíai*, meaning "Inquiries" or "Researches") by **Herodotus of Halicarnassus** (c. 484–425 BCE) is widely regarded as the foundational work of narrative history and descriptive ethnography in Western literature. Written in the Ionic dialect during the middle-to-late fifth century BCE, the work chronicles the epic clash between the Greek city-states and the Persian Achaemenid Empire (the Greco-Persian Wars of 499–479 BCE), set against a vast panorama of the known ancient world.

## Structure, Composition, and Scope

Although composed as a unified prose inquiry intended for oral recitation (*epideixis*), the work was divided by later Hellenistic grammarians of the Library of Alexandria into nine books, each named after one of the nine Muses. Internal textual references (such as mentions of early events in the Peloponnesian War down to 430 BCE in Books VII and IX) indicate that Herodotus composed and refined the text between the 440s and 420s BCE, incorporating material gathered during extensive travels through Egypt, the Levant, Mesopotamia, the Black Sea region, and Magna Graecia (Thurii).

The architecture of the *Histories* balances overarching geopolitical conflict with expansive regional monographs (*logoi*):
- **Books I–IV**: Focus on the rise and territorial expansion of the Persian Empire under Cyrus the Great, Cambyses, and Darius I. Herodotus weaves in extensive geographical and ethnographic explorations of Lydia, Babylonia, Egypt (the famous "Egyptian logos" of Book II), and the nomadic Scythians (Book IV).
- **Books V–IX**: Trace the escalation of hostilities, beginning with the Ionian Revolt (499 BCE) and Darius's first invasion of Greece culminating at the Battle of Marathon (490 BCE), followed by Xerxes' massive expedition, the heroic stand at Thermopylae, the naval victory at Salamis (480 BCE), and the decisive Persian defeats at Plataea and Mycale (479 BCE).

## Historiographical Method: Autopsy and Multiple Testimonies

Scholars commonly describe three elements of Herodotus's approach to evidence:
1. **Autopsy (*Opsis*)**: Firsthand observation of geographical monuments, temple inscriptions, and local customs.
2. **Hearsay and Inquiry (*Akoē / Historíē*)**: Gathering oral reports from priests, local guides, combatants, and civic elites across multiple cultures.
3. **Reasoned Judgment (*Gnōmē*)**: Evaluating conflicting testimonies. Herodotus states a methodological principle in Book VII (7.152): he is obliged to report what is said, but not obliged to believe all of it.

While Herodotus retained traditional Greek conceptions of divine justice—believing that excessive human pride (*hubris*) inevitably provokes divine retribution (*nemesis*) and the mutability of human fortune—he also investigated material, institutional, and environmental causation.

## Reception, Critique, and Modern Appraisal

Herodotus's dual commitment to recording marvelous tales alongside factual reports earned him both admiration and suspicion in antiquity. While Cicero famously conferred upon him the title "father of history" (*pater historiae* in *De Legibus* 1.5), ancient critics such as Thucydides implicitly censured his narrative style, and Plutarch authored a scathing polemic titled *On the Malice of Herodotus* (*De malignitate Herodoti*), accusing him of pro-barbarian bias.

In modern scholarship, twentieth- and twenty-first-century archaeology, epigraphy, and anthropological fieldwork have confirmed some of Herodotus's reports and qualified others; for example, finds in Scythian burial kurgans have been compared with his account of the Scythians. The *Histories* remains a major primary source and a sophisticated meditation on cultural difference.
