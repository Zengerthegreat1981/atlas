# Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong

The Australian-born philosopher John Leslie Mackie published his book *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* in 1977 with Penguin Books. It is one of the most influential texts in analytic metaethics of the twentieth century, as it offered an influential and influentially clear formulation of a position later known as "error theory."

## Central Theses

Mackie opens his book with a striking sentence: "There are no objective values." He stresses that this is not a first-order ethical claim but a metaethical position about the nature of ethics itself. His central thesis rests on two parts. The first is linguistic-semantic: ordinary moral discourse implicitly presupposes the existence of objective moral properties in actions themselves, so that when a speaker says "killing is wrong," he means that this wrongness is a real feature of the act, not merely an expression of his approval or disapproval. The second is metaphysical-epistemic: these presupposed objective properties do not in fact exist in the fabric of the universe, and therefore all ordinary moral judgments that presuppose their existence are systematically false, not merely indeterminate. Hence the name "error theory": ordinary morality is, according to Mackie, built on a collective illusion.

## The Arguments from Relativity and Queerness

Mackie supports his thesis with two main arguments. The first is the "argument from relativity," which points out that the enormous diversity of moral codes across cultures and eras is better explained as a reflection of different forms of social life than as differing degrees of accuracy in perceiving a single objective moral truth. The second, and the most famous, is the "argument from queerness," which holds that if objective moral properties existed, they would be entities utterly strange compared with anything else we know in the universe. They would be intrinsically prescriptive (so that perceiving them would automatically entail acting), and this resembles no other natural property. Our perception of them would also require a special cognitive faculty, a mysterious "moral intuition" with no counterpart among our ordinary means of knowing the world.

## Historical Context

The book belongs to the tradition of moral non-realism found among the logical positivists and emotivists, such as A. J. Ayer, who regarded moral judgments as mere emotional venting with no cognitive content, and Charles Stevenson. Mackie differs from them in that he does not deny that moral judgments have propositional content (that is, that they claim to be true or false). He accepts that they are of this linguistic form, but argues that they are all false because their objective referent is absent from reality. He thus combines a cognitivist semantics (moral claims purport to state facts) with a denial that such facts exist.

## Influence and Reception

The book had a wide impact and became a standard reference in university courses on metaethics. It provoked major critical responses, including from Ronald Dworkin ("Objectivity and Truth: You'd Better Believe It," 1996) and Derek Parfit (*On What Matters*, 2011), who argued that the argument from queerness presupposes a narrow scientific model of what exists and so begs the question against non-natural normative truths. David Enoch and others have pressed "companions in guilt" arguments, holding that similar worries would also threaten epistemic or mathematical truths. On another front, the book stimulated expressivist projects such as Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism, which seek to preserve the practice of moral thought without Mackie's error theory. Mackie draws an analogy with secondary qualities such as colour: we speak as if wrongness were a feature of the act itself, but, on his view, nothing in the fabric of the world corresponds to it; what exists are our attitudes and social conventions.

## Structure of the Book: First-Order and Second-Order Ethics

Mackie carefully distinguishes between what he calls "first-order ethics," the content of ordinary moral rules such as the prohibition of lying or the duty of justice, and "second-order ethics," or metaethics, which investigates the nature and grounding of these judgments. He affirms that his denial of objectivity falls strictly at the second level, and that it does not necessarily entail the collapse of practical ethics or the equality of all choices. He devotes later chapters of the book to building an alternative "practical ethics" based on the idea that morality is a device for counteracting limited human sympathies and securing mutually advantageous cooperation, a line of thought with roots in Hobbes and Hume. In this way morality becomes a kind of practical "invention" necessary for organizing human cooperation, not a reflection of a prior truth. This explains the book's subtitle: in Mackie's view, right and wrong are not discovered but invented, that is, the product of a practical human construction whose aim is to facilitate shared life, in the way conventions are made to organize shared life.
