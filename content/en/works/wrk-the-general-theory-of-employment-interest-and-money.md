# The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money

**John Maynard Keynes** published *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* with Macmillan in London in February 1936 (a Harcourt, Brace edition followed in the United States). It is widely treated as a founding text of modern macroeconomics and as a sustained challenge to the classical assumption that market economies tend, of themselves, toward full employment.

## Context of composition: the Great Depression and the failure of existing remedies

Keynes wrote the book in the midst of the Great Depression, when Western economies experienced enormous unemployment rates that persisted for years without correcting themselves automatically, as classical theory had predicted. Keynes sought to offer an alternative theoretical explanation of why the economy could settle at a long-lasting "equilibrium without full employment," rather than a temporary imbalance corrected by market forces on their own through the flexibility of wages and prices, as the classical economists had assumed.

## Critique of Say's law and the idea of effective demand

The book opens with an explicit critique of the classical "Say's law of markets," the assumption that supply creates its own demand, so that a general and persistent surplus of production cannot occur. In its place Keynes puts the concept of **effective demand**: the actual level of total spending (consumption, investment and government expenditure) determines the level of production and employment in the economy, not the reverse. When this demand falls, there is no automatic mechanism guaranteeing its quick return to the level of full employment.

## The marginal propensity to consume and the multiplier

The book develops the concept of the "marginal propensity to consume" (the proportion of each additional income that individuals spend), and builds upon it the idea of the **multiplier** (introduced by his student Richard Kahn in 1931): any increase in spending (especially government spending) generates a chain of successive rounds of spending and income, raising total income by an amount that exceeds the original expenditure itself. This idea later became the basis of the argument for fiscal intervention by the government to combat depression.

## Liquidity preference: a new theory of interest

The book presents an alternative theory for determining the interest rate, called **liquidity preference**. The interest rate is not the price of waiting or of forgoing present consumption, as the classical economists held, but the price of giving up cash liquidity, determined by the supply of money against the desire of individuals to hold money in liquid form. This opens room for cases in which lowering the interest rate fails to stimulate sufficient investment when deep pessimism prevails. Keynes pointed to "animal spirits" as a key driver of investment decisions in conditions of fundamental uncertainty about the future that cannot be calculated probabilistically.

## The policy conclusion: a theoretical justification for government intervention

The book concludes that the government can, and sometimes should, intervene through public spending or tax cuts to raise total demand when the private sector fails to do so, even if this leads to a temporary budget deficit. This argument became a standard reference for Western economic policymakers in the decades after the Second World War, although Keynes's ideas were not the origin of earlier policies such as the American New Deal.

## Style of writing and difficulty of reception

The book became known for the difficulty of its style and the obscurity of some of its formulations. In the preface Keynes describes the book as for him "a long struggle of escape" from habitual modes of thought. This difficulty opened the door to competing readings of "what Keynes actually meant," from the simplified IS-LM interpretation offered by John Hicks (1937) and the neoclassical synthesis, to the reading developed by post-Keynesian economists around the centrality of fundamental uncertainty. This entry presents the content broadly agreed upon without resolving that dispute.

## Enduring impact on the entire field

Historians of economic thought treat the book as one of the most influential works in twentieth-century economics. It gave macroeconomics a distinctive agenda: the determination of aggregate output and employment. Keynes led the British delegation at the Bretton Woods conference of 1944; the IMF and World Bank that emerged were a compromise between his proposals and the American plan associated with Harry Dexter White, so the institutions are not a straightforward application of the *General Theory*. The Keynesian consensus in policy later faced serious challenges from monetarism and the new classical school, especially from the 1970s.

## Divided reception on publication

On publication the book received a divided academic reception. Younger economists associated with Cambridge, including Richard Kahn and Joan Robinson, took it up quickly, while several established economists doubted how new or how secure its propositions were. A. C. Pigou, whose *Theory of Unemployment* (1933) Keynes had criticised, later engaged the argument at length. Early reviews already disagreed about whether the break with "classical" theory was as sharp as the preface claimed.

## New terms that entered economic language

The book introduced several terms that entered the permanent economic vocabulary and went beyond Keynesian theory itself, among them "animal spirits," "liquidity preference," "marginal propensity to consume" and "involuntary unemployment." (The term "liquidity trap" is later, although the idea is discussed in the book.) These phrases are still used in journalistic and academic economic analysis, whatever one's view of the broader Keynesian framework.
