# Zollikon Seminars: Protocols — Conversations — Letters

The *Zollikon Seminars* are a rare occasion in the history of the existential school when a major philosopher sat down with physicians over many years to explain what he meant. They are not a book Heidegger wrote. They are seminars he gave between 1959 and 1969 to psychiatrists, at the house of the Swiss psychiatrist Medard Boss in Zollikon, near Zurich, edited by Boss and published in German in 1987 — eleven years after Heidegger's death in 1976 — with the protocols of the sessions, private conversations, and correspondence between the two men added. The English translation by Franz Mayr and Richard Askay appeared in 2001. The volume is the primary source for the philosophy behind existential analysis, and the document appealed to in the dispute between Boss and Ludwig Binswanger over who had understood Heidegger correctly.

## How the Seminars Came About

The relationship began with a letter from Boss to Heidegger in 1947. Boss, who had been looking for a philosophical foundation for psychiatry, invited Heidegger to Switzerland, and the seminars began in 1959. The audience was made up of psychiatrists and medical students working at the Burghölzli University Clinic in Zurich, and the sessions were held at irregular intervals at Boss's house in Zollikon over almost ten years. Heidegger moved from the concepts of *Being and Time* (1927) to the questions of medicine, asking the physicians to say precisely what they meant by the body, time, and space, and answering their direct questions. The name "protocols" refers to the minutes of the sessions that Boss edited into the book.

## The Central Thesis

Heidegger's thesis in the seminars is that psychiatry works with an implicit ontology it never examines: it treats the human being as a thing present among things, governed by causality as bodies in nature are governed. This, for Heidegger, is the inheritance of the Cartesian and Galilean projection onto the human being. The alternative is not a rival explanation but a displacement of the question. Instead of asking what causes a symptom, one should ask what makes it possible for a human being to be ill at all. The answer is that the human being is *Dasein* — a being open to a world, not a closed substance whose states can be measured. Illness is then understood as a transformation of that openness, of temporality and of being-in-the-world, rather than as a defect in an inner content. The seminars are therefore a critique of the natural-scientific model in medicine from the inside, delivered to the practitioners who live by that model.

## What the Seminars Offer the Practitioner

Four themes in the seminars matter most for clinical work. The first is the critique of causality: the difference between a *cause* (*Ursache*), which explains the movement of bodies, and a *motive* (*Motiv*), by which human conduct is understood — and the confusion of the two is the error of what Heidegger calls "natural-scientific psychology". The second is the body: the distinction between *Körper*, the extended body the physician measures, and *Leib*, the lived body, understood as *Leiben* — "bodying" as an activity rather than a thing. The limits of my living body are not the limits of my skin but the reach of my openness to the world; a person pointing with a finger extends their body to the thing pointed at. The third is temporality in illness: symptoms such as depression and anxiety are read as changes in the structure of temporality and openness, not as disturbances of an inner mechanism. The fourth is space and place: to be "here" is not a matter of coordinates but of dwelling in a world. Heidegger also treats mood (*Stimmung*) as a primary openness to the world rather than an inner state, and discusses grief and anxiety in that sense.

## Boss, Binswanger, and the Dispute

Boss built on the seminars in his own formulation of *Daseinsanalyse*, presented in *Grundriss der Medizin und Psychologie* (1971), translated into English as *Existential Foundations of Medicine and Psychology* (1979). The seminars are the document appealed to in his dispute with Binswanger, who had been the first to carry *Being and Time* into psychiatry. Heidegger objected that Binswanger read *Dasein* as an anthropological theory of the subject, adding care (*Sorge*) and then love and the I–Thou relation as if they belonged to the analysis. Heidegger regarded Binswanger's use of his work as a misunderstanding of it. The seminars are thus not only a philosophical text but a piece of evidence in the genealogy of existential therapy: they show where Boss's version of the tradition comes from, and why it differs from Binswanger's.

## Reception and Limits

The text is late and limited in its effect compared with Heidegger's major works. It was published after the therapeutic systems had already settled, and its influence has remained within the circle of existential analysis and medical phenomenology — widening in English only after the 2001 translation. The two most common criticisms are that it diagnoses an ontological problem where a clinical protocol is needed, giving the practitioner no procedure to follow, and that its argument does not distinguish adequately between illnesses with a documented neurological basis and disturbed modes of being. Its importance stands: it is the clearest place where a major philosopher addresses psychiatrists directly, corrects them, and says what he considers a misunderstanding of his own book — and it remains the source for the concept of the lived body in medical phenomenology.
