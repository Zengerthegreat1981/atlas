# Global Cities

*The Global City: New York, London, Tokyo* (1991), by the Dutch-American sociologist Saskia Sassen, is a foundational work in the study of global cities. Published by Princeton University Press, the book argues that globalisation, far from making place irrelevant, has made certain cities more important than ever. New York, London, and Tokyo, Sassen shows, have become the command points of the world economy: the places where global finance, specialised services, and corporate control are concentrated. The book popularised the term "global city" and reshaped urban sociology, geography, and the study of globalisation.

## Historical Context and Publication

Sassen wrote the book in the late 1980s, a decade of financial deregulation, the growth of multinational corporations, and the rapid expansion of international capital markets. Born in The Hague in 1947 and raised in Argentina, Sassen later taught at Columbia University; her earlier book, *The Mobility of Labor and Capital* (1988), had already examined the links between foreign investment and migration. *The Global City* appeared as the old industrial order was giving way to a service-based, globally integrated economy, and it offered a systematic sociological account of that transformation at the level of the city. An updated second edition appeared in 2001.

## Thesis and Core Argument

Sassen's central claim is that the dispersal of economic activity across the globe has produced a new kind of centralisation. As production spreads to many countries, the work of controlling and servicing that dispersed production — finance, law, accounting, advertising, management consulting — concentrates in a small number of cities. Sassen identifies four functions of the global city: it is a highly concentrated command point in the organisation of the world economy; a key location for finance and for specialised service firms; a site of production and innovation in those leading industries; and a market for the products and innovations produced. The result is a paradox: the more global the economy becomes, the more it depends on specific places with dense networks of firms, skilled workers, and institutions. Global cities are therefore not simply large cities; they are strategic sites where global processes take concrete, material form.

## Structure and Method

The book first examines the geography and composition of globalisation, tracing how New York, London, and Tokyo restructured from manufacturing centres into centres of finance and producer services during the 1970s and 1980s. It then analyses the economic order of the global city, including the producer services complex and the transformation of financial markets, and the new urban social order, including labour markets and informalisation. Sassen's method combines economic data on employment, investment, and office markets with detailed case studies of the three cities. The comparative design is central to the argument: by showing that three very different cities underwent massive and parallel changes, she argues that a common global process, rather than local circumstance alone, was at work.

## Key Concepts

The book's central concept, the "global city," has become one of the most widely used terms in urban studies. "Producer services" — the specialised business services that serve global firms — explain why corporate headquarters and financial institutions cluster together despite the falling cost of communication. Sassen's analysis of "polarisation" shows that the global city produces both high-paid professional jobs and a growing mass of low-paid service work, widening inequality within the city. Her account of the "informal economy" reveals how immigrant labour, sweatshops, and casual work sustain the gleaming towers of finance.

## Reception and Influence

*The Global City* was widely acclaimed and became a classic across sociology, geography, economics, and urban planning. It founded a large research literature on world and global cities, including the Globalization and World Cities (GaWC) research network, and it influenced how policymakers think about urban competitiveness, financial regulation, and immigration. Sassen's later work extended the analysis to global migration, the denationalisation of territory, and the expulsions produced by advanced capitalism in *Expulsions* (2014).

## Critical Assessments

Critics have questioned the book on several fronts. Jennifer Robinson argued in 2002 that the global city framework privileges a small set of Northern financial centres and treats other cities as off the map, urging attention to "ordinary cities" and to the diverse ways cities engage with global processes. John Friedmann's earlier "world city hypothesis" (1986) offered a related but distinct account, and debates continue over how many cities count as global and by what criteria. Others have noted that Sassen's three cases are all in the Global North, and that the framework fits less well the megacities of the Global South, where globalisation takes different forms. Some critics also argue that the book's focus on finance and producer services underplays the role of the state and of geopolitics. Sassen has responded by extending the framework to a wider range of cities and by stressing that global cities form networks rather than a fixed hierarchy.

## Continuing Relevance

The global city remains an essential concept for understanding the contemporary world. The rise of financial centres such as Shanghai, Singapore, and Dubai, the concentration of technology firms in a few metropolitan regions, and the role of cities in climate policy and in the governance of migration all confirm Sassen's insight that globalisation is produced in places. The book also remains relevant for its analysis of inequality: the polarised labour markets of global cities, where bankers and cleaners inhabit the same streets but different worlds, anticipate the debates about inequality and urban life that dominate sociology today.
