# Religion and Film: Cinema and the Re-Creation of the World

S. Brent Plate's book (Wallflower Press, 2008), a contribution to the contemporary academic field of "religion and film."

## Definition

Plate argues that film and religion are not two separate subjects that meet occasionally when a religious symbol appears on screen. Both, he proposes, are practices of world-making: they organize sensory experience into a coherent, meaningful "world" through the tools of myth, ritual and the symbolic image.

## Historical Context

The book draws on an earlier heritage in the history of religions, especially Mircea Eliade's work on myth and ritual as ways of symbolically creating and renewing the world. Plate applies this framework to cinema as a modern medium that performs related functions in contemporary culture. The book came in the context of the growth of "religion and film" as an academic field since the 1990s, which moved beyond the limited interest in explicitly "religious films" (such as films about the life of Christ) toward broader analysis of how cinema works as a culturally powerful, quasi-religious practice.

## Main Ideas and Method

Plate draws parallels between the cinematic experience and religious practice: the darkened auditorium resembles a ritual space set apart from everyday life, the screen resembles a sacred image, and collective viewing resembles a communal gathering. His analysis is not limited to films with explicit religious subjects; it extends to films with no apparent religious theme, asking how their narrative and visual structures perform world-making functions.

## Plate and the Field

S. Brent Plate is an American scholar of religious studies and a prominent figure in the academic study of religion and film and of religion, image and the senses. His work is often discussed alongside John Lyden's *Film as Religion* (2003), which also argues that film can function in ways analogous to religion.

## Impact and Critique

The book has been widely used in teaching and in later analyses of particular films. Some researchers question whether the broad analogy between cinema and religion explains how a given film works, or imposes a ready-made template on diverse material.

## Film Examples

Plate's examples cross the usual line between popular and experimental cinema. His discussion of how films open and establish a world includes George Lucas's *Star Wars* and Ron Fricke's non-narrative film *Baraka*. He also turns to Stan Brakhage's avant-garde film *The Act of Seeing with One's Own Eyes* (1971), which shows autopsies at close range, to argue that cinema can reawaken viewers' senses and confront them with death, which mainstream film and culture tend to push to the margins. The much expanded second edition (Columbia University Press, 2017) draws on a wider body of work, from early cinema to Hollywood, independent and international films. Accounts of that edition mention David Lynch's *Blue Velvet*, Giuseppe Tornatore's *Cinema Paradiso* and Dziga Vertov's *Man with a Movie Camera* in connection with the organisation of space, and the participatory screenings of *The Rocky Horror Picture Show* as an example of films generating rituals outside the cinema's story. Readers should check which edition they are using, since examples added in 2017 do not necessarily appear in the 2008 text.
