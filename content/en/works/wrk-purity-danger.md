# Purity and Danger

*Purity and Danger: An Analysis of Concepts of Pollution and Taboo* (1966) is Mary Douglas's comparative study of ideas about dirt, contagion, and forbidden contact. Its central proposal is that pollution rules make sense in relation to a system of classification. What a community treats as unclean can show where it draws boundaries and what it regards as disorder.

## Dirt as disorder

Douglas rejects a sharp contrast between "primitive" ritual avoidance and modern hygiene. Medical knowledge of germs changed ideas about cleanliness, but everyday judgments of dirt still depend on place and category. Drawing on an older phrase, she describes dirt as matter out of place: shoes are not dirty in themselves, yet it is dirty to put them on the dining table. Dirt is therefore a by-product of ordering. Cleaning and ritual separation are positive efforts to organise experience.

The book argues against accounts, associated in different ways with James Frazer and others, that divided magic from religion and treated primitive rules as confused or merely fearful. It also rejects explanations that reduce food or purity rules entirely to hygiene.

## Leviticus and anomaly

The chapter on the abominations of Leviticus is the most discussed. Douglas proposes that the dietary rules express an idea of wholeness: creatures that fit the proper pattern for their element are acceptable, while those that cross or incompletely meet categories are excluded. Land animals that chew the cud and have cloven hooves fit the pastoral model; the pig meets only one criterion. She later revised this reading, notably in the preface to a new edition and in *Leviticus as Literature* (1999). Biblical scholars, among them Jacob Milgrom, offered other explanations centred on limiting the taking of life.

Anomalous things need not be treated as polluting. Cultures may avoid them, destroy them, redefine them, or give them ritual power, as in the Lele cult of the pangolin that Douglas had studied in the Belgian Congo.

## Boundaries, bodies, and society

Pollution beliefs can support moral rules and warn against trespass between groups. Douglas treats the body as a natural source of symbols for a bounded system, so concern about its openings and substances may correspond to concern about social margins. Danger attaches to transitional states and ambiguous positions. Some beliefs attribute involuntary harmful power to people in unclear roles, while those who exercise recognised authority wield controlled power. Where principles within a social system conflict, for instance in relations between women and men, pollution ideas may become especially elaborate.

## Reception

The book moved the study of taboo toward classification and influenced anthropology, religious studies, history, sociology, and work on risk. Douglas extended the approach in *Natural Symbols* (1970) and, with Aaron Wildavsky, in *Risk and Culture* (1982). Critics argue that correspondences between body and society can be asserted too readily, that anomalies do not predict a single response, and that attention to order may understate disagreement, historical change, and bodily disgust.
