# Crime and Custom in Savage Society

Bronisław Malinowski's *Crime and Custom in Savage Society* (1926) is a foundational work of legal anthropology, based on his fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands between 1915 and 1918.

## Thesis

Malinowski challenges the then-prevailing image of "primitive" people as slaves to custom, obeying their traditions automatically, spontaneously, and without any need for sanctions. He argues instead that Trobrianders follow their social rules for a mixture of reasons: self-interest, social pressure, and above all the web of reciprocal obligations that binds individuals together in economic and social life, such as the exchanges between partners in gardening, fishing, and the *kula*. Obedience is not mechanical; rules are bent, evaded, and broken, and the sanctions against violation work through the threat of losing the reciprocal support on which people depend.

## Key Examples

One famous case is that of a young Trobriand man who killed himself after being publicly accused of a relationship with a woman of his own clan (his maternal cousin, a clan "sister"), which is forbidden as incest in this matrilineal society. Malinowski uses the case to show how the gap between the rule and actual behaviour is managed: such relationships were not uncommon so long as they remained undiscussed, and it was the public accusation that forced the young man to punish himself. The case shows that social control worked through public opinion and shame as much as through formal sanctions.

## Historical Context

Malinowski wrote the work while teaching at the London School of Economics. It was partly a response to writers such as E. S. Hartland, whose *Primitive Law* (1924) portrayed "primitive" societies as governed by custom followed automatically and by the weight of the group, with no real notion of individual rights and obligations.

## Method

Malinowski adopted a method based on specific cases that he observed directly during his fieldwork, in contrast to abstract generalisation from library sources that was common in earlier evolutionary anthropology. The book applies the participant-observation approach of *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* to a specialised field, the study of law and social order. It also argues for the functional analysis of institutions: law and custom are to be understood through what they do in the life of a community.

## Impact

The book is a foundational text in legal anthropology. It helped establish that "law," understood as social mechanisms for regulating behaviour and resolving disputes, exists in all human societies, even though its forms differ from the formal Western model based on centralised, coercive institutions. This distinction between law as a social function and law as a specific institutional form shaped legal anthropology through the twentieth century, including the comparative work of E. Adamson Hoebel and the studies of judicial processes by Max Gluckman. Malinowski's definition of law has also been criticised, notably by later scholars who argued that he treated almost all binding social obligations as law, thereby blurring the distinction between law and custom.
