# Strangers in Their Own Land

Arlie Russell Hochschild's *Strangers in Their Own Land: Anger and Mourning on the American Right* (2016) is an ethnographic study of Tea Party supporters in Louisiana, researched and published before Donald Trump's election to the presidency. Over five years, Hochschild travelled to Lake Charles and the surrounding parishes, attending rallies, church services and kitchen-table conversations, trying to understand what she calls the "Great Paradox": why do some of the poorest and most polluted communities in the United States vote for politicians who oppose environmental regulation and favour cuts to the very programmes they depend on? The book was a finalist for the National Book Award in 2016, and it became one of the most widely discussed sociological studies of the American right.

## Historical Context and Publication

Hochschild, a professor emerita at the University of California, Berkeley, and the author of *The Managed Heart* (1983), began the research after noticing that she and her liberal colleagues could not explain the appeal of the Tea Party. Louisiana was a deliberate choice: the state ranks near the bottom of the United States in income and health, and near the top in industrial pollution, yet it votes reliably Republican. The book was published in September 2016, two months before the presidential election, and its account of the emotional life of the right was widely read after Trump's victory as helping to explain it. Hochschild returned to the subject in *Stolen Pride: Loss, Shame, and the Rise of the Right* (2024), which extended the analysis to Appalachia.

## The Great Paradox and the Deep Story

The book's central puzzle is the "Great Paradox": the people most harmed by pollution and most dependent on government support are the most hostile to environmental regulation and to government itself. Hochschild's answer is not that these voters are irrational or misinformed, but that they understand their situation through a different story — what she calls a "deep story," a narrative that feels true even when it is not factually accurate. In the deep story of her Louisiana informants, hard-working Americans wait patiently in a long line for the American Dream, only to see others — immigrants, minorities, women, the beneficiaries of affirmative action — cut ahead of them with the help of a government that has stopped noticing people like them. The anger that follows is not about economics alone; it is about recognition, respect and the feeling of being a stranger in one's own land.

## Empathy Walls and Feeling Rules

Hochschild's method is an extension of the sociology of emotion she pioneered in *The Managed Heart*. She argues that political disagreement is sustained by "empathy walls": obstacles that prevent people on each side from understanding how the other side feels. To climb over the wall, the sociologist must take the other side's feelings seriously, bracketing judgement long enough to hear the deep story. The book is therefore also a methodological statement: it shows how ethnography, sustained over years, can produce a kind of understanding that surveys cannot. Hochschild links the deep story to her earlier concept of "feeling rules" (developed in her 1979 article on emotion work and in *The Managed Heart*) — the social norms that tell people what they ought to feel — and shows how the feeling rules of the Tea Party right differ from those of the liberal left: pride in endurance, loyalty to place and contempt for dependency.

## Structure and Method

The book is organised around Hochschild's fieldwork in south-west Louisiana, moving from her arrival and first contacts, through the lives of the people she came to know, to the analysis of the deep story and its political consequences. The evidence is ethnographic: long interviews, participant observation at rallies and in homes, and the author's own reflections on her position as a Berkeley liberal in a deeply conservative community. The narrative is deliberately personal, with Hochschild describing her own mistakes and discomforts, and the book ends with a call for both sides to climb the empathy wall — a conclusion that some reviewers found too even-handed and others found admirably generous.

## Reception and Influence

The book was praised as a model of empathetic ethnography and became a key text in the sociology of politics, emotion and the American right. It shaped the post-2016 literature on populism, contributing the concepts of the deep story and the empathy wall to the vocabulary of political sociology. It has been widely assigned in courses on qualitative methods, as an example of how to study people whose politics the researcher does not share. Its influence extends beyond sociology: journalists and political scientists have adopted the deep story as a framework for understanding populist movements in other countries.

## Critical Assessments and Limitations

Critics have argued that the book is too sympathetic to its subjects, and that by bracketing judgement it understates the role of racism in the politics it describes. Others note that the deep story, while evocative, is difficult to test: it is a reconstruction of feelings rather than a falsifiable claim. Some scholars of the American right argue that Hochschild's focus on emotion neglects the role of organised interests — the fossil fuel industry, conservative media and political donors — in shaping the views of her informants. In defence of the approach, understanding how people feel can be treated as a precondition for political response, whether persuasion, opposition or reform.
