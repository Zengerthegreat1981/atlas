# Course in General Linguistics

A central text of modern structural linguistics, published by Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye in 1916, three years after the death of Ferdinand de Saussure (1913), from notes of his three lecture courses in Geneva between 1906 and 1911.

## Content

The book sets out the basic distinctions on which structural linguistics was built: the arbitrariness of the linguistic sign; the distinction between language (the abstract social system) and speech (individual performance); the distinction between synchronic study (the state of a language at a given moment) and diachronic study (its historical development); and the thesis that the value of a linguistic element is systemic and differential rather than intrinsic.

## The Problem of Authorship

Unlike most other foundational works, Saussure did not write this text himself nor review it. It is an editorial reconstruction from his students' notes after his death, a point that scholars raise when analyzing how accurately it represents his actual ideas. Charles Bally and Albert Sechehaye, neither of whom attended the lectures themselves, relied on the notes of students who did (most notably Albert Riedlinger), together with some of Saussure's own manuscript notes, and merged these scattered notes from different courses into a single text consistent in style. This later raised wide academic debate about how faithfully this merger represents the actual development of Saussure's ideas across the three successive courses. The critical edition by Rudolf Engler (1967–68, with a further volume in 1974) set the students' notes side by side with the published text. Manuscripts by Saussure himself, published as *Écrits de linguistique générale* (edited by Simon Bouquet and Rudolf Engler, 2002), added further material to that debate.

## Central Concepts in Detail

The theoretical system of the book rests on the principle that language is a system of signs, that each linguistic sign consists of a "signifier" (the acoustic or written image) and a "signified" (the mental concept), and that the relation between them is purely arbitrary rather than natural (there is no necessary link between the sequence of sounds "t-r-e-e" and the concept of a tree). The book also distinguishes between syntagmatic relations, which link the elements that follow one another linearly in the speech chain, and associative relations, which group elements that can be substituted for one another in a single position (later called "paradigmatic" by Hjelmslev and others). This distinction became fundamental to every subsequent structural analysis, both in linguistics and in other fields influenced by the structural method, such as anthropology (Lévi-Strauss) and psychoanalysis (Lacan).

## Synchrony and Diachrony

A central methodological contribution of the book is its distinction between the "synchronic" perspective (the study of the state of a language at a single moment, in isolation from its historical development) and the "diachronic" perspective (which traces the change of a language over time). Saussure criticized the dominance of the historical-comparative method that prevailed in European linguistics throughout the nineteenth century (in the work of Bopp, Schleicher and the Neogrammarians). He called for giving methodological priority to studying the linguistic system as it actually operates in the minds of its speakers at a given moment, regardless of its history, as the actual system that governs the daily production and comprehension of speech. This distinction later became the basis of the structural method in linguistics and in other human fields that borrowed its tools.

## Later Influence

The influence of this book went beyond linguistics to several human fields that together formed what is known as French "structuralism" in the twentieth century. The anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss borrowed Saussure's analytical tools to study kinship systems and myths; Roland Barthes used them to analyze cultural and media evidence ("Mythologies"); and Jacques Lacan used them to reread Freudian psychoanalysis on a linguistic structural basis. The book also provided a starting point for later critiques by post-structuralist philosophers such as Jacques Derrida, who in his book *Of Grammatology* (*De la grammatologie*, 1967) criticized the dominance of sound over writing in Saussure's conception of the linguistic sign, a critique known as "phonocentrism."

## Language and Speech

Another central distinction in the book is between "language" (*la langue*), the abstract system of rules and signs shared socially among the speakers of a particular linguistic community, and "speech" (*la parole*), the concrete individual realization of this system in each specific communicative situation. Saussure argued that scientific linguistics should take "language" as the primary object of its study, as the more stable and more amenable to systematic analysis, leaving the study of individual, variable "speech" to other fields such as psychology or individual stylistics. This distinction remains fundamental in linguistic thought today, even though later approaches (such as Labov's sociolinguistics) have argued that the individual and social variation in speech must also be studied as part of the linguistic system rather than outside it.

## Arabic translations

The *Cours* has circulated in Arabic chiefly through *Durūs fī al-alsuniyya al-ʿāmma* (Tunis: al-Dār al-ʿArabiyya lil-Kitāb, 1985), translated by Ṣāliḥ al-Qarmaḍī, Muḥammad al-Shāwish and Muḥammad ʿAjīna. Other Arabic versions exist; the 1985 translation is the one most often cited in Arabic linguistics.

## The Continuing Value of the Book

Despite more than a century since its publication, and despite later critical editions that reveal the complexity of its editorial composition, the book is still taught as an essential introduction for any student of linguistics. It remains the historical starting point that distinguished modern linguistics as an independent science with its own object and method, separate from the comparative historical studies that preceded it in the nineteenth century.
