# The Mind of Primitive Man

*The Mind of Primitive Man* (1911) is Franz Boas's book on the relations among race, language, and culture. Drawing in part on lectures and earlier papers, it challenged the assumption that differences in technology or custom reveal an inherited ranking of mental ability. Its title uses the vocabulary of its time while the argument undermines much that the vocabulary implied.

## The target of the argument

Late nineteenth-century thought often arranged peoples on a single scale from savagery to civilisation and linked position on that scale to bodily type. Boas asked whether the evidence supported either step. European achievements, he argued, did not demonstrate superior racial aptitude, since they depended on historical conditions, contact, and borrowing. Opportunities for civilisations to develop had differed, and the order in which peoples reached particular inventions was shaped by many events.

## Body, language, and culture

Boas argued that variation within any population is large and that ranges for different groups overlap. Physical type, language, and culture do not change together: people can adopt a new language or customs without a corresponding biological change, and related populations can differ culturally. His study of immigrants and their children, conducted for the United States Immigration Commission and published around the same time, reported changes in head form between generations, which questioned the fixity of a measure then used to define race. Later researchers have debated the size and meaning of those changes using his data.

## Mental processes

The book contends that basic mental capacities such as abstraction, inhibition, and reasoning are found among all peoples. Differences in thought arise largely from the traditional material with which new experiences are associated. Boas used linguistic evidence to argue that the absence of a habitual abstract expression does not show an inability to form it. Educated Europeans, too, accept many classifications and customs without critical reflection, supplying reasons for them afterwards.

## Method

Against schemes of uniform cultural evolution, Boas held that similar customs may arise from different histories. Comparison should follow detailed study of particular regions, their contacts, and their internal development. This position is usually called historical particularism.

## Revision and legacy

A substantially revised edition appeared in 1938, as Nazi race doctrine made the questions more urgent. The book became an important text against scientific racism and influenced students including Margaret Mead, Ruth Benedict, and Melville Herskovits. It is commonly associated with cultural relativism, although Boas's argument is primarily about evidence, history, and the independence of race, language, and culture, and the explicit formulation of relativism owes much to his students. The text also retains assumptions of its period, including some comparisons of civilisational levels and cautious statements about possible average biological differences that later science and later readers have rejected or reframed. Historians therefore read it as a turning point containing tensions, rather than a complete break.
