# The Incoherence of the Philosophers by Abu Hamid al-Ghazali

## The central thesis

A methodical attack from within philosophy itself on **Ibn Sina and al-Farabi** in particular (not on philosophy or logic in general). Al-Ghazali **does not reject logic as a tool**; rather he uses the same Aristotelian tools, demonstrative syllogism and the dismantling of premises, to show that the philosophers failed to meet their own condition: certain demonstration. The book examines twenty metaphysical and natural questions, but singles out three of them for explicitly declaring their adherents unbelievers: **the eternity of the world** (denying its temporal origination), **the denial of the bodily resurrection** on the Day of Judgment (holding only to a spiritual return), and **God's not knowing particulars** (limiting His knowledge to universals). As for the remaining questions, he describes them as innovation, not unbelief.

## Structure of the work

A methodological introduction declaring the aim (showing the incoherence of the philosophers, not a direct doctrinal refutation), followed by twenty "questions", each of which presents the philosophers' argument first faithfully and then dismantles it with their own logical tools. Among its most prominent questions outside the three condemned: the denial of the necessary causal relation between causes and effects (the seventeenth question), where he holds that the observed conjunction between fire and burning, for example, is a divine habit and not a necessary rational relation, a distant preparation, by centuries, for David Hume's skepticism about causation.

## Historical context (manuscript and editions)

It was composed in Baghdad in about 488 AH/1095 CE, in the last period of al-Ghazali's teaching at the Nizamiyya and shortly before his well-known spiritual crisis and withdrawal. It represents the culmination of his critical project against the philosophy of the Muslim Peripatetics (al-Farabi and Ibn Sina especially), after he had presented their views faithfully in his earlier book *Maqasid al-Falasifa*. It circulated in manuscripts and later printed editions in various forms, and there are several modern editions, the best known of which are Maurice Bouyges's edition in Beirut in 1927 and Sulayman Dunya's edition in Cairo.

## Reception and criticism

It aroused a direct philosophical reaction: Ibn Rushd replied about eighty years later in *Tahafut al-Tahafut*, defending Ibn Sina and al-Farabi and accusing al-Ghazali of misunderstanding some philosophical arguments or simplifying them for dialectical purposes. The book became a basic reference in the historical controversy over the relation of philosophy to the revealed law in Islamic thought, and a recurring starting point for discussion of the limits of demonstrative reason before revelation. Some contemporary scholars read it as a text in early philosophy of science and not merely a doctrinal refutation, in view of its treatment of the question of causation.

## Importance in the history of Islamic philosophy

One of the most influential critiques of Peripatetic philosophy in the history of Islamic thought, and an indispensable station for understanding the later controversy between al-Ghazali and Ibn Rushd and the place of philosophy in the classical Islamic heritage.

## The four introductions and the method

Al-Ghazali prefaces the work with four introductions that define what he wants, stating that he is not discussing all the philosophers but the followers of Aristotle in his Avicennan form, and showing that some of their sciences, such as arithmetic, logic and natural philosophy, are not the subject of religious dispute, and that the disagreement of the Muslims with them is in metaphysics alone. He then declares that the aim is to show the contradiction of their proofs, not to establish an alternative doctrine, for he speaks from the position of the objector who demands of them proof of what they claimed with certainty. In the last introduction he determines that religion does not call for rejecting everything they said, and that whoever denied the exact sciences on the pretext of religion wronged religion. This position appears in his other two books, "Mi'yar al-Ilm" and "al-Qistas al-Mustaqim", where he presents logic and encourages learning it, and in "al-Munqidh min al-Dalal", which recounts his intellectual journey.

## Eternity, God's attributes and His knowledge

In the first four questions he presents the philosophers' proofs of the eternity of the world, among them that the eternal divine will necessitates an eternal effect, and he replies that the will specifies the time from among similar times, and that action is from will and not from necessitation. In the thirteenth question he presents the question of God's knowledge of particulars: it was attributed to Ibn Sina that God knows particulars in a universal way, and al-Ghazali holds that this comes down to denying God's knowledge of what happens, and says that knowledge relates to the known even if it changes. Ibn Rushd, however, claims in his reply that Ibn Sina's position is not to be understood so. Between these two questions he criticizes the philosophers on the proof of the Maker, on the denial of attributes, and on the claim that God has no quiddity other than existence.

## Causation and the miracle

In the seventeenth question he determines that the conjunction between what is believed a cause and what is believed an effect is not necessary, and that God is the direct agent in every case, so the example of fire and burning cotton is only a habit by which the divine action has proceeded, and God may create burning without fire and create fire without burning. His aim is to justify the possibility of miracles such as the raising of the dead and the turning of the staff into a snake, not to deny the natural order in general. Michael Marmura studied this section and showed that al-Ghazali presents in it two positions: direct divine agency first, and then a dialectical concession accepting the existence of natures in things while allowing their suspension by God's will, and Frank Griffel held that al-Ghazali does not wholly deny secondary causes in his other books. The comparison with Hume remains a comparison in the conclusion of the argument and not in its basis, since al-Ghazali proceeds from belief in God's power and not from epistemic doubt.

## The resurrection and the book's influence

In the twentieth question he presents the philosophers' denial of the bodily resurrection, and argues that reason does not forbid restoring the body, and that the philosophical proofs of its impossibility are not demonstrative. As for the book's influence, it began with replies in the East, then Ibn Rushd's reply in Andalusia, and it turned into material in the modern debate about the causes of philosophy's weakness in the Islamic world. Muhammad Abid al-Jabri in "Nahnu wa-l-Turath" and in "Takwin al-Aql al-Arabi" raised a criticism of al-Ghazali, in the sense that he ended the path of demonstrative reason in the East as against Ibn Rushd in the West, while George Saliba in "Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance" (2007) presented evidence that the Islamic sciences flourished after al-Ghazali. A side of al-Ghazali passed into Latin with the translation of "Maqasid al-Falasifa" in Toledo, so that Europeans in the Middle Ages knew him as a transmitter of Ibn Sina and not a critic of him.

## Limits of the book

Scholars point out that al-Ghazali sometimes presents the philosophical proofs in an abbreviated form that is complete only in Ibn Sina himself, and that he uses what is called the dialectical argument based on constraint (*ilzam*). The declaration of the philosophers as unbelievers on the three questions also constituted a difficult juristic-political problem in Islamic thought, because determining unbelief on the strength of a theoretical question opens the door to accusation. Finally Griffel in "Al-Ghazali's Philosophical Theology" (2009) holds that the book itself is part of al-Ghazali's turn to building a new kalam into which philosophical reasoning enters, and not a withdrawal from philosophy.
