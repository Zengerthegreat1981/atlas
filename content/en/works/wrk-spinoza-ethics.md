# Ethics, Demonstrated in Geometrical Order by Baruch Spinoza (1677)

*Ethica, ordine geometrico demonstrata* — Spinoza wrote it in Latin and refrained from publishing it in his lifetime after the storm he met over the *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670), so it appeared after his death in the same year in the *Opera Posthuma* (Amsterdam, 1677) with only the initials B.d.S. rather than his full name. The Dutch authorities banned it in 1678, and it circulated in Europe underground for a whole century.

## The Central Thesis

There is only **one substance** of infinite attributes, God or Nature (*Deus sive Natura*); and everything we call things and souls is a **mode** (*modus*) of this substance, and thought and extension are two attributes of it, not two worlds. It follows that human emotion is not a flaw in nature to be condemned, but is studied as lines and surfaces are studied, and that freedom is not an escape from necessity but its **understanding**.

## Structure of the Work

Five parts, each built in the form of Euclid's book: **definitions → axioms → propositions → proofs**, with **scholia** outside the chain of proof in which he addresses the reader directly. The geometrical form is not ornament but part of the claim itself: that ethics is deduced, not preached.

1. **On God** — substance and attributes.
2. **On the Nature and Origin of the Mind** — the mind is the idea of the body, and the three kinds of knowledge.
3. **On the Origin and Nature of the Affects** — ending with the "Definitions of the Affects" gathered at its end.
4. **On Human Bondage, or the Powers of the Affects**.
5. **On the Power of the Intellect, or on Human Freedom**.

## Psychology: Conatus and Three Primary Affects

In Part Three Spinoza sets out Proposition 6: each thing, as far as it is in itself, strives to persevere in its being — and this **striving** (*conatus*) is the actual essence of the thing itself. From it all the affects are derived from only three primary ones: **desire** (*cupiditas*), **joy** (*laetitia*) as a transition to a greater perfection, and **sadness** (*tristitia*) as a transition to a lesser perfection; love is joy accompanied by the idea of an external cause, and hatred is likewise sadness. This is an early **derivational system of the affects** in the history of philosophy: a closed list generated from a single principle, not gathered by induction.

Part Four then defines **bondage** as a human being's inability to govern his affects, so that he is ruled by passions whose causes he does not understand, and Part Five defines **freedom** as the transformation of a passive affect into an idea clear and distinct from its cause, whose aim is the **intellectual love of God** (*Amor Dei Intellectualis*).

## Reception and Criticism

- **Pierre Bayle**: devoted to Spinoza in his dictionary (1697) an article in which he described the doctrine as a most monstrous hypothesis, and made him an atheist of a new type who lives a quiet, virtuous life, so that the epithet "Spinozist" broadened to include anyone suspected of atheism.
- **The Pantheism Controversy** (*Pantheismusstreit*): in the *Letters on the Doctrine of Spinoza* (1785) **Jacobi** related that Lessing admitted to him in a conversation that he knew no philosophy other than Spinoza's, and **Mendelssohn** replied with the book *To the Friends of Lessing* (1786), and the debate passed to the minds of Kant, Herder, and Goethe.
- **Hegel**, in his lectures on the history of philosophy, made Spinozism the beginning of all philosophizing, while faulting it at the same time for the finite's drowning in substance without return.
- **The modern psychological revival**: **Antonio Damasio**, in *Looking for Spinoza: Joy, Sorrow, and the Feeling Brain* (Harcourt, 2003), read Part Three as a precedent for a neural theory of emotion — emotion is a bodily event first and feeling an idea of it — and connected that to his hypothesis of **somatic markers**; a connection that stirred controversy among specialists in Spinoza.

## Importance in Psychology

Part Three is a systematic theory of the affects that preceded scientific psychology by two centuries: it defines affect by a change in the power of acting, and derives the affects from desire together with the two poles of joy and sadness. It is read today from within affective neuroscience through Damasio (2003).

## Part One: Necessity and the Denial of Teleology

Spinoza begins by defining substance as what is in itself and is conceived through itself, then proves that the one substance is God, existing necessarily, and that everything happens by the necessity of its nature and not by free will, so that no miracle breaches the order. In the appendix to Part One he criticizes teleological illusions: people imagine that nature works for them because they themselves work for ends, and from that they construct divine systems of good and evil, beauty and ugliness, praise and blame. He proposes that value judgments express our situations and not things, and that teleology reverses the order of causes and makes what is first second. It is a critique both of the idea of an absolute divine will and of the Biblical-Aristotelian conception.

## Psychophysical Parallelism and the Kinds of Knowledge

In Part Two Spinoza presents that the order and connection of ideas are the same as the order and connection of things, so that thought does not act on extension nor the reverse. He holds that the human mind is the idea of the body actually existing, and for this reason the body's knowledge of others has conditions. He then divides knowledge into three kinds: opinion based on experience, imagination, and hearsay; reason based on common notions; and intuitive science, which sees things from the side of their essence in God. This division is the basis of his analysis of error as a privation of knowledge and not a negative force.

## Part Five: Freedom and Eternity

The last part presents a practical method for relieving the affect: we form a clear and distinct idea of the affect, so that the affect ceases to be passive and becomes activity. He links this to the mind's capacity to see things as necessary, without regard to time. In Proposition 23 he says that the mind is not wholly destroyed with the body but something eternal of it remains, which is the thesis hardest to interpret for commentators, some of whom see it as the eternity of truth and not the survival of the person. It ends with his saying that happiness is not the reward of virtue but is virtue itself, and that everything noble is rare and difficult.

## Arabic Presence

Spinoza was introduced to Arabic readership through works on the history of philosophy and Fouad Zakariyya's studies, including his monograph published in the 1960s, and through Hassan Hanafi's translation of the *Theological-Political Treatise*; the *Ethics* was translated into Arabic by Jalal al-Din Said. Comparative studies point to links between Spinoza's concepts and the thought of Ibn Rushd and Maimonides in the debate between reason and revelation and in hermeneutic method, and these remain links of intellectual lineage and not documented citations, except for his explicit criticism of Maimonides in the *Theological-Political Treatise*.

## Limits of the System in Parts Three and Four

Critics object to deriving the affects from striving alone, for it reduces the diversity of emotional life to three origins, and presupposes a geometrical science of the soul that ignores individual differences. The idea that everything seeks to preserve itself faces the problem of suicide and sacrifice, which Spinoza treated by holding that the causes of destruction are external. Part Four then presents the concept of the "free man" as a model to be emulated, while in Part One he denies the principle of the absolute good, a known tension in the literature: how can a meaning remain for model and norm in a necessary system?
