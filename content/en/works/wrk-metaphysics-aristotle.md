# Aristotle's Metaphysics

The first science, which investigates being qua being, its first causes and ultimate principles, and the unmoved mover as the end of all motion in the universe.

## The Central Thesis

There is a science that surpasses all the particular sciences (physics, mathematics, ethics) because it does not investigate a particular existent in respect of its properties, but **being qua being** (τὸ ὂν ᾗ ὄν) and the principles and causes that belong to it as such. This science, which Aristotle called sometimes "first philosophy" and sometimes "theological science" (θεολογική), culminates in establishing the existence of a **first unmoved substance, eternal, pure actuality without potentiality**, which is the cause of the motion of the celestial spheres as a beloved end and not as a pushing mover: it moves the world as the beloved moves the lover, not as an agent pushes its object. This "unmoved mover" is what was later received, through Arabic and Latin medieval philosophy, as the closest pagan philosophical concept to the idea of God.

## The Structure of the Work

Fourteen books (designated literally by the Greek alphabet, the little alpha and the great Alpha together) that do not form a single compositional unit but a collection of lectures and essays gathered later:
- **Book Alpha (A)**: a critical historical survey of the views of earlier philosophers on causes (Thales, Empedocles, Anaxagoras, Plato) as a prelude to presenting Aristotle's theory of the four causes (material, formal, efficient, and final).
- **The middle books (Z, H, Θ)**: the theoretical heart of the work, the inquiry into **substance** (οὐσία): is substance the matter, the form, or the composite of the two? Aristotle concludes that **form** is primary substance in the strictest sense, with a decisive distinction between potentiality (δύναμις) and actuality (ἐνέργεια).
- **Book Lambda (Λ)**: the most famous book of the collection and the most independent: the proof of the first unmoved mover, and the separation between perishable sensible substances and the eternal divine substance.
- Scattered books on the critique of the Platonic theory of Forms (especially in the great Alpha, Mu, and Nu), and on unity and plurality.

## The Historical Context of Publication

There is no "publication date" in the modern sense: the text consists of internal lectures (esoteric/acroamatic) for students of the Lyceum that Aristotle did not prepare for general circulation, unlike his lost "dialogues", which were prepared for publication. According to ancient tradition, these lectures were collected and arranged in the first century BCE by **Andronicus of Rhodes** (c. 1st century BCE), who placed them after the books on nature (Physica) in his catalogue, so they were called "ta meta ta physika" (the things after the physical books), a library-order designation, not a philosophical title chosen by Aristotle himself, though it later came to be read mistakenly as a reference to what is beyond sensible nature. The date given for the work (330 BCE) is approximate for the period of Aristotle's teaching activity at the Lyceum (335–322 BCE), not a specific moment of composition.

## Reception and Arabic Transmission

The work was translated into Arabic in the ninth-century translation movement: Ustath (Eustathius) produced the earliest comprehensive Arabic translation for the circle of al-Kindi, while later scholars including Ishaq ibn Hunayn, Abu Bishr Matta, and Yahya ibn Adi translated and revised specific books and associated Greek commentaries (such as those of Alexander of Aphrodisias and Themistius). This textual transmission formed the indispensable foundation for Islamic peripatetic philosophy: al-Farabi wrote his treatise on the aims of Aristotle's Metaphysics (*Fi Aghrad ma ba'd al-Tabi'ah*), which famously enabled Ibn Sina to master the work. Ibn Sina developed his foundational ontological distinction between essence and existence (*al-mahiyyah wa-al-wujud*) in creative engagement with Aristotle. In Andalusia, Ibn Rushd (Averroes) composed his celebrated Long Commentary (*al-Tafsir al-Kabir li-Ma Ba'd al-Tabi'ah*), which preserved fragments of earlier Arabic translations and directly shaped Latin scholasticism, particularly the metaphysical theology of Thomas Aquinas. 

In twentieth-century scholarship, Werner Jaeger (1923) influentially argued that the *Metaphysics* does not constitute a single, harmonized treatise written at one sitting, but rather reflects diverse developmental phases of Aristotle's thought, challenging traditional scholastic readings of the work as a monolithic system.

## Why It Is Mentioned in the Atlas

Because the inquiry into substance and the distinction between potentiality and actuality in it are the logical foundation on which was later built every discussion of "form" and "matter" in the Aristotelian theory of the soul (*De Anima*), which is the distant origin of any functional approach to the relation between mind and body in Western philosophy. Likewise the idea of the "unmoved mover" as a beloved end and not a pushing cause influenced later conceptions of teleological motivation in the history of philosophical psychology.
