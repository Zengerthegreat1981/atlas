# Holy Anorexia

A book by the American historian of Italy Rudolph M. Bell, published by the University of Chicago Press in 1985 under the title *Holy Anorexia*. It surveys 261 Italian holy women recognised by the Catholic Church who lived between the thirteenth and the twentieth centuries. The surviving record is adequate for only about two thirds of them, and Bell found signs of anorexic behaviour in about half or more of that smaller group, reaching in some cases an almost complete abstention from food for long periods.

## Definition

Bell draws on the records of Church canonisation proceedings (*processi di canonizzazione*) and on saints' lives from Italy. From these he extracts statistical data on patterns of fasting and abstention from food over seven centuries. He attempts to interpret them in light of modern psychopathology, while preserving the historical religious context in which these saints lived.

## Historical Context

Bell wrote his work in the 1980s, at a time of sharp escalation in American medical and media interest in anorexia nervosa among teenage girls. This led him to search for historical precedents for the phenomenon in the archive of medieval Catholic female sanctity. The most prominent example is the life of Catherine of Siena (1347–1380), who died young after years of almost complete abstention from food, apart from the consecrated Eucharist.

## The Thesis

Bell proposes a reading that combines the history of religion with comparative psychiatry. He links medieval saintly fasting and modern anorexia nervosa in a structural, rather than a complete, identity. At the same time he affirms that severe fasting was often the only social means available to women of that era to win spiritual authority and personal independence from the authority of the family, the forced marriage, and the official Church institution.

## The Methodological Debate

Bell's "psychiatric" reading provoked wide debate among historians of religion. Later scholars objected to it (among them Caroline Walker Bynum, in *Holy Feast and Holy Fast*) as projecting a modern psychiatric diagnostic category onto a medieval religious context whose cultural and cognitive framework differs fundamentally. The saints did not understand their fasting as a disorder, but as a conscious spiritual practice directed towards imitating the sufferings of Christ and drawing near to God through mortification of the body. Teresa M. Shaw's *The Burden of the Flesh* treats the earlier patristic roots of Christian discourse on fasting and the body.

## Principal Examples Studied

Alongside Catherine of Siena, Bell examines the lives of other saints, such as Columba of Rieti and Maria Maddalena de' Pazzi. He shows how severe fasting provided these women, in a patriarchal society that deprived them of official religious authority (the priesthood), with an alternative means of acquiring spiritual prestige and social recognition. In some cases, he suggests, it even gave them influence over clergymen who sought their counsel.

## Impact

The book contributed to scholarly interest in religious fasting. It sparked a broad debate, lasting for decades, about the limits of using modern psychiatric diagnostic categories to interpret historical religious phenomena. It became a frequent reference in courses on the history of the body and on medieval female sanctity, despite the methodological disagreement over its central thesis.

## A Note on Bell's Sources and Method

Bell is a historian of Italy, not a psychiatrist, and his psychiatric interpretation rests on modern clinical descriptions of anorexia nervosa applied to historical sources.

Bell's work differs from many descriptive historical studies in its attempt at a quasi-quantitative analysis. He tabulates data on 261 women spread over seven centuries, not all of whom showed severe fasting, in an attempt to identify broader historical patterns. This quantitative ambition provoked a further methodological debate about the reliability of canonisation proceedings as a statistical source, given that they were originally written to prove sanctity, not for neutral medical documentation.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

There is no direct historical connection between the Catholic phenomenon of "holy anorexia", the subject of the book, and the Islamic heritage. Voluntary ascetic fasting does intersect structurally with the ascetic stations of Muslim Sufis, some of whom practised reducing food as a means of purifying the soul. Examples include the discussion of hunger and asceticism in al-Ghazali's *Ihya' Ulum al-Din*. This did not, however, reach the level of almost complete abstention documented in the Italian lives that Bell studies. The crucial difference is that al-Ghazali and writers like him made moderation in hunger a condition rather than an end in itself, warning against excess that harms the body and the mind.
