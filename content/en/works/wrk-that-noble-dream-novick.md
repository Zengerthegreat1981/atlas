# That Noble Dream: The "Objectivity Question" and the American Historical Profession

## Definition

A landmark work by the American historian Peter Novick, published in 1988 by Cambridge University Press. It offers a critical history of the evolution of the idea of "objectivity" across a full century of the American historical profession, from the founding of the American Historical Association (1884) to the 1980s.

## Historical Context

Novick wrote his work in the wake of a wave of critical re-examination of the foundations of the American historical profession, influenced by the linguistic turn and by the rise of social and feminist history, which challenged traditional assumptions of objectivity. Rather than defending a particular philosophical position in the debate over objectivity, Novick chose to write "a history of the question itself": a socio-historical study of how this debate took shape within the American academic institution across generations.

## Content

The book traces how the idea of "historical objectivity," influenced by a simplified reading of Ranke's motto "as it actually happened" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*), moved from a firmly held professional doctrine in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries (the age of the professional founding of American academic history), to a growing source of doubt with the rise of progressive history (Charles Beard and Carl Becker) in the 1920s and 1930s, which questioned whether a historian could fully transcend his own perspective. It then moved to an attempt to restore trust in objectivity after the Second World War, in the face of relativism associated with fascism and communism, and finally to a linguistic turn and critical theory in the late twentieth century, which shook confidence once again. Novick documents sharp internal professional debates (such as the Cold War controversy over the politicization of history and internal debates within the American Historical Association), without himself settling the debate in favor of any party. He presents the historicity of the question itself, rather than a philosophical answer to it, which provoked debate about his actual personal position hidden behind this apparent neutrality.

## Methodological Importance

The work is the most important reference for understanding how Ranke's famous motto was transformed in American reception into a positivist doctrine much simpler than Ranke's original position (which was originally concerned with empathetic understanding as much as with bare facts). It also shows how the historical profession dealt with a succession of philosophical challenges over a full century, using a sociological method for the history of ideas that links philosophical positions to their concrete institutional and political contexts (university appointments, editorial control of journals, cultural wars).

## Impact and Criticism

The book won a prestigious intellectual history prize and became a standard reference in any university course on American philosophy of history. It has met with criticism from some historians who saw Novick's apparent neutrality as concealing an implicit tendency to doubt the possibility of objectivity, and who felt the title itself ("the noble dream") carries an ironic tone toward the founding aspiration of the profession.

## Structure of the Book and Its Detailed Arguments

Novick divides his large book chronologically across several main phases: the professional founding (1884–1900), when objectivity was regarded as self-evident and not open to question; the crisis of progressive history in the 1920s and 1930s, when Beard and Becker explicitly doubted whether a historian could transcend his personal and class perspective; the reaffirmation of objectivity after the Second World War as an ideological weapon against Soviet totalitarianism; and then the crisis of the 1960s and 1970s with the rise of "history from below" and the civil rights movements, which re-politicized historical questions. Novick ties each phase to its immediate American political and institutional context, showing that the abstract philosophical debate about objectivity was always entangled with practical struggles over who holds the right to speak in the name of the historical profession and to direct its resources.

Novick also recounts in detail the story of the founding of the *American Historical Review* and its role in establishing the profession's standards, showing how its editorial and publishing policies over decades came to reflect the same tensions between declared objectivity and the undeclared actual biases of successive editors.

The book is sometimes compared with parallel works in other academic disciplines that wrote similar critical histories of the development of objectivity standards in those fields (such as sociology and anthropology), which places it within a broader current of "history of academic professions" that flourished at the end of the twentieth century in response to the same questions about the relationship between knowledge and power and institution.

Today, more than three decades after its publication, the book remains a standard reference taught at the beginning of any training program for new historians in American universities. It offers a comprehensive historical map that helps the novice student understand his own position within a long series of renewed professional debates about the meaning of historical writing and its possible limits.
