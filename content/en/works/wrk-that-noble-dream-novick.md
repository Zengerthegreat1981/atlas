# That Noble Dream: The "Objectivity Question" and the American Historical Profession

*That Noble Dream: The "Objectivity Question" and the American Historical Profession* (1988), by the American intellectual historian Peter Novick (1934–2012), is a major disciplinary history of American historiography. Tracing the evolving norm of historical objectivity from the founding of the American Historical Association (AHA) in 1884 to the culture wars of the 1980s, the book argued that objectivity was never a fixed epistemological standard, but a contested, socially constructed professional ethos. The title derives from Charles A. Beard's 1935 essay "That Noble Dream," in which Beard questioned the ideal of value-free historical science.

## Four Generations of Professional Debate

Rather than arbitrating whether objective historical knowledge is philosophically possible, Novick adopted an institutional and sociological approach, dividing the century-long trajectory of the profession into four successive eras:

1. **Pioneers and Positivism (1884–1910):** The founders of professional American history appropriated a simplified, positivist version of Leopold von Ranke's dictum to show the past "as it actually was" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*). Divesting Ranke's method of its Lutheran theological foundations, early American professionals treated historical facts like discrete physical specimens awaiting scientific collection, cataloging, and neutral presentation.
2. **The Relativist Challenge (1910–1940):** The Progressive historians, notably Carl Becker in "Everyman His Own Historian" (1931) and Charles Beard in "That Noble Dream" (1935), shattered this initial consensus. Disillusioned by World War I propaganda and influenced by pragmatist philosophy, they argued that every historical reconstruction inevitably reflects the social position, personal values, and contemporary needs of the historian.
3. **Consensual Retrenchment (1940–1960):** Mobilization during World War II and the subsequent ideological imperatives of the Cold War prompted a vigorous reaffirmation of objectivity. Relativism was stigmatized as a dangerous moral and epistemic concession to totalitarianism (both Fascist and Soviet). Mid-century "consensus historians" reasserted professional neutrality, marginalizing radical dissent within university faculties and journals such as the *American Historical Review*.
4. **Disintegration and Pluralism (1960–1980s):** The social upheavals of the 1960s, the Vietnam War, the civil rights movement, and the rise of the New Left, feminist, African American, and social histories ("history from below") fractured the consensus. Compounded by the linguistic turn and post-structuralist theory (such as Hayden White's *Metahistory*), historians abandoned the ideal of a single authoritative national narrative. Concluding with the biblical metaphor "there was no king in Israel," Novick argued that by the late 1980s, the profession had fractured into specialized, mutually uncommunicative subfields lacking shared epistemological ground.

## Reception and the Objectivity Controversy

Awarded the AHA's Albert J. Beveridge Award in 1989, *That Noble Dream* incited intense controversy across the historical profession. A 1991 forum in the *American Historical Review* debated the book, and critics charged that Novick's account left too little room for a defensible notion of objectivity. Thomas L. Haskell, among others, argued that objectivity should not be conflated with detachment or neutrality, maintaining that historians can hold moral convictions while practicing self-critical scholarship. Despite these disputes, Novick's study remains a standard institutional and intellectual history of the American historical profession.
