# The Oil Curse: How Petroleum Wealth Shapes the Development of Nations

The American political scientist Michael Ross published this book in 2012 in an attempt to offer a comprehensive and statistically updated synthesis of the literature on the "resource curse," which he and other researchers had built up over the previous two decades. It draws on cross-national quantitative data covering many oil-exporting countries over several decades.

## The Decisive Distinction: Institutions and Income at the Time Oil Arrives

Ross argues that the adverse political and economic effects of oil are concentrated in countries that were poor and had weak institutions when oil income arrived. Countries that already had strong institutions and high incomes when they became oil producers (such as Norway) were able to absorb oil revenues without their democracy being undermined. Countries that became dependent on oil with weaker institutions (such as Nigeria and Angola, and many authoritarian states of the Middle East) have experienced more serious distortions in the way their states developed.

## Updating the Statistical Relationship Between Oil and Autocracy

Ross extends in this book his earlier research project (his 2001 article "Does Oil Hinder Democracy?", which presented statistical evidence that dependence on oil revenues is associated with a lower likelihood of democratisation). He presents updated data and argues that the relationship persists. He also discusses the three mechanisms he had proposed: the rentier effect (reduced need for taxation weakens accountability), the repression effect (funding larger security apparatuses to suppress opposition), and the modernisation effect (slowing the social change that produces groups demanding political representation).

## The Gender Curse: Oil and Women's Weak Economic Participation

Ross adds to the resource curse literature an analysis of the effect of the oil economy on women's participation in the labour force and in politics. He argues that oil economies tend to expand sectors (such as construction and extractive industries) that are traditionally male-dominated, while reducing the need for broad female employment in other export sectors (such as textiles, which employed women intensively in other manufacturing economies). On his evidence this slows the rate at which women enter the labour market, compared with countries at a similar income level that are not dependent on oil.

## Employment and Subsidies as Instruments of Political Control

Ross discusses how oil-state governments may use rent revenues to expand public employment, subsidies and other benefits as a way of securing popular loyalty in place of political participation, alongside the security apparatus.

## Policy Proposals: Transparency and Sovereign Wealth Funds

Ross devotes concluding chapters to discussing policy tools that might ease the severity of the oil curse. These include international transparency initiatives on extractive revenues (such as the Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative, EITI), and the design of sovereign wealth funds with strict spending rules that limit governments' ability to use oil revenues for short-term political purposes. He cites Norway's experience as a relatively successful model for managing these revenues within democratic institutions that were already firmly established.

## Academic Reception and Methodological Criticism

The book was widely praised as a comprehensive synthesis of the resource curse literature. Some researchers have nonetheless continued to question the causal foundation of certain statistical correlations it presents. They argue that deeper historical and institutional factors (such as the legacy of colonialism and the quality of institutions before oil was discovered) may explain a large part of the observed "curse" more than oil itself as an independent variable.

## Its Place in the Resource Curse Literature

The book is widely read as a leading reference on the relationship between oil and political authoritarianism. It is often taught alongside the classical rentier state literature (such as Beblawi and Luciani's *The Rentier State*, 1987), of which it is in part a quantitative continuation.

## The Connection to the Broader Dutch Disease and Resource Curse Debate

Ross's analysis intersects with the broader economic debate over the "Dutch disease" (the negative effect of a rise in exports of a single resource on the competitiveness of other export sectors, through an increase in the real exchange rate). Ross treats the political dimension (authoritarianism, corruption, conflict and weak institutions) together with the economic dimension (distortion of the economy's structure), rather than only the latter as in much earlier literature.
