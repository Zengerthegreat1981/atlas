# The Narrative Constitution of Identity

"The Narrative Constitution of Identity: A Relational and Network Approach" (1994) is an article by the sociologist Margaret R. Somers, published in *Theory and Society*. It argued that narrative is not merely a way of representing social life but a constitutive feature of identity and action, and it became a leading reference for narrative approaches in sociology.

## Intellectual Context

Somers wrote during the "cultural turn" in the social sciences, when interest in meaning, discourse and representation grew alongside structural and materialist explanation. She criticised social explanations that assume identity follows from membership of a category such as class, gender or race, and she also criticised treating narrative as a mere representation of events that has no explanatory role. A companion article with Gloria D. Gibson appeared in the same year in Craig Calhoun's volume *Social Theory and the Politics of Identity*.

## The Central Thesis

Somers argued that people make sense of themselves and their actions by locating them in narratives, emplotted accounts that connect events across time and place and give them meaning, and that identity is formed through this narrative placement. Action, she argued, is guided by the stories that people can locate themselves within, and not by categorical attributes alone. She proposed a relational and network approach that examines how identities are formed through people's positions within networks of relationships and narratives, instead of classifying individuals by categories imposed from outside.

She distinguished four dimensions of narrativity: ontological narratives, the stories actors tell about themselves and their place in the world; public narratives, the stories circulated by families, workplaces, institutions and nations; conceptual (or analytic) narrativity, the concepts and explanations that social scientists construct; and metanarratives, the master narratives, such as progress or modernity, in which we are embedded as contemporary actors. Somers also stressed that narratives are relational, in that their meaning depends on how events are connected to one another within a plot, and that they are shaped by time and space.

## Application and Later Work

Somers had earlier applied narrative analysis to English working-class formation, for example in "Narrativity, Narrative Identity, and Social Action: Rethinking English Working-Class Formation" (*Social Science History*, 1992). In *Genealogies of Citizenship: Markets, Statelessness, and the Right to Have Rights* (Cambridge University Press, 2008) she turned to the politics of citizenship and examined how the idea of the right to have rights has been constructed and undermined, discussing the treatment of Hurricane Katrina survivors as an illustration.

## Impact and Critique

The article influenced research on identity, social movements, gender, and storytelling. It is often discussed alongside work such as Ken Plummer's study of sexual stories and Francesca Polletta's work on storytelling in protest. Critics have asked how narrative analysis can be made systematic and testable, how to distinguish constitutive narratives from after-the-fact accounts, and whether the framework gives enough weight to material constraints and to power over who can tell and be heard in a story.
