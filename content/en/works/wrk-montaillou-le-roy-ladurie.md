# Montaillou: The Promised Land of Error

A pioneering work by the French historian Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, published in 1975 under its original title *Montaillou, village occitan de 1294 à 1324*. It reconstructs the daily life and religious beliefs of a small French village in the Pyrenees at the threshold of the fourteenth century, and it is one of the most famous works of the Annales school outside specialist academic circles.

## Content

The book draws on the interrogation records of the Inquisition, namely the register of Jacques Fournier, Bishop of Pamiers (who would later become Pope Benedict XII), who recorded the interrogations of hundreds of villagers suspected of Cathar heresy during the first decade of the fourteenth century. This register is an exceptionally rich source for the voices of the peasants of the village of Montaillou, in their language and intimate beliefs. It allows the historian access to details not usually found in official documents: family and marital relations, concepts of kinship and property, beliefs about death, the afterlife and the soul, sexual practices and extramarital love relationships, the methods of village gossip and conflict, and even the peasants' conceptions of time and space. Le Roy Ladurie organized the book into two parts: the first is descriptive and reconstructs the material and social "ecology" (l'écologie) of the village, and the second is analytical and deals with the mental "ethnography" of its inhabitants through the concepts of kinship, authority, and belief.

## Methodological Importance

The work is a model of the flexibility of the Annales method in accommodating multiple scales: from the broad quantitative demographic analysis for which Le Roy Ladurie is known in his other works, such as *Les Paysans de Languedoc*, to an intimate micro-historical reconstruction of a single village here. In this way it methodologically parallels the Italian micro-history (Microstoria) developed by Carlo Ginzburg in his book *The Cheese and the Worms* (1976), although Le Roy Ladurie preceded it by a single year. This makes *Montaillou* one of the early foundational works of the method of reading judicial documents "against the grain" (contre la fable), that is, extracting a social and mental history from a source that was not designed for this purpose but rather to prosecute and try heresy. The book also provoked a methodological debate about the limits of trusting testimony extracted under judicial interrogation, and about the possibility that the peasants' voice passed through a linguistic and theological filter imposed by the writer (the notary) and the investigators themselves.

## Reception and Influence

The book achieved an unusually broad popular success for an academic work. It was translated into dozens of languages, and it helped introduce non-specialist readers to the Annales school and to the possibilities of social and mentalities history. It has also become a basic reference in teaching methods of social and micro-history in Western universities, despite later criticisms made by historians such as Brom Richards of some of its generalizations about a unified "village mentality," and by others of its treatment of the judicial record.

## Figures and Fates

The book portrays in detail a number of the village's real characters, most notably Pierre Clergue, the village priest, who was at the same time a secret Cathar religious leader and a party to several romantic relationships with women of the village, among them Béatrice de Planissoles, a noblewoman whose detailed testimony about her emotional and religious life became a fundamental material of the book. It also follows the fate of the extended Clergue family through a network of village alliances and enmities, down to the trials of some of its members and the fate of several of them by burning or life imprisonment, while others survived through a formal repentance or flight. These individual biographies allow the reader to follow the village not as an abstract unit, but as a fabric of intertwined human destinies.

## Le Roy Ladurie's Broader Method

*Montaillou* falls within Le Roy Ladurie's broader project of applying the tools of quantitative and demographic historical analysis that he developed in his vast thesis *Les Paysans de Languedoc* (1966) to a miniature scale that allows the exploration of mental and emotional dimensions difficult to reach through statistics alone. He later expressed this methodological shift in his famous formulation about moving from the quantitative "static history" to a "mobile history" of mentalities and the individual event. This led to his classification within the second and third generations of the Annales school at once, combining the quantitative legacy of Fernand Braudel with the legacy of Lucien Febvre's interest in mentalities.

## Critical Remarks

Some historians, among them Brom Richards and Robert Fossier, have criticized Le Roy Ladurie's tendency at times to generalize a unified "village mentality" from a relatively limited sample of testimonies, and to read the judicial record with excessive confidence, as though it directly reflected the voice of the peasants without any notable mediation by the ecclesiastical writer and investigator, while the interrogation process itself was directed by specific questions serving the purpose of proving heresy. Nevertheless, the book remains a model for how a rich social history can be extracted from the very archive of ecclesiastical repression.
