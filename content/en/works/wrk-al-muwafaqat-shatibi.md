# Al-Muwafaqat fi Usul al-Shari'ah

Al-Shatibi's principal work in usul, in which he formulated his theory of the **objectives of Sharia** in a complete methodological form. Its full title is sometimes rendered "al-Muwafaqat fi Usul al-Ahkam" or "al-Muwafaqat fi Usul al-Shari'a." The latter is the most prominent in contemporary academic usage.

## General Structure

The book is distributed over major sections dealing with: the introductions (the definition of Sharia and its foundations), the Sharia rulings and their division (rulings of obligation, *taklifi*, and declaratory rulings, *wad'i*), the **objectives** (its best-known section), the Sharia evidences, and juristic reasoning and imitation. The section on objectives is the one on which a whole intellectual field was later built, known today as "maqasid studies."

## The Central Thesis

Al-Shatibi holds that Sharia is **established for the interests of people in both abodes** (this life and the hereafter). These interests are arranged in three levels (the necessities, the needs and the embellishments), all revolving around five universals (religion, life, intellect, progeny and property). His argument for this is **inductive rather than based on a single text**: he gathers scattered particular proofs from the Qur'an and the Sunna, and from their totality, not from any one of them alone, certainty is reached that these objectives are intended by the Lawgiver.

## Its Later Impact

The book was less prominent in the centuries after its author's death than in modern times. It was revived and put to use in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries by contemporary reformers and usulists, who employed the framework of objectives in discussing questions of modern legislation. Its use in modern reform thought is discussed below.

## The Book's Place in the History of Usul al-Fiqh

*al-Muwafaqat* comes at the end of a long course of usuli theorizing. It began with al-Shafi'i's *al-Risala* (late second century AH), which established usul al-fiqh as an independent discipline resting on four sources (the Book, the Sunna, consensus and analogy). Al-Juwayni then developed it in *al-Burhan* and *al-Waraqat*. Al-Ghazali expanded it in *al-Mustasfa* by linking the principles to theology and logic. Izz al-Din ibn Abd al-Salam added an early objectives dimension in *Qawa'id al-Ahkam* by formulating the general principle of "securing benefits and averting harms." Al-Shatibi came to give this scattered objectives line a **complete systematic formulation**, transforming the objectives from a passing juristic observation into an independent methodological theory with its own inductive rules.

## The Context of Composition: Andalusia in the Eighth Century AH

Al-Shatibi wrote his work in Granada, late in the Nasrid state, in a conservative Maliki juristic environment that sometimes viewed with suspicion any reasoning that seemed to depart from the apparent meaning of the texts. For this reason al-Shatibi was keen to affirm that his theory of objectives is **not juristic opinion detached from the text**, but an induction from the texts themselves that uncovers the Lawgiver's underlying objective. This explains his repeated methodological insistence that his argument is inductive and cumulative (obtaining certainty from the totality of probabilities), not individual analogical reasoning that might be accused of arbitrariness.

## The Relationship of Objectives to Public Interest and Ijtihad

Al-Shatibi devotes an important part of the book to determining the controls for reasoning from **public interest** (*maslaha mursala*, where no specific text establishes its consideration or cancellation). He stipulates that the interest must be **consistent with the general objectives of the Lawgiver**, not merely a free rational preference. This distinguishes his position from later, freer uses of the concept of interest. The book also links the theory of objectives to the conditions of ijtihad: the true mujtahid, in his view, is one who has grasped the universal objectives of Sharia to a degree that enables him to apply rulings to new events without departing from the spirit of the legislation.

## Its Influence on Modern Reform Thought

Reformist circles associated with Muhammad Abduh and Rashid Rida, and scholars such as Abd Allah Draz (whose annotated edition circulated widely), helped revive interest in al-Shatibi in the early twentieth century. Then came Muhammad al-Tahir ibn Ashur with his book *Maqasid al-Shari'a al-Islamiyya* (1946), which developed al-Shatibi's project by presenting the study of the objectives as an **independent discipline** with its own methods, not merely an auxiliary tool for understanding texts. This later opened the way to wider contemporary uses of the term "maqasid al-shari'a" in debates on human rights, the codification of personal status, and Islamic economics.

## The Three Levels: The Necessities, the Needs and the Embellishments

Al-Shatibi elaborates a precise hierarchy among levels of interest. The **necessities** (*daruriyyat*) are those without which religious and worldly life cannot subsist, and whose loss leads to general corruption that cannot be tolerated. The **needs** (*hajiyyat*) are what the community requires to remove hardship and difficulty, and whose loss causes hardship without reaching the level of general corruption, such as the concessions for travel. The **embellishments** (*tahsiniyyat*) relate to noble morals and good customs, whose absence does not impair the uprightness of life. This ordering is not merely a descriptive classification. It serves a practical deductive rule: in case of conflict, the necessity takes precedence over the need, and the need over the embellishment. Later jurists widely used this rule in weighing fatwas when interests collide.
