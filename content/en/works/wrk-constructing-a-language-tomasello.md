# Constructing a Language: A Usage-Based Theory of Language Acquisition

A major work by Michael Tomasello (2003), a developmental psychologist then at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig. It sets out his usage-based alternative to theories of innate universal grammar as an account of how children acquire a first language. The argument draws on his experimental and observational research on language and social cognition in young children.

## The Central Argument: Generalization from Specific Examples

Tomasello argues that children do not activate an innate universal grammatical structure. Instead they build grammatical generalizations gradually through successive stages. They begin by memorizing "verb-island constructions" (constructions anchored to specific verbs, as when a child uses a particular verb in only one frame), then progressively generalize these patterns into broader grammatical constructions through general cognitive mechanisms (such as analogy and categorization), rather than through activating ready-made innate grammatical parameters.

## Grammatical Constructions as Complex Linguistic Symbols

In this book Tomasello adopts a theoretical framework close to Construction Grammar. He treats grammatical constructions (from a simple phrase to a complex sentence) as "linguistic symbols" in their own right: units that link a particular form to a particular meaning and communicative function, just as a single word is a linguistic symbol. It follows that acquiring grammar does not differ fundamentally from acquiring vocabulary. Both are learning linguistic symbols tied to particular communicative uses, acquired gradually through repeated exposure rather than through the activation of abstract grammatical parameters in advance.

## Shared Attention and Intention-Reading

Tomasello links the child's capacity to acquire language to deeper social-cognitive abilities that developmentally precede language itself, most notably "joint attention" and "intention-reading": the child's ability to understand that adults intend to convey a specific meaning. He regards this general social-cognitive ability as the real foundation of language acquisition, rather than a separate specialized grammatical faculty. He supports this with his observation that intention-reading appears in infants around nine or ten months of age, several months before the first words emerge, and that it is not developed to the same degree in the closest evolutionary relatives of humans (such as chimpanzees). In his view, this is the true cognitive difference that accounts for the human uniqueness in language, rather than the existence of a separate specialized grammatical organ in the brain.

## Stages of Grammatical Development in the Child

Tomasello details the stages of language acquisition based on detailed longitudinal data (daily recordings of the speech of particular children over months and years). In early development (roughly the second year) the child produces holophrases and utterances tied to specific words and fixed formulas, with little ability to generalize the pattern to other verbs. The child then produces pivot schemas and item-based constructions, centered on a fixed word with a variable slot. Later, more abstract constructions develop, applicable to new verbs the child has not previously heard in that pattern. The ages are approximate and vary across children. Tomasello regards this as evidence that grammatical generalization is a gradual process built on actual linguistic input, not an immediate activation of ready-made innate rules.

## Critique of the Poverty of the Stimulus Argument

The book directly confronts the "poverty of the stimulus" argument used by many generative theorists in favour of linguistic innateness. That argument holds that the speech children hear is insufficient for them to arrive at the grammatical knowledge they show, and therefore that some grammatical knowledge is innate. Tomasello replies that this argument underestimates the richness of the actual linguistic input directed at the child (child-directed speech) and its enormous daily repetition, and ignores the capacity of general cognitive mechanisms (such as statistical analogy and categorization over examples) to extract complex grammatical generalizations from this rich input without any specialized innate grammatical content.

## Impact and Position in the Debate

This book represents one of the most developed challenges to the generative universal grammar hypothesis. It has deeply influenced the development of construction grammar and usage-based grammar. However, the debate over which of the two positions (innate or usage-based) better explains language acquisition remains academically open and unresolved. Generative-oriented researchers (such as Stephen Crain and Rosalind Thornton) have responded that usage-based models are still unable to explain the speed with which certain abstract grammatical constraints (such as constraints on grammatical movement) are acquired, despite such constraints rarely appearing in negative examples in parental speech.
