# Al-Mustasfa min 'Ilm al-Usul

The best-known usulist treatise of Abu Hamid **al-Ghazali**, written late in his life, and regarded as a logically organized book of usul al-fiqh. Al-Ghazali employed his logical tools (he is the author of *Mi'yar al-'Ilm* in logic) to formulate the sections of the field in a rigorously inferential manner.

## Structure and content

After the logical introduction, the book divides usul al-fiqh into four major parts (called *aqtab*, "poles"): **the rulings** (their definition and divisions), **the evidences** (the Book, the Sunna, consensus, and rational proof with presumption of continuity), **the manner of deduction** (the indications of language, and **analogy** in broad detail), and **the one who derives rulings (the mujtahid)**, including ijtihad and the conflict of evidences. The section on analogy is the most influential in later literature.

## Context: between al-Juwayni and the accumulation of usul knowledge

Al-Ghazali had earlier compiled notes drawing on the usul teaching of his teacher **Imam al-Haramayn al-Juwayni** (author of *al-Burhan*) in an earlier work, *al-Mankhul*. He returned to the subject late in life, after withdrawing from public life, embracing Sufism and writing *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, to write his final and most mature formulation of the science of usul. The book opens with a logical introduction, in which he requires the usulist to master logic as a tool of inference. This was not customary in usul writing before him. He thus introduced the definitions of concepts and the forms of syllogism into the heart of the jurisprudential discussions, a methodological precedent that influenced Fakhr al-Din al-Razi and al-Amidi after him.

## The theory of objectives and benefit

One of *al-Mustasfa*'s most notable contributions is its treatment of **unrestricted public interest** (al-maslaha al-mursala) within a broader theory of the **objectives of the Sharia** (maqasid al-shari'a). Al-Ghazali divides benefits by rank into the **necessities** (preservation of religion, life, progeny, property and intellect), then the **needs**, then the **embellishments**. He thereby establishes a preferential hierarchy that, in cases of conflict, gives priority to preserving the necessary over the needed and the embellishing.

This five-part division of necessities later became the foundation on which Abu Ishaq al-Shatibi built his complete theory of objectives in *al-Muwafaqat*. Al-Ghazali is thus regarded as an early and influential contributor to the theory of objectives within usul al-fiqh. His use of benefit, however, is more cautious than that of the later Najm al-Din al-Tufi, who gave benefit precedence over the text in cases of speculative conflict.

## His position on istihsan

In the section on **istihsan** (juristic preference), al-Ghazali opposed the Hanafis, who regarded it as an independent fourth evidence. He reduced it to one of two things: either a hidden analogy whose ratio legis was not explicitly stated, or a benefit appropriate to the purpose of the Law, which amounts to reasoning from unrestricted public interest. He thus denied its independence as a fourth evidence in its own right, without denying the content of the rulings built upon it. Similarly, he discussed the authority of **istishab** (presumption of continuity) as the weakest of the evidences, resorted to only when no other evidence changes the original ruling.

## Conflict of evidence and its resolution

The book devotes its final section, on the mujtahid, to the rules of **preference** (tarjih) when speculative evidences conflict, the conditions of **ijtihad**, the levels of mujtahids, and the question of whether every mujtahid is correct or only one is correct. On this question, al-Ghazali's position is generally reported as the view that every mujtahid is correct in the sense that God has no predetermined ruling for the disputed case apart from the outcome of sound ijtihad.

## Language and semantic indications

Al-Ghazali devotes a broad section to questions of words and their indications: the literal and the figurative, the general and the particular, the absolute and the restricted, the explicit and the implicit, and command and prohibition with what they entail of obligation, recommendation or prohibition. These are presented as a prerequisite for understanding the textual sources before deducing rulings from them. In this section he drew on the science of language and Arabic rhetoric as much as on the speech of the theologians. His treatment of words influenced what came after it in al-Amidi's *al-Ihkam* and Ibn al-Hajib's *Muntaha al-Sul wa al-Amal*.

## Reception and impact

*Al-Mustasfa* has long been regarded as a major and widely studied usulist treatise. Fakhr al-Din al-Razi drew on it, along with al-Juwayni's *al-Burhan* and Abu al-Husayn al-Basri's *al-Mu'tamad*, in composing his *al-Mahsul*, and Shihab al-Din al-Qarafi benefited from it in his *Sharh Tanqih al-Fusul*. Its use of Greek logic as a usulist tool and its reserved stance on Hanafi istihsan were not accepted by all jurists. Nevertheless, Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddima*, counts it among the four principal works of the theologians' method (with Abd al-Jabbar's *al-'Umad*, Abu al-Husayn al-Basri's *al-Mu'tamad* and al-Juwayni's *al-Burhan*), and its standing in the usul tradition has remained firmly established.

## Its place among comparable works

*Al-Mustasfa* distinguishes itself from its classical counterparts by combining logical rigor with openness to purposive questions that had not yet reached their maturity. Abu al-Husayn al-Basri the Mu'tazili, in *al-Mu'tamad*, moved toward a theological analysis of questions of language and evidence. Abu Ya'la the Hanbali, in *al-'Udda*, moved toward recording Hanbali narration on disputed questions. Al-Ghazali came to combine both principles, with a clear inclination toward philosophical abstraction. His book became a reference read by jurists of several schools.

## Date of Composition

*Al-Mustasfa* is among the few classical usul works that can be dated closely. The biographer Ibn Khallikan, in *Wafayat al-A'yan*, reports that al-Ghazali completed it on 6 Muharram 503 AH, corresponding to early August 1109 CE. Modern chronologies of al-Ghazali's writings, including the standard study by Maurice Bouyges, accept the year 503 AH. The date places the book a little over two years before the author's death in Jumada al-Akhira 505 AH (December 1111 CE), and well after the works of his Baghdad period, such as his writings on logic and his critique of the philosophers. It also comes after *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* and after his return to teaching at the Nizamiyya of Nishapur in 499 AH. In the introduction al-Ghazali explains that students asked him for a work on usul that would stand between excessive length and excessive brevity, with better arrangement than a very long treatment and more substance than a short one. The date matters for interpretation, because it allows the positions in the book to be read as his mature views where they differ from those in the earlier *al-Mankhul*.
