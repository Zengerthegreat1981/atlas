# The Travels (Rihla) of Ibn Battuta

*Tuḥfat al-Nuẓẓār fī Gharāʾib al-Amṣār wa-ʿAjāʾib al-Asfār* (*A Gift to Those Who Contemplate the Curiosities of Cities and the Marvels of Traveling*), universally known as the *Riḥla* (The Travels), is the celebrated travel narrative documenting the thirty-year journeys of the Moroccan legal scholar and globetrotter **Shams al-Dīn Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn ʿAbd Allāh ibn Baṭṭūṭa** (703–770 AH / 1304–1368/69 CE). Dictated upon his final return to Morocco to the Andalusi scholar and royal secretary **Muḥammad ibn Muḥammad ibn Juzayy al-Kalbī** (721–758 AH / 1321–1357 CE) at the behest of the Marinid Sultan Abū ʿInān Fāris, the work was completed in Fez in Dhū al-Ḥijja 756 AH (December 1355 CE).

## Scope and Geographic Itinerary

Ibn Baṭṭūṭa departed his native Tangier in 725 AH (1325 CE) at the age of twenty-one to perform the Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca. His journey evolved into an extraordinary twenty-nine-year odyssey covering an estimated 117,000 kilometers across Africa, Europe, and Asia:
- **The Near East and Mediterranean**: Traversed North Africa, Egypt, the Levant, the Arabian Peninsula, Iraq, and western Persia, performing the pilgrimage multiple times.
- **The Indian Ocean and East Africa**: Sailed down the Red Sea and along the Swahili Coast of East Africa, documenting Mogadishu, Mombasa, and the thriving commercial sultanate of Kilwa.
- **Anatolia and the Golden Horde**: Traversed the Turkish beyliks of Anatolia—providing valuable descriptions of the civic and religious *akhi* (chivalric) brotherhoods—before crossing the Black Sea to the court of Özbeg Khan of the Golden Horde, accompanying a Byzantine princess (Bayalun) on a diplomatic journey to Constantinople.
- **India and the Maldives**: Crossed Central Asia (Bukhara, Samarkand) and Afghanistan into the Delhi Sultanate under Sultan Muhammad bin Tughluq, where Ibn Baṭṭūṭa served as a Maliki judge (*qāḍī*) for eight years. He subsequently served as chief judge in the Maldive Islands and visited Ceylon (Sri Lanka).
- **Southeast Asia and China**: Traveled through the Strait of Malacca and the ports of Yuan-dynasty China, including Quanzhou (Zaytūn), Guangzhou (Ṣīn-Kalān), and Hangzhou.
- **Al-Andalus and the Mali Empire**: After returning briefly to Morocco in 750 AH (1349 CE), he crossed the Strait of Gibraltar to the Emirate of Granada, and later crossed the Sahara Desert to the Mali Empire, visiting Walata, Timbuktu, Gao, and the court of Mansa Sulayman along the Niger River.

## Composition, Literary Form, and Textual Criticism

Ibn Baṭṭūṭa did not keep a written daily journal during his travels; he dictated his recollections from memory. Ibn Juzayy played an active editorial role, transmuting Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's oral accounts into sophisticated literary Arabic ornamented with rhymed prose (*sajʿ*), classical poetry, and formal rhetorical flourishes.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century textual scholarship revealed critical complexities in the narrative:
1. **Unacknowledged Literary Borrowings**: Scholars such as C. Defrémery, Ralph Elger, and Amikam Elad have shown that the text borrows descriptive passages from earlier travel writers to flesh out Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's narrative. Most notably, parts of the descriptions of Mecca, Medina, and Damascus follow the twelfth-century Andalusi traveler Ibn Jubayr (540–614 AH / 1145–1217 CE) closely, while other sections drew on earlier writers such as Muḥammad al-ʿAbdarī.
2. **Contested Routes**: Several itineraries—notably Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's claimed journey up the Volga River to Bolghar and the upper reaches of China to Beijing—contain chronological and geographical difficulties, leading some scholars to suspect they rest on hearsay rather than eyewitness experience.

## Historiographical Significance

Despite these rhetorical conventions and borrowed passages, modern historians regard the *Riḥla* as an important primary source for the fourteenth-century Afro-Eurasian ecumene. Ibn Baṭṭūṭa's observations illuminate the integration of the Islamic world (*Dār al-Islām*) through shared juridical, educational, and Sufi networks that allowed a trained Moroccan jurist to find employment, protection, and intellectual community from Mali to China. Furthermore, the text preserves an eyewitness account of the Black Death, which Ibn Baṭṭūṭa observed ravaging Cairo, Damascus, and Aleppo in 749 AH (1348 CE), making the *Riḥla* a significant witness to pre-modern global connectivity and crisis.
