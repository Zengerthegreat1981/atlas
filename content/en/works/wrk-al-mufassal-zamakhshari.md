# Al-Mufaṣṣal fī Ṣanʿat al-Iʿrāb

A concise classical grammatical text by Abu al-Qasim Mahmud ibn Umar al-Zamakhshari (467–538 AH / 1075–1144 CE). It is commonly dated to 513–515 AH / 1119–1121 CE, during his stay in Mecca, where he took the title Jar Allah. The work circulates under more than one title, including *al-Mufaṣṣal fī ṣanʿat al-iʿrāb* and *al-Mufaṣṣal fī ʿilm al-ʿarabiyya* (also *Kitāb al-Mufaṣṣal fī al-naḥw*). It is one of the most widely studied later Arabic grammars and received numerous commentaries, most notably the expanded commentary of Ibn Yaʿish (d. 643 AH / 1245 CE).

## Structure and Methodical Organization

The Mufassal rests on a logically sequential division of the chapters of grammar and morphology. Al-Zamakhshari divides the work into four parts: nouns, verbs, particles, and a final part on matters shared among the three (such as inflection-related phenomena, assimilation, substitution and changes of weak letters). Beneath each division he arranges the material methodically, moving from the general to the specific, and he treats syntax and morphology together in a single integrated order.

## Concision and Technical Formulation

Al-Zamakhshari's style in the Mufassal is marked by extreme brevity, sometimes approaching a condensed coded formulation. He states each grammatical rule in a concise phrase of precise technical terminology, avoiding digression on doctrinal disagreements between the Basrans and Kufans or on the multiplicity of opinions on a single question, as was common in some lengthier grammar books. This brevity made the Mufassal suitable for study in teaching circles, somewhat like the later versified *Alfiyya* of Ibn Malik, although the Mufassal remains in prose.

## Its Place Among Late Grammatical Texts

The Mufassal belongs to a generation of grammatical texts that appeared in the fifth and sixth centuries AH, seeking to summarize the accumulated body of earlier Arabic grammar (since Sibawayh) in a more organized and teachable form. It played a role similar to that later played by Ibn al-Hajib's *al-Kafiya* and Ibn Malik's *Alfiyya*; it preceded them chronologically, and Ibn al-Hajib's work is generally regarded as drawing on it. Some scholars have suggested that his Mu'tazili background, with its interest in rational classification, influenced the logical arrangement of the material, though this is an interpretation rather than an established fact.

## Commentaries on the Mufassal

Because of its extreme brevity, which makes it difficult for beginners to understand without an accompanying explanation, the Mufassal received considerable attention from commentators over the following centuries. The most prominent of them is Ibn Yaʿish (d. 643 AH / 1245 CE), with his expanded commentary *Sharḥ al-Mufaṣṣal*, which unpacked the concise phrases of the text, elaborated them, and discussed their grammatical causes in detail. This commentary became an independent grammatical reference in its own right and a standard companion to the text in later study.

## Its Relation to al-Zamakhshari's Broader Linguistic Project

The Mufassal represents the grammatical component of al-Zamakhshari's integrated linguistic project, which alongside it includes the semantic and rhetorical lexicon *Asas al-Balagha* (The Foundation of Eloquence) and the rhetorical Quranic exegesis *al-Kashshaf 'an Haqa'iq al-Tanzil* (The Unveiler of the Realities of Revelation). This combination of the three fields enabled al-Zamakhshari to apply his grammatical and morphological precision in the Mufassal in the service of his precise rhetorical analysis of Quranic textual constructions in the *Kashshaf*, so that his works form a clearly integrated methodological whole despite their differing apparent purposes.

## Limits and Criticism

As noted above, the Mufassal's extreme concision makes it difficult to understand directly without an accompanying commentary. Some later grammarians regarded this as a merit as well, since it requires analytical effort from the student. The author's Mu'tazili theology, expressed in his other works, did not visibly affect the purely grammatical content of the Mufassal.

## Impact on the History of Teaching Arabic Grammar

The Mufassal remained in use in Arabic grammar study circles for many centuries after its author's death and was transmitted through generations of students and commentators. It is studied today in the history of Arabic grammar as a prominent witness to the late maturity of the classical tradition.

## The Mufassal in Modern Orientalist Scholarship

The Mufassal was edited in the nineteenth century by Jens Peter Broch (Christiania, 1859) and has been studied by modern historians of Arabic grammar as an example of how late grammarians reorganized the accumulated heritage since Sibawayh into a concentrated, teachable form.
