# Imagining Religion: From Babylon to Jonestown

A collection of essays published by Jonathan Z. Smith in 1982, which became an influential work in critiquing the methodological foundations of the field of comparative religion from within. Smith opens the book with his most famous statement: that there is "no data for religion" and that religion is "solely the creation of the scholar's study." Smith does not deny the existence of the practices and beliefs that we classify as "religious." He insists, rather, that gathering them together under a single category called "religion" is a classificatory act performed by the researcher himself, not an objective ready-made truth awaiting field discovery.

## From Babylon to Jonestown: Widening the Scope as an Implicit Trial

The title of the book is itself a methodological program. The scope of the analysis extends from ancient Babylonian myths to the mass deaths at Jonestown in 1978 (the suicides and killings of followers of Jim Jones), in order to show that the tools of comparative religion, if used with explicit methodological control over the foundations of comparison, can handle recent extreme phenomena just as well as ancient religious texts. The condition is avoiding the "magic" that he warned against in his other essay "In Comparison a Magic Dwells" (see the debate file).

## The Impact: Shifting from "What Is Religion?" to "Who Classifies and Why?"

A major effect of the book was to push the field from a sometimes naive search for the "essence" of religion into a critical questioning of the academic classificatory process itself. It asks an epistemological question (who classifies? by what criteria? in whose interest?) rather than an ontological one (what is the nature of religion?). This shift later became the focus of an ongoing methodological debate in the field of religious theory and its methods.

## Intellectual and Historical Context

Smith wrote this work in the early 1980s, after years as a professor of the history of religions at the University of Chicago, where Mircea Eliade had been a leading figure. He developed a critical stance different from that of Eliade, drawing in part on anthropological work on classification (such as that of Émile Durkheim and Marcel Mauss, and of Mary Douglas). The book appeared in a broader context of growing methodological skepticism in Western universities toward the major inherited analytical categories (such as "civilization," "culture," and "religion") that seemed to conceal implicit Eurocentric or Christocentric assumptions.

## The Jonestown Essay in Detail

Smith devotes an essay ("The Devil in Mr. Jones") to the Jonestown massacre (Guyana, November 1978), in which more than nine hundred members of the "People's Temple" movement, led by Jim Jones, died by suicide or were killed. He uses the incident as a test of his theoretical tools: can the traditional concepts of comparative religion explain a recent extreme event of this kind as well as they explain ancient religious movements or "primitive" ones? The essay is usually read as insisting that comparison needs explicit methodological justification, and that the scholar's categories should not be mistaken for the self-understanding of the people studied.

## Wider Impact

The book is widely cited in comparative religious theory. Its opening statement about "no data for religion" influenced later generations of researchers who reformulated the question of defining religion as a question about power and classification rather than about essence. Talal Asad's *Genealogies of Religion* (1993), for example, pursues a related critique of "religion" as a universal category abstracted from its historical power context.

## The Style of Smith's Writing

Commentators observe that Smith's literary style contributed as much to the breadth of his influence as the content of his arguments. His essays are relatively short and dense in argument. They rely on deliberate intellectual provocation (such as the shocking opening statement) as a rhetorical tool to awaken the academic reader from prior assumptions held without examination. This feature also marked his later writings, such as *Drudgery Divine* (1990), which applied a similar critique to the historiography of the comparison between early Christianity and the religions of late antiquity.

## Smith's Intellectual Background

Jonathan Z. Smith (1938–2017) is known for a distinctive writing style that blends precise analysis with irony. He taught for decades at the University of Chicago and did not conduct anthropological fieldwork himself. He relied instead on a deep critical reading of the works of other researchers and ethnographers. This made him a central figure in critique of the field from within.

## The Other Essays

The remaining chapters apply the same programme to varied material. "Fences and Neighbors" treats early Judaism, questioning accounts built on a single essence and proposing a classification based on several characteristics that need not all be present together, with circumcision as a test case. "In Comparison a Magic Dwells" asks what rules could make comparison more than an impression of resemblance. "Sacred Persistence" redescribes canon as a deliberately closed list that then requires an interpreter whose ingenuity extends it to new situations. "A Pearl of Great Price and a Cargo of Yams" reads the Babylonian New Year festival alongside the Ceramese story of Hainuwele, treating each as thought provoked by a historical incongruity, such as foreign rule or foreign goods. "The Bare Facts of Ritual" uses northern bear-hunting rites to argue that ritual presents how things ought to be, in conscious tension with how they ordinarily are. "The Unknown God" examines the relation of myth to history.

## Defences of the Category

Not all scholars accept that the constructed character of "religion" disqualifies it as an analytic tool. Martin Riesebrodt, in *The Promise of Salvation* (German 2007; English 2010), defended a general theory of religion centred on practices that address superhuman powers, arguing that religious actors themselves recognised one another as engaged in comparable activities well before modern Western scholarship. Kevin Schilbrack, in "Religions: Are There Any?" (2010) and *Philosophy and the Study of Religions* (2014), argued from a critical realist position that a socially constructed category can still pick out real social structures, which exist whether or not a scholar names them. On this view the history of the term calls for careful and revisable definition, not abandonment.
