# Governing the Commons

**Elinor Ostrom** published *Governing the Commons: The Evolution of Institutions for Collective Action* in 1990. The book is a central text of the **Bloomington School** on common-pool resources. It was among the works associated with the award of the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences to Ostrom in 2009, shared with Oliver Williamson.

## A question that seemed theoretically settled

The book begins by confronting an assumption that had been entrenched in economics and political science since the biologist **Garrett Hardin**'s article "The Tragedy of the Commons" (1968). That assumption was widely read as holding that any natural resource used collectively, without clearly defined individual property rights, is inevitably doomed to over-exploitation. Each rational user maximizes his or her private gain from additional exploitation while ignoring its cost, which is spread across everyone. On this view, the only theoretically possible solutions are either nationalizing the resource and managing it centrally through the state, or dividing it into tradable, private individual property rights.

## Method: the comparative case study

Ostrom drew on a large body of documented field case studies from around the world of common resources managed over the long term. These included traditional irrigation systems in Spain (organized through old local water courts) and the Philippines, common forests in Switzerland and Japan governed by village customary rules, and local fisheries in many locations. They compared these cases with other fisheries and forests that had actually failed to avoid depletion, in search of the factor that distinguished success from failure.

## The eight design principles

From this comparison Ostrom derived a set of **"design principles"** that she found to be common to cases of sustained success. These include clearly defined boundaries that specify who has the right to use the resource and its scope; rules for use and allocation that fit local conditions and are set by the users themselves; collective-choice arrangements that allow most of those affected by the rules to take part in modifying them; mutual monitoring carried out by the users themselves at low cost; graduated sanctions that start light and become stricter with repeated violations; rapid and cheap local mechanisms for resolving disputes; recognition by external authorities of the local community's right to organize its own affairs; and finally, for large and complex resources, organization through nested layers of governance, from the smallest local unit to wider levels.

## A third alternative between state and market

She also noted that Hardin's model fits open-access resources rather than resources governed by an established community. Ostrom concluded that the assumed dichotomy between state control and individual private property does not cover all observed arrangements. Where the design principles are present, self-organized local institutions can manage a common-pool resource over long periods. She did not claim that they always outperform state or private arrangements.

## Impact and reception

The book became a standard reference in environmental economics, political science and related fields. Later writers have used its ideas in debates on digital commons and on the climate. It was among the works associated with the Nobel recognition of her research in 2009.

## Methodological rigour in case selection

In selecting her cases, Ostrom included failed cases alongside successful ones, rather than presenting only successes. This allowed a more credible assessment of the "design principles": do they appear in successful cases and tend to be absent in failed ones, or does success stem from other factors? Critics have nonetheless noted that the case sample was not randomly drawn, so the principles are best read as empirically grounded hypotheses rather than proven laws.

## Influence on later environmental policy design

The book's framework influenced policy discussion of how common resources, from fisheries to community forests, can be managed, and it strengthened the case for community-based governance as an alternative to uniform, centrally imposed solutions.

## Collective work behind a single name

Although the book carries Ostrom's name alone, it grew out of the collaborative research environment of the Workshop in Political Theory and Policy Analysis at Indiana University, which she co-founded with Vincent Ostrom. The Workshop and its associated researchers gathered and discussed field studies over many years before Ostrom formulated the synthesis presented in the book.
