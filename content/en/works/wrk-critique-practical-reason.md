# Critique of Practical Reason

Immanuel Kant published *Critique of Practical Reason* (*Kritik der praktischen Vernunft*) in 1788. It is the second of his three great critiques, after *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) and before *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790). The book aims to ground ethics on a purely rational foundation, independent of experience, inclinations, and interests. It builds on *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), which sought to identify and ground the supreme principle of morality.

## Structure and Central Argument

The book consists of an "Analytic" and a "Dialectic," on the model of the structure of *Critique of Pure Reason*. But Kant reverses the order of inference. Instead of proceeding from concepts toward principles, he starts from the "fact of reason" (Faktum der Vernunft), that is, from our direct awareness of the universal moral law as a binding imperative that admits no dispute, and from it he infers the possibility of freedom of the will. This is a methodological reversal of great importance. Whereas the *Critique of Pure Reason* failed to prove freedom theoretically and was content to show that it does not contradict natural determinism, Kant here reverses the direction: our awareness of the moral law is what establishes the reality of freedom practically. The book distinguishes between "pure practical reason," which legislates formal universal laws valid for every rational being, such as the Categorical Imperative, and "empirical practical reason," which follows inclinations and partial interests. Kant firmly rejects the grounding of ethics on the principle of happiness or utility. He holds that any ethics that makes the attainment of material good its aim is "heteronomous," that is, governed by a condition external to the will itself. This contrasts with "autonomous" ethics, in which the will legislates its own law for itself.

## The Highest Good and the Postulates of Practical Reason

Kant devotes an important part of the "Dialectic" to the concept of the "highest good" (das höchste Gut), the union of virtue with deserved happiness. Because this union cannot be guaranteed in the world of experience, which is governed by natural necessity, Kant infers three "postulates" (Postulates) of practical reason that must be assumed for the pursuit of the highest good to be rational: the immortality of the soul, since the realization of moral perfection requires an infinite progress that exceeds mortal life; freedom of the will, the condition without which moral obligation has no meaning; and the existence of God, the sole guarantor of the proportion of virtue to happiness in a just cosmic order. These postulates are not theoretical knowledge but a necessary rational faith (Rational Faith), required for the coherence of moral life.

## Historical Context

Interpreters commonly regard the book as reworking the argument of the third section of the *Groundwork*, whose attempt to deduce the authority of the moral law from the idea of freedom is widely thought to be problematic. In the second *Critique* Kant instead treats consciousness of the moral law as a "fact of reason" from which freedom is inferred, while the formal character of the moral law remained a defining feature of his project, and a target of objections. The book also criticizes the eudaemonist and empiricist moral theories that were prominent in the eighteenth century, which ground morality in happiness or moral feeling, and offers in their place a strict deontological alternative.

## Reception and Influence

The book became a cornerstone of modern deontological ethics and a point of departure for German idealists such as Fichte and Hegel. It also shaped the reformulation of Kantian ethics in John Rawls, who drew on the idea of the self-legislation of the will in his concept of the "original position," and in Christine Korsgaard, who rebuilt the theory of practical identity on the basis of Kantian autonomy. Yet the book was also subjected to a scathing critique by Hegel, who regarded the formality of the Kantian moral law as empty and incapable of generating concrete moral content, since the form of logical consistency can justify contradictory rules depending on the content placed within it. An illustrative example of Kant's argument: a person who refrains from lying not because they fear punishment or will lose their reputation, but because they recognize that lying cannot be a universal law they could will for all people without contradiction, is alone acting from pure respect for the moral law, not from calculating consequences.

## The Analytic and the Dialectic

Kant systematically divides the book into "Analytics," which examine the principles of pure practical reason itself, and "Dialectics," which examine the contradictions that arise when reason seeks to go beyond its legitimate limits. In the Analytic, Kant distinguishes between "matter" and "form" in the practical rule. He affirms that any rule determined by a particular material object, as the object of a particular desire, cannot be a universal law valid for every rational being, because objects and individuals' desires vary. The purely formal rule, that is, the form of universal legislation itself apart from any particular matter, is alone capable of being a universal moral law. This is translated into the formula of the Categorical Imperative: act only in such a way that you can at the same time will that the maxim of your will become a universal law. In the Dialectic, Kant shows how reason falls into an apparent contradiction when it tries to combine virtue and happiness within the framework of experience alone. This contradiction can be resolved only by assuming an intelligible world that transcends natural phenomena, which opens the way to the postulates of freedom, immortality, and the existence of God.
