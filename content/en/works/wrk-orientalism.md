# Orientalism

*Orientalism* (New York: Pantheon Books, 1978) is a book by Edward W. Said (1935-2003), a Palestinian-American professor of English and comparative literature at Columbia University. It examines how British, French and later American writers, scholars and officials produced knowledge about the "Orient", chiefly the Arab and Islamic Middle East, and how that knowledge was connected with political power over the region.

## The Argument

Said uses "Orientalism" in linked senses: the academic study of the East; a style of thought resting on a basic distinction between "the Orient" and "the Occident"; and the institutions and habits through which Europe made statements about the East, authorised views of it, governed it and ruled over it. His claim is that these form a discourse, in a sense drawn from Michel Foucault, which shaped what could plausibly be said about Eastern societies. He also draws on Antonio Gramsci's idea of cultural hegemony to explain how such views gained authority through consent as well as force.

Within this discourse the Orient tended to appear as uniform, unchanging and unable to represent itself, while Europe appeared as rational, developing and entitled to describe and administer it. Said distinguishes "latent" Orientalism, a relatively stable set of underlying assumptions and desires, from "manifest" Orientalism, the explicit and changing statements of particular writers and disciplines. Individual authors could differ in style and sympathy while sharing the underlying framework.

## Structure and Materials

An introduction is followed by three chapters. "The Scope of Orientalism" outlines the reach of the subject, including British imperial attitudes to Egypt and the scholarly apparatus that accompanied Napoleon's expedition. "Orientalist Structures and Restructures" discusses the modern philological and literary forms of Orientalism, with figures such as Silvestre de Sacy, Ernest Renan, Edward William Lane, Gustave Flaubert and Richard Burton. "Orientalism Now" follows the tradition into the twentieth century, including H. A. R. Gibb, Louis Massignon and the rise of American area studies and policy expertise. The method is the close reading of texts, related to their historical and institutional settings.

## Relevance to Sociology

For sociologists the book poses questions in the sociology of knowledge: who is authorised to describe a society, through which institutions, and with what consequences for those described. It informed criticism of modernisation theory and of accounts that explain Middle Eastern societies mainly by an essential culture or religion. It became a central reference for postcolonial studies.

## Debates

Critics including Bernard Lewis and Robert Irwin disputed Said's historical accuracy and selection of evidence, noting in particular the limited treatment of German scholarship. Sadik Jalal al-Azm, in "Orientalism and Orientalism in Reverse" (1981), argued that Said's account was inconsistent and warned against essentialist images of East and West being reproduced by Arab thinkers themselves. Aijaz Ahmad criticised its theoretical position from a Marxist standpoint. Others have asked how much room it leaves for resistance and for differences of gender. Said replied to critics in "Orientalism Reconsidered" (1985) and in an afterword to the 1994 edition.
