# Institutional Ethnography

Institutional ethnography is a method of sociological inquiry developed by the Canadian sociologist Dorothy E. Smith, first set out in her book *The Everyday World as Problematic: A Feminist Sociology* (1987). It is not a single book so much as a research programme: a way of doing sociology that begins from people's actual everyday experience and traces how that experience is organised by institutions and relations that stretch far beyond any one locality. Where mainstream sociology, in Smith's view, explained people's lives by fitting them into abstract categories, institutional ethnography asks how those lives are coordinated by texts, procedures and ruling relations — and it does so in order to give people usable knowledge about the forces that govern them.

## Historical Context and Publication

Smith's project grew out of the women's movement of the 1970s and her own experience as a single mother, graduate student and sociologist who found that the sociology she was taught had no place for women's lives. Her 1974 essay "Women's Perspective as a Radical Critique of Sociology" argued that sociology was written from the standpoint of men who governed society, and that women's everyday experience of housework, childcare and paid work was invisible to it. *The Everyday World as Problematic* (1987) turned that critique into a positive method. Smith drew on Marx, on phenomenology and on ethnomethodology, but the result was distinctively her own: a sociology *for* people, rather than *about* them, that starts from where people actually are.

## The Everyday World as Problematic

The method's starting point is the standpoint of people in their everyday and everynight worlds — the world as it is experienced directly, in a particular place and time. The "problematic" is the puzzle that arises when that experience does not fit the official accounts of it: when a mother's knowledge of her child conflicts with the school's assessment, or when a nurse's experience of care conflicts with hospital management's measures of efficiency. The researcher does not resolve this puzzle by theorising about it, but by investigating it: by following the threads that connect a local experience to the institutional processes that organise it. The everyday world is "problematic" precisely because it is organised by social relations that are not visible from within it.

## Ruling Relations and Texts

The central analytic concept is that of "ruling relations": the complex of institutions — government, law, medicine, education, management, the professions — that coordinate what people do across many local settings. Ruling relations work largely through texts. A form, a policy, a diagnostic manual, a curriculum or a computer record is a text that can travel: it is produced in one place, read in another, and it coordinates action in both. Institutional ethnographers study how texts organise work — how a teacher's grading, a social worker's case file or a doctor's referral links local action to extra-local decisions. The researcher's job is to map these connections, producing an account that shows people how their lives are put together by forces they cannot see from their own position alone.

## Method and Structure

Institutional ethnography is a method of inquiry rather than a theory to be tested. It begins with a standpoint — usually the standpoint of people whose experience is marginalised or subordinated — and proceeds by interviewing, observing and collecting the texts that organise the setting. The researcher then works outward, from the local site to the institutional processes that coordinate it, building a map of social relations. Smith's later works codified the method: *Texts, Facts, and Femininity* (1990), *The Conceptual Practices of Power* (1990), and above all *Institutional Ethnography: A Sociology for People* (2005), which set out the method in its mature form. Her final book, *Simply Institutional Ethnography* (2022), written with Alison Griffith, returned to the project as an introduction for new researchers.

## Reception and Influence

Institutional ethnography has had its widest influence in sociology, social work, nursing, education and health research, where it offers a way of studying institutions from the standpoint of those who use and work in them rather than those who manage them. It has been taken up in studies of health care, schooling, welfare administration and policing, and it has become a recognised tradition in feminist sociology and in the sociology of work. Its influence also extends to policy: institutional ethnographies of how services actually work have been used by practitioners and activists to challenge managerial accounts of their own institutions.

## Critical Assessments and Limitations

Critics have charged that institutional ethnography, for all its commitment to starting from experience, can be difficult to distinguish from conventional qualitative research, and that its maps of ruling relations sometimes remain descriptive rather than explanatory. Others argue that its suspicion of theory leaves it without the resources to explain why ruling relations take the forms they do — a question that requires the kind of structural analysis Smith deliberately set aside. Feminist critics have also debated whether the standpoint of women, as Smith originally formulated it, can be generalised to other subordinated positions without losing its specificity. Smith's defenders reply that these criticisms mistake the method's purpose: it is not a theory of society but a way of producing knowledge that people can use, and its value is measured by whether it makes hidden forms of power visible and therefore changeable.
