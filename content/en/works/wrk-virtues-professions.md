# The Virtues in the Professions

This topic addresses a central question in professional ethics: is the integrity of a physician, lawyer, teacher or judge reducible to adherence to written rules and codes of conduct, or does it require the cultivation of firmly established **personal virtues** (honesty, practical wisdom, courage and compassion) that an abstract rule cannot replace? The question represents a revival of the Aristotelian tradition of virtue ethics in the field of modern professions, as an alternative to the Kantian approach (duty) and the utilitarian approach (calculating outcomes), which dominated the teaching of professional ethics throughout the twentieth century.

## The Observed Shortcomings of the "Ethical Code" Model

Contemporary philosophers of professional ethics, such as Edmund Pellegrino, have observed that professional codes of conduct (such as the code of the American Medical Association or the bar associations' rules), although necessary, suffer from a structural shortcoming. They are often concerned with defining the minimum acceptable standard of behavior and preventing the worst violations (such as corruption or gross negligence), but they are unable to guide the agent in complex gray-area situations that require a fine evaluative judgment (such as balancing a physician's telling a terminally ill patient the full truth against attention to the patient's psychological state). Such situations are not resolved by applying a mechanical rule, but by exercising what Aristotle called **practical wisdom** (Phronesis): the capacity to perceive the concrete details of a particular situation and apply the correct principle to it flexibly.

## The Essential Virtues of Professional Practice

Theorists of this trend draw on **Alasdair MacIntyre**'s *After Virtue* (1981) and his concept of "practices" (Practices) that possess internal goods. They identify a set of virtues that distinguish honest professional practice from mere repetition of formal procedures. These include intellectual honesty (refusing to distort scientific truth to please a client or funder), professional courage (confronting pressure from the institution or the market when it conflicts with the interest of the beneficiary), temperance (keeping the desire for profit or fame within the bounds of professional service), and justice in distributing attention and resources among beneficiaries. MacIntyre links these virtues to the idea that every practice (such as medicine or law) has "internal goods" that are attained only through genuine excellence within it, as opposed to "external goods" such as money and status, which can corrupt the practice if they become its sole aim.

## The Islamic Heritage: The Craft Ethics and Professional Etiquette

Islamic jurisprudence and the Arabic ethical heritage possess a rich tradition in professional ethics that precedes this modern debate by centuries. It is embodied in the literature of *hisba* (market supervision), discussed by al-Mawardi and Ibn Taymiyya, the latter in his *Al-Hisba fi al-Islam*, and in books on the *etiquette of the physician*, such as the treatise *Adab al-Tabib* by Ishaq ibn Ali al-Ruhawi, which tied the fitness to practice medicine to personal virtues such as piety, compassion for the patient and self-restraint, not to technical skill alone. Al-Ghazali, in *The Revival of the Religious Sciences* (*Ihya' Ulum al-Din*, in the book on the etiquette of earning and livelihood), also set out a conception of the ethics of earning and trade that requires the merchant to be truthful and to avoid fraud and monopoly, as internal virtues rather than mere external legal constraints. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah*, linked mastery of crafts to firmly established dispositions (*malaka*) acquired through practice and training, a notion that invites comparison with MacIntyre's concept of "practice."

## Reception and Critique

This trend has contributed to renewed interest in ethical formation within medical and legal education, and has moved beyond merely teaching ethical codes as theoretical subjects separate from actual practice. However, its critics have pointed to the difficulty of measuring and assessing "virtue" in professional licensing systems, compared with the clarity of behavioral code standards that can be administratively enforced. They have also pointed to the danger that the discourse of virtue may turn into general slogans lacking practical mechanisms of accountability when it is violated.

## Field Application: The Judiciary and the Legal Profession

The virtue dimension appears with particular clarity in the judicial profession, where it is not enough for a judge to know the text of the law. The judge needs the virtue of **equity** (Epieikeia in Aristotle) which enables them to grasp the spirit of a rule when its literal text fails to achieve justice in an exceptional case that the legislator did not anticipate, as well as the virtue of psychological independence that protects them from pressures of public opinion or the executive authority. Similarly, the legal profession requires a delicate virtue-based balance between the duty to loyally defend the client's interest and the duty of integrity toward the court and the truth. This tension is not resolved by a rigid procedural rule but by practical wisdom rooted in the character of the lawyer themselves. This explains why bar associations have historically insisted on a reputation for good moral character (Good Moral Character) as a condition for admission to the profession, not technical competence alone.

## Virtue Facing Modern Institutional Pressures

This trend faces a growing contemporary challenge: the dominance of bureaucratic and commercial institutional logic over traditional professional practices. The decisions of a physician, for example, are subject to pressure from insurance companies and administrative efficiency standards that may conflict with their independent virtue-based judgment. According to proponents of this trend, this calls for protecting a sufficient space of professional autonomy in which personal virtue can exercise its real role, rather than reducing the professional to an automatic executor of institutional protocols imposed from outside the practice itself.
