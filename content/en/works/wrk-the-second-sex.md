# The Second Sex

*The Second Sex* (*Le Deuxième Sexe*) is a two-volume study of women's situation by the French philosopher and writer Simone de Beauvoir (1908-1986), published by Gallimard in 1949. It is a central text of twentieth-century feminist thought.

## The Question and the Thesis

Beauvoir asks what a woman is. Her answer is that man has been taken as the Subject and the norm, while woman has been defined in relation to him as the Other. Drawing on existentialism, phenomenology and Hegel's account of recognition, she argues that this position is produced historically and socially and is not fixed by nature. The second volume opens with her best-known sentence, "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman."

## Facts and Myths

The first volume examines what biology, psychoanalysis and historical materialism say about women and finds each insufficient as a complete explanation. It then offers a historical account of women's subordination and analyses myths of femininity, including their treatment by particular male authors.

## Lived Experience

The second volume follows women's lives through childhood, adolescence, sexual initiation, lesbianism, marriage, motherhood, social life, maturity and old age. It uses memoirs, literature, psychoanalytic case material and observation to show how femininity is learned and lived. Beauvoir contrasts "immanence", confinement to repetition and the maintenance of life, with "transcendence", the pursuit of freely chosen projects. She links women's liberation to economic independence through work, while holding that work alone is not enough without wider social change.

## Reception and Translation

The book caused controversy in France, particularly for its discussion of sexuality, motherhood and abortion, and the Vatican placed it on its Index of Prohibited Books. Howard M. Parshley's English translation of 1953 cut a considerable part of the text and blurred some philosophical vocabulary, as later scholars, notably Margaret Simons, showed. A complete translation by Constance Borde and Sheila Malovany-Chevallier appeared in 2009.

## Influence and Criticism

The book informed second-wave feminism and later work on the social construction of gender, the family, sexuality and the body. Critics have argued that Beauvoir presents motherhood and the female body in largely negative terms, that she adopts a masculine model of transcendence, and that her comparisons between women, Black people and Jews, together with her focus on particular European experiences, give too little attention to women who belong to several subordinated groups at once. Other scholars defend the book's account of ambiguity and embodiment as more complex than these readings allow.
