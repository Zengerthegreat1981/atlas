# Lisān al-ʿArab

A comprehensive encyclopedic Arabic dictionary by Ibn Manẓūr (Muḥammad ibn Mukarram, 630–711 AH / 1232–1311 or 1312 CE). It is generally said to have been completed in 689 AH / 1290 CE. It is the best-known large classical Arabic dictionary in common use. *Tāj al-ʿarūs* of Murtaḍā al-Zabīdī is larger. Ibn Manẓūr compiled the work mainly from five earlier lexical sources.

## The Five Sources of Compilation

Ibn Manzur did not create his dictionary from nothing. He explicitly relied on five major earlier lexical sources: *Tahdhīb al-Lugha* by Abu Mansur al-Azhari; *al-Muhkam wa al-Muhīṭ al-Aʿẓam* by Ibn Sida; *al-Ṣiḥāḥ Tāj al-Lugha wa Ṣiḥāḥ al-ʿArabiyya* by al-Jawhari; *Ḥāshiyat Ibn Barrī ʿalā al-Ṣiḥāḥ* (corrections and critical glosses on al-Sihah); and *al-Nihāya fī Gharīb al-Ḥadīth wa al-Athar* by Ibn al-Athir. Ibn Manzur gathered the material of these five sources, largely preserving their texts as they stood, sometimes with references indicating the source of each quotation. The result is closer to comprehensive encyclopedic archiving than to independent authorship.

## Arrangement by Rhymes

Ibn Manzur arranged his material according to the "rhyme" system (qawafi), that is, by the last letter of the root rather than the first letter as in other dictionaries. This system was common in some older Arabic dictionaries, such as al-Sihah and al-ʿUbāb, to facilitate particular purposes such as checking poetic rhymes. It is less convenient for the modern researcher, who is accustomed to direct alphabetical order by first letter.

## Size and Comprehensiveness

Printed length depends on the edition. The Bulaq edition (1300–1308 AH / 1883–1890 CE) is in twenty volumes; the Beirut Dār Ṣādir edition (1955–1956) is in fifteen. The dictionary does not limit itself to a single gloss. It cites poetic, Qur'anic and hadith evidence, and it often includes grammatical, morphological and dialectal remarks under the root.

## The Archival Value of the Traditional Arabic Dictionary

The great value of *Lisān al-ʿArab* lies in having preserved an enormous body of lexical material that could otherwise have vanished or become difficult to access. Some of the sources Ibn Manzur relied upon survive only in few manuscripts or were long hard to access, so *Lisān al-ʿArab* serves as an important intermediary preserving their material.

## Impact on Modern Arabic Lexicography

*Lisān al-ʿArab* is one of the principal reference sources for classical Arabic vocabulary and is consulted by compilers of later and modern dictionaries. It also remains an indispensable reference in editing classical Arabic texts and in research on historical semantics and derivation.

## Methodological Limitation

*Lisān al-ʿArab* is criticized for its excessive length, and for occasionally repeating a single item in several slightly different forms. This happens because it transmitted material from more than one of the five sources without removing the resulting duplication. Its near-total reliance on earlier sources also makes it difficult in many places to distinguish any independent addition by Ibn Manzur from material transmitted verbatim. This limits its value as an original "composition," compared with its major value as a comprehensive work that preserves the earlier lexical heritage.

## Ibn Manzur's Motives for Compilation

Ibn Manẓūr lived in the seventh and eighth centuries AH. He worked in Mamluk Egypt and as a judge in Tripoli, and he died in Cairo. The period produced large encyclopedic compilations in several fields, among them al-Nuwayrī's historical encyclopedia and, later, al-Qalqashandī's chancery manual. In the introduction to the *Lisān*, Ibn Manẓūr presents the book as a gathering of earlier dictionaries into one work, so that their material would remain accessible.

## Method of Transmission and Attribution Between Sources

Ibn Manzur frequently gives an explicit indication of the source of each quotation he presents under a single root. He distinguishes between what he transmitted from al-Azhari and what he transmitted from Ibn Sida, al-Jawhari, Ibn Barri or Ibn al-Athir, although this distinction is not achieved with complete regularity throughout the book. This attributive method gives *Lisān al-ʿArab* additional value as a record. It allows the evolution of the interpretation of a particular root meaning to be traced across successive generations of linguists, rather than serving merely as a source for a single final meaning cut off from the history of its formation.

## Its Importance in Editing Classical Texts

Scholars who edit classical Arabic texts frequently consult *Lisān al-ʿArab* when they encounter an obscure or rarely used word in an old manuscript. It offers abundant citations and rare usages that may not be available in more recent and more abridged dictionaries. This makes it a working tool as well as a historical reference, and it has been printed in many editions.

## Its Relation to Later Dictionaries such as Tāj al-ʿArūs

In the twelfth century AH, al-Sayyid Murtada al-Zabidi (d. 1205 AH / 1791 CE) continued the tradition of large-scale compilation in his dictionary *Tāj al-ʿArūs min Jawāhir al-Qāmūs*, a very large commentary on al-Firuzabadi's *al-Qamus al-Muhit*, drawing among many other sources on *Lisān al-ʿArab*. The two works reflect the continuity of the tradition of comprehensive lexicographic compilation across successive centuries.
