# Writing Culture: The Poetics and Politics of Ethnography

***Writing Culture: The Poetics and Politics of Ethnography***, edited by **James Clifford** and **George E. Marcus** and published in **1986**, is the book that forced anthropology to confront the fact that ethnographies are written — that they are made, not found. Its argument was simple and unsettling: an ethnography is not a transparent window onto another society but a text, composed according to literary conventions, shaped by the author's position, and entangled with the history of colonialism. The book grew out of a 1984 seminar at the School of American Research in Santa Fe, and its ten chapters — by Clifford, Marcus, Talal Asad, Renato Rosaldo, Stephen Tyler, Paul Rabinow, Mary Louise Pratt, and others — became the founding texts of the reflexive and postmodern turn in anthropology.

The context was a discipline in crisis. By the early 1980s, anthropologists were increasingly aware that the classic ethnographies — Malinowski in the Trobriands, Evans-Pritchard among the Nuer, Geertz in Bali — had been written from a position of colonial authority, presenting other peoples as timeless objects of study while erasing the conditions of fieldwork, the role of interpreters and informants, and the violence of empire. At the same time, literary theory, feminism, and postcolonial thought were challenging the idea that any text could represent reality transparently. *Writing Culture* brought these pressures to bear on ethnography itself.

## The Argument: Ethnography as Writing

The book's central claim is that ethnographic authority — the right of the anthropologist to speak for another people — is a literary and political achievement, not a natural given. In the classic monograph, authority was produced by devices that the book names and dissects: the ethnographic present, which describes a society as if it existed outside history; the disappearance of the author from the text, which creates an impression of impersonal objectivity; and the use of native voices as evidence while the anthropologist retains the power to interpret them. Clifford's introduction argues that ethnography is always a partial truth — partial in both senses of the word, incomplete and committed to a point of view — and that acknowledging this partiality is the precondition of honest work.

The individual chapters develop this argument in different directions. Talal Asad examines the unequal relationship between the anthropologist and the people studied, arguing that the power to translate another culture into the language of the academy is itself a form of domination. Mary Louise Pratt analyses the travel writing and exploration literature from which ethnography descended, showing how the figure of the "anti-conquest" traveller claimed innocence while participating in empire. Renato Rosaldo, drawing on his fieldwork among the Ilongot of the Philippines, shows how the ethnographer's own emotions — grief, anger, loss — shape what can be seen and said, and how the classic monograph's impersonal tone conceals this. Stephen Tyler pushes the argument furthest, proposing a "post-modern ethnography" that would abandon representation altogether in favour of evocative, poetic, and dialogical texts.

## Key Concepts

Several concepts from the book entered the anthropological vocabulary. **Ethnographic authority** names the problem the book diagnoses: how the writer claims the right to represent others. **Partial truth** names its proposed stance: an ethnography that knows itself to be incomplete and positioned. **Dialogical ethnography** names one alternative: a text in which the voices of the people studied are not merely quoted but allowed to shape the argument, and in which disagreement and misunderstanding are preserved rather than smoothed away. The book also popularised the idea of **culture as text**, extending Geertz's interpretive programme in a reflexive direction: if cultures are texts to be read, then the reading itself must be examined.

Marcus's own contribution looked forward as well as back. He argued that the critique of ethnographic writing should lead not to paralysis but to new forms of fieldwork and writing: multi-sited ethnography, which follows people, objects, and ideas across borders rather than studying a single bounded community; and experimental ethnographies that make their own construction visible. This programme shaped Marcus's later work and much of the anthropology of globalisation in the 1990s.

## Reception and Influence

*Writing Culture* became one of the most cited and most contested books in the history of anthropology. Its supporters credited it with opening the discipline to questions of power, representation, and voice that could no longer be ignored, and with legitimating experimental writing, collaborative ethnography, and the study of anthropology's own history. Its critics — among them Marvin Harris, who attacked it as an abandonment of science, and many fieldworkers who felt that the critique undervalued the knowledge ethnography actually produces — argued that it encouraged navel-gazing at the expense of research, and that its literary focus distracted from the material realities of the people studied.

The debate it launched has never been resolved, but its effects are visible everywhere in contemporary anthropology. Ethnographies now routinely include reflexive passages, acknowledge their informants by name, and discuss the conditions of fieldwork; collaborative and co-authored ethnographies are common; and the history of the discipline is studied as part of the discipline itself. The book's limits are also widely recognised: its authors were mostly American men, its examples were drawn largely from classic ethnography, and it said little about the anthropologies being produced outside the Anglophone world. Later work in feminist, indigenous, and decolonial anthropology has taken the critique further, insisting that the question is not only how ethnographies are written but who gets to write them. *Writing Culture* remains, however, the book that made that question unavoidable.
