# Suhrawardi's Philosophy of Illumination (Hikmat al-Ishraq)

The text in which Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi (549–587 AH / 1154–1191 CE) proclaimed his Illuminationist doctrine independent of Peripateticism, and regarded as the founding work of Illuminationism in the history of Islamic philosophy. He wrote it in Aleppo late in his life, and closed it with an indication that he composed it in a short time, and in it he addresses the "seeker of truth" who has combined contemplation and research and who does not entrust wisdom to the clever alone.

## The Central Thesis

The text in which Shihab al-Din al-Suhrawardi sets up a system alternative to Peripateticism: being is not categories, matters and forms but degrees of light that differ in intensity and weakness, not in quiddity, and their principle is the **Light of Lights**. In knowledge Suhrawardi sets against Peripatetic demonstration **knowledge by presence**: the soul's apprehension of itself and of what it illuminates by presential illumination without an intermediate form. Between them he places the **world of images** as an intermediate world between the abstract and the sensible, to which visions, unveilings and suspended images are referred. The book also formulates a conception of the sapiential heritage: for him Illumination is an eternal wisdom that begins with Hermes and passes through the Greek sages and the ancient Persian sages (the "Khosrawaniyun") and ends with him, so he does not claim an innovation but a renewal of an ancient wisdom that rests on taste and demonstration together. The natural species in the book are explained by the "lords of the species", which are incorporeal dominating lights (anwār qāhirah) corresponding to the Platonic Forms, and which govern each of the material species.

## Structure of the Work

Two parts. The first part is on the "rules of thought" (logic), with three discourses, and its pillar is the critique of the Aristotelian definition by the "limit": the complete definition requires knowledge of all the essentials, and whoever does not know them in advance does not reach them by definition, so definition for Suhrawardi is circular and yields no new knowledge. The second part, "On the Divine Lights", has five discourses, moving from the proof of the Light of Lights to the dominating lights and the governing lights, then to the soul, the return, and the suspended images. The structure itself is an argument: the logical critique precedes and prepares for the luminous ontology. Among his concepts is the basic division of existents into "light", "ghasaq" (the dark substance, that is, body or barzakh) and "dark configuration", and the relation among the lights is governed by the relations of "domination" and "love": the higher dominates what is below it, and the lower loves the higher.

## Historical Context of Publication

The text was written and completed in 582 AH / 1186 CE (Suhrawardi records in its conclusion that he completed it in a few months), that is, about five years before he was killed in Aleppo in 587 AH / 1191 CE by order of the Sultan Saladin after the jurists of Aleppo accused him of corruption of belief, while he was under the protection of al-Malik al-Zahir Ghazi. It is later than the **Talwihat Lawhiyya wa-Arshiyya**; the difference between them is the difference between two stages: the Talwihat works within the Peripatetic framework and discusses it in its vocabulary, whereas the *Hikmat al-Ishraq* is the text in which the Illuminationist system is proclaimed independent.

## Reception and Critique

A whole school arose on commenting on the text: **Shams al-Din al-Shahrazuri** (d. after 687 AH / 1288 CE), author of the first major commentary on it, then **Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi** (d. 710 AH / 1311 CE), whose commentary is the most widely used in teaching, then the glosses of **Mulla Sadra al-Shirazi** (d. 1050 AH / 1640 CE) on Qutb al-Din's commentary, which read the lights from the standpoint of the primacy of existence. The role of the commentators, al-Shahrazuri especially, is the organizing and arranging of the text, whereas the Illuminationist claims themselves (the Light of Lights, knowledge by presence, the world of images) are Suhrawardi's.

In modern reading the reception divided: **Henry Corbin** (the 1952 edition and his later works) read the book as theosophy and a text of the imaginal world, whereas **Hossein Ziai** in *Knowledge and Illumination* (1990) replied that the center of the book is its logical and epistemological part and that reading it as a gnostic text alone discards its most important argument. **John Walbridge** in *The Leaven of the Ancients* (2000) discussed Suhrawardi's claim of connecting his wisdom to the Greek and ancient Persian sages and the limits of its historical validity. Among the criticisms bearing on the text: that the concept of light is a metaphor whose meaning shifts between the physical and the metaphysical without sufficient control, that the arguments for the Light of Lights are less cohesive than his logical critique, and that the connection to the mythical Persian heritage is hard to establish historically. The Peripatetics and the Illuminationists disputed the question of knowledge by presence.

## Language, Editions, and Translations

The text is Arabic in origin, and its major commentaries (al-Shahrazuri, Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi, and Mulla Sadra's glosses) are Arabic as well. The standard critical edition is Corbin's (1952), and the English translation with facing Arabic text is that of Walbridge and Ziai (1999); Corbin also translated parts of the work into French.

## Importance and Place in Islamic Philosophy

It is the founding text of the school of **Illuminationism**, from which the concepts of the Light of Lights, knowledge by presence and the world of images are traced back, and it is one of the two parties of the debate over Peripatetic demonstration versus Illuminationist taste. Its influence remained extended in Iranian philosophy through Mir Damad and Mulla Sadra to the contemporary seminaries.
