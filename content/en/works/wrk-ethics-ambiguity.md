# The Ethics of Ambiguity (Pour une morale de l'ambiguïté)

*Pour une morale de l'ambiguïté* (1947), by Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986), is an important but often underrated text in standard accounts of existentialism, and it addresses a common criticism of it: that existentialism lacks an ethics. Published in Paris by Gallimard in 1947, and translated into English by Bernard Frechtman in 1948, it is a major ethical work of French existentialism. Beauvoir extends and develops Sartre's philosophy in *Being and Nothingness* (1943), but it is her own attempt to build the positive existential ethics that Sartre's book lacks. Sartre described the ontological structure of freedom; Beauvoir asks how we are to live by it, ethically, with others. The book was written in the aftermath of the Second World War and the Occupation, when she and Sartre were preoccupied with political and moral commitment, and two years before her best-known work, *The Second Sex* (1949), which applied this book's theoretical tools to the situation of women in particular.

## The Thesis: Freedom Is Not Private Property

Freedom, Beauvoir argues, is ambiguous rather than absolute. The human being is at once a free subject and a constrained object, and my freedom is not realised in isolation but only interwoven with the freedom of those around me. Ethics, for Beauvoir, is the responsibility for this ambiguity, not flight from it through a false decisiveness. Neither the slogan "you are always free" nor an external determinism is adequate. Willing freedom for myself necessarily involves willing freedom for others, because freedom finds its meaning only when it is exercised in a world of free people — and therefore oppression is a negation of freedom itself, not a difference of values. The central concept is *situation*: the body, sex, class, and history constrain freedom in fact, not only in theory, so that an abstract declaration of absolute freedom is inaccurate even within the existential framework itself. In the final section Beauvoir considers the oppressed — the child, the slave, the colonised person — who lack the instruments of freedom and cannot be held responsible for a situation they did not choose.

## The Structure of the Book

The book falls into three parts: "Ambiguity and Freedom", "Personal Freedom and Others", and "The Positive Aspect of Ambiguity". Its method is to trace a series of false attitudes towards freedom, each a flight from confronting ambiguity. The sub-man avoids freedom through conformity. The serious man dissolves into ready-made, absolute values. The nihilist denies the world because it disappointed his hope for absolute values. The adventurer loves action for its own sake, without purpose. The passionate man — Beauvoir's "man in love" — makes another person the absolute centre of his existence. Against all of these, Beauvoir builds a positive ethics founded on commitment to the freedom of others as the condition of my own freedom. The book is in dialogue with Hegel, Kierkegaard, and Marx as much as with Sartre: Beauvoir criticises Hegel's vision of the end of history as a final resolution that dissolves ambiguity, and takes from Kierkegaard his insistence on the concrete individual while refusing to isolate that individual from others and from the world.

## Context and Development

The book continues *Pyrrhus et Cinéas* (1944), Beauvoir's earlier essay on the relation between individual action and human purposes, and it applies the same questions at a larger scale. Its historical moment matters: written after the war, it is a work of a philosopher who had lived through occupation and resistance and who refused to treat ethics as an abstract exercise. The argument that freedom must be willed for others as well as for oneself is not a pious addition to Sartre's ontology but the point on which the book turns — it is what converts an analysis of freedom into an ethics, and it is also what makes the book political. The oppressor who denies others' freedom denies the condition of his own, and the struggle against oppression is therefore not external to existential ethics but required by it.

## Reception and Importance

The book's presence in standard accounts of existentialism is far smaller than its actual influence — a pattern worth recording rather than merely correcting. The strongest criticism of existential therapy, that it is individualistic and blind to power, has a ready answer in this text, yet the answer is rarely used in that therapeutic context because the book itself is rarely read there. Feminist and philosophical scholarship has restored its standing as an ethical text independent of Sartre, notably Kristana Arp's *The Bonds of Freedom* (2001), which reads it as a work of ethics in its own right. Critics have objected that the demand to will the freedom of others does not show clearly why a person should be bound by it, and that the concept of ambiguity wavers between an ontological description and a normative principle. These objections are live ones, but they are objections to a serious ethical position, not signs of a minor work. French existentialism reached Arab readers in the 1950s through the Beirut journal *Al-Adab*, with its idea of literary commitment, and through Abd al-Rahman Badawi's studies of existential philosophy.
