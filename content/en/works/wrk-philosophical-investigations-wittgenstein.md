# Philosophical Investigations by Ludwig Wittgenstein

## The central thesis

The book is a radical revision that Wittgenstein carries out on Wittgenstein himself: he rejects what he presented in the "Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus" (1921), that language works by the logic of the "picture", that is, that it depicts facts through a structural isomorphism with them. In its place he holds that **the meaning of a word in many cases is its use** in the context of a shared life, not its mental image or its logical referent (paragraph 43). Language for him is a collection of multiple **"language games"** (Sprachspiele) and not a single system, which resemble one another by "family resemblance" (Family Resemblance) and not by a common essence, and they are rooted in the **"forms of life"** that people share. He does not see philosophy as a theory that adds knowledge but as a "therapy" that dissolves confusions that arise when "language goes on holiday", deluding the reader with puzzles that do not exist.

## The structure of the work

It consists of two parts. The first is about 693 numbered paragraphs, which Wittgenstein brought to a near-final form around 1945, and it opens with a passage from Augustine's "Confessions" on learning to speak in order to present the "Augustinian picture of language": that every word names something. It then takes up the primitive builders' language, ostensive definition, language games, family resemblance, the idea of understanding and the rule (about 185–242) and the dilemma of rule-following, then the **private language argument** (243–315) and the example of the "beetle in the box" (293), in which he refutes the idea that inner states such as pain can be named in a language understood by the speaker alone, since there is no criterion of correctness for the use of a sign in the absence of a public use open to correction. As for the second part, which was named in the fourth edition "Philosophy of Psychology: A Fragment", it consists of shorter remarks written between 1946 and 1949 on perception, seeing-as (the duck-rabbit example) and psychological states.

The book does not advance in a linear deductive order but in an intermittent dialogical style, which raises an objection and replies to it and then moves on, a deliberate style that embodies the thesis of the book: that philosophical understanding is therapy by successive examples and not the building of a system.

## Historical context of publication

It was written mainly between 1936 and 1949 in Cambridge and in his Norwegian hut, and Wittgenstein did not publish it in his lifetime (he died in 1951). The preface carries the date January 1945, in which he says he decided to publish his new thoughts together with the old so that they might shed light on one another. He entrusted his literary estate to his students **Elizabeth Anscombe**, **Rush Rhees** and **G. H. von Wright**, and it was edited by Anscombe and Rhees and published in 1953 by Basil Blackwell with the German text and an English translation by Anscombe.

Subsequent editorial scholarship, culminating in the critical investigation of the Nachlass and the fourth revised edition (2009) edited by P. M. S. Hacker and Joachim Schulte, detailed the discrepancies between Wittgenstein's late manuscripts and the 1953 editio princeps. Anscombe and Rhees had assembled Part I from Wittgenstein's typescript TS 227 (dating from 1945–1946), while creating "Part II" out of a separate, unrevised manuscript (MS 144, written between 1946 and 1949) that Wittgenstein had never integrated into TS 227 nor designated as an integral second part of the *Investigations*. Schulte and Hacker revised the text and apparatus and retitled the material previously known as Part II as "Philosophy of Psychology – A Fragment" (PPF) to reflect its status as an autonomous, unfinished investigation. Ray Monk in his biography "The Duty of Genius" (1990) describes how Wittgenstein returned to philosophy after the years of teaching in the villages of Austria and building his sister's house in Vienna, and how he reconsidered the "Tractatus".

## Reception and critique

The book formed, together with Oxford philosophy, Ryle and Austin, what became known as "ordinary language philosophy", although that school developed partly independently of it. It provoked a deep interpretive debate over the rule-following argument: **Saul Kripke** presented in *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) a skeptical reading known as "Kripkenstein", which has remained a matter of dispute and not of consensus. G. P. Baker and P. M. S. Hacker presented an extended analytical commentary on the book (1980–1990), and saw in it a critique of the traditional philosophy of meaning. Commentators divided between a traditional reading that takes the book to be an alternative philosophical theory, and a "therapeutic" reading associated with Cora Diamond and James Conant (whose "resolute" approach was first developed for the *Tractatus*) that sees in it a rejection of the very idea of philosophical theory. A. J. Ayer criticized the private language argument in 1954, saying that Robinson Crusoe can build a private language.

## The Arab reader

The Arab reader knew the book under the title "Tahqiqat falsafiyya", translated by Abd al-Razzaq Bannour and published by the Arab Organization for Translation in Beirut in 2007. Arab interest in Wittgenstein's early "Tractatus" preceded it. The influence of Wittgenstein and ordinary language philosophy appears in the work of the Moroccan thinker Taha Abdurrahman, who exploits language games and pragmatics in "al-Lisan wa-l-mizan" (1998) to build a concept of reason tied to Arab and Islamic usage, although he does not adopt Wittgenstein's method but employs it in another project. Scholars compare the making of meaning into use with the interest of the Arab scholars of the principles of jurisprudence in signification in the context of discourse and custom.

## Limits of the work

Critics fault the book for not offering a theory of meaning in the strict sense but forbidding every theory, so that the question of how correct use is determined is left without an answer, and for the fact that the phrase "meaning is use" is itself qualified by the reservation "in many cases", so it loses its power to decide. The intermittent style of the book makes its interpretation subject to constant dispute. Moreover the private language argument, for its critics, may ignore the difference between an impossible private language and subjective experience that can be described in a public language. From the scientific side, some psychologists and linguists held that reducing meaning to use neglects the role of innate mental structures.
