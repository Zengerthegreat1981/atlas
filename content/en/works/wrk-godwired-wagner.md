# Godwired: Religion, Ritual and Virtual Reality

A book by Rachel Wagner, a scholar of religious studies at Ithaca College in New York, published by Routledge in 2012. It is an early and widely cited work on the relationship between ritual and digital media within religion and digital culture studies, exploring structural parallels between traditional religious ritual and digital interaction, especially video games and virtual worlds.

## Definition

The book belongs to the field of religion and digital media studies, in which scholars of religion moved beyond the descriptive question of how religious institutions use the internet toward questions about how digital media themselves shape ritual, meaning and the sense of the sacred. Wagner argues that digital space does not merely host religion from outside but can reproduce ritual-like structures (rules, bounded spaces, repeated action) in new forms that deserve independent analysis.

## Historical Context

The book appeared after the spread of online games and virtual worlds and amid growing academic debate about digital media and religious authority. It followed Heidi Campbell's *When Religion Meets New Media* (2010), which examined how religious communities negotiate new technologies; Wagner moves the discussion toward the structural analysis of ritual and play.

## Main Ideas and Method

Wagner draws on theories of ritual and play, including Victor Turner's work on liminality (*The Ritual Process*, 1969), to compare worship with digital play in formal terms: rule-governed action, clear boundaries of space and time, and the temporary suspension of everyday reality. She stresses that this parallel concerns structure and function, not doctrinal content. It does not mean that religious and digital rituals have equal value for participants; it offers an analytical tool for understanding both without presupposing the authenticity of one and the falsity of the other.

## Impact and Critique

The book broadened the field's focus from institutional negotiation of technology to the structure of ritual in digital settings and is cited in work on religion and games. Some critics note that the parallel between ritual and play can understate the difference between binding religious commitment and voluntary recreation.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The book does not treat Islam directly or at length. Its questions about sacred text, digital mediation and ritual have parallels in contemporary Muslim debates about Qur'an applications and electronic recitation, but these are not discussed in the book itself.

## Games and Virtual Worlds

Wagner treats virtual reality broadly, as digital technology that people engage through screen-based software, so her examples extend well beyond immersive environments. According to Stephen Okey's review, they range from online worlds such as Second Life to transmedia franchises such as *Avatar* and *Star Wars*, and to flash mobs and digital prayer walls. Second Life is a prominent case. Drawing on Victor Turner's account of liminality, Wagner reads logging in and rendering an avatar as a ritualised entry: newcomers cross a threshold as novices before taking part in the world. The review describes later chapters that take up the virtual sacred in relation to Mircea Eliade's distinction between sacred and profane (chapter four), identity (chapter five) and community (chapter six). The discussion of community considers how religious groups have experimented with online evangelism and religious gaming, and reaches into virtual worlds and games. The examples serve a comparative argument about structure, so they should not be read as a full ethnography of any single game or its players.
