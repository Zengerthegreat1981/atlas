# Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism

**Vladimir Lenin** wrote this pamphlet in 1916, during the First World War, and it was published in full in 1917. Its aim was direct and political: to explain the ongoing war in economic structural terms, and to refute the position of the European socialist parties that had supported their national governments in the war as a just defensive war.

## Theoretical sources: Hobson and Hilferding, and the dispute with Luxemburg

Lenin explicitly built his analysis on two earlier works. The first is the British liberal economist **John Atkinson Hobson**'s *Imperialism: A Study* (1902), which linked colonial expansion to surplus savings and weak domestic consumption. Lenin drew on it for descriptive and statistical material while rejecting its reformist conclusion. The second is **Rudolf Hilferding**'s analysis of finance capital, the merging of banks with industry into monopolistic blocs. From it Lenin took the theoretical framework of the "financial oligarchy", although he attacked Hilferding's political positions. As for **Rosa Luxemburg**, Lenin did not adopt the thesis of her *The Accumulation of Capital* (1913), that capitalism structurally needs non-capitalist markets to realize surplus value, and he regarded her reading of Marx's reproduction schemes as mistaken; the pamphlet itself does not engage her work at length. The convergence between them on the critique of imperialism was thus more political than theoretical. The work also devoted a large part of its polemic to the theory of **Karl Kautsky** on "ultra-imperialism", which predicted a peaceful understanding among international monopolies.

## The five features of the imperialist stage

Lenin identifies five structural economic features that he considers defining of the new imperialist stage. First, the concentration of production and capital to the point of creating monopolies that play a decisive role in economic life. Second, the merging of banking and industrial capital into a single "financial oligarchy". Third, the exceptional and growing importance of the export of capital (not only of goods, as in the stage of free competition). Fourth, the formation of international monopolistic capitalist alliances that divide the world markets among themselves. Fifth, the completion of the colonial territorial division of the world among the major capitalist powers.

## The war as an inevitable outcome, not an incidental event

Lenin uses this framework to explain the First World War in direct structural terms. Since the world had become entirely divided among the major colonial powers (Britain, France and Germany), any desire by a rising power (such as Germany) to expand its share of colonies and markets could be realized only through a **redivision** of a world already fully divided. In this sense, the war is not a passing political mistake of certain leaders, but an expected structural outcome of the logic of monopolistic capitalist expansion itself.

## Direct political impact

The work's influence went beyond the theoretical field into direct political practice. Lenin and his Bolshevik comrades used it as a theoretical justification for their position against supporting the war. Later it became an analytical framework for understanding the position of Russia and of colonized countries within the global capitalist system. This gave the work immense political and ideological weight in national liberation and anti-colonial movements throughout the twentieth century in Asia, Africa and Latin America.

## The enduring legacy in dependency theories

Together with the works of Hilferding and Luxemburg, the pamphlet entered the set of Marxist texts later used by writers on dependency and uneven development. Samir Amin engaged Lenin's account directly. Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems analysis drew on a wider set of sources, including this tradition, rather than on this pamphlet alone.

## Conditions of writing during the war and censorship

Lenin wrote this work in 1916 while in exile in Switzerland. He had to deal with the tsarist censorship imposed on Russian publications during the war. In the preface to the 1917 edition he explains that he therefore wrote in guarded language and avoided saying some things openly, although the political content was strongly opposed to the tsarist government and the war it was waging.

## An important subtitle: "a popular outline"

The work carries a descriptive subtitle in Russian that can be translated as "a popular outline" (Popular Outline), which makes clear Lenin's declared intention: to offer a concentrated political simplification of more complex economic analyses developed by others (above all Hobson and Hilferding). It was addressed to a broad worker and party audience, not to academic specialists. This direct political character accounts for its relatively simple argument compared with the complexity of the original texts on which it draws.
