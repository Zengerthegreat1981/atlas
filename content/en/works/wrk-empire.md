# Empire of Time: Calendars, Clocks and Cultures

***Empires of Time: Calendars, Clocks, and Cultures***, by the anthropologist and astronomer **Anthony F. Aveni**, was first published in **1989** and reissued in a revised paperback edition in **2000**. Its subject is one of the most basic and most overlooked facts of human life: that different cultures keep time differently, and that the way a society measures time shapes how its people think, work, worship, and remember. Aveni, a leading figure in archaeoastronomy at Colgate University, takes the reader on a comparative journey across the calendars of the Maya, the Aztecs, the Chinese, the Greeks, and the modern West, and argues that time is not a neutral background to human life but a cultural construction — an empire that each civilisation builds for itself.

The book's starting point is a simple observation. The modern Western calendar feels natural: twelve months, seven-day weeks, hours of sixty minutes, years counted from the birth of Christ. But this system is a historical achievement, the product of centuries of astronomical observation, religious authority, and political decision. Other societies solved the problem of time differently, and their solutions reveal different ideas about what time is. For the Maya, time was a burden carried by gods; for the Chinese, it was a cycle of dynastic fortune; for the Greeks, it was the measure of human life against the movements of the stars. Aveni's aim is to recover these alternative times and to show that our own is one possibility among many.

## Sensing and Marking Time

The book is organised in three parts. The first, "Sensing and Marking Time," examines how human beings perceive time through the rhythms of the body and the sky: the day and night, the phases of the moon, the seasons, the cycles of birth and death. Aveni shows that all calendars begin from these natural rhythms, but that every culture selects, emphasises, and combines them differently. The prehistoric bone markings of Africa, the stone circles of Neolithic Europe, and the temple alignments of the Maya are all attempts to fix the rhythms of the heavens in durable form, and each attempt reflects the priorities of the society that made it.

The second part, "The Calendar and the Clock," traces the history of the Western time system from the Greek pastoral calendar through the Julian and Gregorian reforms to the mechanical clock and the modern standardisation of time. Aveni shows how the calendar was repeatedly remade by political and religious authority: Julius Caesar's reform of 45 BCE, Pope Gregory XIII's correction of 1582, and the adoption of Greenwich Mean Time in the nineteenth century were all acts of power as well as of astronomy. The mechanical clock, invented in medieval monasteries to regulate prayer, became the symbol of a new attitude toward time — time as a commodity to be saved, spent, and wasted, an attitude that reached its fullest expression in industrial capitalism.

## Maya, Chinese, and Western Time

The third part of the book is its comparative core. Aveni devotes extended chapters to the Maya and Aztec calendars, drawing on his own research in Mesoamerican archaeoastronomy. The Maya, he shows, maintained several interlocking calendars at once: a 260-day ritual calendar, a 365-day solar calendar, and a Long Count that measured time from a mythical creation date. These calendars were not merely practical tools; they were the framework within which history, prophecy, and kingship were understood. A Maya king's authority depended on his ability to read the calendars and to perform the rituals they prescribed, and the great inscriptions of the Classic Maya are, in large part, records of time itself.

The Chinese calendar offers a different model. Organised around the sixty-year cycle of heavenly stems and earthly branches, and tied to the Mandate of Heaven, it made the emperor the regulator of time: a new dynasty proclaimed a new calendar, and the calendar's accuracy was proof of the dynasty's legitimacy. Aveni contrasts these cyclical systems with the linear time of the modern West, in which time moves forward from a beginning toward an end, and progress is measured by accumulation. He does not argue that one system is superior; he argues that each is adapted to the society that produced it, and that understanding a culture's time is a key to understanding its worldview.

## Reception and Influence

*Empires of Time* was praised by reviewers — *Nature* called it one of the best books on a scientific theme for the serious general reader — and it became a standard introduction to the anthropology of time. It has been widely used in courses on archaeoastronomy, the history of science, and cultural anthropology, and it helped to establish the comparative study of time as a recognised field. Its influence can be seen in later work on the social construction of time, including Eviatar Zerubavel's studies of hidden rhythms and the sociology of time, and in the growing literature on indigenous calendars and timekeeping.

The book has also been read critically. Some scholars have noted that Aveni's comparisons sometimes flatten the differences between the societies he discusses, and that his account of "Western" time underplays the diversity of time practices within modern societies. Others have pointed out that the book says little about the politics of time in the contemporary world: the standardisation of global time, the time discipline of the workplace, and the struggle over working hours. These are fair qualifications, but they do not diminish the book's achievement: a lucid, wide-ranging, and genuinely comparative account of how human beings have made time, and how time has made them.
