# The Concept of Anxiety: Freedom, Dread, and the Possibility of Sin

*The Concept of Anxiety* (1844) by Søren Kierkegaard is an early and influential analysis of anxiety as an existential condition rather than merely an emotion. Its full Danish title announces its ambition: *The Concept of Anxiety: A Simple Psychologically Orienting Deliberation on the Dogmatic Problem of Hereditary Sin*. Written under the pseudonym Vigilius Haufniensis — "the watchman of Copenhagen" — it treats anxiety not as a symptom to be cured but as the psychological state that makes sin possible in the first place.

## The Central Claim: Anxiety Is Not Fear

Kierkegaard's founding distinction is between fear and anxiety. Fear has an object: something definite that threatens. Anxiety falls upon "nothing" — upon possibility itself. It appears before freedom, not before danger. Adam in Eden, before the Fall, is not afraid of anything; he is anxious because the divine prohibition awakens in him the knowledge that he *can* choose, without knowing what he can choose. Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom, and Kierkegaard's famous image captures it exactly: the dizziness of a person looking down into an abyss comes not from the abyss alone but from the awareness that he could throw himself into it. Anxiety is therefore an effect of freedom, not of threat, and it precedes sin rather than following it. Sin enters the world by a qualitative leap that no psychological condition can explain or compel; anxiety is only the state that makes the leap possible.

## Structure: Five Chapters and a Theological Frame

The book opens with an introduction and proceeds through five chapters: anxiety as explaining hereditary sin; anxiety as explaining the advance of sin in the individual; anxiety as the consequence of sin, which is the absence of the consciousness of sin; the anxiety of sin, or anxiety as the consequence of sin in the individual; and anxiety as that which saves through faith. Kierkegaard insists from the outset that hereditary sin is not a question for psychology at all. Psychology can only offer a deliberation that illuminates the way toward the dogma without replacing it. The book builds its analysis on a definition of the human being as a synthesis of body and soul, held together by spirit; anxiety appears when spirit relates to its own synthesis. In innocence the self is at peace with itself, yet that peace carries within it a "nothing" that generates anxiety. The prohibition in Genesis awakens possibility: Adam knows he "can" without knowing what he can do. Kierkegaard also introduces the moment (*Øieblikket*), the point where the eternal and the temporal intersect and faith becomes possible, and analyses the "demonic" — closed-off inwardness and anxiety before the good — in terms that anticipate later descriptions of avoidance and withdrawal.

## The Argument with Hegel and Psychology

The book takes a critical position against Hegel and against the Danish school that treated sin as a necessary logical stage in the development of spirit. Kierkegaard insists that ethics begins where logic ends: sin cannot be derived as a necessity. He also refuses psychological reduction, making anxiety a "schooling" through which the individual must pass in order to learn faith — whoever has learned to be anxious in the right way, he writes, has learned the most that can be learned. He maps varieties of anxiety across forms of consciousness: anxiety before the sexual, anxiety before death, anxiety before history, making awareness of finitude the key to freedom. The language is dense, drawing on Hegelian and theological terminology, and much common misunderstanding comes from reading the book as modern psychology. The book's anxiety is not a psychological disorder but a structure of freedom. Walter Lowrie's 1944 translation rendered it *The Concept of Dread*, a title that preserves the older, heavier resonance of the Danish *Angest*.

## Reception and Influence

The book's direct influence runs through Heidegger, who took up existential anxiety in *Being and Time*, and Sartre, for whom anxiety before the infinite freedom of choice is central. What travelled, above all, was the distinction between anxiety and fear and the idea that anxiety is not an illness but a basic structure of free existence — two ideas that became central in existential therapy. What did not travel was the full theological frame of original sin: a significant concession, and one rarely announced in later therapeutic readings. The book's limits are those of its age: its view of sexuality and femininity carries the judgments of its time, and its analysis presupposes a Danish Lutheran Christianity that defines the frames of sin and redemption. Read on its own terms, however, it remains a key source for the later claim that anxiety discloses something true about the human condition rather than merely malfunctioning within it.
