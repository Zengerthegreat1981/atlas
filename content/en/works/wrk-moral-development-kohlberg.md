# Moral Development

## Context and Background

The American psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg developed his theory of "stages of moral development" beginning with his doctoral dissertation at the University of Chicago in 1958. He then gathered his mature thinking in his principal volume, *The Philosophy of Moral Development: Moral Stages and the Idea of Justice*, published in 1981 by Harper & Row, which was the first volume of his *Essays on Moral Development*. Kohlberg built his work on a foundation laid by Jean Piaget in his study *The Moral Judgment of the Child* (1932), but he expanded it radically. Whereas Piaget described broadly two phases of moral thinking in childhood, Kohlberg designed a longitudinal method in which he tracked the responses of the same sample of boys (about seventy-five, from the Chicago area) over decades. He used hypothetical "moral dilemmas" designed specifically to reveal the structure of moral reasoning rather than its content. The best known of these is the "Heinz dilemma," which asks whether a poor man may steal an expensive drug to save the life of his dying wife.

## The Central Thesis: Six Stages at Three Levels

The core of the theory rests on an idea derived from Piaget: that what matters in moral judgment is not "what" an individual decides to do in a dilemma (steal the drug or not), but "why," that is, the structure of reasoning behind the decision. On this basis Kohlberg formulated six stages distributed across three levels, which he claimed follow a fixed sequence that cannot be skipped. The first level, "Preconventional," includes Stage 1, in which right is judged by avoiding direct punishment, and Stage 2, in which it is judged by achieving reciprocal personal benefit ("you serve me and I serve you"). The second level, "Conventional," includes Stage 3, in which right is measured by the approval of the group ("the good boy/good girl"), and Stage 4, in which it is measured by maintaining the social order and the law itself. The third level, "Postconventional," includes Stage 5, in which law is viewed as a social contract that can be amended to serve the common good, and Stage 6, the highest and rarest, in which moral judgment becomes independent of any positive law and rests on abstract universal moral principles such as justice and equality in human dignity, in the manner conceived by Kant and Rawls.

## Method and the Concept of Justice

Kohlberg drew his philosophical framework from John Rawls's theory of justice. He held that the highest stages of moral development are those in which the individual makes decisions from behind a hypothetical "veil of ignorance," that is, by stripping themselves of their particular interests in order to judge what serves universal justice. Kohlberg designed a standardized test known as the "Moral Judgment Interview" (MJI) to classify individuals' responses precisely into one of the six stages, based on the structure of their justifications rather than on the action they choose. He repeatedly emphasized that two people may make the same decision (for example, to steal the drug) from two entirely different stages of reasoning.

## Reception and the Major Critique: Gilligan

The theory faced its most important critique from his Harvard colleague Carol Gilligan in her seminal book *In a Different Voice* (1982), which showed that Kohlberg's original sample was entirely male, and that applying his scale to girls and women often placed them in "lower" stages (particularly Stage 3), not because they were less morally mature, but because their moral thinking tends to center on "the ethics of care" and concrete relationships rather than the abstract "ethics of justice" that Kohlberg's scale favored from the outset. This critique contributed to the development of "the ethics of care." Kohlberg's later work also qualified the scheme: Stage 6 was eventually treated as a theoretical endpoint rather than a stage regularly found in his data. Other criticisms were also directed at the theory: the empirical link between advanced moral reasoning and actual behavior is weak (one may articulate a sophisticated philosophical justification of ethics while acting selfishly), and the claim that the stages are universal across cultures carries an individualistic Western bias that does not suit societies that value collective harmony more than independent moral reasoning.

## Enduring Impact

Despite these fundamental criticisms, Kohlberg's theory remains one of the most influential frameworks in the history of moral psychology. It established the applied field of "moral education" through school programs that use the discussion of moral dilemmas to push students to advance one step beyond their current stage. This educational principle is still used today, despite the declining academic confidence in the rigidity of the stages themselves.

## The Heinz Dilemma as an Analytical Model

The famous "Heinz dilemma" illustrates Kohlberg's method precisely. A poor man named Heinz cannot afford a drug, discovered by a local pharmacist, that could save the life of his wife, who suffers from a serious illness. The pharmacist refuses to lower the price or to defer payment. Should Heinz steal the drug? According to Kohlberg, the answer itself reveals nothing about the individual's moral stage. Someone who says "yes, he should steal it, because he needs his wife and she would do the same for him" reasons at a level of reciprocal personal interest (Stage 2). By contrast, someone who says "yes, he should steal it, because a person's right to life is higher than the right to private property" thinks in the logic of Stage 6 (universal principles), even though the behavioral decision is identical in both cases. This precise distinction between the "content" of a decision and the "structure" of its justification is what gives Kohlberg's method its unique analytical value in the history of moral psychology.
