# An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change

**Richard Nelson** and **Sidney Winter** published their joint book *An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change* in 1982, after about a decade of research collaboration. In it they set out a systematic account of what became known as evolutionary economics. They took up Joseph Schumpeter's emphasis on innovation as a driver of economic change, and set it in a formal framework offered as an alternative to the standard neoclassical model of firm equilibrium.

## Critiquing the Assumption of Maximal Rationality of the Firm

The book starts from an explicit critique of a basic assumption in neoclassical theory of the firm: that the firm always calculates the optimal mathematical decision that maximises its profits, with complete or near-complete information about all possible alternatives. Nelson and Winter argue that this assumption fits poorly with what is observed about actual firm behaviour. Their alternative draws on Herbert Simon's work on bounded rationality and on behavioural theories of the firm (such as Cyert and March's).

## The Routine as an Economic Gene

The central concept of the book is the **"routine"**: a relatively stable, repetitive pattern of organisational behaviour that a firm follows in familiar situations (the method of producing a particular product, procedures for hiring and pricing). Routines accumulate through the firm's past experience and are transmitted within it with a stability that is functionally similar to the stability of genes in a living organism. Firms for the most part act according to these routines almost automatically, rather than through a comprehensive recalculation at every moment of decision.

## Mutation and Selection in the Market

The authors adopt an additional explicit biological analogy. **Innovation** arises as a "mutation": an accidental deviation or deliberate experiment away from the usual routine, whether it results from an internal attempt at improvement or from imitating the practice of a competing firm. This deviation then undergoes a competitive **selection** process in the market. Firms that adopt more profitable routines grow, while those that retain relatively less efficient routines shrink or exit the market. This functionally parallels Darwinian natural selection, without assuming that any individual firm calculated the optimal outcome in advance with superhuman rationality.

## Computer Simulation as an Analytical Tool

The book introduced an important methodological innovation: the use of **computer simulation models** to trace how an entire industry evolves over time as multiple firms interact, each carrying different routines and differing capacities for innovation and imitation. The simulations, including growth models of Schumpeterian competition, showed that aggregate patterns such as productivity growth, together with persistent differences among firms, can emerge from this evolutionary dynamic without assuming that firms or markets are always in equilibrium.

## Impact on research on innovation

The book became a principal reference for research in the economics of innovation, technological change and growth. Its influence extended into strategic management, notably in the literature on "dynamic capabilities", and into the study of "national innovation systems", which Nelson later developed as editor of a comparative volume (1993).

## The Fruit of a Multi-Year Research Collaboration

The book was the culmination of a collaboration between Nelson and Winter that began in the early 1970s, during which they published a series of joint articles developing components of the model (the concept of the routine, the mechanism of selection, the logic of simulation) before bringing them together in a single synthesis.

## A Mixed Reception Among Economists

On its appearance the book received a mixed reception within the mainstream academic economics of the time. Researchers interested in technical change and innovation welcomed it as a major contribution. Economists more committed to the standard neoclassical model were sceptical of abandoning the assumption of full rationality, arguing that the standard model, despite its simplifications, remains a useful predictive tool in many contexts.

## A reference point for a research field

The book contributed to a research field that acquired its own journal, the *Journal of Evolutionary Economics* (founded in 1991), and scholarly associations, including the International Schumpeter Society (founded in 1986).
