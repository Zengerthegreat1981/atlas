# Steven Hayes's Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (1999)

The first complete presentation of **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)**: *Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: An Experiential Approach to Behavior Change*, by **Steven C. Hayes**, **Kirk D. Strosahl**, and **Kelly G. Wilson** (The Guilford Press, 1999). It has three authors, and attributing it to Hayes alone is a recurring error.

## The central thesis

That **the systematic attempt to get rid of painful thoughts and feelings is the problem, not the solution**. Suffering, in the book's presentation, is not an exceptional malfunction but a natural result of the structure of language (the authors' "destructive normality"): because thought is symbolic and relational, the sufferer ruminates on his pain and projects it onto every situation. Hence **experiential avoidance** becomes a common factor among many disorders. The alternative is not correcting thoughts but changing one's **relationship** to them: accepting what cannot be changed, and moving toward one's values while the pain remains.

## Structure of the work

- **The theoretical foundation**: functional contextualism and the relational account of language as the ground of the therapy, followed by an analysis of experiential avoidance and "cognitive fusion".
- **The clinical wing**: a progression that begins with **"creative hopelessness"** (stopping the agenda of control after showing its failure), then "control is the problem", then **willingness and acceptance**, then **cognitive defusion**, then **the self as context**, then **clarifying values**, and finally **committed action**.
- **The style**: **experiential**, as its subtitle says: exercise, metaphor, and paradox rather than logical argument, with an arsenal of metaphors that became a hallmark of the school (such as "the man in the hole" and "the chessboard"), followed by closing chapters on the therapeutic relationship and the pitfalls of application.

## What is not in this book

- **Relational Frame Theory (RFT)** is its theoretical basis, but its detailed elaboration is not in it: its book is *Relational Frame Theory* by Hayes, Barnes-Holmes, and Roche (2001), two years after the 1999 book.
- **The "Hexaflex" diagram and the central place of "psychological flexibility"** belong to a **later** formulation (especially the article by Hayes, Luoma, Bond, Masuda, and Lillis in *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 2006, and the 2012 second edition); the processes of 1999 are present in substance but not in this diagram.
- **The term "third wave"** was popularized by Hayes in a 2004 article in *Behavior Therapy*, not in this book.

## The historical context of publication

The therapy had a precursor called **"comprehensive distancing"** that Hayes worked with in the 1980s, and the project remained for about two decades in behavior-analytic journals before being gathered in the 1999 book directed at practitioners. The **second edition appeared in 2012** with a different subtitle, *The Process and Practice of Mindful Change*, a reformulation in the vocabulary of psychological flexibility, so it cannot be cited as though it were the first.

## Reception and criticism

The model spread quickly to anxiety, depression, chronic pain, and addiction, and a scientific society (ACBS) grew up around it. The serious criticism centers on two matters:

- **The quality of the evidence**: a review by **Lars-Göran Öst** (*Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 2008) found third-wave trials, ACT among them, methodologically inferior to those of cognitive behavioral therapy, and judged that ACT had not reached the threshold of a "well-established" treatment.
- **Is it actually new?**: a dispute over whether it differs fundamentally from cognitive behavioral therapy or renames elements within it, raised explicitly by **Hofmann and Asmundson** (*Clinical Psychology Review*, 2008) under the title "new wave or old hat?".

## Why it is included in the Atlas

Because it is the turning point by which the aim of therapy shifted from **changing the content of thought** to **changing one's relationship to it**, and from it branched widely used terms (acceptance, defusion, values). Its presence here makes it possible to trace each term back to the book that actually introduced it.
