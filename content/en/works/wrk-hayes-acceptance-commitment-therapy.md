# Steven Hayes's Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (1999)

*Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: An Experiential Approach to Behavior Change* (The Guilford Press, 1999) is the foundational text of **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)**, co-authored by **Steven C. Hayes**, **Kirk D. Strosahl**, and **Kelly G. Wilson**. Attributing the volume solely to Hayes is a common oversight; the book brought together behavioral philosophy, primary-care clinical pragmatism, and experiential psychotherapy.

## The Central Thesis

The central thesis is that **experiential avoidance — the systematic effort to avoid, suppress, or control unpleasant private experiences (thoughts, feelings, bodily sensations) — is a primary driver of psychological suffering**. Drawing on radical behaviorism and functional contextualism, the authors argue for "destructive normality": suffering is not an anomalous biological malfunction, but a predictable consequence of the bidirectional, symbolic nature of human language and cognition. 

Because human thought operates relationally, individuals chronically relive past pain and project hypothetical future catastrophes, becoming trapped in "cognitive fusion" (treating thoughts as literal realities). ACT posits that the solution is not attempting to dispute, suppress, or modify the content of thoughts (as in traditional cognitive therapy), but radically **changing one's relationship to thoughts and feelings** through acceptance and mindfulness, redirecting energy toward values-based committed action.

## Structure and Core Processes

The manual blends clinical philosophy with practical exercises, structured around a treatment sequence involving processes such as the following (the later hexaflex formalizes six core processes):
1. **Creative Hopelessness**: Helping the client recognize that experiential control strategies have functioned as the problem rather than the solution ("control is the problem").
2. **Acceptance and Willingness**: Actively allowing private experiences to come and go without fighting or fleeing them.
3. **Cognitive Defusion**: Techniques designed to reduce the literal, dominant grip of language (such as repeating a painful thought rapidly until it sounds like meaningless acoustic sound).
4. **Contact with the Present Moment**: Fostering non-judgmental, ongoing mindfulness of immediate internal and external events.
5. **Self-as-Context**: Cultivating an awareness of the "observing self" as the context in which private events occur, distinct from the thoughts and emotions themselves.
6. **Values and Committed Action**: Identifying deeply held personal values and building concrete, recurring patterns of values-consistent behavior.

The clinical pedagogy relies heavily on experiential metaphors that became trademarks of ACT, such as the "man in the hole," the "chessboard," and the "passengers on the bus."

## The Co-Authors: Strosahl and Wilson

Although the theoretical model developed within Hayes's laboratory, the 1999 manual was a collaborative synthesis:
- **Kirk D. Strosahl**: A clinical psychologist specializing in community health and primary care medicine. Strosahl championed the operationalization of ACT into brief, scalable clinical interventions, later pioneering Focused Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (FACT).
- **Kelly G. Wilson**: A clinical psychologist who emphasized the deeply experiential, emotional, and values-centered dimensions of the therapy. Wilson led the development of experiential metaphors and values assessment instruments, including the Valued Living Questionnaire (VLQ).

## Historical Context and Later Developments

The treatment evolved from an earlier protocol named **"comprehensive distancing"** developed by Hayes in the 1980s. Key elements developed subsequently include:
- **Relational Frame Theory (RFT)**: The comprehensive post-Skinnerian account of human language underlying ACT was fully detailed two years later in *Relational Frame Theory* (Hayes, Barnes-Holmes, & Roche, 2001).
- **The "Hexaflex" Diagram**: The unified graphic model of psychological flexibility was formalized by Hayes, Luoma, Bond, Masuda, and Lillis in *Behaviour Research and Therapy* (2006) and in the 2012 second edition (*The Process and Practice of Mindful Change*).
- **"Third Wave" Classification**: The concept of a third wave of behavioral therapy was introduced by Hayes in an influential 2004 article in *Behavior Therapy*.

## Reception and Criticism

ACT stimulated widespread international interest, leading to the formation of the Association for Contextual Behavioral Science (ACBS) and extensive clinical trials across chronic pain, anxiety, depression, and addiction. 

Major criticisms have focused on two issues:
- **Evidence Quality and Specificity**: A prominent systematic review by **Lars-Göran Öst** (*Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 2008) argued that early third-wave trials exhibited lower methodological rigor than standard CBT and questioned whether ACT had satisfied the criteria for a firmly established treatment.
- **Novelty and Relation to CBT**: In a review titled "Acceptance and mindfulness-based therapy: New wave or old hat?", **Stefan G. Hofmann and Gordon J. G. Asmundson** (2008) compared ACT with cognitive-behavioral therapy through emotion-regulation research. They argued that, despite fundamental differences in philosophical foundation, the two target different stages of emotion generation (antecedent-focused and response-focused regulation) and that ACT techniques are fully compatible with CBT.

## Why It Is Included in the Atlas

The 1999 volume represents the founding manual of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, marking the paradigm shift in contemporary behavior therapy from content-focused cognitive modification to relational mindfulness, cognitive defusion, and values-based behavioral action.
