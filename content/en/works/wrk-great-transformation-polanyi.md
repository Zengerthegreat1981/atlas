# The Great Transformation

A book published by Karl Polanyi in 1944, regarded as one of the most important founding works of critical economic sociology, offering a radical historical analysis of the rise of the modern "free" market.

## Historical Context

Karl Polanyi, an economist and social historian of Hungarian origin, wrote this work in the early 1940s while living in the United States, and published it in 1944, in the midst of the Second World War and after the collapse of the European liberal economic order in the interwar period (the Great Depression, the rise of fascism and Nazism). Polanyi sought to understand how the self-regulating market system of the nineteenth century led to the catastrophic collapse it witnessed in the first half of the twentieth century, drawing on economic history and on studies of non-market economies.

## The Central Thesis

Polanyi argued that "free" markets independent of social, moral and religious control are not an eternal natural given, but a relatively recent historical invention that arose in industrial Britain in the nineteenth century. In all historically earlier societies, economic exchange was "embedded" (Embedded) in wider social relations that regulated it (reciprocity among relatives and neighbors, central redistribution through a political or religious authority, and householding), not the abstract mechanism of supply and demand detached from any social context as classical economic theory assumes. Polanyi argued that industrial capitalism's attempt to "disembed" the economy completely from social control, by turning land, labor and money into "fictitious commodities" (Fictitious Commodities) bought and sold in complete freedom like any ordinary commodity although they were not originally produced for sale (land is part of nature, not a manufactured commodity; labor is human activity itself, not something separate from the human being; and money is a symbolic medium of exchange, not a commodity), inevitably produces destructive social disintegration (mass unemployment, peasant displacement, societal collapse). This necessarily calls forth a "counter-movement" (Double Movement) from the state and society to re-restrict the market and protect the social fabric from its destructive effects, such as labor unions, factory legislation and later the welfare state. Polanyi saw the rise of fascism itself as a catastrophic form of this "counter-movement" when liberal democracy fails to provide sufficient social protection by peaceful means.

## Influence and Criticism

This book offered a deep theoretical and historical foundation for a critique of neoclassical economics' assumptions about the naturalness of the free market. It deeply influenced the later founding of the field of "new economic sociology" by Mark Granovetter, who borrowed the concept of "embeddedness" and developed it in a partly different sense (micro-level embeddedness in personal networks rather than macro-historical embeddedness). It continues as a basic reference in contemporary debates about economic globalization, the welfare state and the social regulation of markets. It has been criticised for an implicit optimism about the possibility of re-embedding the economy, for a one-sided explanation of the rise of fascism, and for its account of nineteenth-century Britain, which some economic historians regard as overstating the self-regulating character of markets. The book was widely rediscovered after the global financial crisis of 2008, when commentators drew on the notion of fictitious commodities to discuss the commodification of land, labour and money, and it is widely taught in courses on political economy and economic sociology.
