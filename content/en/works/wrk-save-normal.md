# Saving Normal

**Full title:** *Saving Normal: An Insider's Revolt Against Out-of-Control Psychiatric Diagnosis, DSM-5, Big Pharma, and the Medicalization of Ordinary Life*
**Author:** Allen Frances
**Year of publication:** 2013 (William Morrow)
**Original language:** English

## Context

The book was written by Allen Frances, Emeritus Professor of Psychiatry and former chair of the Department of Psychiatry and Behavioral Sciences at Duke University, who had himself chaired the task force for the **Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV)**, published in 1994. It was published in 2013, in the same year as **DSM-5**, at a moment when Frances himself was one of the most prominent critics of the new edition, which had been overseen by David Kupfer.

## Ordinary Sorrow in the Arab Heritage

The book's title relates to an old question: when does sorrow become normal, and when does it become an illness? In his treatise on dispelling sorrows (*Fi al-Hila li-Daf' al-Ahzan*), the philosopher Ya'qub ibn Ishaq al-Kindi treated sorrow as a psychological pain that arises from the loss of a beloved person or the disappearance of possessions. He held that it is not inevitable and can be alleviated through reason and through recognizing that what is lost was never permanently one's own. Ritter and Walzer published this treatise in 1938. Miskawayh devoted a chapter in *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* to treating sorrow, grief, and fear, treating them as ailments of the soul to be addressed through reflection and moral self-discipline rather than with medication. In Arabic medicine, intense and persistent sorrow that overwhelmed its sufferer fell under melancholia, which required treatment. This is close to Frances's thesis: ordinary sorrow is an understandable human response, and the dividing line between it and illness requires careful clinical judgment rather than a mechanical checklist of symptoms, although the philosophical frameworks of the two sides differ considerably.

## Central Thesis

The book is built on two main themes: **diagnostic inflation** and **overmedication**. Frances argues that American psychiatry, driven by the expansion of diagnostic criteria, by the pressures of major pharmaceutical companies ("Big Pharma"), and by a medical culture that "medicalizes" growing aspects of ordinary daily life, has increasingly classified large numbers of "normal worried" people (the worried well) as psychiatric patients, exposing them to unnecessary and possibly harmful drug treatments. Frances specifically revisits his critique of DSM-5, warning that it would aggravate diagnostic inflation. He cites his own experience with DSM-IV, which he believes unintentionally led to false "diagnostic epidemics" in attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, autism, and bipolar disorder in children.

## Structure and Arguments

The book traces the history of the classification of mental disorders from DSM-I to DSM-5, showing how the boundaries of "mental illness" widened with each new edition. Frances offers practical criteria for distinguishing ordinary psychological distress that does not require medical intervention from disorder that requires actual treatment, and calls for more cautious and conservative diagnosis, particularly in child and adolescent psychiatry.

He draws on evidence including the finding of the US National Comorbidity Survey that about a quarter of US adults met criteria for a DSM-IV disorder in a 12-month period (Kessler and colleagues, 2005), which he argues reflects the breadth of the criteria more than a pathological prevalence. He notes CDC parent surveys in which roughly one in ten American children had at some point received an attention-deficit/hyperactivity diagnosis, and he attributes part of the rise to marketing of medication and to broadened criteria. The surveys count reported diagnoses, not a clinical re-examination of each child.

He devotes chapters to the DSM-5 proposals that he considers the most dangerous: removing the bereavement exclusion from the diagnosis of major depression, so that grief after the death of a relative becomes an illness after two weeks; lowering the threshold for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder in adults to five symptoms while raising the age of symptom onset to twelve; adding binge eating disorder, disruptive mood dysregulation disorder in children, and mild neurocognitive disorder; and proposing "attenuated psychosis syndrome," which was ultimately moved to the section of conditions for further study. He argues that each of these adds millions of people to the circle of illness, and that the new names are promoted by the industry before the evidence settles them.

## Reception and Impact

The book sparked wide debate in American clinical and media circles as a critique "from the inside," issued by the psychiatrist who had chaired the DSM-IV task force. Around the time of the book and his earlier articles, Frances became involved in a public dispute with the leadership of the American Psychiatric Association and the DSM-5 task force. The book remains a widely cited insider critique of diagnostic expansion and of ties between psychiatry and the pharmaceutical industry. It is a polemic with notes, not a systematic review.

## Limits and Criticism

Kupfer and Regier argue that the book exaggerates the scope of the DSM-5 proposals and ignores the processes of public review and field trials, and that removing the bereavement exclusion does not mean that every grieving person is diagnosed, since the manual calls for clinical judgment and provides a note clarifying the difference between grief and depression. Other critics observe that Frances speaks of the authority of evidence while many of his arguments about the effect of the manual on diagnostic rates are inferential, and that diagnostic inflation is a phenomenon fed by health insurance, schools, and marketing rather than the text of the manual alone. The book is also addressed to the general reader and carries a polemical tone, and it may underestimate the number of cases that go undiagnosed and untreated. This entry is educational and not clinical advice. The book's basic idea, that caution in diagnosis is necessary, is nonetheless widely shared, though critics disagree about how far DSM-5 departed from it.
