# Law's Empire

Ronald Dworkin's major work of legal theory, published in 1986, in which he presents the full statement of his theory of **law as integrity**.

## The Starting Point: Genuine Judicial Disagreement

Dworkin opens with a simple observation: judges genuinely disagree in hard cases, not because one of them is ignorant of the "legal truth," but because the **essence of the concept of law itself** is the subject of an interpretive dispute (an interpretive concept). He invokes the case of *Riggs v. Palmer* (New York Court of Appeals, 1889: a grandson who murdered his grandfather in order to inherit, and whom the court denied the inheritance despite the terms of the will and the statute of wills, relying on the principle that no one may profit from a wrong) to show that courts use **principles** not explicitly written in any text, yet which judges regard as part of the law.

## The Critique of Rival Theories

The book devotes chapters to criticizing **conventionalism** (the view of legal positivists that law is confined to what has been explicitly agreed upon) and **pragmatism/realism** (the view that law is a tool the judge shapes freely in service of the future). In their place, Dworkin proposes the model of **constructive interpretation**: every social practice, law included, requires an interpretation that balances respect for its history with the pursuit of the best possible justification of it.

## Law as Integrity: Between Conventionalism and Pragmatism

Dworkin presents **integrity** as a third alternative lying between conventionalism and pragmatism. The judge should interpret the legal history of the community (its legislation and precedents) as expressing, as far as possible, a coherent set of moral principles, as if the community were a single person speaking with one consistent moral voice over time. Dworkin uses the metaphor of the **chain novel**: each judge writes a new chapter in a legal story begun by others, bound to honour what came before while seeking to offer the best moral continuation of the story. The judge neither starts a new story from nothing nor copies earlier chapters word for word.

## Judge Hercules and the Single Right Answer

Dworkin invokes the figure of **Judge Hercules**, a hypothetical judge with unlimited learning and reasoning power, to show that every hard case, however complex, has in principle **one right answer** attainable through the best constructive interpretation. It is not merely a set of equally balanced choices settled by the judge's free discretion, as Hart claimed. This position is a direct extension of Dworkin's earlier critique of Hart in *Taking Rights Seriously*, but here it is developed within a complete interpretive theory of the nature of social practices in general, not of law alone.

## Three Stages of Interpretation

Dworkin proposes a model of constructive interpretation that passes through three stages: the **pre-interpretive stage** (identifying the raw material of the practice, namely the relevant laws and precedents), the **interpretive stage** (proposing a general justification that fits that material), and the **post-interpretive stage** (adjusting the understanding so that the practice becomes the best it can be in light of that justification). This three-part model makes legal interpretation a special case of moral and artistic interpretation in general, rather than a technical activity separate from them.

## Impact

The book provoked a direct, though incomplete, response from **Hart**, published posthumously as a postscript to the second edition of *The Concept of Law* (1994); see **the Hart–Dworkin debate**. Since its appearance, the book has remained a basic reference in discussions of constitutional and judicial interpretation in the Anglo-American legal tradition.

## Criticism from Positivists and Realists

The thesis of the "single right answer" was widely criticized by positivists, who saw it as overstating the existence of a moral coherence that does not in fact exist in pluralistic societies, and by legal realists and critical legal scholars, who regarded it as denying the reality that judicial decisions are often shaped by political or personal factors rather than objective interpretive standards. The model of "Judge Hercules" was also criticized as an unrealistic example that no actual human judge could achieve. Dworkin responded that Hercules is an idealized regulative standard rather than a description of judicial practice, just as any ideal moral standard (such as the complete and virtuous person) is not invalidated by the inability of real people to fully achieve it.
