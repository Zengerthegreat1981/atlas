# Being and Nothingness: Consciousness, Freedom, and Bad Faith

*Being and Nothingness* (1943) by Jean-Paul Sartre is the systematic foundation of existentialism and the direct source of three concepts that work in the consulting room every day: bad faith, the Look of the Other, and the project. Published in Paris in the middle of the German occupation, it is a work of phenomenological ontology — an attempt to describe the structures of being as they appear to consciousness — and it remains Sartre's most ambitious and most difficult book.

## The Central Claim: Consciousness Is Not a Thing but a Negation

Sartre's ontology divides being into two regions. *Being-in-itself* (*en-soi*) is the being of things: solid, full, identical with itself, what it is. *Being-for-itself* (*pour-soi*) is the being of consciousness: it is always what it is not, and not what it is. Consciousness is a nothingness — a gap, a negation — at the heart of being, and it is from this nothingness that freedom comes, as structure rather than as choice. Human beings are not free because they occasionally choose; they are free because consciousness, by its nature, perpetually distances itself from what it is. Hence Sartre's famous formula, developed here and stated plainly later, that existence precedes essence: there is no human nature given in advance that a life then expresses; a person becomes what he makes of himself through his projects.

## Bad Faith: Sartre's Alternative to the Unconscious

The book's most clinically fertile concept is *bad faith* (*mauvaise foi*). Where psychoanalysis explains self-deception through an unconscious that hides things from the self, Sartre argues that nothing is hidden from me — I am the one avoiding knowing. Bad faith is the act of persuading myself that I am compelled when I am not: the waiter in the café who plays at being a waiter, moving a little too precisely, a little too eagerly, as if his role were his nature; the woman on a date who treats her companion's hand as a thing, refusing to notice the intentions it carries. In each case the person flees the anguish of freedom by identifying with a fixed identity, a fact, or a role. Bad faith is not a lie told to another, which requires knowing the truth; it is a lie told to oneself while knowing the truth, a structure possible only because consciousness is simultaneously the deceiver and the deceived. For the therapist this reframes the patient's "I can't" as, often, "I will not see that I can."

## The Look of the Other

The second great analysis concerns the Other. When I am seen by another person, I become an object in his world: my body, my gestures, my past are fixed in a meaning I did not choose. Shame is the consciousness of being as the Other sees me — I discover myself as an object, and my freedom is suddenly alienated in the Other's gaze. Sartre's famous example is the voyeur at the keyhole, absorbed in looking, who hears footsteps behind him and is instantly transformed from a pure subject into someone seen, judged, caught. This is why relations with others carry, for Sartre, a permanent conflict: each consciousness seeks to recover its freedom by making the Other an object, and each resists being objectified in turn. The tone about human relationship is pessimistic — "hell is other people" belongs to the play *No Exit* of the following year, but its logic is worked out here — and this is one reason Sartre is used selectively in the clinic: the framework offers little to build a therapeutic alliance on.

## Death, Freedom, and the Structure of the Book

Sartre disagrees with Heidegger on death, and the disagreement matters for therapy. For Heidegger, death is my ownmost possibility, and facing it gives life meaning; for Sartre, death is an external absurdity, the point at which my projects are cut off by a freedom that is not mine, and it confers no meaning at all. This is an objection from inside existentialism to the therapeutic use of death, and it is worth hearing. The book proceeds in four parts: the problem of nothingness; being-for-itself; being-for-others; and having, doing, and being, ending with a sketch of an existential psychoanalysis that would read a life through its fundamental project rather than through childhood complexes. The English translation by Hazel Barnes appeared in 1956 and fixed the standard vocabulary — bad faith, the Look, the project — in which the book is discussed in English.

## Reception and Influence

*Being and Nothingness* made Sartre the central philosopher of post-war France and shaped existential psychiatry, above all through R. D. Laing, who took the analysis of the Look and of objectification into the study of schizophrenia, and through the existential psychoanalysis sketched at the book's end. Heidegger himself disowned Sartre's reading of him in the *Letter on Humanism* (1947), insisting that Sartre had turned an analysis of Being into a metaphysics of the subject — a repudiation that marks the split between the two thinkers. The book's difficulty is legendary, and its influence has often travelled through summaries rather than readings. Its limits are real: the account of the body and of sexuality is thin, the analysis of the Other leaves little room for genuine reciprocity, and the philosophy of freedom it defends makes social and material constraint hard to theorise. Read carefully, however, it remains the sharpest available account of how people deceive themselves about their own freedom — and of why that self-deception is so hard to give up.
