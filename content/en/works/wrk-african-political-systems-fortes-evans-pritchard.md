# African Political Systems

*African Political Systems* (1940), edited by Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard, is a foundational text of political anthropology. Published by Oxford University Press for the International Institute of African Languages and Cultures (later the International African Institute), the volume asks a question that had barely been posed before: how is political order maintained in societies without states, kings, or courts? Its answer — that order can rest on kinship, age sets, and balanced opposition rather than on centralised authority — reshaped anthropology's understanding of politics and remains a reference point today.

The book appeared at a moment when British social anthropology, under A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, was consolidating itself as a science of social structure. Fortes and Evans-Pritchard, both trained under Charles Seligman and Malinowski and influenced by Radcliffe-Brown (who contributed the volume's Preface), applied a structural-functionalist approach to politics. Their introduction proposes a typology that organised the field for a generation: African societies divide into Group A, states with centralised authority and administrative machinery, and Group B, stateless societies in which political relations are ordered through segmentary lineage systems and other non-centralised institutions.

## Structure and Cases

The volume contains eight case studies, five of centralised systems and three of stateless ones. The Group A chapters cover the Zulu under their king, the Ngwato of Botswana, the Bemba of Zambia, the Ankole kingdom of Uganda, and the Kede of northern Nigeria. The Group B chapters cover the Bantu of Kavirondo (including the Logoli) in Kenya, the Tallensi of Ghana — Fortes's own fieldwork — and the Nuer of South Sudan, Evans-Pritchard's classic study.

The chapters were written by different authors (Gluckman on the Zulu, Schapera on the Ngwato, Richards on the Bemba, Oberg on Ankole, Nadel on the Kede, Wagner on the Bantu of Kavirondo, Fortes on the Tallensi, Evans-Pritchard on the Nuer), but all address the same core questions: the system of political offices, the mechanisms of sanction and dispute settlement, and the relationship between political and kinship organisation. By holding the questions broadly constant across cases, the editors aimed at systematic comparison.

The two most influential chapters are Fortes on the Tallensi and Evans-Pritchard on the Nuer. Fortes showed how the Tallensi, who had no centralised government, maintained order through the lineage system: authority rested with the elders of maximal lineages, disputes were settled by mediation, and the ritual office of the earth priest complemented the office of the chief. Evans-Pritchard's Nuer chapter introduced the principle of segmentary opposition — the idea that Nuer lineages unite against outsiders at one level and divide against each other at another, so that political unity is always relative to the scale of conflict. In this model, a Nuer man stands with his lineage against a neighbouring lineage, and with both against a more distant one. Order emerges from this fission and fusion, not from any authority above it.

## Argument and Method

The volume's argument is that politics is not the same as government. Where earlier writers had assumed that political life required a state, Fortes and Evans-Pritchard showed that political relations — authority, obligation, sanction, collective decision — exist in stateless societies in forms that a narrow, European definition of politics had missed. The editors' introduction insists that political systems must be studied as systems: the question is how the parts work together to maintain order, not whether a society has a king.

The method is structural-functionalism at its most disciplined. Fieldwork-based case studies are compared to extract general principles, and history is largely set aside: the societies are presented as functioning wholes, as they were at the time of study. That synchronic focus was the book's strength as comparison and its greatest weakness as history.

## Reception and Influence

*African Political Systems* was immediately recognised as a landmark and dominated political anthropology for two decades. Its typology of centralised and stateless societies organised teaching and research, and the Nuer model of segmentary opposition became one of anthropology's most cited ideas, applied far beyond Africa — to Bedouin societies, to highland New Guinea, and to the study of feuds and vendettas.

Later criticism was substantial. The book was written under colonial rule, and several contributors worked within colonial administrations; the volume says little about the colonial state that surrounded and constrained the societies it describes. Its static, structural picture ignored history, resistance, and change, and its typology flattened the diversity of African politics into two boxes. The "stateless" label itself came to be criticised as a negative definition — societies defined by what they lacked. Postcolonial scholars have also noted that the volume's neat systems were partly artefacts of colonial indirect rule, which froze fluid political arrangements into fixed "tribes" and chiefs.

Even so, the book's central move has endured: the insistence that politics takes many institutional forms, and that the absence of a state is not the absence of order. Every later debate in political anthropology — about power, resistance, the state, and the politics of everyday life — starts from the ground *African Political Systems* cleared.
