# Debt: The First 5000 Years

David Graeber's central work, published in 2011 by Melville House. It is considered a radical rethinking of the economic and moral foundations of human civilisation. The book reverses the standard narrative with which economics textbooks themselves open, concerning the origin of money and exchange.

## Central Thesis

Contrary to the classical account (from Aristotle to Adam Smith) that people **first bartered goods for goods, then invented money to facilitate barter, and debt arose only later**, Graeber argues that **debt is older in time than both money and barter**. His evidence is purely anthropological and historical: there is no documented evidence of a pure barter economy in any known human society. Economies have always rested on systems of debt and mutual dependence before money entered the picture.

## Ethical Thesis

But the book does not stop at economic history. Graeber moves to an essential ethical dimension: the sentence "debts must be repaid" is not a statement of economic fact but a **deliberate conflation of a moral, emotional obligation with a quantitative contract that must be settled arithmetically**. This conflation, which the state and the law rely on, is what allows the feelings of guilt and moral obligation to be converted into a legal duty enforceable by force. The book traces how this conflation has been used to justify violence and coercion, including the pressure on indebted countries and individuals to repay at any cost.

## The Political Dimension

In the second half of the book, Graeber moves from history to contemporary politics: **debt cancellation**. He argues that the cancellation of debts (total or partial) was not an emergency economic decision but a normal policy in civilisation. The ancient empires (Babylon, Egypt) regularly declared debt cancellations to prevent the collapse of society. In the modern era, he argues, debt cancellation has come to be treated as a **moral taboo** rather than a legitimate political decision.

## Direct Reception

The book achieved wide circulation and became a reference in the radical critique of capitalism and debt, and it became associated with the Occupy Wall Street movement and the slogan of debt cancellation. It faced discussions from economists, historians and anthropologists who disagreed about how to assess its historical argument.

## Its Connection to Anthropological Work

The book builds on a genuine anthropological foundation: Graeber's own fieldwork in Madagascar (published in *Lost People*, 2007) concerned magic, slavery, and social memory in a rural community. He also draws on earlier anthropologists:

- **Marcel Mauss** (*The Gift*, 1925): the model of mutual exchange that creates social bonds.
- **Marshall Sahlins** (*Stone Age Economics*, 1972): the economy is socially and culturally instituted.
- **Karl Polanyi**: the economy embedded in social relations.

## Structure of the Book and Its Ethical Principles

Graeber divides history into four major periods: the age of the first agrarian empires until around 800 BCE; then the Axial Age between 800 BCE and 600 CE, when minted money, mercenary armies and the world religions appeared; then the Middle Ages between 600 and 1450 CE; then the age of capitalist empires from 1450 to 1971; followed by a still undetermined period since 1971. At the heart of the analysis are three moral principles that he argues govern economic relations: **baseline communism**, in which people give according to ability and receive according to need without accounting; **exchange based on equivalence and the possibility of offsetting**; and **hierarchy**, which rests on giving and taking between unequals. He argues that debt appears when exchange turns into a relationship of inequality, and that violence is what converts sums into obligation.

## Debt in the Islamic Tradition

The Arab-Islamic tradition has a presence in the subject of the book in two respects. The first is textual: the Qur'an forbids usury and calls for granting respite to a debtor in hardship (Qur'an 2:280), and regards remitting the debt as an act of charity better still. In this horizon, debt is an ethical obligation rather than a purely quantitative calculation, which is close to what Graeber calls the conflation of duty and contract. The second is historical: in the chapter on the medieval period, Graeber treats the Islamic world in the Abbasid era as a civilisation that built its law and ethics around the merchant and the free contract, and he argues that jurists and theologians combined a positive view of the market as a legitimate system with a religious sensitivity to usury and to the enslavement of debtors.

## Madagascar and Anthropological Work

Graeber drew from his fieldwork in rural Madagascar, which he recorded in *Lost People* (Indiana University Press, 2007), an interest in how obligation, slavery, and memory shape everyday morality in a rural community. He also discusses Caroline Humphrey's argument in "Barter and Economic Disintegration" (*Man*, 1985) that anthropology has never found a society based purely on barter, which is the field-based foundation of his critique of Adam Smith's story.

## Reception and Criticism

Many praised the critique of the barter myth, but economists and historians felt the book generalises from scattered evidence, and that its division into critical periods simplifies a more diverse history. They also argued that the dichotomy between virtual credit and precious metal that Graeber proposes does not by itself explain the cycle of money. Some argued that the book replaces careful historical writing with political rhetoric.
