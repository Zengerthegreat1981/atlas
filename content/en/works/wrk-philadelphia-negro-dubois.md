# The Philadelphia Negro: A Social Study

The pioneering work of W. E. B. Du Bois (1899), a comprehensive empirical social study of an American Black urban community, which predates the Chicago school of urban sociology that traditional histories of American sociology celebrated as the starting point of urban fieldwork.

## Context

Du Bois, who had received his doctorate from Harvard University in 1895 (the first Black American to do so), was invited to conduct this study by the University of Pennsylvania, at the instigation of reform-minded Philadelphians who wanted to understand what they regarded as the "Negro problem" in the city, amid white elite anxiety over the growing numbers of Black residents, many of them migrants from the South. Du Bois and his wife, Nina, lived for some months in the neighborhood studied (the Seventh Ward) so that he could carry out the survey himself from house to house. He did so with the modest status of an assistant instructor, and was never offered a permanent academic post at the university despite his qualifications.

## Method

Du Bois adopted a pioneering field survey method in which he gathered quantitative and qualitative data together: house-to-house interviews with thousands of residents, statistical data on income, work, education, health, crime, and housing, together with direct ethnographic observation of daily life and of community institutions (churches, clubs, charitable associations) within the neighborhood, a methodology that drew on the social-survey tradition of Charles Booth's London survey and the Hull-House settlement studies, and that preceded the Chicago School's urban research by a generation.

## The Central Thesis

Du Bois rejected the prevailing biological racist explanations of the time, which explained poverty and crime among Black people by an alleged innate deficiency in the group, offering instead a careful structural-historical explanation: the institutional legacy of slavery, continuing discrimination in the labor market (the systematic exclusion of Black people from unions and skilled trades despite their qualifications) and in housing, and a cumulative historical deprivation of educational opportunities, all external structural factors that can be changed politically, not a fixed intrinsic deficiency in individuals or the group. Du Bois also distinguished, within the Black community itself, between distinct social classes (rather than treating it as a homogeneous mass), documenting a precise internal class, occupational, and cultural diversity that reflects the complexity of actual social reality in place of the flat stereotyped image that prevailed.

## Relation to the Chicago School

Both the *Philadelphia Negro* and the Chicago School's studies of the 1910s and 1920s relied on direct field investigation of a city. The Chicago work of Robert Park and Ernest Burgess was organised around human ecology and the process of urban succession, whereas Du Bois combined a house-to-house survey with historical and institutional analysis, and located the causes of Black disadvantage largely in discrimination rather than in neighborhood processes alone.

## Impact

This work remained marginalized for a long time in the traditional historiography of American sociology despite its temporal and methodological precedence over the Chicago School, since that historiography celebrated Robert Park and Ernest Burgess as the pioneers of the urban field survey instead of Du Bois, a direct reflection of the racial discrimination that excluded Du Bois himself from the white academic establishment despite his pioneering role. His founding standing has been restored in recent decades within a critical revision of the history of the field ("Du Boisian Sociology"), which highlights his early contribution to founding American empirical sociology and contemporary critical race sociology.

## The Relation of the Work to the Concept of Double Consciousness

This work is sometimes read alongside Du Bois's better-known concept of "double consciousness," set out in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903). The two are best understood as complementary projects of the same period: the *Philadelphia Negro* documents empirically the structural discrimination that shaped Black life in the city, while *The Souls of Black Folk* gives an interpretive account of the inner experience of living under it. Together they illustrate Du Bois's attempt to join empirical inquiry with reflection on the American racial experience.

## Public Policy Recommendations

Du Bois's work was not limited to purely empirical documentation, but concluded with practical policy recommendations addressed to both white and Black society together: he called on the Black elite to bear greater leadership responsibility in improving the conditions of the poorer classes within the group itself (a theme he later developed in his essay "The Talented Tenth," 1903), and at the same time called on white institutions (employers, unions, local government) to remove the direct discriminatory barriers that the study had documented with figures, thereby refusing to content himself with blaming any one party in isolation from the responsibility of the other.

## The Method of Precise Class Classification

Du Bois divided the Black residents of the Seventh Ward into four grades by social and economic condition, ranging from the well-to-do at the top, through the hard-working and the poor, to a "submerged tenth" of criminals, paupers and idlers at the bottom, instead of treating them as a homogeneous mass as the racial writing of the time did. Applying class analysis within a single racial group was an early and important innovation, although the classification, and especially its language about the lowest grade, reflects the moralising assumptions of the period.

## Residential Segregation and Economic Opportunities

Du Bois documented with statistical evidence how actual residential segregation (through discrimination in the renting and selling of property, not by an officially declared law as in the South) confined most Black residents to particular neighborhoods of lower housing quality and narrower job opportunities, regardless of their income or individual education, showing that spatial segregation itself, not merely direct individual discrimination, is an independent structural mechanism for reproducing deprivation across generations. Later urban sociology on the effects of residential segregation on economic opportunity returned to these themes.
