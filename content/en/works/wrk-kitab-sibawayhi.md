# Al-Kitab (Al-Kitāb)

The earliest surviving comprehensive work of Arabic grammar, written by **Amr ibn Uthman ibn Qanbar**, known as **Sibawayh** (d. c. 180 AH / 796 CE). He was a Persian by origin who grew up and was educated in **Basra**, one of the two great centers of the Arabic grammatical school (alongside Kufa). The work is known simply as *al-Kitab* ("The Book") without any other title, in recognition of its unique standing, and in tradition it is sometimes described as "the Qur'an of grammar."

## Content and method

The work, in five volumes in the standard modern edition, offers a comprehensive systematic description of the system of classical Arabic: the topics of grammar (inflection, governing elements and constructions), morphology, and some questions of sounds (early phonetics). Sibawayh adopted an inductive method based on collecting **evidence** (*shawahid*) from the speech of eloquent Arabs (especially the desert tribes, which were regarded as closest to original eloquence), from pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry, and from the Noble Qur'an. He derived grammatical rules from this actual usage rather than imposing them a priori, in a scientific method sometimes compared with that of the Indian linguist **Panini** (Pāṇini) in codifying Sanskrit.

## Context: the Basran school and the dispute with Kufa

Sibawayh was a pupil of **al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi**, whom the tradition credits with putting Arabic prosody (*'arud*) into a system and with the lexicon *Kitab al-'Ayn*. The attribution of the lexicon is traditional, and Sibawayh cites al-Khalil throughout the *Kitab*. Later grammarians treated the book as the chief statement of a Basran method, strict in analogy (*qiyas*), and set it against a Kufan willingness to admit rare poetic usage. That contrast between two schools was drawn more sharply after Sibawayh's death. It is too neat as a picture of his own generation, but it became the frame in which the tradition read the book for centuries.

## Reception and influence

Upon its appearance, the work became the reference on which almost the entire subsequent Arabic grammatical school was built. Generations of grammarians studied, explained and discussed it (such as al-Mazini, al-Mubarrad and al-Sirafi), and later works such as al-Zamakhshari's *al-Mufassal* build on its framework. It has remained to this day the primary source to which one returns in settling disputed grammatical questions. It has been regarded as an exceptional work in the history of linguistics in general, for its chronological priority and the depth of its structural analysis. Some modern linguists have compared his treatment of ellipsis and grammatical estimation (*taqdir*) with later notions of underlying and surface structure, although such comparisons should be made with caution.

## Caveats

The year of Sibawayh's death, and therefore of the book's completion, is disputed. Classical reports range from about 177 to 194 AH (793 to 810 CE). Many modern scholars prefer a date around 180/796. His writing is very dense and technical, so much so that his readers themselves needed extensive commentaries to understand his precise intentions (such as al-Sirafi's commentary in the tenth century CE). This made it a specialized text that is difficult to access directly without progressing through its commentaries, unlike later grammatical works written primarily for simpler teaching purposes.
