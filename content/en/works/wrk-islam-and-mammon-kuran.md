# Islam and Mammon

*Islam and Mammon: The Economic Predicaments of Islamism* is a book by Timur Kuran, published in 2004. It offers a comprehensive critique of the contemporary Islamic economics project, regarding it as an ideological intellectual movement more than a coherent analytical economic theory able to compete with the tools of modern economic analysis.

## Central thesis: ideology rather than an alternative economic theory

Kuran argues that contemporary Islamic economics, as developed by figures such as Abul A'la Mawdudi, Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr and Umer Chapra, arose essentially as a political-ideological reaction to the competition between capitalism and socialism in the mid-twentieth century, rather than as the product of an independent analytical development offering new explanatory tools. He also argues that the project served to strengthen a distinct Muslim identity. These are Kuran's critical positions, which Islamic economists contest. He contends that much of what is presented as an alternative "Islamic model" is in essence a religious relabelling of well-known conventional economic instruments.

## Critique of murabaha: its closeness to interest-based lending

The book devotes a detailed analysis to the **murabaha** formula, the most widely used practical instrument of Islamic finance in contemporary Islamic banks. Kuran argues that economically (in terms of actual risk distribution, the real rate of return on the financed capital, and the structure of time-based repayment) it produces results very close to conventional interest-based loans, despite the difference in formal contractual wording (a deferred sale with a known profit margin instead of a cash loan with interest). This raises the fundamental question of whether the distinction between the two systems is economically real or is primarily a formal jurisprudential one.

## Zakat and the limits of its actual redistributive effect

The book also discusses the limits of the effectiveness of zakat as an instrument of redistribution in contemporary practice. It argues that its voluntary application in most Islamic countries (outside a few cases such as Saudi Arabia and Pakistan at certain periods, which mandate its centralized collection) limits its actual impact compared with modern legally binding progressive tax systems. It argues that the gap between the declared jurisprudential principle of zakat and its actual application on the ground remains wide in many contemporary contexts.

## The debate over the authenticity of the Islamic model

Islamic economists have objected that partnership and profit-sharing contracts, unlike murabaha, assign the financier a share of losses if a project fails, and that this differs from an interest-bearing loan. Writers associated with that reply include Monzer Kahf and Muhammad Anas Zarqa. On this view, Kuran's point about the predominance of murabaha in banking practice does not remove the distinction drawn in the theoretical literature on Islamic finance.

## Impact

The book is a widely cited critique of contemporary Islamic economics, and a frequent target of replies, in discussions of whether Islamic economic doctrine is distinct from conventional economics.

## The context of authorship: the rise of political Islam

The book, a collection of essays developed over the 1990s and early 2000s, appeared at a time of growing Western academic interest in political Islam, especially after the events of 11 September 2001. It addresses the economic dimension of political Islam, rather than only its security or ideological dimensions.

## Structure of argument and style of presentation

In this book Kuran uses an argumentative style that combines historical presentation (tracing the origins of contemporary Islamic economics since the 1940s) with technical economic analysis (analysing how Islamic finance contracts work using modern risk theory). The book thus addresses an academic audience while remaining accessible to readers interested in the economic politics of the Middle East and the Islamic world.

## Its influence on subsequent academic debate

The book is frequently assigned in university courses on Islamic economics and finance, usually alongside replies from Islamic economists, and it remains a common point of reference whatever one's view of its central claims.
