# The Book of Healing (al-Shifa') by Avicenna (Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

## The Central Thesis

*The Book of Healing* (*Kitab al-Shifa'*, Latin: *Sufficientia*) is the monumental philosophical encyclopedia composed by the Persian polymath Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Abd Allah ibn Sina (Avicenna, c. 980–1037 CE), begun around 1020 and largely completed by about 1027 CE. Its fundamental thesis is that the totality of theoretical knowledge forms a unified, demonstrative continuum: starting from the formal canons of logic, passing through the observation of physical nature and the proportions of mathematics, and culminating in the metaphysical demonstration of the Necessary Existent (*Wajib al-Wujud*) and the celestial emanation of being.

A common popular misconception confuses *al-Shifa'* with a medical manual due to the title's literal meaning. However, Avicenna's medical masterpiece is *The Canon of Medicine* (*al-Qanun fi al-Tibb*). The title is usually understood as referring to healing the soul of ignorance through philosophical knowledge.

## Structure of the Work

The encyclopedia is organized into four expansive divisions (*jumal*), covering the full curriculum of late antique and Islamic Peripatetic (*Mashsha'i*) philosophy:

1. **Logic (*al-Mantiq*)**: Structured into nine treatises corresponding to the late antique Alexandrian expanded Aristotelian Organon:
   - *al-Madkhal* (Porphyry's *Isagoge*)
   - *al-Maqulat* (Aristotle's *Categories*)
   - *al-'Ibara* (*De Interpretatione*)
   - *al-Qiyas* (*Prior Analytics* / The Syllogism)
   - *al-Burhan* (*Posterior Analytics* / Demonstration)
   - *al-Jadal* (*Topics* / Dialectic)
   - *al-Safsata* (*Sophistical Refutations*)
   - *al-Khitaba* (*Rhetoric*)
   - *al-Shi'r* (*Poetics*)
2. **Natural Philosophy / Physics (*al-Tabi'iyyat*)**: Eight treatises covering the physical cosmos:
   - *al-Sama' al-Tabi'i* (*Physics* / General Principles of Nature)
   - *al-Sama' wa-l-'Alam* (*On the Heavens and the World*)
   - *al-Kawn wa-l-Fasad* (*On Generation and Corruption*)
   - *al-Af'al wa-l-Infi'alat* (*Actions and Passions*)
   - *al-Ma'adin wa-l-Athar al-'Ulwiyya* (*Minerals and Meteorology*)
   - *Kitab al-Nafs* (*De Anima* / On the Soul)
   - *al-Nabat* (*On Plants*)
   - *al-Hayawan* (*On Animals*)
3. **Mathematics (*al-Riyadiyyat*)**: Four parts:
   - Geometry (an epitome and commentary on Euclid's *Elements*)
   - Arithmetic (grounded in the Neo-Pythagorean arithmetic of Nicomachus of Gerasa)
   - Music (mathematical acoustics and modal theory)
   - Astronomy (based on Ptolemy's *Almagest*)
4. **Metaphysics (*al-Ilahiyyat*)**: Ten books investigating being qua being (*al-mawjud bima huwa mawjud*), the real distinction between essence (*mahiyya*) and existence (*wujud*), the contingency of the created world, the Proof of the Sincere (*Burhan al-Siddiqin*) demonstrating God as the Necessary Being by virtue of Himself, divine providence (*'inaya*), and the celestial hierarchy of intelligences.

## Historical Composition and Transmission

Avicenna composed *al-Shifa'* in turbulent circumstances while in the service of the Kakuyid ruler Ala al-Dawla, traveling between Hamadan and Isfahan. Sections were often drafted during evenings following state duties or while in hiding during military campaigns.

In twelfth-century Toledo, Spain, Archbishop Raymond sponsored the translation of key sections of *al-Shifa'* into Latin. The philosophical translation team of **Dominicus Gundissalinus** and **Avendauth** (the Jewish philosopher Abraham ibn Daud) translated the psychological book as *Liber de anima seu Sextus de naturalibus* and major parts of the Metaphysics as *Liber de philosophia prima*. These translations ignited the thirteenth-century intellectual revival in Western Europe, becoming foundational authorities for **Albertus Magnus**, **Thomas Aquinas**, and **John Duns Scotus**. In particular, the ontological distinction between essence and existence that underpins Aquinas's *De Ente et Essentia* (c. 1256) was strongly influenced by Avicenna's *Ilahiyyat*.

## Psychological Foundation: The Internal Senses and the Flying Man

The primary psychological significance of *al-Shifa'* resides in the *Kitab al-Nafs* (Book VI of Natural Philosophy), which became the cornerstone of medieval Islamic and Latin faculty psychology:
- **Taxonomy of the Internal Senses (*al-hawas al-batina*)**: Avicenna developed from Aristotle's remarks a model of five distinct internal faculties located in the brain:
  1. *Common Sense (al-hiss al-mushtarak / sensus communis)*: Integrates disparate external sensory impressions (color, sound, texture) into a unified perceptual object.
  2. *Retentive Imagination (al-khayal / formativa)*: Stores the sensible forms received by the common sense after the external object is no longer present.
  3. *Compositive Imagination (al-mutakhayyila / imaginatio)*: Actively combines and divides stored images (termed *al-mufakkira* or the cogitative faculty when directed by the rational intellect).
  4. *Estimative Faculty (al-wahm / aestimatio)*: Intuitively perceives non-sensible, immaterial intentions (*ma'ani*) inhering in particulars—such as the sheep perceiving the hostility of a wolf.
  5. *Memory and Recollection (al-dhakira / memoria)*: Stores and retrieves the non-sensory intentions perceived by the *wahm*.
- **The Flying Man Argument (*al-Rajul al-Mu'allaq*)**: Avicenna formulated a celebrated thought experiment to demonstrate the immateriality and self-subsistence of the soul. He asked the reader to imagine a human being created all at once in adulthood, completely formed, and suspended in an absolute void or air. The individual's eyes are covered, the limbs are separated so that they do not touch, and no sensory stimulus reaches the body. Avicenna argued that even while completely deprived of bodily sensations, sensory perception, and limb awareness, this person would maintain an unmediated, immediate awareness of their own existence and selfhood (*dhatuhu*). Avicenna took this to show that the soul is an immaterial substance distinct from the body; the argument is often compared with Descartes's *cogito*.

## Historical Reception and Critiques

- **Al-Ghazali (d. 1111)** made Avicenna's metaphysics a primary target of his polemic *The Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahafut al-Falasifa*, c. 1095), issuing a fatwa of heresy on three specific points: the pre-eternal existence of the world, God's knowledge of particulars only in a universal manner, and the denial of bodily resurrection in the afterlife.
- **Averroes (Ibn Rushd, d. 1198)** in *Tahafut al-Tahafut* defended philosophy against al-Ghazali, but simultaneously criticized Avicenna for deviating from Aristotle by treating existence as an accidental property added to essence and introducing Neo-Platonic emanationist schemes into Peripatetic ontology.
- Avicenna later produced a concise summary of *al-Shifa'* entitled *al-Najat* (*The Book of Salvation*), which served as an accessible textbook for subsequent generations of students.
