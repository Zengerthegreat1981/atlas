# Beyond Good and Evil

## General Information About the Work

The German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche published his book *Beyond Good and Evil: Prelude to a Philosophy of the Future* (*Jenseits von Gut und Böse*) in 1886, paying for the printing himself (it appeared with the Leipzig publisher C. G. Naumann). It followed *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, and Nietzsche still had few readers. The book comes in nine chapters divided into two hundred and ninety-six numbered paragraphs (aphorisms) of varying length, ranging from a single sentence to a complete essay. Nietzsche chose this style deliberately, as an escape from the "systematic method" of German systematic philosophy (such as Hegel's), preferring a form closer to the French essay (Montaigne, La Rochefoucauld), which shocks the reader and provokes their thinking rather than leading them step by step.

## Structure and Content

Nietzsche opens the book by attacking the "philosophical prejudice" that has pervaded the history of Western philosophy, asking about the "will to truth" itself: why do we assume that truth is more valuable than illusion, and that life needs truth rather than beneficial errors? He attacks the traditional metaphysical dualities (truth/falsehood, good/evil) as false simplifications of a more complex reality governed by the "will to power" (*Wille zur Macht*), which is the central concept in which Nietzsche sees the deepest drive of every living being, surpassing Darwin's principle of "survival" itself. For Nietzsche, a living being does not seek merely to survive, but to expand, dominate and excel.

Nietzsche devotes an entire chapter to criticizing earlier philosophers (particularly Kant and Schopenhauer), accusing them of presenting their "systems" as mere justifications of prior psychological or moral judgments they already held, rather than as neutral rational conclusions as they claim. In the fifth chapter ("On the Natural History of Morals") he analyzes the "herd instinct" in morality, and in the ninth ("What Is Noble?", especially section 260) he presents his best-known distinction (developed further in *On the Genealogy of Morality*, 1887): between "master morality" (*Herrenmoral*), based on the evaluation by the strong of themselves according to standards of nobility, power and self-superiority, and "slave morality" (*Sklavenmoral*), which, in his genealogical analysis, arose from the resentment of the weak and their reaction to the oppression of the powerful. The weak reversed the values upside down: humility became a virtue instead of pride, compassion instead of strength, and "evil" (*Böse*) became what the weak fear in the strong, in contrast to the older "bad" (*Schlecht*), which simply meant what is base and degenerate rather than frightening.

Nietzsche argues that Christianity, which he saw as growing out of Judaism, carried slave morality across Europe and, together with modern democratic movements, produced a civilization that favors herd conformity ("herd morality") and suspects exceptional individuals. (His remarks on Jews and Judaism in the book are complex and mix criticism of Christian morality's origins with sharp criticism of anti-Semitism.) In contrast, he looks toward the "free spirits" and "philosophers of the future," higher types who create their own values rather than receiving them ready-made (the figure of the *Übermensch* belongs mainly to *Zarathustra*), transcending the traditional duality of good and evil by redefining what is "good" and "bad" from the perspective of their own superior life, not from the perspective of a fearful herd seeking security and equality.

## Reception and Conflicting Interpretations

Nietzsche's work was widely misused in the twentieth century. His sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, who edited his unpublished papers (including the compilation *The Will to Power*) in ways that distorted them, promoted his ideas to provide intellectual support for Nazism, taking concepts such as "will to power" and "masters and slaves" out of their context. Yet Nietzsche explicitly attacked anti-Semitism and German chauvinistic nationalism in numerous letters, and his project was an individual critique of Western culture, not a call for the domination of a race or a state. Later scholars, most prominently Walter Kaufmann in *Nietzsche: Philosopher, Psychologist, Antichrist* (1950), worked to correct this reception and to present Nietzsche as a critic of nationalism and anti-Semitism; later scholars have also stressed the genuinely aristocratic and anti-egalitarian strands of his thought.

From a philosophical standpoint, some interpreters (such as Brian Leiter in *Nietzsche on Morality*, 2002) argue that Nietzsche is not a simple relativist but a critic of the dominant moral outlook (which he calls "morality in the pejorative sense") on the basis of its effects on the flourishing of higher human types. His judgment on morality is thus evaluative rather than neutral, even though he rejects the existence of moral truths independent of any perspective. His view that knowledge is always from some perspective is known as "perspectivism."

## Objections and Replies

Nietzsche is accused of self-contradiction: how can he reject absolute truth while presenting his genealogical analysis of morality as an objective historical truth? His defenders reply that Nietzsche does not deny the possibility of contextual explanatory knowledge, but denies the existence of a "view from nowhere" (View from Nowhere) free from all value bias, and that his own analysis is itself one perspective among possible perspectives, offered argumentatively rather than as a final revelation. He is also accused of glorifying cruelty and despotism. Defenders reply that the "higher human being" is a metaphor for creative and intellectual excellence (such as Goethe, whom Nietzsche cites as an example), not for political oppression.

## Influence and Legacy

The book exerted an immense influence on existentialist and phenomenological thought (Heidegger, Jaspers, Sartre), on post-structuralism (Foucault, who drew on Nietzschean genealogy to analyze power and discourse, and Derrida), and on psychology (Freud professed to avoid reading Nietzsche but acknowledged that his insights anticipated psychoanalytic ideas about the unconscious and repression). Genealogy is more fully developed in *On the Genealogy of Morality*. The book remains one of the most provocative texts in the history of Western moral philosophy, and one of the most subject to contradictory interpretation.
