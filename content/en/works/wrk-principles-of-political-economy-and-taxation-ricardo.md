# Principles of Political Economy and Taxation

**David Ricardo** published *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* in 1817. It is widely seen as moving political economy from the historically rich, descriptive manner of Adam Smith towards a more abstract deductive system. It combined a theory of value, a theory of distribution and a theory of international trade in a single analytical structure.

## The deductive method: from Smith to Ricardo

Unlike Smith's narrative style, rich in historical and institutional detail, Ricardo adopted an abstract deductive method in this book. He began from explicit simplifying assumptions, such as the fixed quantity of land suitable for cultivation and the diminishing marginal fertility of that land. He then deduced from them a chain of strict logical conclusions about the distribution of income among the three great classes: landlords, capitalists and workers.

## The theory of differential rent

Ricardo devoted a central chapter to the theory of **rent**, which he developed in the context of the sharp political debate over the protectionist **Corn Laws** in Britain. When farmers were forced to cultivate less fertile marginal land to meet growing demand for food, landowners of the most fertile land earned a **differential rent** equal to the difference between the productivity of their land and that of the least productive marginal land, without any additional effort on their part. Ricardo used this theory as a direct political argument against the Corn Laws: protecting domestic grain prices raised landlords' rents at the expense of the profits of industrial capitalists and the wages of workers alike.

## Comparative advantage: the most famous chapter in the history of economics

The book includes the chapter that is among the most influential single chapters in the history of economic thought: the chapter on "Foreign Trade", in which Ricardo presented the argument for **comparative advantage**. Through his famous example of the trade in wine and cloth between Portugal and England, he demonstrated that specialization and free exchange benefit both countries, even if one of them is more efficient at producing every good in absolute terms. Each country benefits by specializing in the good in which it has a relatively lower opportunity cost compared with the other country.

## The theory of value: the attempt and its internal tensions

Ricardo sought in the book to establish the **labour theory of value** as the governing law of the exchange of commodities, in contrast to Smith's hesitation between several theories of value. Ricardo himself, however, faced internal logical difficulties in this attempt. In particular, there was the problem of how to measure a stable "absolute value" that could serve as a general standard of value across commodities with differing proportions of labour and fixed capital. Ricardo acknowledged this problem in his correspondence and in his unfinished last paper, "Absolute and Exchangeable Value" (1823), without resolving it to his satisfaction. The third edition of the *Principles* (1821) also qualified his early formulations of the labour theory.

## Taxation and public policy

The book also devotes detailed chapters to analysing the effects of different types of taxes (on land, on rent, on profits, on wages) on distribution and economic growth. It offers a systematic analytical framework for evaluating tax policy that remained a basic reference in public finance for decades afterward.

## Lasting influence

This book formed the basis on which the Ricardian school of political economy was built. It influenced two opposing directions later: neoclassical economics, which inherited the rigour of the deductive method, and **Karl Marx**, who built his radical critique of capitalism itself on Ricardo's labour theory of value.

## Three editions and an evolving text

Ricardo published three editions of the book between 1817 and 1821, introducing revisions and additions in each in response to his continuing debates with **Malthus** and others. These included an important additional chapter "On Machinery", added in the third edition of 1821. In it he seriously examined the possibility that the introduction of machinery could displace workers and produce lasting unemployment for some of them, partly retreating from his earlier optimism that machinery always benefits all parties without exception.

## A long-lasting teaching influence

Despite the relatively abstract and dense style of the book, compared with the smooth narrative style of Smith, it became a central text of British political economy in the nineteenth century. It shaped later British economists, among them **John Stuart Mill**, who absorbed its basic principles before developing or criticizing them.

## Political advocacy of free trade in Parliament

Ricardo did not confine himself to publishing his ideas academically. He defended them directly as a member of the British House of Commons after being elected in 1819. He used the arguments of his book on rent and free trade in actual parliamentary debates on trade and tax policy. This made him one of the early economists to take part directly in parliamentary debate on trade and tax policy, including the Corn Laws and tariffs.
