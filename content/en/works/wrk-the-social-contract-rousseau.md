# The Social Contract by Jean-Jacques Rousseau

*Du contrat social, ou Principes du droit politique* was published in Amsterdam by the publisher Marc-Michel Rey in 1762.

## The Central Thesis

The question with which Rousseau opens the book is how people can unite in a political body without any of them losing his freedom: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." His solution is that each individual alienates himself entirely to the whole community, and so to no one in particular, and recovers what he has given as a member of the sovereign and not as a subject. The concept that carries all this is the general will (volonté générale), which for Rousseau is not the sum of private wills (the "will of all") but what remains of them after their conflicts are subtracted. It follows that sovereignty cannot be represented, divided or alienated; and government for him is a delegated executive organ that can be dismissed, not a party to the contract.

## Structure of the Work

Four books. The first concerns the origin of the political bond and the critique of founding it on force, on paternity or on slavery, and contains chapter six on the contract itself and chapter seven on the sovereign. The second concerns sovereignty and its limits, law, and the "Legislator" (Législateur) as an exceptional figure who makes laws and does not rule. The third concerns the forms of government (monarchy, aristocracy, democracy) and the tendency of every government to degenerate, and contains the remark that perfect democracy is suited only to a people of gods. The fourth concerns voting, elections, censorship and Roman institutions, and ends with the chapter on "civil religion" (religion civile), which proposes a simple civil creed that supports the social bond, the chapter that ignited condemnation more than any other.

## How the Book Came to Be

Rousseau began to think about politics from his stay in Venice as secretary to the French ambassador in 1743, when he saw how the nature of government governs the character of a people. He had been preparing a broader project entitled *Political Institutions* (*Institutions politiques*), which he abandoned without completing, and *The Social Contract* came as remains of it that he polished and issued. In the book Rousseau engages with Grotius, Hobbes, Pufendorf, Locke and Montesquieu, opposing the jurists who found sovereignty on surrender or slavery, and opposing Hobbes in holding that sovereignty remains with the people and cannot be handed over to a ruler, and that the individual does not simply give up his freedom in return for security. An earlier draft of the work, the *Geneva Manuscript*, survives and was first published in the late nineteenth century. He cited the republic of Geneva, his birthplace, as an example of the size of state suited to popular sovereignty.

## Historical Context of Publication

The book and *Émile* were published in the same year (1762), and this fact was the most consequential in its history: both books were condemned and burned in Paris and Geneva, and a warrant for arrest was issued against Rousseau, who fled France. Publication outside the borders in Amsterdam was no coincidence but a customary precaution for texts that would not pass the French censorship. Thus the text entered European circulation as a banned text, printed in Holland and smuggled into France and Geneva.

## Reception and Critique

In the eighteenth century it was read as a revolutionary text and became associated with the language of the French Revolution and the discourse of popular sovereignty, and the Jacobins in particular carried it. In the nineteenth century Benjamin Constant, in his lecture *The Liberty of the Ancients Compared with That of the Moderns* (1819), opened the line of critique most lasting in effect: that Rousseau transferred to the modern state an authority over the individual suited to the ancient city and not to modern commercial society. In the twentieth century J. L. Talmon, in *The Origins of Totalitarian Democracy* (1952), offered a reading that makes the "general will" a theoretical origin of totalitarianism, a reading answered by students of Rousseau, among them Raymond Derathé and Judith Shklar, as reading the book retroactively through the twentieth century. The question not closed to this day is the phrase "forced to be free" in Book One: is it disguised coercion or a logical condition for the contract's survival.

## Arab Presence

'Adil Zu'aytir produced an early Arabic translation of the book, published in Cairo in the mid-twentieth century, followed by other translations. The book came to the Arab reader in the context of national liberation movements and then the debate over constitutional government, and the idea of popular sovereignty was invoked in it in the face of despotism, and some scholars compared it with the concept of bay'a and shura in Islamic thought, though the foundations differ: bay'a is a contract between ruler and ruled, while Rousseau makes the contract among individuals themselves and sovereignty belongs to the community. Constant's criticism of the transformation of freedom into permanent political participation was the subject of Arab discussions on direct and representative democracy.

## Limits and Distinction from Other Works

The book's connection to psychology is weak and its directness nil, and what makes it relevant is two things: that it is the theoretical source of the question of voluntary compliance, that is, why people obey an authority they regard as their own, a question that would become an empirical subject for social psychology two centuries later; and that the idea of turning the individual into a citizen through civic formation links it to the course of education and social conditioning. A decisive distinction is necessary: "natural man," the "noble savage" and the critique of inequality are not from this book but from the *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality among Men* (1755), and the theory of education is from *Émile* (1762). Mixing the three in one book is among the most famous errors of summary about Rousseau. The book retains an ambiguity about the role of the "Legislator," who stands above the people and drafts their laws, and about determining who decides what the general will is when it is not unanimous.
