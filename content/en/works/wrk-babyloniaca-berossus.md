# Babyloniaca — Berossus

## Definition

**Babyloniaca** is a vast historical work written by **Berossus**, a Babylonian priest (c. 340–278 BCE), in Greek, to document **the history of Mesopotamia from its origins to his own time**. Despite its enormous importance, **the original text is entirely lost**, and we have only **preserved quotations** from later historians. This makes it a classic example of the value of a "lost text" that is partly reconstructed through indirect testimony, and of the methodological importance of editing the "intermediate secondary sources" that preserved a partial survival for it across the centuries.

## Historical Context

Berossus wrote his work at the beginning of the Hellenistic age, shortly after the conquests of Alexander the Great, which brought the ancient Near East under Greek rule, and under the Seleucid dynasty that inherited the rule of Babylon and Mesopotamia. Berossus dedicated his work to the Seleucid king Antiochus I. He was part of a cultural context in which several priests of the ancient East (such as the Egyptian Manetho in Ptolemaic Egypt, roughly at the same time) tried to present the history of their ancient civilizations to the new Greek rulers and public, in a language and method the audience could understand. They aimed to preserve the status of their civilizational heritage against the dominance of the incoming Hellenistic culture, and to refute what they regarded as Greek prejudice or ignorance of the depth and antiquity of their ancient Eastern civilizations.

## Known Contents

The work appears to have consisted of three books: the first dealt with the geography of Babylon and the origin of the world according to Babylonian myth; the second with the history of the kings from the Flood to Nebuchadnezzar II; and the third with the history of the later period down to the Hellenistic age. On the basis of the quotations, the *Babyloniaca* covered:

- **The mythical origins** of Babylon and Babylonian civilization
- **The lists of kings** and the sequence of rulers
- **Major religious events** (the Flood, the construction of temples)
- **Military conquests** (especially in ancient times)
- **Miracles and divine signs**, the most famous being Berossus's account of Oannes, the half-fish creature who in Babylonian myth was regarded as the bearer of civilization, writing, and sciences to humanity at the dawn of history. This story was preserved for us by Eusebius of Caesarea among his lengthy quotations from the original work.

## Method and Purpose

Berossus **sought to reconcile the two civilizations**:
- **He presented Babylonian history** in sound Greek language
- **He linked Babylonian dates** with Greek and Egyptian chronologies (to document contemporaneity)
- **He explained Babylonian beliefs** to a Greek audience

## Major Works or Representatives

Berossus himself (c. 340–278 BCE) is the only known central figure of this work. He was a priest of the god Marduk's temple in Babylon before, according to some accounts, moving to the Greek island of Kos, where he founded a school of Chaldean Babylonian astrology for which the ancient East was famous in the eyes of the Greeks. In this way he transmitted part of the ancient Babylonian astronomical and astrological sciences to the Hellenistic world, alongside his documentary historical project.

## The Fate of the Text

- The copies of the work were not preserved in temple archives (as happened in Egypt)
- It did not attract Roman interest as it did the interest of the early Church
- **Its survival through quotations alone**

## Influence Despite Its Loss

Despite its loss, the *Babyloniaca* was a **trusted source** for:
- **Josephus**: he used it to support the antiquity of the Jewish people (if Babylon was very old, then the Jews **were contemporaries** of an ancient civilization)
- **Eusebius**: he relied on Berossus's figures for religious chronology
- **Modern Egyptologists**: they compare Berossus's figures with those of Manetho to establish dates, since the two represent roughly contemporaneous parallel attempts (Berossus on Babylon and Manetho on Egypt) to present the history of an ancient Eastern civilization in Greek to a new Hellenistic audience. Both works suffered the same fate of near-total loss, surviving only through scattered later quotations.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The fate of the *Babyloniaca*, a lost text that reached us only through the quotations of later historians, represents a familiar pattern in the history of ancient texts in general. In the Arab-Islamic heritage it finds a parallel in many early historical and literary books whose originals were lost and survive only through later quotations by historians such as al-Tabari, al-Mas'udi, and Ibn al-Nadim, who preserved fragments of earlier works written by historians such as Ibn Ishaq or al-Waqidi in their original forms, now lost. Some later Muslim scholars, such as al-Mas'udi in *Muruj al-Dhahab*, also drew on Babylonian and Persian traditions that reached them through multiple Greek and Syriac intermediaries, a chain of transmission similar in structure to the chain of the *Babyloniaca* through Josephus and Eusebius.
