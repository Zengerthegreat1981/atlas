# Babyloniaca — Berossus

## Definition

The ***Babyloniaca*** (also known as the *Babylonian History* or *Chaldaica*) was an ancient historical work written in Greek by **Berossus** (active early third century BCE), a native Babylonian priest of Bel-Marduk. Composed around 281 BCE and dedicated to the Seleucid monarch Antiochus I Soter, the three-volume work aimed to present the comprehensive history, cosmology, and king lists of Mesopotamia to the Greek-speaking Hellenistic world. Although the original text is completely lost, substantial fragments and summaries have survived through indirect quotations preserved by Jewish, Christian, and pagan authors, making it a critical bridge between native cuneiform archives and Greek historiography.

## Historical and Cultural Context

Following Alexander the Great's conquest of the Persian Empire and the establishment of the Seleucid dynasty in Mesopotamia, indigenous elites sought to communicate their heritage to the newly dominant Graeco-Macedonian ruling class. Berossus's project directly mirrored that of his contemporary, the Egyptian priest Manetho, who composed the *Aegyptiaca* in Greek under Ptolemy II Philadelphus. Both priests sought to correct Greek misconceptions, counter the romanticized ethnographies of Herodotus and Ctesias, and assert the supreme antiquity and civilizational precedence of their respective temple traditions.

## Tripartite Structure and Contents

Drawing directly on cuneiform temple records, astronomical texts, and mythological epics in Babylon, Berossus structured the *Babyloniaca* into three distinct books (*logoi*):

1. **Book One: Cosmography and Oannes**: This book described the geography of Babylonia and recounted the creation of the cosmos according to the Babylonian tradition (closely corresponding to the *Enuma Elish*). It introduced the revelatory figure of Oannes—a composite amphibious being (*apkallu*) who emerged from the Persian Gulf to instruct early humanity in letters, mathematics, sciences, agriculture, city building, and law.
2. **Book Two: From the Antediluvian Kings to Nabonassar**: This section drew upon Babylonian king-list traditions comparable to the Sumerian King List, recording ten long-lived antediluvian kings ruling for astronomical spans (*saroi*) down to Xisuthrus (Ziusudra/Utnapishtim). It narrated the Great Flood, the preservation of animal life in a great vessel, and the burial of sacred cuneiform tablets at Sippar, before chronicling postdiluvian dynastic sequences down to the era of Nabonassar (747 BCE).
3. **Book Three: From Nabonassar to Alexander**: Covering the Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, and Achaemenid empires, this book provided detailed accounts of rulers such as Tiglath-Pileser III, Sennacherib, Nabopolassar, and Nebuchadnezzar II (including the construction of the Hanging Gardens of Babylon), concluding with the Persian conquest and the arrival of Alexander the Great.

## Transmission and Fragmentary Survival

Because the original manuscript did not survive antiquity, modern knowledge of the *Babyloniaca* depends on a complex chain of intermediate citations. In the first century BCE, the polymath Alexander Polyhistor compiled an epitome of Berossus. Polyhistor's compilation was later utilized by the Jewish historian Flavius Josephus (in *Antiquities of the Jews* and *Against Apion*) to prove the antiquity of Near Eastern records, and by the Christian apologist Eusebius of Caesarea (in his *Chronicle* and *Praeparatio Evangelica*) to correlate biblical chronology with secular pagan timelines. Through Eusebius, fragments were subsequently preserved in Armenian translations and by Byzantine chronographers such as George Syncellus.
