# Pure Theory of Law (Reine Rechtslehre)

The principal statement of **Hans Kelsen**'s legal theory, first published in German in 1934, then reformulated and greatly expanded in a second edition in 1960. The second edition was later translated into English as *Pure Theory of Law* by Max Knight in 1967.

## The Central Thesis

The book proposes a project for establishing a **pure legal science** (*Reine Rechtslehre*), whose subject is limited to the **validity of legal norms and their formal structure**, methodologically isolated from every political, moral or sociological interpretation of their content. The separation between "legal science" (*Rechtswissenschaft*) and the sociology of law or the philosophy of justice is the cornerstone of the whole project.

## The Argumentative Structure: The Hierarchy of Norms and the Grundnorm

Kelsen builds his system on a hierarchical gradation of norms (*Stufenbau der Rechtsordnung*, a theory first developed by his student Adolf Merkl). Each norm derives its validity from a higher norm: the judicial decision derives its validity from the law, and the law from the constitution. This chain reaches the **basic norm** (*Grundnorm*), presupposed logically at the apex of the pyramid. The basic norm is a norm whose validity is not derived from a higher norm, since none exists. It is instead presupposed at the outset as a logical condition for interpreting any system of norms as a legally binding system, rather than as mere naked material coercion. The book elaborates this hierarchical structure with considerable formal precision, treating how legal validity passes from the constitution to ordinary legislation, then to executive regulations, and then to individual judicial decisions.

## The State and International Law: Rejecting Dualism

The book treats the theory of the state, as a legal order rather than a metaphysical entity independent of law. This thesis dissolves the traditional distinction between "state" and "law" by reducing the former to the latter. It also treats international law, which Kelsen, unlike many of his contemporaries, regarded as a genuine legal system, though less centralized and with weaker enforcement mechanisms than domestic law. He rejects the traditional **dualism** between domestic and international law as two separate systems, and proposes instead a normative **unity** (*monism*), in which domestic and international law form a single system; Kelsen's preferred version gives primacy to international law, at the apex of the legal pyramid.

## A Comparison with His Earlier Project: The Basic Norm versus the General Theory

The second edition of this book is a more mature reformulation, more focused on pure method, than Kelsen's earlier English-language work *General Theory of Law and State* (1945), which had a broader scope in treating questions of political state theory. Here the focus is limited to justifying the methodological project of a pure legal science in itself. This makes the two books complementary rather than identical, despite sharing the essence of the theory.

## Reception and Critique

The project faced criticism from two main directions. **Legal realists** objected that isolating the validity of a norm from its actual social effectiveness (whether it is actually obeyed) empties the analysis of practical realism. **Natural law advocates** objected that the notion of a presupposed basic norm without moral content logically permits the legitimation of any effective legal system, however unjust, so long as it has a minimal social effectiveness. Kelsen addressed this objection by discussing how a successful revolution or coup changes which basic norm is presupposed, in line with the principle of effectiveness.

## The Context of Composition: Intellectual and Political Exile

Kelsen had been the principal drafter of the constitution of the Republic of Austria (1920) and a judge of its Constitutional Court until he was removed from the court in 1930 for political reasons. He then taught at Cologne, from which he was dismissed in 1933 after the Nazi seizure of power. He wrote the first edition in 1934 after moving to Geneva, later taught in Prague, and ultimately settled in the United States (University of California, Berkeley), where the greatly expanded second edition (1960) was prepared. This turbulent political context partly explains Kelsen's methodical insistence on isolating "legal science" from any particular political or ideological content, in an effort to establish objective legal knowledge that would withstand the fluctuations of political regimes themselves.

## Its Relationship to Kantian Philosophy

Kelsen draws his philosophical inspiration from Kantian critical philosophy and from Neo-Kantianism (especially Hermann Cohen). Just as Kant distinguished between the world of facts (*Sein*) and the world of obligation (*Sollen*), refusing to derive one logically from the other, Kelsen insists that the validity of a legal norm (*Sollen*) cannot be derived from purely social facts (*Sein*), such as people's actual obedience to it. It always requires an independent normative presupposition (the basic norm), which makes it possible to read the facts as legal norms.

## The 1934 and 1960 Editions

Kelsen described the 1960 German edition, in its preface, as a complete reworking of the subjects treated in 1934 and a considerable widening of their range. Matthias Jestaedt's comparison of the two German texts finds that the main text of 1960 is about four times as long as the 1934 book, and about five times as long once the new appendix on the problem of justice is counted. That appendix is absent from the first edition. The sequence of argument stays largely the same, but the chapter count changes: two chapters of 1934 are joined, and the old third chapter is absorbed into other chapters, so that nine chapters become eight. Jestaedt judges most of the added material to be elaboration and greater precision rather than wholly new themes. Max Knight, translating the 1960 text, presents the second edition as an attempt to work through the problems of a general theory of law and to locate legal science among the sciences, while treating the underlying theory as continuous with the 1934 statement. His notes mark the main shifts. The English *Pure Theory of Law* (1967) follows this second edition, not the shorter 1934 book.

## Distinguishing the Basic Norm from the First Historical Constitution

Kelsen clarifies that the "basic norm" is not the first historical constitution of the state, which is a historical fact. It is a **transcendental-logical presupposition** that makes it possible to interpret that historical constitution as legally binding at all. This precise distinction between the historical fact and the normative presupposition is what distinguishes his theory from any sociological attempt to explain the legitimacy of law merely by its social effectiveness.
