# Politics of Piety: The Islamic Revival and the Feminist Subject

Saba Mahmood's principal book (2005, Princeton University Press), the fruit of her ethnographic fieldwork among women of the "da'wa movement" in the mosques of Cairo during the 1990s.

## Definition

The book follows the circles of women's religious study in the mosques of Cairo, where participants voluntarily seek to refine the self through repeated bodily and emotional practices as a path to building virtue, not as a constraint imposed from outside.

## Historical Context

Egypt has seen since the 1970s a popular religious revival, including what Mahmood calls the women's mosque movement, part of the broader "da'wa" (call to piety) current. It is a broad social phenomenon that went beyond the political frameworks of organized Islamic movements into daily popular religious practices. It included especially the spread of women's religious study circles in mosques and the widespread wearing of the veil among women from diverse class and educational backgrounds. Mahmood conducted her field research in this context in the 1990s, following specific women's groups in Cairo through long-term participant observation.

## Main Ideas and Method

Mahmood argues that the dominant liberal feminist theoretical frameworks, which link agency to resistance and liberation from imposed social norms, are unable to understand what these women are doing. They voluntarily practice disciplined prayer, modesty and the veil as bodily-ethical trainings aimed at shaping the virtuous self from within, not as submission to external coercive authority. Mahmood borrows the Aristotelian concept of "virtue" as reinterpreted by Alasdair MacIntyre, and employs Michel Foucault's analysis of technologies of the self, to propose an alternative concept of agency. This concept does not assume in advance that individual liberation from norms is the only valid measure of human self-agency. It suggests that agency can also be realized through the disciplined, voluntary adherence to an ethical norm that is actively and not passively re-adopted by the self.

## Principal Works and Figures

Saba Mahmood (1962–2018) was a Pakistani-American anthropologist and professor at the University of California, Berkeley. She was a prominent voice in the critique of secular liberal feminism within the anthropology of religion. An important article by her in *Cultural Anthropology* in 2001 preceded the book and carried the seeds of the same argument. Her work stands in critical dialogue with Talal Asad's anthropology of the secular and of religious discipline, and with Michel Foucault.

## Impact and Criticism

The book has been an influential and much-debated work in the anthropology of religion and feminist theory since its publication. It challenged entrenched assumptions in Western liberal feminist thought about the inevitable link between agency and liberation from religion. Liberal feminists criticized it for the possibility that its analysis might provide a theoretical cover for justifying actual social restrictions imposed on women. Others defended it as a serious attempt to take religious agents seriously, apart from prejudging them from outside their own ethical framework.

## Relation to Arab-Islamic Heritage

Mahmood draws on the classical Islamic ethical heritage in her analysis, referring to classical Islamic writing on the cultivation of the self, to show that the practices of the women she studied can be understood in relation to a long Islamic ethical tradition that links repeated bodily discipline with the building of inner virtue. Mahmood sees this link as absent from modern Western theoretical frameworks, which usually separate the body and ethics sharply.

## Additional Notes on Ritual Performance

Mahmood documents the details of the bodily training that participants undergo in study circles: repeating the movements of prayer until they become automatic behavior that does not require continuous conscious thought, and training facial features in the expressions of modesty and humility as acquired skills refined through repeated practice, not ready-made innate traits. Here Mahmood draws on the older Aristotelian notion of *habitus*, an excellence acquired through repeated practice, which also appears in Islamic thought (as *malaka* in Ibn Khaldun), and distinguishes it from Pierre Bourdieu's use of the term: bodily habituation can be a conscious, intended act that the individual pursues to build a specific virtue, not mainly the unconscious social conditioning that Bourdieu's analysis emphasises.

## Additional Notes on the Da'wa Movement's Context

Mahmood describes how the da'wa movement grew in Egypt as a decentralized network of home and mosque study circles, often led by women preachers who were not formally trained in the religious sciences. This made the movement a parallel educational setting alongside formal religious institutions such as al-Azhar, giving women a space for religious learning and teaching outside frameworks that had historically been less open to them.
