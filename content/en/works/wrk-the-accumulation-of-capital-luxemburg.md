# The Accumulation of Capital

**Rosa Luxemburg** published her book *The Accumulation of Capital: A Contribution to the Economic Explanation of Imperialism* in 1913. It is her longest and most theoretically ambitious economic work. In it she sought to fill what she saw as an unresolved gap in Karl Marx's analysis of capital accumulation in Volume II of *Capital*.

## The problem that drove the book: how is surplus value actually realized?

Luxemburg started from a precise analytical problem. In his schemes of expanded reproduction in Volume II of *Capital*, Marx built a hypothetical closed system consisting of only two sectors (the production of means of production and the production of consumer goods), which exchange with one another in balance. Luxemburg observed that this model, if it were confined to capitalists and workers alone with no other parties, faces a structural problem: how can the full value of the surplus produced be **realized** in money, rather than merely being produced as commodities, when workers consume only a limited part of it with their wages, and capitalists cannot consume the remainder of the surplus themselves, since they seek to reinvest it rather than consume it?

## The proposed solution: the necessity of non-capitalist markets

Luxemburg answers with a radical thesis: capitalism structurally needs **non-capitalist environments**, such as traditional agrarian economies and colonies that have not yet been fully drawn into capitalist relations of production, to buy its surplus output, which its closed domestic economy cannot fully absorb. From this starting point Luxemburg concludes that **colonial and imperialist expansion** is not an incidental political option that capitalism can do without, but a structural existential condition for the continuation of the accumulation process itself. Exhausting these non-capitalist markets gradually, as global colonization completes, would eventually lead capitalism to a final structural crisis that cannot be overcome.

## Documentary history of contemporary colonialism

The book also includes an extensive historical analysis of contemporary colonial examples, including British colonialism in India, the opium wars in China, and French colonialism in Algeria. Luxemburg uses these as empirical evidence for her theoretical thesis: that colonial expansion serves specifically the need of European capitalism for new external markets and cheap sources of raw materials, rather than being merely an expansionist political or cultural tendency independent of economic logic.

## The critical reception: Bukharin and others

The book faced sharp methodological criticism from other Marxists, including Otto Bauer, Karl Kautsky's circle and, most prominently in later years, Nikolai Bukharin (*Imperialism and the Accumulation of Capital*, 1924). They argued that Luxemburg made errors in applying Marx's reproduction models, and that capitalism could in principle realize its surplus value without an inevitable need for non-capitalist outside markets, so long as capitalist investment itself continued to expand (that is, through purely internal capitalist expansion). Nevertheless, the book's general analysis, namely the structural link between imperialist expansion and internal economic needs of capitalism, remained an influential reference in later Marxist theories of imperialism and dependency.

## Its position within the trilogy of classical theories of imperialism

Together with Rudolf Hilferding's *Finance Capital* (1910) and Lenin's *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917), this book is conventionally grouped among the classic Marxist works on imperialism. Lenin, however, did not adopt her central thesis, and her account of imperialism differs from his and from Hilferding's.

## The context of writing

Luxemburg wrote this work while teaching economics at the Social Democratic Party school in Berlin, in the context of the broader debate within the German socialist movement over whether capitalism was moving towards breakdown, as Marx had predicted, or adapting and stabilizing, as Eduard Bernstein argued in his revisionism (which she had attacked earlier in *Social Reform or Revolution*, 1899). As a defender of revolutionary Marxism, Luxemburg sought to present an economic account of why capitalism faces structural limits (the exhaustion of non-capitalist markets), which would make its eventual breakdown a long-run tendency rather than a remote theoretical possibility.

## Immediate and sharp critical reception

On its publication in 1913, the book provoked sharp debate within German and international Marxist circles. Critics from within the Marxist current itself (not only from her reformist opponents) accused it of technical errors in applying the reproduction schemes Marx had developed. This led Luxemburg to write a reply, the *Anti-Critique* (written in 1915 while she was in prison and published only posthumously in 1921), to defend her thesis. This reflects the vitality of the internal theoretical debate the book provoked from the moment of its first publication.

## Late translations and later revival of interest

The book was not translated into English until 1951, decades after its German publication, which delayed its diffusion in English-speaking academic circles. The thesis later experienced a renewed academic revival of interest in the context of subsequent debates about globalization, dependency and uneven development in the second half of the twentieth century.
