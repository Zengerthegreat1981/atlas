# Majallat al-Ahkam al-Adliyya (The Mecelle)

The first modern attempt to codify Hanafi jurisprudence in the form of numbered legal articles on the European pattern. It was drafted by an Ottoman committee chaired by **Ahmed Cevdet Pasha** between 1869 and 1876, within the framework of the Ottoman **Tanzimat** reforms, and it became the first codified Islamic civil code in the modern sense of the term.

## Historical Context: The Ottoman Tanzimat

The Majalla came in the context of a broad wave of Ottoman reforms known as the **Tanzimat** (formally begun with the Gülhane Rescript in 1839). Through them the Ottoman state sought to modernize its administrative and legal institutions on the European model, without entirely abandoning Islamic legal reference in the field of civil transactions. This contrasted with other fields, such as penal and commercial law, in which the Ottoman state adopted codes derived directly from French law.

## The Jurisprudential Sources of the Majalla

In selecting the rulings to be formulated as articles, the drafting committee drew on the accumulated heritage of Hanafi jurisprudence, especially Ibn Abidin's commentary *Radd al-Muhtar ala al-Durr al-Mukhtar*, which was regarded as a reliable summary of what the school had practically settled on by the late Ottoman period. It also drew on the Hanafi literature of legal maxims, most prominently Ibn Nujaym's *al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir*, from which, together with other Hanafi works, the introductory articles of the Majalla (article 1 defines fiqh; articles 2–100 set out ninety-nine maxims) drew in large part. These articles set out a number of overarching juristic maxims before entering into the substantive details of contracts, obligations, and property.

## Structure and Content

The Majalla consists of sixteen books, covering the main areas of Hanafi civil transactions: sales, leases, suretyship, delegation of debt, pledge, deposits, gifts, usurpation and damage, interdiction and coercion, partnership, agency, settlement and release, acknowledgment, claims, evidence and oath, and adjudication. It totals some 1,851 articles, drafted in concise, clear legal language on the European pattern, unlike the lengthy style of traditional juristic opinion (fatwa) or commentary.

## Its Impact and Extension

The Majalla was applied in the Nizamiye courts, the modern Ottoman civil courts that operated alongside the traditional Sharia courts. After the fall of the Ottoman Empire, it continued to be applied, or parts of it did, in a number of successor Arab states (Iraq, Syria, Lebanon, Jordan, Palestine, and Kuwait) for long decades. Modern civil codes gradually replaced it in these states, such as the Syrian civil code of 1949 and the Iraqi civil code of 1951, both influenced by the Egyptian civil code drafted by al-Sanhuri (1948, in force 1949), which itself was never based on the Majalla. In the Republic of Turkey itself, the Majalla was abolished in 1926, when the Swiss-based Turkish Civil Code came into force, as part of Kemalist secularization.

## Selection Among Opinions Within a Single Hanafi School

A notable methodological feature of the committee's work is that it did not merely translate prevailing Hanafi jurisprudence mechanically into legal articles. Where several narrations existed on one question within the Hanafi school, it performed a function of preference among them, generally choosing the view most practically suited to speedy modern judicial application, not necessarily the historically most famous view or the one strongest in pure usul evidence. This makes the Majalla the product of a conscious legislative choice, rather than a passive codification of what had already been settled. That choice remained confined within the limits of the Hanafi school alone, without drawing on other schools. This contrasts with the later Ottoman Family Law of 1917, which adopted an eclectic method of combining and choosing among the four schools.

## A Critical Note

Historians of codification observe that transforming a flexible, cumulative Hanafi jurisprudence, which allowed multiple adopted views depending on time, place, and the jurist, into a single binding legal text reduced the room for traditional interpretive flexibility. On the other hand, it supplied legal certainty and procedural clarity that were not as easily available in the traditional, uncodified system of fatwa and adjudication. This trade-off recurs in all subsequent modern projects to codify Islamic jurisprudence.

## Ahmed Cevdet Pasha's Standing

Ahmed Cevdet Pasha (1822–1895) was the central figure in this project. He was a religious scholar, jurist, and prominent Ottoman historian who held high judicial and ministerial posts. He combined a deep traditional juristic formation with broad familiarity with European legal systems, which qualified him to lead a project seeking to reconcile Hanafi juristic authenticity with modern legal drafting. In his time he opposed calls within the Ottoman state to adopt the French Civil Code in full instead of codifying Hanafi jurisprudence locally. His project succeeded in preserving an Islamic reference for civil legislation despite the rising Westernizing pressure of that period.
