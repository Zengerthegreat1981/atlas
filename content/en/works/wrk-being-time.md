# Being and Time: Dasein, Temporality, and Being-toward-Death

*Being and Time* (1927) by Martin Heidegger is one of the most influential and demanding texts in the background of existential philosophy. It is not a book about the human being but about the meaning of Being itself. The human being enters it because he is the one entity for whom his own being is a question — *Dasein*, being-there — and is therefore taken as the entry point, not the subject. The thesis is that the understanding of Being must begin not from the analysis of objects present before us but from the analysis of the structure of this questioning being, in what Heidegger calls the existential analytic of Dasein.

## Structure: An Unfinished Project

Only the first two of the six planned divisions (the preparatory analysis of Dasein and the interpretation of Dasein as temporality) were published — the book is structurally incomplete, and the part that was to re-read the history of Western philosophy in the light of the question of Being was never written. The published divisions analyse the structures of everyday being: being-in-the-world, not an inside and an outside placed side by side but a single unitary structure; care (*Sorge*) as the unifying structure of concern with the world and anxiety about the self; *das Man*, "the They," the anonymous collective into which one dissolves; understanding and mood (*Befindlichkeit*). It then re-reads these structures from the angle of time and finitude, and it is there that the analysis of being-toward-death falls. Heidegger published the book in 1927 in Husserl's phenomenological yearbook, hurried into print for academic reasons — the confirmation of his professorship — and that haste is one of the reasons commonly given for its remaining unfinished. It began within Husserlian phenomenology and then broke with it radically, making the ontological question, not pure phenomenological description, the centre.

## Four Concepts the Therapist Uses

Four concepts from the book do the most work in the clinic. *Being-in-the-world* dissolves the picture of a subject sealed inside a mind looking out at an external world; the person is always already involved with a world that matters to him. *Das Man* names the levelling-down of everyday life, in which one does what "one" does, thinks what "one" thinks, and loses oneself in the anonymous; inauthenticity is this unreflective absorption, not moral failure. *Thrownness* (*Geworfenheit*) names the fact that each person finds himself in a situation he did not choose — a body, a history, a language, a time — and must take it up. *Anxiety* (*Angst*) is not a symptom but a disclosure: in anxiety the familiar world slips away and the nothingness at the heart of existence shows itself, revealing Dasein to itself. The most commonly misunderstood term is *authenticity* (*Eigentlichkeit*): it is not moral sincerity, not individuality, and not "be yourself." It is taking over one's own being as one's own, above all in anticipating one's death. Translating it into a motivational slogan is the most widespread distortion of the book. The practical way into it is not from the beginning: the sections on *das Man* and on anxiety, then the section on being-toward-death, are the working entrance, and reading it whole without a guide is usually a waste of time.

## Reception: Admiration, Critique, and the Turn

The book was received from its appearance with exceptional resonance in German circles. Karl Löwith, a student of Heidegger, later described it as having overturned the philosophical climate, yet Löwith himself became one of its sharpest critics, arguing that the concepts of decision and authenticity carried a rigid subjectivism and that the absence of an ethical dimension and of relation to the Other emptied the analysis of social content. Emmanuel Levinas, an early interpreter of Heidegger for French readers, later turned away from it, insisting on the priority of ethics over ontology. Theodor Adorno criticised the language of authenticity as a jargon concealing its ideological structure. Heidegger himself stopped completing the project after the *Kehre*, the turn of the 1930s, moving from the analysis of Dasein to thinking about the history of Being, as he explained in the *Letter on Humanism* (1947).

## The Clinic, the Black Notebooks, and the Limits

Everything in the book is ontological, not psychological, and its transfer to the clinic is the subject of the oldest dispute in the school — between Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss within *Daseinsanalyse*. Its concepts reached psychiatry through both men, and the Zollikon seminars Heidegger held at Boss's invitation (1959–1969) helped shape that analysis; other students took other paths — Hans-Georg Gadamer in hermeneutics, Hannah Arendt in political philosophy, Herbert Marcuse and Sartre in Marxism and existentialism, Foucault and Derrida in the critique of metaphysics. The book reached Arabic early through Abdel Rahman Badawi, whose *Existential Time* (1943) expounded Heidegger's philosophy in detail, and the complete Arabic translation, *Al-Kaynuna wa-l-zaman*, by Fathi al-Miskini, appeared from Dar al-Kitab al-Jadid al-Muttahida in Beirut in 2012. The debate deepened after the publication of the *Black Notebooks* (2014), which revealed antisemitic remarks in Heidegger's private notebooks and his association with Nazism from 1933, renewing the question of the relation between authenticity and collective destiny (*Geschick*) and those positions. Historians do not agree on one answer: some hold that the fundamental ontology cannot be reduced to its author's politics, others that its very structure is open to that bias. The book also passes over the body, sex, and the tangible Other — gaps Sartre and Merleau-Ponty tried to fill — and its treatment of death concerns my own death, not the death of others.
