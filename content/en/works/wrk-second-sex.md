# The Second Sex

*The Second Sex* (1949), by the French philosopher Simone de Beauvoir, is one of the founding works of modern feminism and among the most influential books of the twentieth century. Published in French as *Le Deuxième Sexe* by Gallimard, it asks a question that had rarely been posed systematically: what does it mean to be a woman? Beauvoir's answer — that woman is made, not born, and that she has been constructed as the Other of man — reshaped philosophy, sociology, and feminist politics.

## Historical Context and Publication

Beauvoir wrote *The Second Sex* in post-war France, a society in which women had only just won the vote, in 1944, and in which the return to domesticity after the war was in full swing. Beauvoir, companion of Jean-Paul Sartre and a central figure of French existentialism, was already known as a novelist and philosopher. The book appeared in two volumes in 1949 and caused an immediate scandal: it was condemned in Catholic circles, later placed on the Vatican's Index of Prohibited Books, and attacked for its frank discussion of female sexuality, lesbianism, and abortion. The first English translation, by Howard M. Parshley in 1953, was heavily abridged and flawed, shaping Anglophone reception for decades until the unabridged translation by Constance Borde and Sheila Malovany-Chevallier appeared in 2009.

## Thesis and Core Argument

Beauvoir's central claim is that woman is the Other. Drawing on Hegel's account of the master and the slave and on Sartre's existentialism, she argues that man has positioned himself as the Subject, the Absolute, the essential human being, while woman is defined only in relation to him — as the inessential, the relative, the second sex. Unlike other oppressed groups, women are dispersed among men and bound to them by intimacy and dependence, which has made collective resistance harder. Beauvoir examines why this subordination persists: biology, she insists, does not determine destiny, but the facts of reproduction have been used to justify confinement; economic dependence, legal inequality, and cultural myth all conspire to keep women in "immanence" — a life of repetition and passivity — rather than "transcendence," the free projection of oneself into the future.

## Structure and Method

The book is organised into two volumes. The first, "Facts and Myths," examines the explanations offered for women's subordination: biology, psychoanalysis, and historical materialism are each found wanting, and a historical survey traces women's situation from prehistory to the present, followed by an analysis of the myths of woman in literature and culture. The second volume, "Lived Experience," follows a woman's life from childhood through adolescence, sexual initiation, marriage, motherhood, and old age, drawing on memoirs, novels, case histories, and her own observations to show how femininity is learned and lived. Beauvoir's method is phenomenological and interdisciplinary: she combines philosophical analysis with historical and literary evidence and with the close description of lived experience, treating women's own accounts as a source of knowledge that philosophy had ignored.

## Case Material and Later Studies

The second volume works with concrete material, though not with systematic sampling. Beauvoir draws on psychiatric and psychoanalytic case histories, including those reported by Wilhelm Stekel and Helene Deutsch, on diaries such as those of Marie Bashkirtseff and Sophia Tolstoy, and on the writing of Colette. These show girls and women describing puberty, sexual initiation, marriage and domestic work, but the sources are selective and lean toward literate and clinical subjects. Later research has examined similar processes directly. Karin Martin's observation of preschool classrooms in the United States, published in 1998, showed how routine adult instructions and classroom practices encouraged girls and boys to move, speak and occupy space differently. The study speaks to Beauvoir's claim that femininity is acquired, without testing her philosophy as a whole.

## Applications Across Disciplines

In anthropology, Sherry Ortner's essay on whether female is to male as nature is to culture took up Beauvoir's analysis to ask why women's subordination appears so widely. In philosophy, Iris Marion Young's "Throwing Like a Girl" (1980) combined Beauvoir's account of situation with Maurice Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology of the body to analyse feminine movement. In theology, Mary Daly's *The Church and the Second Sex* (1968) applied the critique to Catholic teaching and institutions. In literary studies, Beauvoir's reading of male authors' myths of woman is a precedent for feminist criticism.

## Key Concepts

*The Second Sex* introduced concepts that remain central to feminist sociology. "The Other" names the position of the group defined only in relation to a dominant Subject. "Immanence" and "transcendence" describe the existential contrast between a life confined to repetition and a life of free self-projection. "Becoming a woman" captures the insight that femininity is not a natural essence but a social achievement, produced through upbringing, education, and the constant pressure of expectation. Beauvoir's analysis of "bad faith" — the refusal of freedom — explains how the oppressed can collude in their own oppression, and her account of the "eternal feminine" as a myth exposes how culture naturalises what history has made.

## Reception and Influence

*The Second Sex* was a landmark whose influence is hard to overstate. It shaped the second-wave feminism of the 1960s and 1970s, and its argument that gender is made rather than given prefigures the work of Judith Butler and the sociology of gender. It influenced feminist theory, the sociology of the family, and the study of sexuality, and it remains a standard text in courses on social theory and gender. Beauvoir's insistence that women's experience is a source of knowledge helped legitimate standpoint approaches in sociology, and her analysis of the body, of ageing, and of the double burden of work and domesticity opened research agendas that continue today.

## Critical Assessments

Critics have engaged with the book from many directions. Margaret Simons and others have shown that Parshley's translation cut a substantial part of the text and distorted Beauvoir's philosophical vocabulary, a finding that reshaped Beauvoir scholarship. Some feminist critics argue that Beauvoir's account of motherhood and of the female body is ambivalent, at times seeming to endorse a masculine ideal of transcendence and to devalue women's embodied experience. Others note that her analysis centres the experience of white, middle-class French women and pays little attention to race, colonialism, and class — a limitation later Black feminist thinkers addressed directly. Her treatment of lesbianism has also been criticised as dated.

## Continuing Relevance

*The Second Sex* remains a living text. Its central question — what does it mean to be a woman? — is asked anew in debates about gender identity, reproductive rights, care work, and the persistence of the pay gap and of violence against women. Sociologists continue to use Beauvoir's framework to study how femininity is produced in childhood, in workplaces, and in intimate life, and how women negotiate the tension between immanence and transcendence in their own biographies. The book's insistence that biology is not destiny and that freedom must be claimed rather than granted remains the starting point for feminist sociology.

## Further Points of Argument and Debate

Beauvoir ties women's liberation to economic independence through work, while holding that work alone is not enough without wider social change. Critics have also questioned her comparisons between women, Black people and Jews, which can obscure those who belong to several subordinated groups at once. Other scholars defend the book's account of ambiguity and embodiment as more complex than hostile readings of its treatment of motherhood and the body allow.
