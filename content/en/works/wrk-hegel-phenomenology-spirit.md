# Phenomenology of Spirit (Phänomenologie des Geistes) by G.W.F. Hegel (1807)

The first major book of Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831), issued in Bamberg and Würzburg in 1807 by the publisher Joseph Anton Goebhardt. It is a book unlike familiar philosophical treatises: no theory of knowledge presented directly and no system of principles, but a narrative of the adventure of consciousness as it tests its own criteria, and for this reason it became known for its difficulty and for the many contending readings of it.

## The Central Thesis

The book is a biography of the formation of consciousness, not the presentation of a ready-made system: Hegel follows the course of "natural consciousness" as it tries out a series of claims to knowledge, discovering at each station that its criterion of certainty contradicts its actual experience, so that it is forced to move beyond its position to a higher one. This movement itself, not only its final result, is the thesis of the book: the truth is a "whole" that is not grasped at one stroke but appears only through the course of its internal contradiction and its overcoming (Aufhebung), reaching what Hegel calls "absolute knowing", where consciousness's awareness of its object coincides with its awareness of itself. In the Preface, written after the book was completed, Hegel declares that the true is the whole, that substance must be understood as subject too, and that philosophy is not extracted by quick definition but by "the effort of the concept".

## The Structure of the Work

The book proceeds from the simplest forms of certainty (immediate sense-certainty, then perception, then understanding) to self-consciousness, where the chapter on "the independence and dependence of self-consciousness" falls, containing the dialectic of master and slave: two parties struggle for recognition, one submits to the other, but labor reverses the balance of power, since the slave through his work on the thing gains a deeper awareness of himself that the master, content with immediate consumption, lacks. This chapter is followed by an analysis of Stoicism and Skepticism and then the "unhappy consciousness", where religious consciousness is divided against itself between a transcendent, fixed essence and a changing self unable to reach it. The book then moves to reason in its forms (observing reason and active reason), then spirit (with its ethical and civilizational stations, among them his reading of Antigone in the ethical world, his treatment of the Enlightenment and absolute freedom and the French revolutionary Terror, then morality, conscience and the beautiful soul), then religion (natural religion, the religion of art, and revealed religion), then absolute knowing.

## The Historical Context of Publication

Hegel wrote it between 1805 and 1806 in Jena and finished his manuscript, according to a circulating account, shortly before the battle of Jena in 1806, in which he witnessed Napoleon's army enter the city, and he describes in a letter to his friend Niethammer seeing the Emperor, "the world-soul", on horseback. The book appeared in 1807. It was his first completed philosophical book, and he originally intended it as an "introduction" to his more comprehensive philosophical system before it became a self-standing work that Hegel would later surpass in his logical and encyclopedic project. In its intellectual setting the book was a response to Fichte and Schelling, especially to the "intellectual intuition" from which Schelling begins his system directly; Hegel chose to show the road that leads to the absolute standpoint instead of presupposing it.

## Reception and Criticism

The French reading of the book in the 20th century (its most prominent voice being Alexandre Kojève in his lectures between 1933 and 1939, published in 1947 under the editorship of Raymond Queneau) gave the master-slave dialectic a central place that influenced Sartre, Lacan and Foucault, to the point that it sometimes became the best-known reading of the book in France more than its original German reading. Jean Hyppolite, in *Genesis and Structure of Hegel's Phenomenology of Spirit* (1946), offered a reading closer to the whole text and more tied to history. Karl Marx recovered the idea of alienation and labor from this book specifically in the *1844 Manuscripts* and reformulated it materialistically, although he counted the book as "the birthplace and secret of Hegelian philosophy" with criticism of its turning labor into merely mental labor. The book has traditionally been accused of the extreme obscurity of its style, and has remained a point of dispute between a "metaphysical" reading of it (an actual historical transformation of absolute spirit) and a more recent "non-metaphysical" reading that focuses on the logic of recognition and normativity independently of any commitment to a cosmic spirit, represented in the works of Robert Pippin and Terry Pinkard.

## Arabic Echo and Arabic Translations

An Arabic translation by Naji al-Awnali is reported to have been published by the Arab Organization for Translation (Beirut, 2006), with an introduction by Imam Abd al-Fattah Imam; its bibliographic details should be checked against the volume itself. In Arabic scholarship, Imam Abd al-Fattah Imam had the merit of introducing Hegel and his dialectical method in *The Dialectical Method in Hegel* (Cairo, 1969).

## Limits of the Thesis

The book has aroused structural objections: the continuation of the movement of consciousness toward "absolute knowing" presupposes a historical teleology that many reject, and also presupposes that the criterion by which consciousness tests its claims emerges from it and not from outside, which critics (from Kierkegaard to Adorno) regard as closing experience inside the system. The consistency of the book with the *Science of Logic* and its relation to the later *Encyclopedia* are also disputed among scholars, because Hegel himself no longer regarded the *Phenomenology* as a necessary introduction to his system.

## Influence in Psychology

The master-slave dialectic specifically, not the whole Hegelian system, later entered into the formation of the concepts of French psychoanalysis (Lacan) about desire and recognition, and influenced indirectly the theories of alienation and identity within social psychology through Marxist and existentialist mediation.
