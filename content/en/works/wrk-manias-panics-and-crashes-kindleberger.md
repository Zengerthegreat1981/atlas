# Manias, Panics, and Crashes: A History of Financial Crises

In 1978 the American economist **Charles Kindleberger**, professor of economics at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, published *Manias, Panics, and Crashes: A History of Financial Crises*. It is an encyclopaedic work surveying the history of major financial crises, from the South Sea bubble of the eighteenth century to crises of the twentieth century contemporary with the author. It searches for recurring patterns shared by these crises, which are separated in time and place, despite the many surface differences between their details.

## The Theoretical Framework: Minsky's Model of Financial Instability

Kindleberger builds the historical narrative on a model of financial instability drawn from Hyman Minsky, and on the lender-of-last-resort doctrine associated with Walter Bagehot. In the sequence Kindleberger uses, an external "displacement" creates new profit opportunities, such as a technical innovation or the opening of a new market. Credit expansion feeds a boom, then speculative "euphoria." "Financial distress" and profit-taking follow, as some investors sell. A "panic" can then produce a sharp fall in prices as many try to sell at once.

## The Recurrence of Patterns Across Centuries of Financial History

Kindleberger surveys in rich historical detail dozens of financial crises separated in time. These range from the Dutch tulip mania of the seventeenth century, to the South Sea and French Mississippi bubbles of the eighteenth century, up to the Wall Street crash of 1929 and multiple international banking crises of the twentieth century. He concludes that the surface details of each crisis (the commodity or asset that was speculated upon, the country, the specific financial instruments) keep changing throughout history. The deep psychological and institutional structure of the cycle of mania and panic, however, remains remarkably constant across centuries of financial and technological development.

## A Critique of the Efficient Markets Hypothesis

The book constitutes a strong, implicit historical and empirical critique of the "efficient markets" hypothesis prevalent in mainstream neoclassical academic finance. That hypothesis assumes that financial asset prices always rationally reflect all available information, and cannot systematically and persistently deviate from their true "fundamental" value. Kindleberger, drawing on the abundance of historical evidence he gathers, argues that financial markets are structurally and repeatedly prone to waves of relatively irrational collective behaviour (collective euphoria followed by collective panic). They are not subject only to the calm, continuous rational corrections that the classical theory of efficient markets assumes.

## The Lender of Last Resort and the Role of Central Banks

Kindleberger devotes an important analysis to the role of the "lender of last resort," meaning the central bank's readiness to intervene by injecting emergency liquidity during acute banking panics. The aim is to prevent a systemic collapse that reaches even fundamentally sound financial institutions. He surveys historical cases in which this intervention succeeded in containing the crisis, and others in which its absence or delay allowed the crisis to worsen catastrophically. The analysis has often been cited in discussions of central-bank interventions during the global financial crisis of 2008, thirty years after the book was published.

## Repeated Republication and Renewed Relevance

The book was reissued in several editions. Kindleberger revised it through the fourth edition (2000). Robert Aliber appears as co-author of the fifth edition (2005), prepared after Kindleberger's death in 2003, and revised later editions. Those updates discuss later crises, including the Asian crisis of 1997 and, in editions after 2008, the global financial crisis. The book is often reread when a new crisis occurs.

## Kindleberger's Practical Career Before Academia

Kindleberger's academic career at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology was preceded by a long practical experience in American government financial institutions. This included work at the Federal Reserve, the Bank for International Settlements and the US State Department, where he was involved in planning the Marshall Plan for the reconstruction of Europe after the Second World War. This direct field experience with the mechanisms of financial crises and economic recovery policies gave him a rare practical sense among purely academic economists. It shaped his analytical approach in this book, with a clear tendency toward concrete institutional history rather than the mathematical abstraction that increasingly dominated academic financial economics in his time.

## Its Influence on Behavioural and Financial Economics

Kindleberger's historical analysis, although it did not use advanced mathematical tools, is often associated with the themes of later behavioural finance, which studies with statistical and experimental tools how the decisions of real investors deviate from the rational model assumed by efficient-markets theory. Robert Shiller, among others, has drawn on Kindleberger's historical examples in his work on speculative bubbles.

## Kindleberger and Minsky: Biographical Notes

Charles Poor Kindleberger (1910–2003) took his doctorate in economics at Columbia University in 1937. He worked at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the Bank for International Settlements and the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve, served in wartime intelligence and then in the State Department, and joined the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1948, where he spent the rest of his academic career. He wrote widely on international economics and economic history, including *The World in Depression, 1929–1939* (1973), which linked the severity of the slump to the absence of a country able and willing to stabilize the international system.

Hyman Philip Minsky (1919–1996) studied mathematics at the University of Chicago and received a doctorate in economics from Harvard, where he worked with Joseph Schumpeter and Wassily Leontief. He taught at Brown University, the University of California, Berkeley, and Washington University in St. Louis, and ended his career at the Levy Economics Institute of Bard College. His financial instability hypothesis, developed in *John Maynard Keynes* (1975) and *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986), holds that long periods of stability encourage increasingly fragile financing, moving from hedge to speculative and Ponzi positions.
