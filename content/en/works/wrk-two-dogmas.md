# Two Dogmas of Empiricism

## The Central Thesis

An essay, not a book, published by Quine in 1951 in the *Philosophical Review*, in which he attacks two dogmas that he regards as the foundation of logical positivism and most contemporary empiricism:

1. The analytic/synthetic distinction: the claim that there is a fundamental difference between propositions true by meaning alone (analytic) and propositions whose truth depends on facts (synthetic). Quine argues that every attempt to define "analyticity" ends in circularity: defining it by synonymy requires defining synonymy itself, and defining it by "semantical rules" presupposes the very concept that is to be defined.
2. Reductionism: the claim that every meaningful proposition is logically reducible to a construct of terms referring to immediate sensory data (on the pattern of Carnap's program in the *Logical Structure of the World*), so that each proposition can be confirmed or disconfirmed individually by reference to its own experience.

Quine's alternative to reductionism is confirmation holism: our scientific propositions are tested not one by one but as an interconnected system (an image Quine later called a "web of belief") that faces experience as a single body, so that when the web collides with a recalcitrant observation, "confidence" can be redistributed among any number of its elements, including, in principle, the most entrenched laws.

## The Structure of the Work

A short, tightly built essay that proceeds in six sections: the background of analyticity, then definition, then interchangeability, then semantical rules, then the verification theory and reductionism, and finally a sixth section titled "Empiricism without the Dogmas" that presents the alternative of confirmational holism. In the early sections Quine uses familiar examples, such as our saying that "all bachelors are unmarried," where truth seems to be by meaning alone, and then shows that the synonymy of "bachelor" and "unmarried man" requires a criterion of synonymy that does not depend on analyticity. He ends with a famous image: science as a field of force that touches experience only at its edges, and no result compels any particular proposition to be revised.

## The Historical Context of Publication

The essay was originally presented at a meeting of the American Philosophical Association in late 1950, and published in 1951 in the *Philosophical Review*. It is widely regarded as a major blow, from within the analytic tradition itself, to logical positivism. The target of the critique was Carnap and his book *Meaning and Necessity* (1947) and his conception of formal languages; Quine later developed many of his ideas (such as the thesis of the indeterminacy of translation) in his book *Word and Object* (1960), a later independent work whose contents are not attributed to this essay.

## Reception and Criticism

The essay provoked wide controversy in analytic philosophy. Paul Grice and Peter Strawson wrote "In Defense of a Dogma" (1956), arguing that the distinction rests on a settled linguistic practice even if definition fails; Carnap replied in later writings, and others defended the essay with varied replies. Donald Davidson and Thomas Kuhn drew on it for later positions in the philosophy of language and the philosophy of science, while Jerry Fodor and Ernest Lepore later criticized the holism it inspired. It is regarded today as one of the most influential texts in twentieth-century analytic philosophy, and it contributed to the rise of what was later called "Quinean naturalism," which dissolves the traditional boundaries between philosophy and empirical science. Researchers linked the holism thesis to that of Pierre Duhem, hence it was called the Duhem-Quine thesis, although Duhem confined it to physics.

## The Arabic Reading

The distinction between the analytic and the synthetic entered Arabic thought first through logical positivism, as Zaki Naguib Mahmoud presented it in *al-Mantiq al-Wad'i* (*Positive Logic*, 1951) as the basis for distinguishing meaningful propositions from others.

## Limits

Critics hold that Quine demands of the definition of analyticity what he does not demand of other concepts, such as the word "science" or "experience," and that the failure of a formal definition does not imply the disappearance of the difference in use. They also hold that holism may be read in a weak reading that holds of every scientific philosophy, or in a strong reading that commits to nothing being exempt from revision, and the author does not defend the second with an independent argument. For this reason the essay is more successful in shaking conviction in the distinction than in offering a completed alternative theory.
