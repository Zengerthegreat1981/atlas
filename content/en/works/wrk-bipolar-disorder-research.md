# Manic-Depressive Illness: Bipolar Disorders and Recurrent Depression

**Authors:** Frederick K. Goodwin and Kay Redfield Jamison
**Year of publication:** 1990 (first edition), 2007 (second edition)
**Publisher:** Oxford University Press
**Original language:** English

An encyclopedic academic reference in psychiatry, regarded as one of the most important reference books in the history of the study of bipolar disorder and recurrent depression. It was written by Frederick Goodwin, a psychiatric researcher and professor of psychiatry (formerly director of the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health, NIMH), together with Kay Redfield Jamison, professor of psychiatry at Johns Hopkins University, a clinical psychologist also known for her memoir of her personal experience with the illness, *An Unquiet Mind* (1995).

## Background: Melancholia and Mania before Kraepelin

The book rests on a long history that predates its publication. Rhazes, in *al-Hawi*, and Ibn Sina, in *al-Qanun*, described melancholia and mania; Ishaq ibn Imran wrote a treatise on melancholia; and Arabic medical sources discussed the alternation of elation and depression in some patients. Latin medicine then transmitted these descriptions to Europe. In the nineteenth century Jean-Pierre Falret described "circular insanity" in 1854, and Baillarger described "insanity with a double form" the same year. In 1899 Emil Kraepelin brought these forms together in the category of "manic-depressive insanity," distinguishing it from dementia praecox by its course. The book reviews this background in historical chapters, showing that Kraepelin unified the category, and that the modern term "bipolar" came after Karl Leonhard separated bipolar from unipolar disorder in 1957, followed by Angst and Perris in 1966.

## Content and Method

When it appeared in 1990, the book provided a comprehensive survey of the accumulated scientific literature on manic-depressive illness (bipolar disorder in its contemporary terminology). It combined three levels of analysis: biological and genetic evidence (twin and family studies, the neurochemical basis), epidemiological and clinical evidence (the natural course of the illness, relapse rates, and response to treatment), and the human and experiential dimension, documenting the experience of the illness as recounted by patients, their families, and the treating physicians themselves. This distinguished it from the dry academic references prevalent at the time. The second edition runs to more than a thousand pages and covers the spectrum from acute mania to recurrent depression, with chapters on suicide, creativity, and how the illness presents across the lifespan.

## Principal Theses

- **Recurrent illness and the course of relapse**: The book holds that bipolar disorder is a recurrent illness in most patients, and that the first episodes tend to multiply and draw closer together over time, so that long-term prevention becomes a primary goal rather than the treatment of the acute episode alone. It reviews the evidence on lithium in prevention and the more varied evidence for other mood stabilizers.
- **Suicide**: The book devotes considerable space to the risks of suicide, noting that its rate is very high compared with the general population and discussing the evidence that long-term treatment, especially with lithium, is associated with lower suicide rates. This is a central concern of Goodwin and Jamison's research.
- **Heredity**: It presents the results of twin and adoption studies that estimate a high heritability of the disorder, and points to multiple genes and to shared risk factors with schizophrenia.
- **Creativity**: It devotes a section to the relationship between intense mood and artistic and literary achievement, a subject of Jamison's research in her book *Touched with Fire* (1993), which preceded her *An Unquiet Mind*.

## The Second Edition (2007)

The second edition revised the biological and genetic literature accumulated over some seventeen years, with updated epidemiological, psychological, and clinical reviews. The edition included findings from neuroimaging studies, the role of atypical antipsychotics and anticonvulsants in treating mania and in prevention, and addressed bipolar II disorder and its broader spectra.

## Reception and Impact

The book was widely received as the standard reference of its field, and it became a text widely cited in both academic research and clinical training in psychiatry around the world. It remains one of the most comprehensive and frequently cited references on bipolar disorder, combining scientific review with attention to patients' experience.

## Limits and Critique

This entry is educational and not clinical advice; treatment statements describe the book's content and are not recommendations. A book of this size cannot by itself keep pace with the speed of research. The second edition appeared before the results of large-scale genome studies identifying genetic loci associated with the disorder had become clear, and before the diagnostic revisions in DSM-5. The authors' perspective leans toward the biomedical model, with a strong belief in the role of preventive medication, a perspective opposed by currents that hold psychological and social treatments deserve greater weight, and that social pressures and the pace of life affect relapse. It is also noted that Goodwin, one of the authors, was the subject of a U.S. Senate inquiry in 2008 that revealed his financial ties to pharmaceutical companies, which raised questions about the neutrality of some treatment recommendations, although the evidence the book presents is published and open to independent verification. The book is also dominated by a Western context and does not address the cultural expression of disordered mood.
