# Manic-Depressive Illness: Bipolar Disorders and Recurrent Depression

**Authors:** Frederick K. Goodwin and Kay Redfield Jamison
**Year of publication:** 1990 (first edition), 2007 (second edition)
**Publisher:** Oxford University Press
**Original language:** English

A long academic reference on manic-depressive illness, bipolar disorders and recurrent depression. It was written by Frederick K. Goodwin, a psychiatric researcher who directed the U.S. National Institute of Mental Health from 1992 to 1994, together with Kay Redfield Jamison, a clinical psychologist and professor of psychiatry at Johns Hopkins University, also known for the memoir *An Unquiet Mind* (1995).

## Background: Melancholia and Mania before Kraepelin

The book reviews a history older than its own argument. Greek and Arabic medical writing described melancholia and mania inside humoral theory: al-Razi's *al-Hawi*, Ibn Sina's *al-Qanun*, and Ishaq ibn Imran's treatise on melancholia (Kairouan, around 900). Those works are not a bipolar diagnosis. In 1854 Jean-Pierre Falret described *folie circulaire* and Jules Baillarger described *folie à double forme*. Emil Kraepelin, especially in the 1899 edition of *Psychiatrie*, gathered episodic mania, depression and mixed states under manic-depressive insanity and separated that group from dementia praecox by course. Karl Leonhard's *Aufteilung der endogenen Psychosen* (1957) distinguished bipolar from monopolar (unipolar) forms. Jules Angst and Carlo Perris, in separate 1966 studies, supplied family and course evidence for that separation. Goodwin and Jamison treat this sequence as the clinical background of the modern category.

## Content and Method

When it appeared in 1990, the book provided a comprehensive survey of the accumulated scientific literature on manic-depressive illness (bipolar disorder in its contemporary terminology). It combined three levels of analysis: biological and genetic evidence (twin and family studies, the neurochemical basis), epidemiological and clinical evidence (the natural course of the illness, relapse rates, and response to treatment), and the human and experiential dimension, documenting the experience of the illness as recounted by patients, their families, and the treating physicians themselves. This distinguished it from the dry academic references prevalent at the time. The second edition runs to more than a thousand pages and covers the spectrum from acute mania to recurrent depression, with chapters on suicide, creativity, and how the illness presents across the lifespan.

## Principal Theses

- **Recurrent illness and the course of relapse**: The book holds that bipolar disorder is a recurrent illness in most patients, and that the first episodes tend to multiply and draw closer together over time, so that long-term prevention becomes a primary goal rather than the treatment of the acute episode alone. It reviews the evidence on lithium in prevention and the more varied evidence for other mood stabilizers.
- **Suicide**: The book devotes considerable space to the risks of suicide, noting that its rate is very high compared with the general population and discussing the evidence that long-term treatment, especially with lithium, is associated with lower suicide rates. This is a central concern of Goodwin and Jamison's research.
- **Heredity**: It presents the results of twin and adoption studies that estimate a high heritability of the disorder, and points to multiple genes and to shared risk factors with schizophrenia.
- **Creativity**: It devotes a section to the relationship between intense mood and artistic and literary achievement, a subject of Jamison's research in her book *Touched with Fire* (1993), which preceded her *An Unquiet Mind*.

## The Second Edition (2007)

The second edition revised the biological and genetic literature accumulated over some seventeen years, with updated epidemiological, psychological, and clinical reviews. The edition included findings from neuroimaging studies, the role of atypical antipsychotics and anticonvulsants in treating mania and in prevention, and addressed bipolar II disorder and its broader spectra.

## Reception and Impact

The book was widely used in research and in specialist training as a large narrative review of manic-depressive illness. It is often cited for its historical chapters, its synthesis of course and suicide literature, and its attention to patients' accounts. It is a secondary synthesis, not a primary trial or a diagnostic manual.

## Limits and Critique

This entry is educational and not clinical advice; treatment statements describe the book's content and are not recommendations. A book of this size cannot by itself keep pace with the speed of research. The second edition appeared before the results of large-scale genome studies identifying genetic loci associated with the disorder had become clear, and before the diagnostic revisions in DSM-5. The authors' perspective leans toward the biomedical model, with a strong belief in the role of preventive medication, a perspective opposed by currents that hold psychological and social treatments deserve greater weight, and that social pressures and the pace of life affect relapse. In 2008 Senator Charles Grassley's inquiry into financial conflicts documented that Goodwin had received large payments from pharmaceutical companies, much of them for promotional lectures, which had not been disclosed on his radio program *The Infinite Mind*. That record is separate from the 1990 and 2007 books, but it is relevant to how readers weigh an author's treatment chapters. The studies the book cites remain published and open to checking on their own. The book is also written from North American and European clinical literature and says little about how disordered mood is named outside that setting.
