# The Mind of Primitive Man

*The Mind of Primitive Man* (1911) is a foundational statement of Franz Boas's position and a major break with the racial science of the nineteenth century. Based on lectures and essays and published by Macmillan, it argues that there is no hierarchy of human minds: the mental capacities of all peoples are the same, and the differences between societies are historical and cultural, not biological. In a period when scientific racism was mainstream, the book was a deliberate act of intellectual resistance.

Boas was a German-born scholar trained in physics and geography who turned to anthropology after fieldwork among the Inuit of Baffin Island in 1883-84, and later worked extensively with the Kwakwaka'wakw (Kwakiutl) and other peoples of the Northwest Coast. He built the anthropology programme at Columbia University and trained the generation — Ruth Benedict, Margaret Mead, Alfred Kroeber, Edward Sapir — that would dominate American anthropology. *The Mind of Primitive Man* distils the argument he had been making in lectures and essays since the 1890s into a single, systematic work.

## Thesis and Structure

The book's thesis is that culture, not race, explains human difference. Boas attacks the evolutionary anthropology of his predecessors, who had ranked societies from "savagery" through "barbarism" to "civilisation" and attributed the ranking to innate mental differences. He argues that the evidence points the other way: there is no demonstrated difference in mental capacity between peoples, and the apparent differences in achievement are explained by history, environment, and the accidents of cultural transmission.

A central chapter takes on the measurement of intelligence and the skull. Boas draws on his research on the bodily form of immigrants to the United States, which reported that the head shapes of children born in America differed measurably from those of their European-born parents, evidence in his view that even supposedly fixed racial traits respond to environment (the study was published in full in 1912). The argument was controversial at the time and has been debated ever since, but its direction was unmistakable: race, for Boas, was a plastic, historical phenomenon, not a biological destiny.

The book also develops Boas's theory of culture. Each culture, he argues, is a historical product, the result of diffusion, borrowing, invention, and accident, and it must be understood on its own terms rather than placed on a ladder of progress. The task of the anthropologist is to record each culture's particular history — its languages, its myths, its technologies — before generalising about human nature. This historical particularism became the methodological core of American anthropology for half a century.

## Language, Thought, and Race

One of the book's most influential arguments concerns language. Boas shows that the languages of Indigenous peoples are as complex, systematic, and expressive as European languages, and that the apparent "poverty" of a language — its lack of a word for some abstraction — reflects not mental deficiency but different cultural interests. A language obliges its speakers to attend to some distinctions and not others; the grammar of a language shapes habitual thought without limiting the capacity to think. This line of argument, developed further by his student Edward Sapir, was important to the development of linguistic anthropology and, indirectly, to later debates on linguistic relativity.

Boas also takes up the psychology of "primitive" thought, arguing that the reasoning of people in small-scale societies is not childlike or illogical but follows the same processes as modern reasoning, applied to different premises. Where earlier writers saw superstition, Boas saw tradition: beliefs persist not because minds are weak but because social life enforces them. The difference between the "primitive" and the "civilised" mind, he concludes, is a difference of knowledge and circumstance, not of kind.

## Reception and Influence

*The Mind of Primitive Man* was revised in 1938, and the revised edition — which Boas updated with new evidence on race, heredity, and environment — became the standard text. The book's influence on American anthropology is hard to overstate: it supplied the discipline's central doctrine, cultural relativism, and its central ethic, the refusal to rank peoples. It also shaped public debate. Boas's arguments were used by critics of racism, and the UNESCO statements on race of 1950 and 1951 reflect the anthropology that he and his students had established.

Criticism came from several directions. Later anthropologists found Boas's historical particularism too cautious: by refusing general theory, it left anthropology as a collection of particulars without explanation. His emphasis on culture as a bounded, integrated whole — later developed by Benedict into the "patterns of culture" — has been criticised for underplaying conflict, change, and the internal diversity of societies. And his own data on immigrant head form have been re-examined and qualified by later researchers.

Yet the book's central achievement stands. Before Boas, anthropology provided a scientific vocabulary for ranking peoples; after him, it provided a scientific vocabulary for refusing to. *The Mind of Primitive Man* is the book in which that reversal was argued, in detail and in public, and it remains a central text of anti-racist anthropology.

## Independence of Race, Language and Culture

Boas argued that variation within any population is large and that the ranges of different groups overlap. Physical type, language and culture do not change together: people can adopt a new language or customs without any corresponding biological change, and related populations can differ culturally. The immigrant study that informed the argument was conducted for the United States Immigration Commission. Historians also note that the book keeps some assumptions of its period, including comparisons of levels of civilisation and cautious remarks about possible average biological differences, so that it reads as a turning point containing tensions. The explicit formulation of cultural relativism owes much to Boas's students.
