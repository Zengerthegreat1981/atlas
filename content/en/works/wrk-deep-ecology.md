# Deep Ecology

## Origin of the Term and the Movement

The Norwegian philosopher Arne Naess coined the term "Deep Ecology" for the first time in his seminal article "The Shallow and the Deep, Long-Range Ecology Movement: A Summary" (1973). In it he distinguished between two currents in environmental thought. "Shallow ecology" fights pollution and the depletion of resources because they harm human health, welfare, and economic interests; it is conservation for the sake of humans. "Deep ecology," by contrast, rejects this "anthropocentrism" (Anthropocentrism) at its root. It proceeds from a metaphysical-ethical premise: living beings and entire ecological systems have intrinsic value (Intrinsic Value) independent of any utility to humans, and the human being is not the master of nature but one part of the wider cosmic web of life, not superior in worth to the rest of creation.

Naess did not present deep ecology as a single closed ethical theory, but as a flexible general "platform" (Platform) of principles that can be derived from different philosophical or religious foundations (Buddhist, Christian mystical, Spinozist, or purely secular). All of these converge on an eight-point platform that he formulated with the American philosopher George Sessions in 1984 (published in Devall and Sessions, 1985). The most important of them are: that the flourishing of human and non-human life has value in itself, regardless of its usefulness to humans; that the diversity and richness of life forms are values in themselves; that humans have no right to reduce this diversity except to meet vital needs; and that the flourishing of human and non-human life requires a substantial decline in population and in excessive economic growth.

## The "Greater Ecological Self" and Self-Realization

Naess developed a central concept, "Self-realization" (Self-realization), drawing on influences such as Spinoza, Gandhi and Mahayana Buddhist thought. According to it, when a person deepens their awareness of themselves, they discover that the narrow boundaries of the "ego" (self with a small "s") are a psychological illusion, and that their true identity extends to encompass the entire ecological system (the "greater Self," with a capital "S"). Harming nature therefore becomes harming the expanded self itself, not harming a separate "other." From this arises what he called "ecological identification" (Ecological Identification), a deep feeling of sympathy with non-human beings that makes the protection of nature spring from a sincere inner impulse rather than an external imposed duty.

## How Deep Ecology Differs from Other Environmental Ethics

Deep ecology is distinguished from the "land ethic" (Land Ethic) formulated by Aldo Leopold in his book *A Sand County Almanac* (1949). Leopold gives priority to the health of the "biotic community" (Biotic Community) as an integrated whole: an act is right if it preserves the integrity, stability, and beauty of the ecosystem. Under this ethic, sacrificing individuals of a particular species may be justified for the balance of the system as a whole, which has led some critics (such as Tom Regan) to accuse land-ethic holism of "environmental fascism"; whether the charge applies to Leopold himself is disputed. Deep ecology is also distinguished from the animal-liberation and animal-rights theories of Peter Singer (a utilitarian) and Tom Regan, which focus on sentient individual animals, not on ecosystems or non-sentient beings such as plants and rocks. Deep ecology, by contrast, affirms the intrinsic value of all living beings and, in many formulations, of ecosystems and landscapes, with a principle of "biospherical egalitarianism" that Naess himself qualified as applying "in principle."

## Objections and Responses

The most prominent objection came from the Australian philosopher Val Plumwood and other ecofeminist researchers. They charged that deep ecology, despite claiming to transcend anthropocentrism, slides into a metaphysical "merging" (Merging) that erases the real differences among beings and ignores the social structures of domination (such as patriarchy and colonialism) that produced the environmental crisis in the first place. In their place they proposed an "ecofeminist" framework that links the exploitation of nature with the exploitation of women and colonized peoples through a single hierarchical dualistic logic (mind/body, man/woman, civilization/nature). Another objection concerns practical policy: does the call for a radical reduction in population accord with social justice, especially when it is addressed to developing countries whose individual carbon footprint is already low? Defenders reply that the platform is flexible in its practical application and does not prescribe specific policies, and that its cornerstone is a transformation of awareness.

## Illustrative Example: "Earth First!" and the Question of Ecological Sabotage

The movement "Earth First!," founded in 1980 by Dave Foreman and others, drew partly on deep-ecology ideas to justify forms of direct action, including sabotage ("ecotage") of logging and construction equipment, which Foreman described in *Ecodefense* (1985). Its supporters held that protecting wilderness and rare ecosystems outweighs respect for private property or even for the law. This provoked sharp debate within the environmental movement itself about the limits of extremism that can be morally justified in the name of defending the intrinsic value of nature. The debate remains alive in applied environmental ethics.

## Impact and Influence

Deep ecology influenced the formulation of wilderness preservation movements around the world (Wilderness Preservation), and it became in academic environmental philosophy a major current opposed to anthropocentrism. It also provoked critical currents such as "social ecology" (associated with Murray Bookchin) and ecofeminism. It also influenced the drafting of some modern legal frameworks that granted legal "rights" to particular rivers, such as the Whanganui River in New Zealand (legal personhood, 2017), a development often linked to the broader idea of nature's intrinsic value, though it arose chiefly from Maori understandings of the river.
