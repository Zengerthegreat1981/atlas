# Causation in the Law

The joint book of **H.L.A. Hart** and **Tony Honoré**, published in 1959 (with a second edition, again co-authored, in 1985), is regarded as a leading reference work on the analysis of the concept of **legal causation** in the tradition of Anglo-American analytical legal philosophy. It offers a methodological framework that has been cited in judicial practice.

## Factual Causation versus Legal Causation

The book draws a pivotal distinction between **factual or actual causation** (also called but-for causation), namely whether the act was a necessary factual condition without which the result would not have occurred, and **legal causation or normative attribution** (also called proximate cause), namely whether the act, normatively and legally, is the cause for which the actor deserves legal liability, even if other later causes intervened in the causal chain leading to the final result. This precise distinction resolves a recurring practical problem in the courts: how do we determine when the liability of the first actor ends despite his remaining a genuine factual causal condition of the result?

## Free Deliberate Intervention and the Severing of the Causal Chain

The book elaborates a criterion for determining when a later intervention by a third party **breaks** the chain of legal causation: when that intervention is free, conscious and deliberate (*novus actus interveniens*), in contrast with ordinary expected interventions or natural reactions (such as the body's natural response to injury, or the intervention of ordinary natural forces), which do not sever the causal chain and leave liability with the original actor. The book surveys dozens of English and American judicial precedents to illustrate the application of this criterion across varied contexts in both criminal and civil law.

## Abnormal Conditions and Coincidence

The book also stresses that a consequence produced only by an extraordinary coincidence or by an abnormal intervening event is less readily attributed to the first act, whereas consequences within the ordinary run of events, even if not intended, remain attributable to it. Hart and Honoré treat this idea of the abnormal and the coincidental as distinct from, and more careful than, a simple test of what the defendant could have foreseen.

## Criticism of the Purely Utilitarian Reduction of Causation

Hart and Honoré reject attempts to reduce legal causation entirely to purely political or utilitarian considerations (such as merely asking: which party is socially and economically best placed to bear the loss?). They insist that causation has an independent conceptual structure rooted in the common understanding of cause-and-effect relations in everyday life, and that the law borrows this common understanding and adapts it to its particular purposes, rather than inventing the concept of causation from a purely political void justified only by distributive considerations of risk.

## The Method of Ordinary Language Philosophy

The book adopts an analytical method inspired by the Oxford ordinary language philosophy, which influenced Hart in his broader project of legal positivism. It seeks to extract the underlying conceptual structure of causation through a precise analysis of how ordinary and judicial language uses concepts such as "cause," "result" and "intervention," rather than imposing a prior abstract philosophical definition on these varied uses.

## Applications Across Different Branches of Law

The book is distinguished by applying its analytical framework across multiple branches of law, not only criminal law. It extends to civil liability in tort (Tort Law) and contract law, explaining how the same causal concepts are used in substantially similar ways even though the procedural details differ between these branches. This reflects the authors' ambition to offer a general theory of legal causation that goes beyond the boundaries of any single branch of law to cover the common conceptual structure of the law as a whole.

## The Book's Place in Hart's Intellectual Career

This book represents a less famous aspect of Hart's output compared with his best-known work *The Concept of Law* (1961), but it reflects Hart's same methodological commitment to applying the tools of analytical philosophy of language to central legal concepts. Hart and Honoré combined Hart's general analytical philosophical background with Honoré's specialized expertise in Roman law to produce a work that combines philosophical rigor with deep practical legal knowledge of actual judicial precedents.

## Impact and Criticism

The book is a standard reference in teaching the theory of legal causation, and it has been cited in English and other common-law judicial decisions. Later philosophers, such as Michael Moore, criticized it for relying excessively on ordinary linguistic intuition without a sufficient metaphysical grounding of the concept of causation itself. Nevertheless, its analytical framework has remained widely cited in judicial practice despite this continuing philosophical criticism.
