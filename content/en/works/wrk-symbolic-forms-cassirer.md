# The Philosophy of Symbolic Forms (Philosophie der symbolischen Formen) - Cassirer

## The central thesis

The human being is not essentially "the rational animal" (*animal rationale*) but **the "symbolizing animal"** (*animal symbolicum*, a formula Cassirer states explicitly only in *An Essay on Man*, 1944, though it summarizes the trilogy): he does not confront reality directly but through a network of symbolic systems (language, myth, religion, art, science, history), and each of these does not **reflect** a ready-made reality but **shapes** a particular reality of its own, with its own logic and internal standard. "Truth" in language is not of the same kind as "truth" in mathematics or myth. This is the foundation on which Cassirer built his new Kantian project: transforming Kant's question about the conditions for the possibility of scientific knowledge into a broader question about the conditions for the possibility of every form of building human meaning. In this way he moves from the "Copernican revolution" in knowledge in Kant to a revolution in the understanding of culture: symbolic forms are "organs" with which the spirit makes its objects.

## Structure of the work

A trilogy of independently published volumes, sequential in argument:
1) *Language* (*Die Sprache*, 1923): the first symbolic form and the construction of the world through naming and speech. In it Cassirer presents the stages of expression from the sensory to the representational to the purely symbolic, and studies language in Humboldt and in research on childhood and aphasia.
2) *Mythical Thought* (*Das mythische Denken*, 1925): the logic of myth and religion as a symbolic form with its own coherence, not as an earlier cognitive error preceding science. It contains an analysis of time, space, and number in mythical consciousness, where the symbol and what it symbolizes are not distinguished.
3) *Phenomenology of Knowledge* (*Phänomenologie der Erkenntnis*, 1929): the culmination of the project, linking the symbolic forms to the theory of knowledge and modern science. It discusses the aphasia cases of Goldstein and the loss of the capacity for abstract thought.

There is no single publication year for the work as a whole; the trilogy is a project written over six years, not a single book published at once.

## Historical context of publication

Cassirer wrote the trilogy as a thinker formed in the **Marburg school** of neo-Kantianism, alongside Hermann Cohen and Paul Natorp. Its three volumes appeared between 1923 and 1929 in Weimar Germany, a period of German intellectual flourishing before the rise of Nazism, which later forced Cassirer, as a Jew, to emigrate from Germany in 1933. The idea of the book originated in Hamburg, where he benefited from the library of Aby Warburg (*Kulturwissenschaftliche Bibliothek Warburg*), which gathered material on myth, art, and religion, and where he was in contact with the Hamburg psychologists Wilhelm Stern and Heinz Werner.

## Comparison with the Arab heritage on language and logic

Classical Arab thought regards language as the gateway to reason. Al-Farabi, in *Enumeration of the Sciences* (*Ihsa' al-'Ulum*) and in *The Terms Used in Logic* (*al-Alfaz al-Musta'mala fi al-Mantiq*), links grammar and logic: logic for him is a "general grammar" of reason, and the definition of the human being as the "speaking animal" combines speech and reason. Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani, in *Dala'il al-I'jaz* (*Proofs of Inimitability*), argued that meaning is generated by "composition" (*nazm*), that is, by relations of structure rather than by individual words, an idea scholars compare to viewing language as a form that builds its own meaning. There is no known evidence that Cassirer read these texts, so the comparison is interpretive, and it also differs in purpose: the Arab philosophers sought logical demonstration, whereas Cassirer sought an understanding of all forms of culture.

## Reception and criticism

The trilogy was received with philosophical seriousness in Germany, but its wider influence came through **Susanne Langer**, who translated his *Language and Myth* (1946) and developed similar ideas in her independent book *Philosophy in a New Key* (1942). That is a separate later work, not a literal extension of the trilogy, and its theses should not be attributed to Cassirer himself. The project was also criticized by Heidegger at the Davos debate of 1929 and in his review of the second volume (1928), on the ground that "forms" is a concept left hanging without an existential foundation in Dasein. Edward Skidelsky, in *Ernst Cassirer: The Last Philosopher of Culture* (Princeton University Press, 2008), offers an intellectual biography that places the project in the context of Cassirer's Marburg background, his Hamburg years and his later exile. The documented connection to psychology runs through developmental psychology, in Heinz Werner and Bernard Kaplan, who built *Symbol Formation* (1963) in part on Cassirer's conception. Claims of a direct influence on cultural-historical psychology (Vygotsky) are not documented and are not made here.

## Limits of the work

The trilogy is criticized for its great complexity and for lacking a clear criterion to distinguish the forms and count them (are they six or more?), and for giving each form an internal "logic" that may obscure value comparisons between science and myth. Other scholars faulted it for not addressing the interaction between symbolic forms and economic and political structures, which Cassirer tried to remedy in *The Myth of the State* (1946).

## Significance for the philosophical-psychological bridge

It is a central bridge point between philosophy and psychology: the thesis of the "symbolizing animal" precedes and frames later debates in cognitive psychology and cultural psychology about the role of the symbol and language in constructing perception and meaning, not as a fully established historical source for all these fields, but as a broader philosophical background that shaped the vocabulary later borrowed from it.
