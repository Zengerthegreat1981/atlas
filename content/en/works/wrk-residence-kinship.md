# Residence Rules and Kinship Terminology

**"Residence Rules"** is a short 1956 article by the American anthropologist **Ward H. Goodenough** that solved a long-standing confusion in kinship studies: how to describe where a married couple lives, and how that choice shapes the words people use for their relatives. Published in the *Southwestern Journal of Anthropology*, the article argued that residence is not a single fact about a household but a set of rules — and that those rules can only be understood if each one is defined precisely, in terms of whose kin the couple lives with, rather than by loose labels such as "matrilocal" or "patrilocal" that different anthropologists had used to mean different things.

Goodenough wrote at a moment when kinship was the centre of anthropological theory. George Peter Murdock's *Social Structure* (1949) had correlated residence patterns with descent and terminology across hundreds of societies, but the correlations were blurred by inconsistent definitions. Goodenough's aim was to clean up the conceptual tools so that comparison could be rigorous.

## The Problem of Residence

The problem was simple to state and hard to solve. When a couple marries, where do they set up house? With the husband's parents, the wife's parents, an uncle, or on their own? Earlier writers had answered with a handful of terms — patrilocal, matrilocal, neolocal, avunculocal — but used them inconsistently. Some meant residence with the husband's father, others with the husband's kin group in general; some applied the same word to residence after marriage and to the place where children were raised. The result was that two ethnographers could describe the same society with different terms, or different societies with the same term.

Goodenough proposed to define each residence rule by the kin relationship that determines it. A rule is **virilocal** if the couple lives with or near the husband's kin, **uxorilocal** if with the wife's kin, **neolocal** if they establish an independent household, and **avunculocal** if with the husband's maternal uncle. He further distinguished the rule that governs where a couple lives immediately after marriage from the rule that governs where they live later, and the rule for a first marriage from that for subsequent marriages. He also separated the question of where people live from the question of who owns the land they live on and who holds authority over the household. Each of these, he insisted, is a distinct variable, and only by keeping them apart can anthropologists discover how they are related.

## Residence and Terminology

The second half of the argument connects residence to kinship terminology — the words people use for their relatives. Goodenough argued that terminology is not a passive reflection of social structure but a system of categories that people use to classify their social world, and that it can be analysed formally. A terminology groups some relatives together under one term and separates others, and the pattern of groupings reveals the distinctions that matter in that society: generation, sex, relative age, lineality, and the side of the family through which a relative is connected.

Residence matters because it determines which relatives a person grows up among and interacts with daily. Where couples live virilocally, a man's agnatic kin cluster together, and the terminology tends to distinguish the father's side from the mother's side in particular ways; where residence is uxorilocal, the clustering is different, and the terminological distinctions shift accordingly. Goodenough did not claim a one-to-one determination — terminology also responds to descent, to marriage rules, and to history — but he showed that residence is one of the structural conditions that make certain terminological systems intelligible and others unlikely.

## Method and Evidence

The evidence came chiefly from the societies of Micronesia and Polynesia, where Goodenough had done fieldwork on Truk (now Chuuk) and where residence patterns were unusually varied and well documented. On Romonum in Truk, property was held by matrilineal lineage groups and managed by a lineage elder, and the relationship between senior and junior lineages was reflected in the terminology, in which senior and junior lineage members addressed each other as "father" and "children." Goodenough used such cases to show that residence, land tenure, and terminology must be described independently before their connections can be asserted — otherwise the analyst merely finds in the terminology what the residence description already assumed. Exceptions to a rule are informative rather than embarrassing: they show the competing pressures — economic need, personal preference, political advantage — that pull against it.

## Reception and Influence

"Residence Rules" became a standard citation in kinship studies and a foundation text for the formal analysis of kinship. Its terminology — virilocal, uxorilocal, neolocal, avunculocal — entered the general anthropological vocabulary and is still the standard set of terms in textbooks. The article also helped to establish the programme of componential analysis, which Goodenough pursued in *Description and Comparison in Cultural Anthropology* (1970) and which influenced a generation of cognitive anthropologists.

The work also provoked productive disagreement. David Schneider argued that formal analyses of terminology mistake the analyst's categories for the people's own understanding, and that kinship is organised by cultural symbols rather than by structural rules. The debate between Goodenough and Schneider over the value of formal analysis became one of the defining controversies of post-war kinship theory. Later scholars have qualified Goodenough's approach in other ways, noting that residence in practice is often negotiated rather than rule-bound. Yet the article's central demand — that anthropologists define their terms before they compare — remains a basic disciplinary standard.
