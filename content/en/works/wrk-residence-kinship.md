# Residence Rules

"Residence Rules" is a 1956 article by the American anthropologist Ward H. Goodenough, published in the *Southwestern Journal of Anthropology* (vol. 12). It is a contribution to the methodology of comparative kinship studies. Goodenough argued that residence is better analysed as a matter of rules than of loosely labelled "types" of society, and that the terms used to describe where married couples live need to be defined carefully so that comparisons across societies are reliable.

## Context

Goodenough wrote when kinship was central to anthropological theory. George Peter Murdock's *Social Structure* (1949) had used a cross-cultural sample to correlate residence, descent and kinship terminology, and such correlations depended on how residence patterns were classified. Goodenough, who had done fieldwork on Romonum island in Truk (now Chuuk), Micronesia, in 1947, and published *Property, Kin, and Community on Truk* (1951), was concerned that ethnographers used terms such as "matrilocal" and "patrilocal" inconsistently.

## The Problem of Residence

When a couple marries, where do they set up house? Possible answers include with the husband's parents, the wife's parents, the husband's maternal uncle, or in a new household of their own. These possibilities have conventional names in anthropology (patrilocal or virilocal, matrilocal or uxorilocal, avunculocal and neolocal), but ethnographers did not always use them in the same way. Some applied the same word to a couple's residence immediately after marriage and to their later residence, or to the rule and to the actual pattern. Goodenough's article argued that these distinctions had to be made explicit: a society's rule about residence, the arrangements people actually make, and the variation between first and later marriages are different matters, and exceptions to a rule are informative rather than embarrassing, since they reveal the pressures (economic need, personal preference, political advantage) that act against it.

## Related Work in Formal Analysis

In the same year Goodenough published "Componential Analysis and the Study of Meaning" (*Language* 32, 1956), which applied the methods of structural linguistics to the study of kinship terms, treating a term as defined by a set of components such as generation, sex and lineality. He developed this approach further in *Description and Comparison in Cultural Anthropology* (1970). Together these works are associated with the "new ethnography" or cognitive anthropology of the 1950s and 1960s, which sought to describe a culture in terms of the categories its members use.

## Reception and Critique

Goodenough's terms and distinctions entered the standard vocabulary of kinship studies. His formal approach was criticized by David Schneider, who argued that analyses of kinship terminology tend to treat the anthropologist's categories as the people's own and that kinship should be studied as a system of cultural symbols (as in Schneider's *American Kinship: A Cultural Account*, 1968). Later scholars have also noted that residence in practice is often negotiated rather than rule-bound.
