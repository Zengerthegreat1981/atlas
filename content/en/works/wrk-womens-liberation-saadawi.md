# Woman and Sex

## Definition

*Woman and Sex* is a book by the Egyptian physician and writer Nawal El Saadawi (1931–2021). Its first edition was published in Cairo in 1972, and it is her first major non-fiction work. With it, El Saadawi moved talk of the female body, of its circumcision and of the hymen from the circle of medical and juristic whispering into the sphere of public writing. It provoked reactions that changed the course of her life: the health magazine she edited was closed, and in 1972 she lost her post as director of health education in the Egyptian Ministry of Health.

## Preceding Arab Context

The book did not come from a vacuum. In Egypt it was preceded by the Nahda debate on women since Qasim Amin (*Tahrir al-Mar'a*, 1899) and Huda Sha'rawi and the Egyptian Feminist Union (1923). But this discourse concentrated on education, the veil, divorce and polygamy, and most researchers avoided the body and desire. In the juristic and moral heritage, talk of sex was more explicit than it became in the twentieth century. Al-Ghazali, in the chapter on marriage in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, counted caressing and the wife's right to enjoyment as part of good companionship, and writers and physicians such as al-Tifashi in *Nuzhat al-Albab fi ma la yujad fi Kitab* wrote on matters not found in a book. El Saadawi's opponents and supporters both draw on this heritage: the former invoke moral discipline, and the latter argue that classical Arabic writing did not deny the body.

## Subjects and Argument

The book deals with female circumcision, the standard of virginity and the hymen, the relationship between honour and ownership, and the image of women in proverbs and customs. El Saadawi builds her argument on her medical observations in the countryside and in the city. She describes circumcision as a bodily and psychological wound with no medical need and no decisive religious warrant, and she reads the control of the female body as part of the patriarchal family's control of lineage and inheritance. She holds that sexual repression cannot be separated from class and political repression, a thesis she later set out in *The Hidden Face of Eve* (1977). Later public-health studies have found no medical benefit in the practice and describe it as a social custom that predates both Islam and Christianity and is widespread in the Nile Valley. The Egyptian Demographic and Health Survey of 2008 found that it had affected about 91 percent of married women aged 15 to 49, and Egyptian law criminalised it in 2008, after a ministerial decree of 1996 that was not firmly enforced.

## Circumcision Between Jurisprudence and Medicine

Classical jurists were divided on the ruling on circumcision: some made it obligatory, some regarded it as a Sunna, and some as a noble act. They relied on hadiths, including the report of Umm 'Atiyya about "cutting lightly and not excessively", which Abu Dawud himself weakened in his *Sunan*. This explains why El Saadawi's position, that there is no decisive religious warrant, later found an official echo. In 2007, after the death of a girl during surgery, the Academy of Islamic Research at al-Azhar declared that circumcision has no basis in the Qur'an or in sound Sunna, and the Mufti of Egypt issued a fatwa prohibiting it. This shows that the debate opened by her book did not end with a juristic or medical argument alone, but required decades of social and political pressure.

## Reception

The book was met with attacks from clerics, the press and official institutions. It was attacked from religious and official quarters, and its circulation was reportedly restricted for a time. El Saadawi then turned to fiction with *Woman at Point Zero* (1975), and to political activism that led to her imprisonment in 1981 under Sadat. In the West her ideas spread through translations of her later works, and she became one of the best-known Arab voices. Fedwa Malti-Douglas, in *Men, Women, and God(s)* (1995), studied El Saadawi's writings as an Arab feminist poetics.

## Criticism and Limits

El Saadawi relied in the book on her clinical experience more than on a controlled field method, a point some researchers have raised against her. Lila Abu-Lughod, in *Do Muslim Women Need Saving?* (2013), criticised the Western discourse that uses examples such as circumcision to judge the whole of Islamic culture, and held that some Western reception of El Saadawi overlooks local differences. Her supporters, for their part, argued that the charge of serving colonialism ignores that her criticism is directed first at local patriarchy. The book is also faulted for generalising in linking religion with repression, which angered Muslim women who saw religious reform as a better path.
