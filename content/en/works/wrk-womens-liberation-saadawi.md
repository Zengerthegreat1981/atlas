# Woman and Sex

## Definition

*Woman and Sex* is a book by the Egyptian physician and writer Nawal El Saadawi (1931–2021). It first appeared in 1972. It is the non-fiction book that brought her medical criticism of women's conditions into public controversy. With it, El Saadawi moved talk of the female body, of its circumcision and of the hymen from the circle of medical and juristic whispering into the sphere of public writing. It provoked reactions that changed the course of her career. The health journal she edited was closed, and she was removed from her post in the Ministry of Health, where she had directed health education.

## Preceding Arab Context

The book did not come from a vacuum. In Egypt it was preceded by the Nahda debate on women since Qasim Amin (*Tahrir al-Mar'a*, 1899) and Huda Sha'rawi and the Egyptian Feminist Union (1923). That debate concentrated on education, the veil, divorce and polygamy, and said less about the body and sexual desire. In the juristic and moral heritage, talk of sex could be explicit. Al-Ghazali, in the book on marriage in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, treats caressing and the wife's enjoyment as part of good companionship, and Ahmad al-Tifashi's *Nuzhat al-Albab fima la Yujad fi Kitab* is an erotic compilation. El Saadawi's opponents and supporters both draw on this heritage: the former invoke moral discipline, and the latter argue that classical Arabic writing did not deny the body.

## Subjects and Argument

The book deals with female circumcision, the standard of virginity and the hymen, the relationship between honour and ownership, and the image of women in proverbs and customs. El Saadawi builds her argument on her medical observations in the countryside and in the city. She describes circumcision as a bodily and psychological wound with no medical need and no decisive religious warrant, and she reads the control of the female body as part of the patriarchal family's control of lineage and inheritance. She holds that sexual repression cannot be separated from class and political repression, a thesis she developed further in *al-Wajh al-'Ari lil-Mar'a al-'Arabiyya* (Beirut, 1977), published in English as *The Hidden Face of Eve* (1980). Later public-health studies have found no medical benefit in the practice. It is a regional custom of the Nile Valley, found among Muslims and Christians there, and it is not prescribed by the Qur'an. The Egypt Demographic and Health Survey of 2008 reported that about 91 percent of ever-married women aged 15 to 49 had undergone it. A ministerial decree of 1996 restricted the practice but was weakly enforced. After further deaths of girls, including in 2007, the Ministry of Health tightened the ban, and the practice was made a criminal offence in 2008.

## Circumcision Between Jurisprudence and Medicine

Classical jurists differed on female circumcision. Some treated it as obligatory, some as a sunna, and some as an honorable custom. They cited reports such as the words, addressed to Umm 'Atiyya, about cutting slightly and not going too far. That report is preserved in Abu Dawud's *Sunan* and is widely graded weak. In the mid-2000s the Islamic Research Academy at al-Azhar stated that the practice has no basis in the Qur'an or in sound Sunna, and the Mufti of Egypt ruled against it. Those rulings, and the 2008 criminalization, came after decades of medical argument, public campaigning and further deaths. They were not a simple consequence of the 1972 book.

## Reception

The book was attacked in the religious and official press, and its circulation was reportedly restricted for a time. El Saadawi then turned to fiction with *Woman at Point Zero* (*Imra'a 'inda Nuqtat al-Sifr*, 1975). In September 1981 she was among the opponents imprisoned by Sadat, and she was released after his death. In the West her later books circulated in translation, and she became one of the best-known Arab feminist voices there. Fedwa Malti-Douglas, in *Men, Women, and God(s)* (1995), reads the writings as an Arab feminist poetics.

## Criticism and Limits

El Saadawi relied in the book on her clinical experience more than on a controlled field method, a point some researchers have raised against her. Lila Abu-Lughod, in *Do Muslim Women Need Saving?* (2013), criticizes Western campaigns that take practices such as genital cutting as a verdict on Islamic culture as a whole and that flatten differences among Muslim women. That argument bears on how El Saadawi's case histories have been read abroad. Her supporters, for their part, argued that the charge of serving colonialism ignores that her criticism is directed first at local patriarchy. The book is also faulted for generalising in linking religion with repression, which angered Muslim women who saw religious reform as a better path.
