# Syntactic Structures

The book that launched the generative grammar revolution in linguistics. Noam Chomsky published it in 1957 with Mouton in The Hague, based on his lectures at MIT.

## Historical Context

Chomsky wrote this work as a summary of his lecture notes for a course at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT). It was published by the Dutch press Mouton in 1957 (in the series *Janua Linguarum*), reportedly with the help of Morris Halle and after some difficulty finding a publisher. The book appeared at a time when American linguistics was dominated by the descriptive structuralist approach (as represented by Leonard Bloomfield and Zellig Harris, Chomsky's direct teacher) and by the behaviorist approach in psychology (as represented by B. F. Skinner). These viewed language as a set of habits acquired through repetition and reinforcement, describable through mechanical *discovery procedures* applied to corpora of recorded speech without prior theoretical assumptions.

## Content

The book presents a formal model of transformational grammar, in which phrase-structure rules generate basic (kernel) structures and formal **transformational rules** convert them into the other sentence forms. (The terms **deep structure** and **surface structure** became prominent only in *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax*, 1965, and are linked to this entry as the later development of the idea.) The book uses structural examples such as its analysis of the famous sentence "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" to show that grammaticality is entirely independent of semantic plausibility or the statistical probability of a word sequence. This was a direct critique of the behavioral and Markovian probability models that had dominated American linguistics before it, since this sentence is grammatically well formed despite being semantically implausible and statistically unlikely to occur in any text corpus.

## Main Ideas and Method

The book introduces the concept of **kernel sentences**: simple, affirmative, active declarative sentences that undergo transformational operations (such as the passive transformation, the question transformation and the negation transformation) to produce the other sentence types. It also distinguishes three components of grammar: phrase-structure rules, which generate the basic structures; transformational rules, which modify those structures; and morphophonemic rules, which convert the final output into an actual phonetic representation. Chomsky also presents a central methodological argument: the goal of linguistic theory is not only to "describe" what an observer hears in speech, but to explain how a speaker can produce and understand an infinite number of new sentences never heard before, on the basis of limited knowledge (finite means generating infinite output, a notion later developed as linguistic "competence").

## Impact and Criticism

The book is regarded as one of the most influential works in the history of modern linguistics. It is often seen as the spark of the "generative revolution" or the "Chomskyan revolution," which displaced American descriptive structuralism from its central position. It set off a long series of successive theoretical reformulations led by Chomsky himself: the Standard Theory of *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), the Extended Standard Theory of the 1970s, Government and Binding Theory in the 1980s, and the Minimalist Program in the 1990s. Critics, including functional linguists such as Joan Bybee, have criticized the model for neglecting the role of usage, frequency and context in shaping linguistic structure. Nevertheless, the book remains an indispensable starting point for understanding the course of theoretical linguistics throughout the second half of the twentieth century.

## An Additional Example of the Independence of Grammar from Meaning

Alongside the example "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously," the book offers other comparisons to illustrate the independence of grammatical judgment from semantic or statistical judgment. Among them is the contrast between a sentence that is semantically unintelligible but structurally well formed, and a sentence that is structurally broken despite containing familiar words (such as the same words arranged at random). Chomsky used this contrast as an argument that a speaker's knowledge of their language is an independent formal grammatical knowledge, not merely the ability to absorb word strings that are statistically familiar, as statistical models of language inspired by information theory (Claude Shannon and others) were thought to imply.

## Detailed Mechanics of Transformational Rules

The book explains in detail how transformational rules work through converting a basic affirmative declarative sentence (the kernel sentence) into its passive form. The process starts from a kernel structure ("John hit the ball"). A sequence of precise formal transformations is then applied: exchanging the positions of subject and object, adding the appropriate auxiliary verb, and inserting the preposition "by," to produce the passive sentence ("The ball was hit by John"). The book notes that this transformation is systematic and can be stated formally with precision, so that it can be applied mechanically to any similar basic sentence, rather than being an isolated special case that must be memorized on its own.

## The Book's Influence on Philosophy of Language and Cognitive Science

The book's influence went beyond technical linguistics to reach the philosophy of language and the cognitive sciences then emerging. The generative model of formal rules encouraged the view of the mind as a system operating by explicit rules. Together with Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*, it contributed to the cognitive revolution in psychology, and it linked linguistics with computer science and formal language theory.
