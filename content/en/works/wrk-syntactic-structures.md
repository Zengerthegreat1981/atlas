# Syntactic Structures

An early book-length statement of generative grammar. Noam Chomsky published it in 1957 with Mouton in The Hague. It grew out of his lectures at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT).

## Historical Context

Chomsky prepared the book from notes for an MIT course. Mouton published it in 1957 in the series *Janua Linguarum* (Series Minor). Morris Halle, and through him Roman Jakobson, encouraged its publication. American linguistics at the time was shaped by post-Bloomfieldian structuralism, especially the distributional methods of Zellig S. Harris, Chomsky's teacher at the University of Pennsylvania, in the line of Leonard Bloomfield's *Language* (1933). In psychology, B. F. Skinner's behaviorism treated verbal behavior as habit shaped by reinforcement. Many structuralist programs aimed at *discovery procedures*: mechanical routines for inducing a grammar from a recorded corpus without an explicit prior theory of what grammars may look like.

## Content

The book presents a formal model of transformational grammar, in which phrase-structure rules generate basic (kernel) structures and formal **transformational rules** convert them into the other sentence forms. (The terms **deep structure** and **surface structure** became prominent only in *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax*, 1965, and are linked to this entry as the later development of the idea.) The book uses structural examples such as its analysis of the famous sentence "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" to show that grammaticality is entirely independent of semantic plausibility or the statistical probability of a word sequence. This was a direct critique of the behavioral and Markovian probability models that had dominated American linguistics before it, since this sentence is grammatically well formed despite being semantically implausible and statistically unlikely to occur in any text corpus.

## Main Ideas and Method

The book introduces the concept of **kernel sentences**: simple, affirmative, active declarative sentences that undergo transformational operations (such as the passive transformation, the question transformation and the negation transformation) to produce the other sentence types. It also distinguishes three components of grammar: phrase-structure rules, which generate the basic structures; transformational rules, which modify those structures; and morphophonemic rules, which convert the final output into an actual phonetic representation. Chomsky also presents a central methodological argument: the goal of linguistic theory is not only to "describe" what an observer hears in speech, but to explain how a speaker can produce and understand an infinite number of new sentences never heard before, on the basis of limited knowledge (finite means generating infinite output, a notion later developed as linguistic "competence").

## Impact and Criticism

Historians of the field, notably Frederick J. Newmeyer, treat the book as a turning point in American theoretical linguistics and as the start of what is often called the generative, or Chomskyan, turn. It did not by itself remove descriptive structuralism from teaching or research. It did open a series of reformulations led by Chomsky: the Standard Theory of *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), the Extended Standard Theory of the 1970s, Government and Binding Theory in the 1980s, and the Minimalist Program in the 1990s. Later usage-based linguists, including Joan Bybee, have argued that frequency and use shape grammatical structure in ways this early formal model set aside. The book remains a standard point of departure for the history of theoretical syntax in the second half of the twentieth century.

## An Additional Example of the Independence of Grammar from Meaning

Alongside the example "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously," the book offers other comparisons to illustrate the independence of grammatical judgment from semantic or statistical judgment. Among them is the contrast between a sentence that is semantically unintelligible but structurally well formed, and a sentence that is structurally broken despite containing familiar words (such as the same words arranged at random). Chomsky used this contrast as an argument that a speaker's knowledge of their language is an independent formal grammatical knowledge, not merely the ability to absorb word strings that are statistically familiar, as statistical models of language inspired by information theory (Claude Shannon and others) were thought to imply.

## Detailed Mechanics of Transformational Rules

The passive is one of the book's worked examples of a transformation. A kernel sentence of the form noun phrase–auxiliary–verb–noun phrase is mapped to a passive by exchanging the two noun phrases, adding a form of *be* plus the past participle, and introducing *by*. Chomsky's own illustration of the *by*-phrase is "the food was eaten by the man." The rule is stated generally, so that it applies to a class of sentences rather than to one memorized pair. Phrase-structure illustrations in the same discussion use sentences such as "The man hit the ball."

## The Book's Influence on Philosophy of Language and Cognitive Science

The book's influence went beyond technical linguistics to reach the philosophy of language and the cognitive sciences then emerging. The generative model of formal rules encouraged the view of the mind as a system operating by explicit rules. Together with Chomsky's 1959 review of Skinner's *Verbal Behavior*, it contributed to the cognitive revolution in psychology, and it linked linguistics with computer science and formal language theory.

## In Arabic

The book was translated into Arabic by the Iraqi linguist Yu'il Yusuf 'Aziz under the title *al-Buna al-Nahwiyya*, with revision by Majid al-Mashita, and was published in Baghdad in 1987, the same year in which an edition also appeared in Morocco. The translation therefore came about thirty years after the English original, by which time generative theory had already passed through the model of *Aspects* and into Government and Binding. Arabic readers could thus meet the 1957 model after, or alongside, accounts of much later stages, and it is useful to keep the stages apart: terms such as deep and surface structure do not belong to the central apparatus of this first book. The translation also had to settle Arabic equivalents for the technical vocabulary of phrase structure and transformation, an area where usage among Arab linguists has varied, with competing renderings in circulation for *transformational* and *generative* in particular. This section records the best-known version and does not attempt a complete list of Arabic renderings or reprints.
