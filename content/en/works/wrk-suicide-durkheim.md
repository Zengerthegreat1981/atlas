# Suicide: A Study in Sociology

One of Émile Durkheim's (1858–1917) best-known works, published in 1897, and an early major systematic application of quantitative empirical sociology to a phenomenon that seemed purely individual. It became a reference model for the possibility of studying the most private individual acts (a person's ending his own life) with objective sociological tools.

## Overview

A methodological-empirical book representing the practical application of Durkheim's method, which he had laid down theoretically in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), holding that "social facts" (Faits Sociaux) must be treated as objective things external to individual consciousness, open to study with rigorous quantitative tools.

## Historical Context

Durkheim wrote his work in the context of his effort to establish sociology as an independent academic field recognized scientifically in France. He deliberately chose a subject that seemed at first glance the most private and individual of human phenomena, the decision to end one's own life, to prove that even this phenomenon is subject to structural social laws open to objective scientific explanation, not to purely individual psychological or biological explanations as was then prevalent.

## Content

Durkheim relied on official European suicide statistics across different countries, regions, religions and marital statuses, showing that suicide rates vary with statistical regularity with structural social variables and not purely individual psychological ones. He classified four types. **Egoistic suicide** (Suicide Égoïste) arises from the weak integration of the individual in his group (higher rates among Protestants than Catholics, and among the unmarried than the married, according to his data). **Altruistic suicide** (Suicide Altruiste) arises from the individual's excessive absorption into the group to the point of sacrificing his life for it (as in some traditional military societies). **Anomic suicide** (Suicide Anomique) arises from a sudden collapse in the balance between the individual's desires and the means available to satisfy them, whether in an economic crisis or in a sudden prosperity that expands ambitions without clear limits. And **fatalistic suicide** (Suicide Fataliste) arises from excessive social regulation that stifles every future horizon for the individual (Durkheim referred to it briefly in a footnote without wide elaboration).

## Influence and Criticism

This work became a reference methodological model for quantitative empirical sociology, and the "anomic suicide" type in particular provided a starting point for Robert Merton's broader theory of "strain" (Strain) and anomie in the American context. The book was later subjected to an influential methodological critique by Jack Douglas (1967), who doubted the reliability of the official European statistics on which Durkheim built his entire analysis, arguing that suicide statistics are the product of the classification practices of coroners and other officials and so cannot be treated as neutral records; related concerns about possible under-reporting in Catholic regions have also been raised, and these may partly undermine the confessional differences on which Durkheim relied. Despite this methodological critique, the book remained an indispensable teaching model for clarifying the difference between correlation and causation in quantitative social research. Thanks to this work and his other works (*The Division of Labour in Society*, *The Rules of Sociological Method*, *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*), Émile Durkheim is commonly counted, with Marx and Weber, among the classical founders of modern sociology. Durkheim taught some of the earliest university courses in sociology in France (at Bordeaux from 1887, and later at the Sorbonne) and founded the journal *L'Année sociologique* (1898), which gathered around him a school of researchers including Marcel Mauss and Maurice Halbwachs.

In his analysis Durkheim used the concept of "social solidarity" as the deepest variable explaining the variation in suicide rates: the more cohesive and integrated the group (religious, family, occupational), the stronger the psychological and social protection it provided its members against individual despair, and the more this solidarity weakened or exceeded its healthy limits (as in excessive altruistic suicide), the more fragile the individual became before individual existential crises. This idea connects the book with the analysis of mechanical and organic solidarity in his earlier *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893).

Durkheim devoted an important methodological chapter to distinguishing his sociological explanation from the psychological, biological and climatic explanations competing at the time, refuting each one in detail statistically. He showed, for example, that suicide rates are not consistently related to temperatures or seasons, as some earlier climatic theories had suggested, but to the rhythm of social activity across the year, thereby supporting the precedence of the social explanation over any alternative individual or natural one.
