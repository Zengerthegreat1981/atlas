# The Nuer: A Description of the Modes of Livelihood and Political Institutions of a Nilotic People

*The Nuer: A Description of the Modes of Livelihood and Political Institutions of a Nilotic People* (1940) is E. E. Evans-Pritchard's study of a cattle-keeping people of the southern Sudan. It asks how political order exists among people who had no central government, and answers through an analysis of ecology, time and space, territorial divisions, lineages, and age-sets. It became one of the most influential ethnographies in social anthropology.

## Research and setting

Evans-Pritchard worked among the Nuer in several periods between 1930 and 1936, amounting to about a year. The research was supported by the government of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, which had recently used military force, including bombing from the air, to subdue Nuer communities. The introduction describes illness, hard conditions, and the suspicion with which he was met. He learned the language during the work. The book is the first of three on the Nuer, followed by *Kinship and Marriage among the Nuer* (1951) and *Nuer Religion* (1956).

## Cattle, ecology, time, and space

The account begins with cattle, which supply food and materials, pass at marriage and in compensation for homicide, link people to ancestors and spirits, and pervade names and conversation. Seasonal flood and drought require movement between wet-season villages and dry-season camps, and this mixed life of herding, cultivation, and fishing sets conditions for cooperation.

Evans-Pritchard distinguishes oecological time, reflecting the annual round of activities, from structural time, reflecting relations between groups and generations. Distance is likewise social: two settlements separated by the same number of miles may be near or far according to the terrain and the relations between their residents.

## Segmentary politics

A Nuer tribe, in his usage, is divided into primary, secondary, and tertiary sections. Segments that oppose one another at one level combine against a larger segment of the same order. A person's political identification therefore depends on who the opposing party is. Within the tribe there is a recognised obligation to settle homicide by compensation; between tribes there is not. The feud is both a source of conflict and a mechanism that defines and balances groups.

The political system is related to a system of agnatic lineages, also segmented. In each tribe a dominant clan provides a framework to which territorial sections are linked, although most residents of an area need not belong to it. Evans-Pritchard keeps lineage and local community analytically distinct while showing how one supplies an idiom for the other.

## Mediation and age

The leopard-skin chief is a ritual specialist who can shelter a killer, perform cleansing, and negotiate compensation, including by threatening a curse. He lacks the power to enforce a judgment. Evans-Pritchard famously characterised the Nuer polity as an "ordered anarchy". Age-sets, formed through male initiation, cross territorial and lineage divisions and govern forms of respect and conduct, but are given less political weight in the analysis.

## Criticism

Later writers questioned how well the model matched events. Kathleen Gough reexamined Nuer kinship and argued that conquest, expansion, and demographic disruption affected the arrangements recorded. Others noted that residence and personal choice often diverge from agnatic principles, and that lineage theory drawn from the book was applied too widely. Renato Rosaldo analysed how the narrative creates detached authority while saying little about the colonial violence that framed the research. Women appear mainly in relation to marriage, cattle, and men's descent. Douglas Johnson's history of Nuer prophets showed that figures treated briefly in the book had important political and religious roles. Sharon Hutchinson's *Nuer Dilemmas* (1996) traced how money, war, and state power transformed the meaning of cattle and kinship.

## Legacy

Together with *African Political Systems* (1940), the book made segmentary opposition a central concept for studying societies without states. It also remains admired for connecting environment, categories of thought, and social structure in a compact argument.
