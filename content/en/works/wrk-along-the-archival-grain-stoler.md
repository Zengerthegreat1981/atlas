# Along the Archival Grain: Epistemic Anxieties and Colonial Common Sense

## Definition

The most important work of **Ann Laura Stoler** and the key reference of the archival turn in contemporary colonial history. Published in 2009 by Princeton University Press, it transformed the way historians treat the colonial archive itself, from a neutral source into an object of analysis in its own right.

## Historical Context

Stoler, professor of anthropology and history, built this book on decades of her field and archival research in the archives of the Dutch colonial administration in Indonesia (formerly the Dutch East Indies). It draws on her earlier work on race, gender and colonial power (*Carnal Knowledge and Imperial Power*, 2002). The book emerged in the context of the wider "archival turn" in the humanities in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, influenced by Michel Foucault's archaeology of knowledge and by Jacques Derrida's work on "archive fever" (*Archive Fever*, 1995).

## Content

The book proposes reading the archives of the Dutch colonial administration in Indonesia "along the grain" and not only "against the grain." Rather than searching the archive for hidden truths about the colonized (reading "against the grain"), Stoler proposes studying the anxieties of the colonial officials themselves: their hesitations, their repeated reformulations of racial categories, their contradictory drafts and incomplete reports. The aim is to reveal the structure of colonial power from within, while it was still taking shape, rather than as a coherent system ready-made in advance.

## Main Ideas and Method

- **The concept of "colonial common sense"**: Stoler develops this concept to describe how "self-evident" racial assumptions took shape through everyday repeated bureaucratic practice (classifying mixed marriages, determining who counted legally as "European"), rather than through a single explicit doctrinal decree.
- **The archive as a "technology of rule" rather than a repository**: the colonial archive is read as an effective tool for producing knowledge and controlling populations, not merely a passive place for storing documents after events occur.
- **The epistemic anxiety of the colonial state**: Stoler shows that colonial authority was not as self-confident as later narratives portray it. It lived with continuous doubts and internal contradictions about its racial and legal categories.

## Methodological Importance

The work represents a methodological shift from treating the archive as a neutral repository to treating it as an object of analysis in itself, influenced by Foucault's archaeology of knowledge. It had a deep impact on postcolonial and colonial studies and on contemporary social history methodologies, which now ask how the document itself was produced, not only about its content.

## Reception and Criticism

The book became a standard reference in any contemporary discussion of archival methodology in the humanities. It has been debated on the question of how far its conclusions (based specifically on the Dutch-Indonesian case) can be generalized to other colonial contexts with fundamentally different administrative structures, such as direct French or British colonialism.

## Additional Notes

In developing her analysis, Stoler drew on Foucault's concept of "archaeology," but with an important field-based modification. Instead of the abstract theoretical analysis of discursive structure that Foucault often practised, Stoler immersed herself in very specific field details of the actual Dutch colonial archive (cancelled drafts, marginal comments, reports contradicting one another between different officials), to show how power is actually formed in the details of everyday bureaucratic practice rather than in a single comprehensive theoretical decree. This method later influenced similar studies of other colonial archives (British in India, French in North Africa), and helped establish what is known today as "Critical Archival Studies," taught in programmes of both information science and history.

Stoler also discusses in later chapters the concept of colonial "Sentiment": the unstated emotional dimensions of administrative documents (fear, disgust, forbidden attraction), which form an integral part of the mechanism of colonial rule even though official documents pretend to a cold bureaucratic neutrality.

The book is now widely taught in postcolonial studies and information science programmes alike, as a rare bridge between two academic fields that often develop in isolation from each other. This reflects Stoler's stated ambition to move beyond the traditional disciplinary boundaries between anthropology, history and archival science in her approach to the colonial source.

The final chapter of the book also addresses the problem of the researcher's own position relative to the archive he studies. Stoler acknowledges that her own reading of the Dutch colonial documents is inevitably shaped by her contemporary academic position, and calls on researchers to maintain a continuous critical self-awareness of the temporal and methodological gap between them and the document they study, however precise their reading of it.
