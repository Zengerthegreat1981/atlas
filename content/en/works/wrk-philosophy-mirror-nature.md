# Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature

## The central thesis

Rorty attacks the idea that the human mind has the function of being a **"mirror of nature"**, that is, an apparatus that reflects external reality with increasing accuracy as its cognitive tools improve. This is the metaphor which, he holds, has governed Western philosophy since Descartes, Locke and Kant, and has made of the theory of knowledge (epistemology) the supreme "tribunal" that adjudicates the validity of every other discourse, whether scientific, moral or aesthetic. His thesis is that this picture is illusory and can be dismantled historically and not only logically, and that the alternative is not a better epistemology but giving up the very ambition in favor of a philosophy as a **"conversation"** among the traditions of human discourse, with no final arbiter claiming to possess an accurate representation of reality "as it is". The book sets this out by counting knowledge as a social practice of justification: we count something as known when our interlocutors accept it.

## The structure of the work

The book is in three parts and eight chapters. **Part One** ("Our Glassy Essence") dismantles the Cartesian mind-body duality and the idea of the "inner eye" that contemplates its internal representations, and shows that the concept of "mind" as a special object appeared in seventeenth-century philosophy as a historical invention. **Part Two** ("Mirroring") deals with the idea of "the theory of knowledge" and privileged representations and presents his most prominent theses: that Quine, by his critique of the "two dogmas of empiricism", and Sellars, by his critique of the "myth of the given", undermined the distinction between the given and the added on which epistemology was founded, and that attempts to rescue it by empirical psychology or by the philosophy of language fail to rescue the idea of "accurate representation". **Part Three** ("Philosophy") presents the alternative: **hermeneutics** and "edifying" philosophy as against epistemology, giving up the search for a certain foundation in favor of a continuing conversation with no final stopping point, drawing inspiration from Heidegger, Dewey and the later Wittgenstein, with Kierkegaard added to them.

## Historical context of publication

The book appeared from Princeton University Press in 1979, and Rorty was then professor of philosophy at the same university after a training in analytic philosophy. The book met wide controversy among his colleagues in analytic philosophy because it attacks from within the foundationalist ambition on which twentieth-century analytic philosophy was built. Rorty then moved in 1982 to a chair in the humanities at the University of Virginia, after receiving a MacArthur fellowship in 1981, and began writing with his readers directed to the fields of literature, politics and culture more than to narrow academic philosophy.

## Reception and critique

Reception was sharply divided. **Hilary Putnam** criticized it in his later writings, including "Renewing Philosophy" (1992), refusing that giving up correspondence should lead to saying that there is no difference between truth and social acceptability. **Jurgen Habermas** criticized, in "Richard Rorty's Pragmatic Turn" in "Truth and Justification" (2003), that Rorty's radicalism drops any unconditional claim, such as truth or moral validity, that guides our practices from within, whereas Habermas holds that the pragmatic linguistic turn does not entail this renunciation. **Richard Bernstein** in "Beyond Objectivism and Relativism" (1983) presented an independent critique of the charge of implicit relativism against Rorty. On the other hand **John McDowell** drew an avowed inspiration from the book in "Mind and World" (1994), but rejected Rorty's conclusion and tried to rescue the idea of the "openness of the mind to the world".

Robert Brandom and others conducted an extended dialogue with Rorty in "Rorty and His Critics" (2000). The book remained a subject of controversy between those who see it as a necessary dismantling of the illusions of foundationalist philosophy and those who see it as a surrender to relativism.

## Relation to the Arab context

The book's thesis connects with an old question in Islamic philosophy, the standing of certain demonstration as the criterion of the sciences, the picture that Rorty attacks in its modern form: al-Farabi and Ibn Rushd made Aristotelian demonstration the highest rank of syllogism, and al-Ghazali in "al-Munqidh min al-dalal" founded his methodological doubt in the senses and reason and then returned to "a light that God cast into the breast", a moment that invites comparison with the loss of foundation that Rorty describes. The Arab reader finds in the book something that touches the debate over authenticity and modernity, in its rejection of the idea of a universal criterion of rationality by which every culture is measured.

## Limits of the work

Critics note that Rorty reads the great philosophers selectively in service of his thesis, making Descartes, Locke and Kant responsible for the "invention" of the mind, whereas historians hold that the visual metaphor for knowledge is older among the Greeks. His thesis is subject to the objection that it is self-contradictory when it presents a universal argument against all universal arguments, and ends in a cultural relativism from which it tries to disown itself. He offers no criterion for distinguishing fruitful from barren conversation. The book confines its subject to Anglo-American analytic philosophy and its heritage, and so deals with empirical psychology only as far as it is called on to rescue epistemology.
