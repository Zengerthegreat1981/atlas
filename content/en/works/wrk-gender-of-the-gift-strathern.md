# The Gender of the Gift: Problems with Women and Problems with Society in Melanesia

## Overview

Marilyn Strathern's major work, published in 1988 by the University of California Press. It is a radical critique of the universality of Western feminist concepts of gender and the person. It draws on her fieldwork among the Hagen people of the Papua New Guinea highlands, together with the wider Melanesian ethnographic literature.

## Historical Context

The book was a direct response to the feminist anthropological debate that was dominated in the 1970s and 1980s by two frameworks: Sherry Ortner's linking of women with "nature" and men with "culture," and Gayle Rubin's notion of the "sex/gender system." Strathern studied Hagen society within the tradition of Melanesian studies of exchange and the gift, which goes back to Malinowski's work on the Kula and to Marcel Mauss's essay on the gift.

## Main Ideas

Strathern argues that the Western dichotomy between nature and culture is not universal, and that understanding gender among the Hagen requires a radically different concept of the person itself. Instead of the Western "individual," meaning a single, separate entity with fixed bodily and social boundaries, she describes Melanesian persons as "dividual" (a term taken from McKim Marriott's work on South Asia) or "partible": persons are composites of the relationships that produced them (with mother, father, exchange partners), and parts of a person can be detached and transferred in gifts and exchanges, which help constitute identity rather than merely express it.

Gifts and social exchanges between men and women among the Hagen, especially the ceremonial exchange of Moka pigs, show how gender identity is built through these relationships rather than before them. Men and women among the Hagen are not defined by fixed traits that precede exchange, but by their changing positions within the network of giving and receiving itself. Strathern thus criticizes the projection of the Western feminist concept (as a project to liberate the "individual" woman from structural oppression) onto contexts in which the individual that this concept presupposes does not exist in the first place.

## Key Works and Figures

This work is a critical extension of Marcel Mauss's tradition on the gift. Strathern engaged closely with Roy Wagner's work on Melanesian symbolism and with the feminist debates of the period. Her analysis of relational personhood influenced later work in Melanesian studies and the "new kinship studies" associated with Janet Carsten. The book intersects with Strathern's later project on reproductive technology in *After Nature* (1992).

## Impact and Critique

The book offered a radical critique of the universality of Western individualism and gender dualism in kinship theory. It became one of the most influential works in feminist anthropology and Melanesian studies, and it influenced the new kinship studies. It has been criticized for the difficulty of its densely theoretical writing style, and for questions about whether the concept of the "dividual" can be applied beyond the specific Melanesian context without being flattened.

## Further Detail on the Moka Pig Exchange

Strathern analyzes in detail the Moka ceremony, in which Hagen men exchange pigs and valuable shells in complex competitive rounds that last for years. She shows that men in these ceremonies do not act apart from their wives. Their ability to organize major Moka displays depends on their wives' work in raising and tending the pigs. Women are thus the actual producers of the "social capital" that the man publicly displays as his alone. Strathern uses this to dismantle the simplistic view that only men are "agents" in Melanesian gift economies, while women are merely passive instruments in them.

## Critique of Western Individualism

Strathern argues that Western feminist and social-scientific analyses often assume a prior individual or society that relations then connect or constrain. In her account of Melanesian sociality there is no such pre-given individual standing apart from social relationships. The relationships themselves constitute the person from the ground up, and are not imposed on the person from outside. This deep ontological difference makes it difficult to import Western feminist vocabulary, such as "women's oppression" or "liberation," without reformulating it.
