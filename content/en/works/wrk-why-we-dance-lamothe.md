# Why We Dance: A Philosophy of Bodily Becoming

Kimerer LaMothe's book, published by Columbia University Press in 2015, in which she proposes a philosophy of bodily movement and dance as a practice that generates meaning and identity, rather than a secondary expression of ideas that precede it. Its central concept is "bodily becoming." It extends concerns of her earlier work *Nietzsche's Dancers* (2006) into a broader philosophical framework.

## Central Thesis

LaMothe starts from a question that seems simple: why do humans dance in every known culture? She argues that rhythmic movement is not a marginal luxury but central to how human beings become creatures of meaning. Through movement the body learns its boundaries and its relation to the world, and through shared, organised movement (including ritual dance) bonds of belonging and communal identity are formed. Dance in this sense is not only "an expression of" something prior (an idea, an emotion, a doctrine) but an act that helps to produce it.

## Critique of Earlier Frameworks

LaMothe criticises long-standing Western philosophical and religious traditions that devalued the body and movement in favour of mind or spirit, regarding dance as mere amusement or even a moral danger. She draws on Nietzsche and on phenomenological and related approaches to the body to restore bodily movement to a central place in the formation of meaning and identity. She also criticises language that treats the body as a passive object moved by a mind, proposing instead descriptions of movement as emerging from the living body as agent.

## Impact

The book belongs to the growing literature on religion, dance and embodiment. It offers a general philosophical framework that scholars can apply to practices across cultures, treating them as acts that produce meaning rather than as folkloric accompaniment to a separate doctrine.

## Background of the Author

LaMothe is a philosopher of religion with a PhD from Harvard University and experience as a dancer and teacher of dance, and she writes for both academic and practitioner audiences.
