# Stakeholder Capitalism

The term "stakeholder capitalism" refers to a model in business ethics and corporate governance. It holds that a commercial company is not morally and legally obliged to serve the interests of its shareholders alone. Instead, it bears responsibility toward a broader circle of **stakeholders**: workers, customers, suppliers, local communities and the environment. The model sharply conflicts with the dominant model in neoliberal economics, known as **shareholder primacy**.

## The Intellectual Context: From Friedman to Freeman

The debate on this subject became acute after the economist **Milton Friedman** published his famous article in *The New York Times Magazine* in 1970, titled "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits." In it he argued that claiming a company has social responsibilities beyond maximizing profit, within the limits of the law, is a dangerous confusion between the role of the chief executive as an agent of the shareholders and the role of that executive as a political actor. He also argued that spending shareholders' money on social goals without their explicit authorization amounts to an illegitimate form of "taxation." The most prominent theoretical response to this view came from the philosopher **R. Edward Freeman** (1984). In his founding book, *Strategic Management: A Stakeholder Approach*, he argued that managers should take account of all groups that can affect or are affected by the firm's purposes ("stakeholders"), rather than treating the firm merely as a tool for serving its owners.

## The Ethical Argument and the Managerial Argument

The defense of stakeholder capitalism rests on two complementary lines of reasoning. The first is an ethical argument: workers and local communities bear real risks (loss of jobs, environmental pollution) that are no less substantial than the financial risks borne by shareholders, and so they deserve comparable moral consideration in the company's decisions. The second is a managerial, pragmatic argument: neglecting the interests of other stakeholders actually harms long-term profitability, through damage to the company's reputation, declining worker loyalty and growing regulatory risk. When the long time horizon is taken into account, the interest of the shareholders themselves converges with the care of these other parties.

## The Islamic Dimension: Stewardship Ownership and the Objectives of Wealth

Islamic jurisprudence offers an advanced theoretical basis for restricting absolute individual ownership by the rights of the community. This is the concept of **stewardship** (*istikhlaf*), which regards wealth as a trust from God that a person manages as a deputy rather than an absolute owner: "And give them from the wealth of God which He has given you" (Qur'an 24:33). This undermines the idea that the owner has an absolute right to dispose of their property without regard for its effect on others. Classical Islamic jurisprudence also established clear rights for workers, such as the worker's right to his wage before his sweat dries, and prohibited exploitation and monopoly that harm consumers. It restricted freedom of contract with rules that protect the weaker party, such as the prohibition of excessive uncertainty (*gharar*) and usury (*riba*). In essence, this parallels the idea of protecting the weakest stakeholders from the domination of pure capital. The principle of **public interest** (*al-maslaha al-amma*), which jurists of legal theory developed as a restriction on individual economic transactions when they conflict with the welfare of the community, is also relevant in this context.

## Contemporary Reception and Criticism

This model gained substantial institutional momentum when a round table of chief executives of major American companies (the Business Roundtable) issued a statement in 2019 formally abandoning the principle of shareholder primacy in favor of an explicit commitment to all stakeholders. Many regarded this as a historic shift in American business discourse. Critics from within the liberal economic current, however, questioned the seriousness of this change in practice. They regarded it as public relations rhetoric more than a binding, accountable commitment. Others argued that the absence of a clear standard for balancing the interests of multiple parties (unlike the clarity of the profit standard) may in fact weaken management's accountability to any party at all.

## The Weak and the Strong Versions

Researchers in this field distinguish between a "weak" version of stakeholder capitalism and a "strong" one. The weak version treats the interests of other parties merely as an instrumental constraint on the pursuit of long-term shareholder profit. In this view, caring for workers and the environment is simply a smart investment in the sustainability of profit. The strong version recognizes stakeholders as having independent rights, separate from any profit calculation for shareholders. On this view, the interest of a worker or local community may take precedence over that of the shareholder, even when doing so does not serve long-term profit. The distinction resembles the one drawn by Donaldson and Preston (1995) between instrumental and normative stakeholder theory; Freeman's own position is generally read as closer to the normative view. Critics argue that many companies that have adopted the language of stakeholders in practice lean toward the weak version, which is more compatible with the logic of the traditional market.

## Legal Regulation: Benefit Corporations

Some legislation has translated this approach into binding legal forms. The most prominent are the **benefit corporation** and its close relative the public benefit corporation, created by American states beginning with Maryland (2010) and, for the Delaware public benefit corporation, in 2013. (A "B Corp" certification, awarded by the private nonprofit B Lab, is a separate, voluntary label.) It legally obliges a company's board of directors to consider the effect of its decisions on workers, the community and the environment alongside shareholders. It thereby gives management explicit legal protection against shareholder lawsuits that might accuse it of failing in its duty to maximize profit. This represents a shift from purely voluntary ethical discourse to a binding legal framework for stakeholder capitalism.
