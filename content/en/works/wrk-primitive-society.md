# The Raw and the Cooked

*The Raw and the Cooked* (1964) is Claude Lévi-Strauss's most ambitious work and the opening volume of the *Mythologiques*, his four-volume study of the mythology of the Americas, which begins in South America. Published in French as *Le Cru et le cuit* and in English in 1969, the book attempts nothing less than to decode the logic of myth — to show that the hundreds of myths told by the Indigenous peoples of the Americas are transformations of one another, generated by a single underlying system of thought. It is structuralism's great demonstration piece and one of the most demanding books in anthropology.

Lévi-Strauss's starting point is a single Bororo myth from central Brazil, the story of the bird-nester, a young man who offends his father, is abandoned on a cliff face, survives, and returns to take revenge — myth M1 in his numbering. In related Ge versions the stranded bird-nester is rescued by a jaguar, from whom humans obtain cooking fire. Rather than interpreting the myth for its hidden moral or its reflection of Bororo life, Lévi-Strauss treats it as one version of a structure. He collects variants of the story from neighbouring peoples, then variants of those variants, following the myth as it travels across the continent and changes form, with characters and elements inverted or transformed from variant to variant. By the end of the book he has assembled and analysed 187 myths, and the argument is that all of them are transformations of the same logical armature.

## Thesis and Method

The thesis is that myths think. Lévi-Strauss famously wrote that myths do not exist to be explained by human minds; rather, myths operate in human minds without their knowledge, thinking themselves through people. What myths think about, in *The Raw and the Cooked*, is the passage from nature to culture — and its central opposition is the one named in the title. The raw and the cooked stand for nature and culture, for the given and the made; around them cluster a series of related oppositions — fresh and rotten, the sky and the earth, silence and noise — each myth arranging these terms in a different pattern.

The method is structural analysis, borrowed from linguistics. Just as a linguist studies a language not by asking what a word means in isolation but by mapping its relations to other words, Lévi-Strauss studies a myth by mapping its relations to other myths. He borrows the language of music — theme, variation, fugue, counterpoint — to describe how myths transform: an element is inverted, displaced, or negated, and the pattern remains recognisable. The book's famous opening, a long overture on Wagner and the form of the fugue, announces this musical model. Myth, like music, is a structure that can be heard only by attending to the relations between its parts.

The analysis is organised around the opposition of the raw and the cooked, and its main demonstration concerns the origin of cooking fire. In the Ge myths, humans first eat their meat raw or dried in the sun, and the jaguar, who possesses fire, is the figure from whom fire is obtained. Lévi-Strauss reads the myths as thinking about the passage from nature to culture through cooking. The theme of honey, which he treats as a food of nature that needs no cooking, is developed mainly in the following volume, *From Honey to Ashes*.

## Reception and Influence

*The Raw and the Cooked* made structuralism the dominant intellectual movement of the 1960s, influential far beyond anthropology — in literary theory, philosophy, psychoanalysis, and the study of religion. Its admirers praised the audacity of the project: a single mind drawing order from hundreds of apparently chaotic tales, and discovering a logic that no storyteller knew they were following. Its demonstration that "primitive" thought is rigorous, systematic, and intellectual — not a primitive confusion but a different science — was a powerful blow against the evolutionist assumption that myth is failed explanation.

Criticism was equally strong. Anthropologists complained that the method ignored the social context of myth-telling: who tells a myth, to whom, on what occasion, and to what political effect. Lévi-Strauss's myths float free of the societies that tell them, and his transformations depend on choices of which variants to compare that are never fully justified. Philosophers and historians questioned whether the structures he found were in the myths or imposed by the analyst. And the book's difficulty — its dense argument, its constant cross-reference, its refusal of summary — made it admired more often than read.

The *Mythologiques* continued with *From Honey to Ashes* (1966), *The Origin of Table Manners* (1968), and *The Naked Man* (1971), but *The Raw and the Cooked* remains the point of entry and the purest statement of the method. Whatever its limits, it established a permanent question for anthropology: whether beneath the bewildering variety of human stories there is a common architecture of thought, and whether the study of myth can reveal it.
