# The Stranger

"The Stranger: An Essay in Social Psychology," published by Alfred Schutz in 1944 in the *American Journal of Sociology*, is a classic of phenomenological sociology and one of the most cited short essays in the discipline. In barely ten pages, Schutz analyses what happens when a person tries to join a group whose way of life is not their own: the immigrant, the newcomer, the convert, the stranger. Drawing on Georg Simmel's earlier essay "The Stranger" (1908) but giving the theme a new phenomenological depth, Schutz shows that membership in a group depends not on formal status but on sharing its unspoken stock of taken-for-granted knowledge.

## Historical Context and Publication

Schutz wrote the essay as a refugee himself. Born in Vienna in 1899, trained in law and the social sciences, and deeply influenced by Edmund Husserl's phenomenology, he was in Paris at the time of the Anschluss of 1938, did not resume life in Austria, and emigrated to the United States in 1939, where he later taught at the New School for Social Research in New York. The essay appeared in 1944, while the war that had displaced millions was still raging, and it was followed a year later by its companion piece, "The Homecomer." Schutz was writing at a moment when sociology was dominated by structural and statistical approaches, and his insistence that social order rests on the shared, unreflective assumptions of everyday life offered a radically different starting point.

## Thesis and Core Argument

Schutz's central claim is that every group lives by a "cultural pattern" — a body of recipes, rules, typifications, and taken-for-granted assumptions that its members use to interpret the world and act within it. For members, this pattern is not a theory but simply "thinking as usual": the world is as it has always been, other people can be relied on to behave in familiar ways, and the knowledge handed down by parents and teachers is sufficient for practical life. The stranger is someone who approaches a group without sharing this pattern. What was unquestioned becomes questionable: the newcomer's old recipes no longer work, the new group's ways appear strange rather than natural, and every ordinary interaction requires conscious attention. The stranger is thus defined not by physical distance but by a crisis of relevance — a breakdown in the shared assumptions that make everyday life run smoothly.

## Structure and Method

The essay proceeds by a careful phenomenological analysis rather than by empirical fieldwork. Schutz begins with the concept of the cultural pattern and the "thinking as usual" of the in-group, then introduces the stranger as the figure for whom this pattern fails. He traces the stages of the stranger's experience: the initial shock of finding familiar recipes useless, the attempt to learn the new group's pattern as an outsider, the gradual acquisition of its taken-for-granted knowledge, and the persistent insecurity that accompanies this learning, since knowledge acquired as an adult never has the self-evidence of knowledge absorbed in childhood. The companion essay on the "homecomer" contrasts this position with that of someone returning to a familiar group, where both have changed. Schutz's method is the description of the structures of the life-world, developed from Husserl's philosophy and applied to a concrete social situation.

## Illustrative Cases

Schutz's essay offers no fieldwork, and its approaching immigrant is an ideal type. Related cases show what such analysis can bring into view. In "The Homecomer" Schutz drew on the situation of servicemen returning from war, who expected a familiar home while both they and those awaiting them had changed. Harold Garfinkel's *Studies in Ethnomethodology* (1967) reports exercises in which students behaved as lodgers in their own homes. Treating familiar routines as unfamiliar exposed expectations that relatives ordinarily took for granted, and often provoked bewilderment or anger. The same book presents the case of Agnes, a young transgender woman whom Garfinkel studied through interviews at a university medical centre. Agnes had to learn and display conduct that others treated as natural, while avoiding disclosure, and Garfinkel described this as a continuing practical accomplishment. Neither study is a test of Schutz's account of immigrants. They extend a related concern with tacit knowledge to situations of disrupted or newly acquired membership.

## Key Concepts

The essay's concepts have become part of the standard vocabulary of interpretive sociology. The "cultural pattern" names the shared stock of knowledge that organises a group's life-world. "Thinking as usual" captures the unreflective confidence of the insider. "Relevance" describes how people select what matters from the flow of experience, so that the same situation presents different relevances to insider and outsider. The "stranger" is not simply a migrant but an ideal type of anyone who must learn a new pattern of group life. The paired figure of the "homecomer" extends the analysis to return migration.

## Reception and Influence

"The Stranger" has been highly influential despite its brevity. It is a staple of courses on sociological theory, migration, and the sociology of everyday life, and it shaped the development of ethnomethodology: Garfinkel's studies of how people repair breaches in shared understanding owe a direct debt to Schutz's analysis of what happens when "thinking as usual" breaks down. The essay also influenced the sociology of migration, providing a framework for understanding assimilation, integration, and the experience of refugees that goes beyond economic or demographic indicators to capture the lived experience of not knowing how things are done.

## Critical Assessments

Critics have noted the essay's limits as well as its strengths. Because it is an ideal-typical analysis, it says little about the power relations that structure the stranger's situation: the immigrant is not only cognitively disoriented but often legally vulnerable, economically exploited, and racially marked, and the host group may actively refuse membership regardless of how well the stranger learns its ways. Schutz's account also generalises from a particular kind of stranger — the educated European immigrant of his own experience — and may fit less well the experiences of colonised peoples, forced migrants, or those whose difference is visible and stigmatised.

## Continuing Relevance

In an era of mass migration, refugee displacement, and increasingly diverse societies, Schutz's essay is more relevant than ever. It explains why integration is so much harder than learning a language or finding a job: it requires acquiring a whole world of tacit knowledge, from humour and politeness to the unspoken rules of institutions. It also explains the insider's experience, illuminating why established residents can experience newcomers as a threat to a world they had never needed to think about.
