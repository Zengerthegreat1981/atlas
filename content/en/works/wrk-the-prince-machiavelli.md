# The Prince by Niccolò Machiavelli

## The Central Thesis

The separation of rule from traditional moral virtue: the ruler is judged by the result, that is, the preservation and stability of the state, not by the conformity of his conduct to prior moral or religious ideals. Machiavelli announces his method explicitly in chapter fifteen: he will follow the "effectual truth of the matter" (verità effettuale della cosa) and not imagine virtuous republics that never existed, because the gulf between how people live and how they ought to live is so wide that whoever abandons what is done for what ought to be done pursues his ruin and not his preservation. From this thesis is derived his most famous saying: it is better for a ruler to be feared than loved, if it is impossible to have both, since love is a bond that people break whenever they wish, while fear is held fast by the dread of punishment. But he qualifies this with an explicit warning against hatred: the ruler must be feared without being hated, and the most dangerous cause of hatred is touching the property and the women of his subjects.

## The Structure of the Work

A short treatise of twenty-six chapters addressed to Lorenzo de' Medici. The first eleven chapters begin by classifying the kinds of principalities and the ways of acquiring them: by inheritance, by arms, by force and fortune as in the example of Cesare Borgia, whom Machiavelli regards as a model of decisive action, by crime, or by the support of citizens, and then ecclesiastical principalities. Chapters twelve to fourteen deal with armies, and call for reliance on one's own force and not mercenaries. Chapters fifteen to twenty-three move to the qualities of the prince and his practical conduct: generosity and miserliness, cruelty and mercy, keeping faith, avoiding contempt and hatred, and choosing ministers and avoiding flatterers. The chapters conclude with an account of the reasons Italy's princes lost their states, then a chapter on fortune, and finally an ardent call to liberate Italy from foreigners.

## The Basic Concepts

The book revolves around three concepts. The first is virtue (virtù), which does not mean moral virtue but the capacity and skill to act appropriately to circumstance. The second is fortune (fortuna), which Machiavelli compares in chapter twenty-five to a raging river or to a woman who is overcome by boldness, and he estimates that half of affairs are in the hands of fortune and half in the hands of man. The third is necessity (necessità), which justifies a harsh act when there is no choice. In chapter eighteen he asks the prince to combine the natures of the lion and the fox: the lion frightens the wolves, and the fox avoids the traps. In the same chapter he observes that people judge by their eyes more than by their hands, so it suffices for the prince to appear endowed with the virtues. This observation is what gives the book an indirect connection to psychology: an early theory of the psychology of power and the management of public impression.

## The Historical Context of Publication

The book was written in 1513 when Machiavelli was in his rural exile at Sant'Andrea in Percussina after being removed from his post in the Republic of Florence and imprisoned and tortured following the return of the Medici to power in 1512. He told his friend Francesco Vettori in a letter of December 1513 that he had composed a little treatise on principalities. He ultimately dedicated it to Lorenzo de' Medici seeking a return to public service. It was not published in his lifetime: it circulated in manuscript among his acquaintances, and was published for the first time after his death in 1532 in Rome by Antonio Blado with a license from Pope Clement VII of the Medici, and in the same year the Giunta edition appeared in Florence. This gap between the dates of writing and publication is essential: it was written seeking practical employment at a specific political moment, not as a philosophical system published for the public.

## The Book in Light of the Mirrors-for-Princes Literature

The book belongs in form to the "mirrors for princes" literature that flourished in the European and Islamic worlds alike, for the Arab reader has known since the medieval age texts such as al-Ghazali's *Counsel for Kings*, Nizam al-Mulk's *Siyasat-nama*, al-Mawardi's *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya*, and Ibn al-Muqaffa's *Adab*. But Machiavelli departs from this tradition in that his advice does not begin from the image of the just, ideal ruler but from a description of what rulers actually do, so he does not present religion or justice as a foundation of kingship but regards them as possible instruments. Some scholars have tried to compare him with Ibn Khaldun in explaining the rise and fall of states, but the difference remained clear in that Ibn Khaldun analyzes group solidarity (asabiyya) in civilizational phases, while Machiavelli addresses an adviser who wants to make a decision.

## Reception and Criticism

From its appearance the book provoked sharp controversy: it was placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Prohibited Books (1559), and the name Machiavelli itself became a term of abuse in European political literature for centuries. From another angle, historians of thought contradicted this superficial reading by comparing it with his broader republican work, the *Discourses on the First Decade of Titus Livy* (written c. 1513–1519 and published in 1531), where he explicitly prefers the republic to the principality, so *The Prince* becomes for them a response to an emergency situation and not a comprehensive doctrine. In the twentieth century the name became a psychological term: Richard Christie and Florence Geis in 1970 formulated the "Machiavellianism" scale (Mach-IV) to measure a personality trait consisting of manipulation, emotional coldness, and contempt for conventional morality, and it became one of the sides of the "Dark Triad" with narcissism and psychopathy, a selective reading that ignores the republican face of the author.

## Limits

The book remains bound to its contemporaries: its examples are from Renaissance Italy and the politics of city-states, and from the conditions of new princes, so it is not suitable as a general guide for every society. It contains an apparent contradiction between praise of effective cruelty and the warning against hatred, and between the claim of realism and its ardent call in the last chapter to liberate Italy, a call that approaches oratory. Machiavellian realism sometimes ignores the effect of legitimacy on the stability of rule, which the social contract philosophers would later restore.
