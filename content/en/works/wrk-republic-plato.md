# The Republic of Plato (Politeia)

## The Central Thesis

Justice (dikaiosynē) is not a utilitarian social agreement but an **inner harmony**: each part of the soul, and each class in the city, performing its own function without encroaching on another. Plato builds the argument through the analogy of the city as the soul written large (the city is easier to read than the individual), and so arrives at the conclusion that individual justice and political justice are one structure repeated at two levels. Against it he sets the challenge that Glaucon presents in Book Two: that people do justice only for fear of punishment, and if they possessed the ring of Gyges that makes them invisible, no one would adhere to it. The aim of the book is to demonstrate that justice is good for the soul in itself, not for its fruits.

## The Structure of the Work

Ten books, a dialogue in Socrates' voice with Thrasymachus, Glaucon, Adeimantus, and others, in the house of Cephalus in the Piraeus, narrated by Socrates himself:

- **Book One**: the debate over the common definitions of justice (Cephalus: returning what is owed; Polemarchus: benefiting friends and harming enemies; Thrasymachus: the interest of the stronger) and their collapse.
- **Books Two and Three**: the challenge of Glaucon and Adeimantus, and the building of a city in speech that arises from need, then a luxurious city that requires guardians, the education of the guardians through music and gymnastics, the supervision of poetry, and the "noble lie" about the origin of the classes.
- **Book Four**: the virtues of the city (wisdom, courage, moderation, and justice) and the **tripartite soul**: reason (logistikon), spirit or will (thymoeidēs), and appetite (epithymētikon), corresponding to the three classes of the city: the philosopher-rulers, the guardians, and the producers.
- **Books Five to Seven**: the "three waves": the equality of women guardians with men, the community of women and children among the guardians, and the rule of the philosophers. Then the image of the Good through the analogy of the sun, the divided line, and the **allegory of the cave** as a symbol of the passage from shadow and opinion to light and rational knowledge, and the method of educating the philosopher through mathematics and then dialectic.
- **Books Eight and Nine**: the decline of the forms of government, from aristocracy to timocracy to oligarchy to democracy to tyranny, as a gradual disintegration of the tripartite soul, and the life of the tyrant as the most wretched of lives.
- **Book Ten**: the critique of poetry and dramatic art as an **imitation of imitation**, three degrees removed from the truth, then a proof of the immortality of the soul and the myth of Er on the fate of souls.

The correspondence of the city's three classes with the three parts of the soul is an **early structural model of the soul** in Western philosophy, and it is compared, with great caution, to Freud's triad (id, ego, and superego): the similarity is structural, for the Platonic concepts are ethical-political and the Freudian ones clinical-analytic, and between them lie more than two thousand years with no documented direct line of derivation.

## Composition and Manuscripts

There is no sense in speaking of a "first edition" of a work from the fourth century BCE: **the work was not published in the modern sense**, but circulated in the Platonic Academy as manuscript copies. It is dated to about 375 BCE, in the middle of Plato's intellectual life. The text came down through a Byzantine manuscript tradition, among the earliest complete witnesses of which is the Paris Greek manuscript 1807 (known by the siglum A, 9th century). The reference numbering accepted worldwide goes back to the edition of Henri Estienne, 1578, and is still used in every academic citation. The critical edition accepted today is that of S. R. Slings (Oxford, 2003), which replaced Burnet's edition.

## Reception and Criticism

The work had a foundational influence on political philosophy (the theory of the philosopher-ruler), epistemology (the divided line and the cave), and the theory of art (the critique of imitation, an early and influential philosophical attack on poetry and theater in the West). Aristotle, Plato's student, replied to his project in Book Two of the *Politics*, criticizing the abolition of private property and the family in the guardian class on the ground that what belongs to everyone is cared for by no one. In the twentieth century Karl Popper, in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), made a sharp critique of the project of the ideal city as an intellectual origin of totalitarianism, a reading that is still debated among historians of political philosophy, and Leo Strauss offered a counter-reading that sees the ideal city as a tool for disclosing the limits of politics rather than a program for implementation. Julia Annas, in *An Introduction to Plato's Republic* (1981), made ethics and the happiness of the individual the axis of the book, not politics.

The Muslim philosophers received the book in the form of summaries and political treatments by al-Farabi and Averroes, not as a complete text.

## Limits of the Work

Critics fault the book because the analogy between city and soul presupposes that the city has a unity like the unity of the soul, an assumption that is not demonstrated, and because the demonstration that the just person is happier passes from the just soul as harmonious to its happiness without a sufficient intermediary. Many regard its political project, including the noble lie, the supervision of poetry, and the abolition of the family, as at odds with what they call individual freedom. Others, among them Strauss, hold that the dialogue contains an irony that makes it a test of the limits of the ideal city rather than a call to build it.
