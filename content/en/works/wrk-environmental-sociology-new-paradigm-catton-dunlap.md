# Environmental Sociology: A New Paradigm

"Environmental Sociology: A New Paradigm" (1978) is an article by William R. Catton Jr. and Riley E. Dunlap, published in *The American Sociologist*. It is widely treated as a founding statement of environmental sociology as a subfield.

## Historical Context

Catton and Dunlap, both then at Washington State University, wrote at a time when environmental concern had risen in the United States after the first Earth Day (1970) and reports such as *The Limits to Growth* (1972). Sociology, however, had largely treated the natural environment as the concern of other disciplines and had focused on social, cultural and economic structures.

## The Central Thesis

The authors argued that mainstream sociology, including much of the classical tradition, rested on an implicit "Human Exceptionalism Paradigm" (later renamed the Human Exemptionalism Paradigm): the assumption that humans, because of culture and technology, are exempt from the ecological constraints that govern other species. This assumption led sociologists to ignore the biophysical environment as a variable, to treat technological progress as able to overcome limits, and to regard social causes as explaining social facts. They proposed instead a "New Environmental Paradigm", later called the New Ecological Paradigm, that situates human societies within ecosystems of limited resources and emphasises the interdependence of humans with other species and the existence of ecological limits to growth. Dunlap and Van Liere also contrasted the new paradigm with the "Dominant Social Paradigm", a term borrowed from Pirages and Ehrlich, which embodied belief in abundance, progress and the unlimited growth of industrial society.

The article also defined environmental sociology as the study of interactions between the environment and society, a field in which the environment is a variable and not only a context. Catton and Dunlap developed the argument further in "A New Ecological Paradigm for Post-Exuberant Sociology" (1980) and in the review essay "Environmental Sociology" (1979) in the *Annual Review of Sociology*, and Catton's book *Overshoot* (1980) set out the ecological argument at length.

## The NEP Scale

The "New Environmental Paradigm" scale is a separate instrument, developed by Riley Dunlap and Kent Van Liere (1978) to measure public endorsement of the ecological worldview, and revised as the New Ecological Paradigm scale by Dunlap and colleagues in 2000. It is widely used in surveys of environmental attitudes around the world.

## Impact and Critique

The article helped consolidate environmental sociology as a recognised subfield, and it prepared the ground for later theories such as Allan Schnaiberg's treadmill of production (set out in *The Environment: From Surplus to Scarcity*, 1980) and John Bellamy Foster's work on Marx's metabolic rift (1999). Critics have argued that the paradigm contrast is broad and that the 1978 formulation offered little explanation of the social and economic mechanisms that produce environmental degradation, an area where treadmill and political-economy approaches later contributed. Others from constructivist perspectives questioned the realist stance toward environmental problems.
