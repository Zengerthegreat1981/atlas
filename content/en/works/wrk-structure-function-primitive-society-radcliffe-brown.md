# Structure and Function in Primitive Society

***Structure and Function in Primitive Society: Essays and Addresses*** is a collection of essays by **A. R. Radcliffe-Brown**, published in **1952**, with a foreword by E. E. Evans-Pritchard and Fred Eggan. It states the classic programme of British structural-functionalism. Its central claim is that a society can be studied as a system of relations, and that every custom, belief, and institution can be explained by the part it plays in maintaining that system. The book did not present new fieldwork; instead it gathered the theoretical essays Radcliffe-Brown had written over three decades and turned them into a single, systematic doctrine. For a generation of anthropologists it was the textbook of how to think about society.

Radcliffe-Brown (1881–1955) was, with Bronislaw Malinowski, one of the two leading founding figures of modern British social anthropology. Where Malinowski explained institutions by the individual needs they satisfied, Radcliffe-Brown explained them by their function in the social system as a whole. The distinction matters: for Malinowski a ritual might satisfy hunger or anxiety; for Radcliffe-Brown it maintained the solidarity of the group. The 1952 collection gathers his most important statements of this position. An introduction treats process, structure, and function. The essays include "The Mother's Brother in South Africa" (1924), "Patrilineal and Matrilineal Succession", "The Study of Kinship Systems", "On Joking Relationships" (1940) and "A Further Note on Joking Relationships", "The Sociological Theory of Totemism", "Taboo", "Religion and Society", "On the Concept of Function in Social Science", "On Social Structure" (1940), "Social Sanctions", and "Primitive Law". The word "primitive" in the title belongs to the usage of the period.

## Structure and Function

The book's first key idea is **social structure**: the network of actually existing relations between persons — kinship ties, political authority, economic obligation — that persists while individuals come and go. He distinguishes this from **structural form**, the relatively constant pattern the observer describes as persons are replaced. A society, in this view, is not a collection of people but a structure of relations, and the anthropologist's task is to describe that structure accurately. The second key idea is **function**: the contribution that a particular institution or practice makes to the maintenance of the structure. A funeral rite, for example, is not explained by what the participants believe about the dead, but by how it reaffirms the solidarity of the surviving group and reassigns the dead person's social roles.

Radcliffe-Brown borrowed the analogy of the organism from Durkheim and from the natural sciences, while noting limits to the analogy: just as the heart's function is to circulate blood and thereby keep the body alive, so a clan's function is to organise cooperation and thereby keep the society alive. In "On the Concept of Function in Social Science" he prefers to speak of necessary conditions of existence rather than of needs, so as to avoid a teleological reading. He was careful to distinguish this "social function" from historical origin. To ask what a custom does now is a different question from asking where it came from, and he argued that anthropology should answer the first question before it speculates about the second. This was a deliberate rejection of the older evolutionary anthropology, which had explained customs as survivals of earlier stages of human development.

## Kinship, Joking, and the Mother's Brother

The most celebrated essays in the collection concern kinship. In "The Mother's Brother in South Africa," Radcliffe-Brown showed that among the Tsonga and other southern African peoples, the relationship between a man and his mother's brother follows a regular pattern in patrilineal societies: the maternal uncle is a figure of indulgence and generosity, while the father is a figure of authority and discipline. He explained this not by psychology but by structure, and he rejected the older claim that a sister's son's privileges were a survival of an earlier matrilineal stage. The mother's brother belongs to a different descent group from the father's, and the contrast in behaviour expresses the structural opposition between the two lines. Principles such as the unity of the sibling group and of the lineage are used to account for terminologies and behaviour. The essay became a model of structural analysis, showing how a small piece of behaviour — a joke, a gift, a gesture of deference — can reveal the architecture of a whole society.

The essay "On Joking Relationships" extended the same logic to the patterned familiarity between certain categories of relatives — for example, between a man and his wife's brothers — where insult and teasing are not merely permitted but required. Radcliffe-Brown argued that joking relationships manage structural tension: where two people are linked by a relationship that combines closeness and hostility, institutionalised joking allows the hostility to be expressed without breaking the bond. The analysis remains a classic example of how anthropology can find order in what looks like disorder.

In the essays on ritual, rites express and sustain the sentiments on which social cohesion depends. Radcliffe-Brown argues that a rite may create anxiety as well as relieve it, in contrast with Malinowski's account of magic. A custom is placed among related customs, compared with similar arrangements elsewhere, and explained by a general principle about relations between persons or groups. On totemism, he asks why natural species acquire ritual value and links that value to the segmentation of society and to the importance of those species in social life. On sanctions and law, he distinguishes diffuse approval and disapproval from organised penalties, and treats law as social control through the systematic application of force by a politically organised society. That definition implies that some societies have no law in the strict sense, a conclusion later anthropologists of dispute settlement contested.

## Method and Influence

The book's method was comparative. Radcliffe-Brown argued that anthropology should be a comparative science, discovering general laws of social life by comparing structures across societies, just as comparative anatomy discovers general laws of organic life. His own comparisons ranged from the Andaman Islanders, whom he had studied in the field in 1906-08 and described in *The Andaman Islanders* (1922), to the peoples of Australia, Africa, and the Pacific. The comparative ambition was grand, but it was also the book's vulnerability: critics noted that the comparisons often rested on thin ethnography and that the "laws" discovered were frequently little more than restatements of the definitions.

The influence of the collection was nevertheless enormous. It influenced the generation of British and Commonwealth anthropologists who dominated the discipline in the 1950s and 1960s, including Evans-Pritchard, Fortes, Gluckman and Leach, even when they rebelled against it. Through his teaching at the University of Chicago it also shaped part of American anthropology. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) is often cited as a classic work in the structural tradition (though Evans-Pritchard later rejected the label of natural science for anthropology), and Meyer Fortes's work on the Tallensi developed its analysis of descent and political structure. The book's critics were equally important: Edmund Leach attacked its static view of society, arguing that structure is an ideal that people manipulate rather than a reality they inhabit; Max Gluckman and the Manchester school insisted on conflict, change, and individual agency; and later structuralists, Marxists, and interpretivists all defined themselves in part against Radcliffe-Brown. Critics also argued that explaining a practice by its contribution to continuity does not show why it arose, and that conflict, individual strategy, and colonial history receive limited analysis. Yet the questions he posed — what holds a society together, and what does each of its parts contribute to that holding — remain at the centre of anthropological theory.
