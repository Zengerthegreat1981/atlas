# The Mahabharata as Historical Record

## Definition

The **Mahabharata** ("The Great Tale of the Bharatas") is considered the **longest epic poem in history**: about **1.6 million words** in Sanskrit, many times longer than the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* combined. It describes itself as ***Itihasa*** ("thus indeed it was"), meaning a **historical record** rather than pure myth or purely literary fiction. This self-classification distinguishes it in the Indian tradition from straightforward epic literature.

## Historical Context

The text of the Mahabharata accumulated over many centuries of collective composition and editing. Its mythical authorship is attributed to the sage **Vyasa**, but most contemporary scholars hold that an older poetic nucleus (perhaps dating to the late second or early first millennium BCE) gradually expanded through successive additions until it reached something close to its final form around the fourth century CE. This makes it a layered, cumulative text rather than a single work composed at a particular time.

## Alleged Historical Content

### The Battle of Kurukshetra

- A conflict **between the Pandava and Kaurava families** over the throne
- **18 days** of fierce fighting
- It includes a **debate on the ethics of war** (discussions of the duties of the warrior)

### Kings and Genealogy

- A **detailed sequence of kings and lineages**, similar to the Mesopotamian king lists
- **Geographic specifications** of the kingdoms of ancient India
- **Treaties and alliances** between kings

### Social Order

- **The varna system**: Brahmins, Kshatriyas (warriors), Vaishyas, Shudras
- **Laws and customs** (*Dharmashastra*, the science of governance)
- **Royal duties** (*rajadharma*)

## A Multi-Layered Nature

The Mahabharata is not a single linear narrative:
- **The main narrative**: the Battle of Kurukshetra
- **The embedded stories**: hundreds of secondary tales explaining the ethics of war and the state
- **Philosophy**: especially the **Bhagavad Gita**, a philosophical dialogue between Krishna and Arjuna about duty and ethics, which is today regarded as an independent religious and philosophical text read outside its original epic context.

## Its Historical Value

**Scholars today are divided**:
- **The traditional school** (the Indian source): the Mahabharata is a **reliable historical record**, and the Battle of Kurukshetra is **real**, dated to roughly 1500–900 BCE.
- **The modern school** (Western archaeologists): there is **no conclusive archaeological evidence** for the battle on the epic scale claimed, and it may be a **blend of several smaller historical events** that oral tradition magnified over generations.

## Impact and Criticism

This debate reflects a general tension in traditional Indian historiography between a religious-traditional reading that treats the *Itihasa* as a reliable historical record, and a modern academic critical reading that treats it as a multi-layered literary-religious text carrying genuine historical seeds mixed with later mythical and philosophical elements that cannot be separated with absolute certainty.

## Comparison with Other Traditions

The historical status of the Mahabharata can be compared with that of other foundational epics in different civilisations. For example, archaeological excavations (Troy) have shown that the Greek *Iliad* partly had a possible historical core behind its mythical events, and biblical chronicles are discussed by scholars in the same way regarding their actual historicity. This cross-civilisational parallel provides a useful comparative framework for understanding how ancient societies generally dealt with the boundary between collective historical memory and the religious-mythical sanctification of a founding past, without necessarily separating them with the sharpness that modern historical method presupposes.

Indian archaeologists also continue to excavate sites believed to be linked with events of the epic (such as the site of Hastinapura), without yet reaching a decisive consensus that proves or disproves the occurrence of a battle on the scale described in the epic. This keeps the question open between the traditional religious interpretation and the cautious archaeological interpretation.

Passages of the epic, especially the Bhagavad Gita, are also used as material taught in comparative philosophy programmes worldwide, apart from the broader debate on the historicity of the epic, as a philosophical and ethical text in its own right that deserves study independent of the question of the historical accuracy of the war events around it.

The Mahabharata today is also a rich resource for comparative cultural studies on how the great civilisations dealt with the question of foundational memory. Some researchers compare its status with that of the Chinese and ancient Persian epic texts (such as the *Shahnameh*), in terms of the balance between the historical function and the moral-religious function that each text performed for its original society through centuries of retelling and interpretation.

Finally, it is worth noting that the modern complete translations of the Mahabharata, such as the academic project of the University of Chicago that continued for decades in producing a complete critical English translation, have allowed researchers who are not specialists in Sanskrit to access this vast text directly and with scholarly accuracy for the first time. For centuries it had been confined to specialist readers in the original language, or to abridged and selective translations that did not reflect the vast size and full layered complexity of the original.
