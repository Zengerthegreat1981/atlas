# The Mahabharata as Historical Record (Itihasa)

## Definition

The ***Mahabharata*** ("The Great Epic of the Bharata Dynasty") is an ancient Indian Sanskrit epic encompassing over 100,000 verses (*shlokas*) arranged into eighteen books (*parvans*), making it the longest monumental epic composition in world literature. Attributed in the indigenous tradition to the legendary sage **Krishna Dvaipayana Vyasa**, the work defines itself fundamentally as ***Itihasa*** (literally, "thus indeed it was")—a category denoting traditional history or commemorative chronicle—rather than as purely aesthetic poetry (*kavya*) or purely cosmic myth (*purana*).

## Compositional History and Textual Stratification

Modern philological scholarship, culminated by the landmark Pune Critical Edition prepared by V. S. Sukthankar and colleagues at the Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute (1933–1966), indicates that the *Mahabharata* is not the product of a single author or historical moment, but a multi-layered cumulative text that developed over nearly a millennium, between approximately 400 BCE and 400 CE:
Scholars often describe the growth of the text through stages (a model that remains hypothetical):
1. ***Jaya*** ("Victory"): An early bardic nucleus of around 8,800 verses focusing directly on the martial conflict between the cousins.
2. ***Bharata***: An expanded version of roughly 24,000 verses recited by Vaishampayana to King Janamejaya.
3. ***Mahabharata***: The massive encyclopedic text of over 100,000 verses, into which brahminical redactors incorporated extensive legal, political (*rajadharma*), philosophical, and ethical treatises, including the *Bhagavad Gita* (situated in Book VI, the *Bhishma Parva*), the *Shanti Parva*, and the *Anushasana Parva*.

## Narrative Architecture and Historiographical Classification

The central historical narrative recounts the eighteen-day war fought on the plain of Kurukshetra (in modern Haryana) between the five Pandava brothers and their cousins, the one hundred Kauravas, over the succession to the Kuru kingdom centered at Hastinapura.

In early Indian historiography, as analyzed by historian Romila Thapar, *Itihasa* represents an indigenous "embedded history" characterized by:
- **Dynastic Genealogies (*Vamshanucharita*)**: Recording royal lineages, ancestral descents, and succession lines of the Lunar dynasty (*Chandravamsha*).
- **The Codification of *Dharma***: Providing normative case studies on royal statecraft, treaty obligations, legitimate warfare, and social obligations according to the fourfold class structure (*varna*).
- **Temporal Cycles (*Yugas*)**: Framing the conflict as a cosmic transition between the *Dvapara Yuga* and the degenerate *Kali Yuga*, marking the end of heroic antiquity.

## The Problem of Historicity and Archaeology

The question of whether the Kurukshetra War corresponds to an actual historical event has been the subject of extensive archaeological and philological investigation:
- **Archaeological Investigation**: In the 1950s and subsequent decades, Indian archaeologist B. B. Lal excavated sites associated with the epic—including Hastinapura, Kurukshetra, Tilpat, and Barnawa—uncovering levels of the **Painted Grey Ware (PGW) culture** (c. 1200–600 BCE). Lal interpreted flood evidence at Hastinapura as matching the tradition that the Kuru capital was moved to Kaushambi; such correlations between archaeology and the epic remain debated.
- **Scholarly Views**: Many historians hold that the monumental scale, supernatural weapons (*astras*), and divine avatars (Krishna) belong to mythological expansion, while some see in the epic a distant echo of clan rivalries and state formation among the Late Vedic Kuru-Panchala groups of northern India; others doubt that any specific historical war can be recovered.
