# The Divided Self: An Existential Study in Sanity and Madness

*The Divided Self: An Existential Study in Sanity and Madness* (1960), by the Scottish psychiatrist R. D. Laing (1927–1989), is the most famous attempt in English to read psychosis from the inside, and still the most controversial. Published by Tavistock in London when Laing was about thirty-three, and issued in a popular Penguin edition in 1965, it argues that speech which looks meaningless becomes intelligible when it is read in the context of the speaker's life and family rather than in a table of symptoms. Traditional psychiatry, Laing charges, describes the patient as it would describe an object, and in doing so loses all access to their experience. His alternative is to understand the person as an embodied being-in-the-world with others. The book made Laing a public figure, helped launch the anti-psychiatry movement he later refused to join, and remains the text through which most readers first meet the existential approach to madness.

## Ontological Insecurity and the Divided Self

The book's key concept is ontological insecurity: not being sure of the reality, continuity, and identity of one's own existence. A person who lacks this basic security lives under the permanent threat of three dangers Laing names engulfment, implosion, and petrification — being swallowed up by the other, being invaded and destroyed from within, or being turned into a thing. In response, the person divides. A false-self system is constructed to face the world and deal with others, while the inner self withdraws and separates itself from the body and from action, until the body becomes something owned by others rather than an expression of the person. When the division finally breaks down, what is called psychosis appears. Laing's point is not that this account explains the causes of schizophrenia. It is that the experience has a structure that can be described from the inside, and that without such a description psychiatry is working blind.

## Philosophical Sources

Laing's framework is assembled from several traditions. From Kierkegaard's *The Sickness unto Death* he takes the idea of despair as not wanting to be oneself; from Heidegger and Sartre, the analysis of being-in-the-world and of the gaze; from Binswanger and Minkowski, phenomenological psychiatry; from Martin Buber, the I–Thou relation; from Paul Tillich, the idea of the courage to be. He was also trained in the British object-relations school of psychoanalysis through his work at the Tavistock Clinic. One distinction needs care: the concepts of the true self and the false self as psychoanalytic terms belong to Donald Winnicott, who formulated them in his 1960 paper on ego distortion. Laing's false-self system is a different, existential-phenomenological formulation with different origins, and the origin of the distinction should not be attributed to this book.

## The Cases

The book falls into two interwoven parts: a theoretical section on why clinical description fails to reach experience, on the existential-phenomenological basis of a science of persons, and on ontological insecurity, the embodied and disembodied self, and the false-self system; and a clinical section of cases described in detail. The cases move from the schizoid position to frank schizophrenia, and the most famous is that of Julie, a young woman speaking in fragmented language about the "ghost of the weed garden". Laing reconstructs her speech to show that it is consistent with her history — that she is speaking about her fear of being lost and engulfed — and that what looks like nonsense has a meaning once the listener stops translating it into symptoms. Other cases, David and Peter among them, serve the same purpose: to demonstrate that the patient's words deserve listening rather than reduction.

## Aftermath: Family, Kingsley Hall, and Anti-Psychiatry

Laing followed the book with *Sanity, Madness and the Family* (1964, with Aaron Esterson), which read schizophrenia in the light of family communication, and with *The Politics of Experience* (1967). In 1965 he founded the Philadelphia Association with colleagues and opened Kingsley Hall, a residential therapeutic community run without medical hierarchy, which operated until 1970 and attracted wide attention and controversy. The phrase "anti-psychiatry" was coined by David Cooper in 1967, and Laing refused to be placed under it.

## Reception and Criticism

Mainstream psychiatry described the thesis as untestable and as ignoring biological and genetic evidence. The sharpest criticism came from Anthony Clare in *Psychiatry in Dissent* (1976), who examined Laing's claims about the family and concluded that they do not stand as a causal explanation. Peter Sedgwick criticised the Kingsley Hall experiment from the left in *Psycho Politics* (1982), and a biography by Laing's son Adrian (1994) documented the collapse of his personal life and his addiction, which entered into the assessment of his legacy. The costs Laing's influence carried were real: delayed effective treatments, families burdened with blame without evidence, and a damaged scientific reputation that spread to the whole existential school. What has survived, in contemporary phenomenological psychopathology, is the weaker formulation: that psychotic experience has a structure that can be described from within, and that family and relational context shape its course. The stronger claim — that psychosis is a meaningful journey towards healing — has fallen away.
