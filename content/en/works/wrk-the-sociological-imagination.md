# The Sociological Imagination

*The Sociological Imagination* (Oxford University Press, 1959) is a book by the American sociologist C. Wright Mills (1916-1962), who taught at Columbia University. It combines a statement of what social science can offer public life with a sharp criticism of the sociology of its time.

## The Promise

Mills argues that people often feel trapped in their private lives because they cannot see how those lives are shaped by large historical changes. The sociological imagination is the quality of mind that connects biography, history and social structure. He distinguishes "personal troubles of milieu", which lie within an individual's immediate surroundings, from "public issues of social structure". One person's unemployment may be a trouble explained by that person's circumstances; mass unemployment is an issue that calls for an examination of economic and political institutions. Mills applies similar reasoning to war, marriage and city life.

## Criticism of Sociology

Two tendencies receive the strongest criticism. "Grand theory", represented chiefly by Talcott Parsons and *The Social System*, is said to elaborate abstract concepts at a distance from observable historical problems. "Abstracted empiricism", associated with survey research of the kind developed by Paul Lazarsfeld and others, is said to let technique decide which problems are studied. Mills also criticises the bureaucratic organisation of research, in which social science serves administrative clients.

## The Classic Tradition and Intellectual Craft

Against both tendencies Mills defends what he calls classic social analysis, which asks about the structure of a society as a whole, its place in history and the kinds of people it produces. He holds that social scientists should state their values openly and help turn private troubles into issues open to public reason, in the service of freedom. The appendix, "On Intellectual Craftsmanship", offers practical advice: keep a file of notes and ideas, connect personal experience with research, write clearly and avoid fetishising method.

## Place in Mills's Work

Mills had already studied class and power in *White Collar* (1951) and *The Power Elite* (1956). *The Sociological Imagination* sets out the conception of inquiry that links these studies of American society with his public interventions.

## Reception and Criticism

The book provoked hostile responses from parts of the profession and later became one of the most widely assigned introductions to sociological thinking. It informed radical and public sociology from the 1960s onwards. Critics argue that its portraits of Parsons and of survey research are polemical and selective, that it is clearer about the questions to ask than about how to test answers, and that it gives limited attention to gender and race, as its masculine language also suggests.
