# Tristes Tropiques

***Tristes Tropiques***, by **Claude Lévi-Strauss**, published in French in **1955**, is one of the most famous books ever written by an anthropologist — and one of the strangest. It is at once a travel memoir, a work of ethnography, a philosophical meditation, and an attack on the very idea of travel writing. Its opening line is one of the most quoted sentences in the discipline: "I hate travelling and explorers." From that provocation, Lévi-Strauss unfolds an account of his fieldwork in Brazil in the 1930s among the Caduveo, Bororo, Nambikwara, and Tupi-Kawahib, and uses it to ask what anthropology is for, and what it means to encounter peoples whose worlds are being destroyed by the civilisation that sends the anthropologist to study them.

Lévi-Strauss (1908–2009) was a French anthropologist who became the leading figure of structuralism in anthropology. Before *Tristes Tropiques* he had published *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949), a dense theoretical work on marriage and alliance. *Tristes Tropiques* was written in the mid-1950s, after his return from New York, where he had spent the war years, and it was addressed to a general reader rather than to specialists. It became a bestseller in France (the Goncourt jury stated its regret that, not being a novel, it could not receive the prize) and made its author a public intellectual. A first, abridged English translation by John Russell appeared in 1961 under the title *A World on the Wane*, and the complete translation by John and Doreen Weightman in 1973.

## The Journey and the Peoples

The book's narrative follows Lévi-Strauss's expeditions into the Brazilian interior between 1935 and 1939. He had gone to Brazil to teach sociology at the newly founded University of São Paulo, and from there he travelled by train, river, and mule into the Mato Grosso and Amazonia. The peoples he encountered were, by his own account, already shattered by contact: the Caduveo, descendants of the once-powerful horse-riding Mbaya, were a small population; the Bororo, famous for their elaborate villages and funeral rites, were in decline; the Nambikwara, whom he presents as the most "primitive" of the groups he met, lived in small nomadic bands with almost no material possessions; and the Tupi-Kawahib, in the remote forests of the upper Madeira region, were visited on a brief and arduous expedition.

The ethnographic passages are vivid and unsentimental. Lévi-Strauss describes the face-painting of Caduveo women, the Bororo village laid out as a map of their social and cosmological order, the Nambikwara's nomadic life, the episode known as the "writing lesson", in which a Nambikwara chief imitated writing for his own prestige, and the desolation of a people whose numbers had collapsed. But he refuses to present these peoples as survivals of a pristine past. His point is the opposite: what he saw was already the aftermath of conquest, disease, and displacement, and the anthropologist arrives too late, to document a disappearance he is himself part of. The "sad tropics" of the title are sad because the encounter between Europe and the Americas was a catastrophe, and because the anthropologist's own presence is a further stage of that catastrophe.

## Structure, Myth, and the Critique of Travel

The book's second movement turns from narrative to reflection. Lévi-Strauss argues against the romanticism of travel writing, which treats the journey as self-discovery and the foreign as a mirror for the traveller's own longings. He insists that the peoples he met are not stages in human evolution or symbols of lost innocence, but societies with their own internal logic — a logic that can be grasped only by the patient, structural analysis he had developed in his kinship studies. The Bororo village, for example, is not merely picturesque: its circular layout, with houses arranged around a central men's house, encodes the society's dual organisation, its moieties, and its cosmology. To understand it is to decode a structure, not to admire a spectacle.

From this follows the book's philosophical argument. He does not romanticise the societies he describes; he notes their violence, their rigidity, and their own forms of cruelty. But he questions the West's faith in progress and criticises the destruction of other societies in the name of civilisation. (The contrast between "hot" and "cold" societies, often associated with him, belongs to his later writings and interviews rather than to this book.) In its closing pages he reflects on entropy, coining the half-serious term "entropology" for the study of disintegration, and on the possibility that anthropology's task is to bear witness to the diversity of human life before it disappears.

## Reception and Influence

*Tristes Tropiques* transformed the public image of anthropology. It showed that an ethnographic book could be a work of literature, and it made Lévi-Strauss famous far beyond the discipline. Within anthropology it was read as a manifesto for structuralism and as a challenge to the functionalist ethnography of the British school: where Malinowski had presented the Trobrianders as a functioning society, Lévi-Strauss presented the peoples of Brazil as systems of signs to be decoded. Clifford Geertz, in *Works and Lives* (1988), included a chapter on the book as an example of ethnographic writing, and many readers have remarked on the paradox that a book which opens by denouncing travel writing is itself a major work of that genre.

The book has also been criticised. Some readers have found its tone melancholic, and its portrait of the Nambikwara has been challenged by later fieldwork. Feminist and postcolonial critics have noted its masculine perspective and its tendency to treat the peoples it describes as illustrations of a theory rather than as historical agents. Yet its central themes — the belatedness of the anthropologist, the violence of contact, the search for structure beneath surface variety — have shaped the discipline ever since.
