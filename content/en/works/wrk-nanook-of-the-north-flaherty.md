# Nanook of the North

A documentary film directed by Robert Flaherty (1922), which depicts the life of an Inuit man and his family in northern Quebec on the eastern shore of Hudson Bay. Flaherty was a filmmaker and former explorer, not an academically trained anthropologist. It is regarded as one of the earliest influential feature-length documentary films with an ethnographic character.

## Content and Controversy

Flaherty filmed scenes of hunting, building and the daily life of an Inuit family. However, some scenes (such as the famous scene of building an igloo) were re-enacted and arranged under Flaherty's own direction for cinematic purposes. This later raised a methodological debate about the difference between "documentation" and "staging" in the ethnographic film, and about the ethics of a filmmaker's intervention in the reality he claims to document.

## Impact

Despite the debate over its full authenticity, the film is regarded as a historical starting point for both the documentary and the ethnographic film, and it influenced the development of later visual anthropology standards, which emphasized a clear distinction between direct documentation and directed re-enactment.

## Production Context

Flaherty shot the film in the Inukjuak (then Port Harrison) region of northern Quebec, with funding from the fur company Revillon Frères, working closely with the Inuk hunter Allakariallak, who played the title role of "Nanook" (the name means polar bear in Inuktitut). The film's "family" was assembled for the film. Flaherty had no academic training in anthropology; he had worked as an explorer and prospector in the Canadian north before turning to filmmaking. He relied on his Inuit collaborators for knowledge of hunting and daily life and for the staging of scenes.

## Methodological Controversy

The film is a classic case study in the debate over the limits of "documentary" film. Some of the hunting techniques that were filmed were no longer in everyday use (for example harpoon hunting of walrus, although the Inuit by then had rifles), and an oversized igloo, cut open, was built so that interior scenes could be lit and filmed. This direct intervention by the filmmaker in the "reality" he claimed to document later raised fundamental questions about whether an ethnographic film can be a neutral record at all, or whether every cinematic image necessarily involves an authorial intervention by its maker.

## Early Historical Impact

Despite this controversy, or perhaps partly because of it, the film is still taught today in visual anthropology and film studies programs as a starting point for discussing the ethics of ethnographic representation. It has remained a reference point for later debates in documentary and ethnographic film about staging, collaboration and the ethics of representation.

## Reception and Distribution

Distributors were at first doubtful about a feature-length documentary without stars or a conventional plot, but *Nanook of the North* was released in 1922 (distributed by Pathé) and achieved wide commercial and critical success, showing that a feature-length documentary could reach a mass audience.

## The Real Fate of Nanook

A frequently repeated detail is that Allakariallak, the man who played "Nanook", died about two years after the film was made. Popular accounts say that he starved during a hunting trip, but accounts differ and the cause is not firmly established. The story has been used to underline the contrast between the film's heroic image and the real hardships of Inuit life.

## Its Influence on World Documentary Cinema

Flaherty's own later films include *Moana* (1926, on Samoa) and *Man of Aran* (1934, on the Aran Islands of Ireland). His method of living with his subjects and building films around their lives influenced later documentary and ethnographic film-makers, and Jean Rouch, who developed a collaborative approach in ethnographic film (and a style he called cinéma vérité), acknowledged Flaherty as an inspiration.

## Archival and Historical Value

Despite all the methodological problems, the film remains an important visual document that records techniques and aspects of Inuit life in the early 1920s, though shaped by the filmmaker's selection. This gives it an archival and historical value that goes beyond the debate over its methodological purity alone, although its content should be approached with critical awareness of the need to distinguish between what was filmed directly and what was re-enacted under the director's guidance.

## Inclusion in the United States National Film Registry

The film was among the first group of films selected for the United States National Film Registry in 1989, for its historical, cultural and aesthetic importance.
