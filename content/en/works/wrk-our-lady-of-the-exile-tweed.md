# Our Lady of the Exile

An ethnographic-historical study by Thomas Tweed of the Shrine of Our Lady of Charity (Nuestra Señora de la Caridad del Cobre) in Miami, which was built by Cuban Catholic exiles after the Cuban Revolution of 1959.

## Definition

The book examines one specific case in ethnographic and historical depth: how a generation of Cuban exiles, who fled Cuba after Fidel Castro seized power, turned a traditional popular Catholic devotion (honoring Cuba's national patroness, Our Lady of Charity) into a new religious site in an American city, loaded with deep political and national meanings that go beyond traditional Catholic piety alone.

## Historical Context

The veneration of Our Lady of Charity in Cuba dates back to the early seventeenth century, when the appearance of a statue of the Virgin Mary to fishermen in Nipe Bay is said to have occurred, and it later became a symbol of Cuban national identity, until it was officially declared patroness of Cuba in 1916. After the Cuban Revolution of 1959 and the major waves of migration that followed to Florida, Cuban exiles in Miami established a new shrine to this same patroness (completed in 1973), thereby maintaining the continuity of the devotion on new ground, far from the homeland they had lost.

## Main Ideas and Method

Tweed argues that the shrine is not merely a place of traditional Catholic worship, but a site where the Cuban exiles symbolically rebuild "Cuba" through ritual, memory and architecture. The shrine's design, site and murals evoke Cuban history and the Cuban homeland; annual celebrations are held there that connect the community with its lost national history; and symbols that combine religious piety with political nostalgia opposed to the Cuban regime are sometimes displayed. In this way Tweed presents a methodological model for studying "diasporic religion" in which national longing intertwines with popular religiosity in the context of forced migration.

## Key Works and Representatives

Thomas A. Tweed is an American researcher in religious studies who has worked in depth on questions of religion, migration and cultural boundaries. This case study (the Miami shrine) informed the broader general theory of religion that Tweed later developed in *Crossing and Dwelling: A Theory of Religion* (2006), in which he developed a general theoretical framework that understands religions as symbolic-bodily flows crossing boundaries, rather than fixed systems confined to a single place.

## Impact and Critique

The book is an early and influential work in the field of "religion, migration and transnationalism", in which other researchers such as Peggy Levitt have also worked. Many scholars praised it for combining direct field observation with deep historical analysis of the entangled religious-national symbolism, and it has become a standard reference cited in studies of Latino and Caribbean religious diaspora in North America.

## Final Notes on Field Observation

Tweed discusses the annual celebration of 8 September (the feast of Cuba's patroness) associated with the shrine, when thousands of Cuban Americans gather, with the main public Mass often held at venues larger than the shrine itself, in an event that mixes religious piety with mourning for the lost homeland and opposition to the Cuban government. Tweed shows how religious devotion and political longing for Cuba are interwoven in the celebration.

## Additional Notes on Symbolic Architecture

Tweed analyzes the shrine's architecture and setting: its location on Biscayne Bay gives visitors a sea view that symbolically suggests the distance separating them from the original homeland across the sea. Tweed also discusses how the shrine's mural depicts scenes from Cuban history, blending national memory with religious imagery in a way that embodies the book's central thesis.

## Additional Notes on the Concept of "Crossing and Dwelling"

Tweed later developed in *Crossing and Dwelling* a general theoretical framework that defines religions as confluences of organic-cultural flows that intensify joy and confront suffering by making homes and crossing boundaries, a framework that resonates with his Miami case: how the exiles symbolically "cross" the boundaries of the lost homeland through ritual, and how they simultaneously "dwell" in a new place without abandoning their original belonging. The move from a specific case study to a general theory is often noted in discussions of Tweed's work.
