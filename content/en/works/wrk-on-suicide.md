# On Suicide: The Strategic Meanings of Self-Destruction

*On Suicide* is Jean Baechler's study of suicide as meaningful human conduct, published in French as *Les Suicides* (Paris: Calmann-Lévy, 1975) and in English as *Suicides* (New York: Basic Books, 1979). It began as Baechler's doctoral thesis, supervised by Raymond Aron, and it is the most important sociological challenge to Durkheim's *Suicide* since the book it argues against. Where Durkheim treated suicide as a social fact to be explained by rates and currents, Baechler treats it as a behaviour to be understood by the meaning the actor gives it — a strategic response to a problem the person cannot otherwise solve.

## The Strategic Approach

Baechler's starting point is a definition. Suicide, for him, is not an act but a behaviour: all behaviour by which an individual seeks to resolve a problem, where the behaviour is one that will kill him, or one that he expects will kill him. The distinction matters, because it shifts the question from "why did he die?" to "what problem was he trying to solve?" Suicide is a means, not an end in itself, and it must be understood through the actor's own logic — what the sociologist of action calls the *in order to* motive, the purpose the act is meant to serve, rather than the *because* motive, the past causes that pushed the person toward it. Baechler's method follows from this: he works from case histories, coroners' reports, and clinical accounts, reconstructing the internal logic of each case as an ideal type in Max Weber's sense — a pure model against which real cases can be compared. The approach owes as much to Alfred Schutz's phenomenology of motives as to Durkheim, and it deliberately reverses Durkheim's procedure: instead of aggregating cases into rates, it disaggregates them into meanings.

## The Typology: Four Types, Eleven Meanings

From his cases Baechler derives a typology of the typical meanings suicide can carry, grouped into four broad families. The *escapist* type covers suicide as flight from an unbearable situation and suicide as grief, the response to a loss that empties life of its point. The *aggressive* type covers suicide as self-punishment, as vengeance against those who will be made to suffer by it, as crime — the murder of the self as an attack on others — and as blackmail, the threat or act designed to compel others to behave differently. The *oblative* type covers suicide as sacrifice, offered for a cause or a person, and as transfiguration, the passage to another state or a higher existence. The *ludic* type covers suicide as ordeal, the test of fate or of one's own courage, and as game, the play with death whose outcome is left to chance. A final meaning, appeal — the cry for help addressed to others — runs across the typology and shows how porous its boundaries are: the same death can carry several meanings at once, and the typology is a map of possibilities, not a set of boxes. What the typology establishes is that suicide is not one thing. Durkheim's four types described variations in rates; Baechler's describe variations in sense.

## The Critique of Durkheim

Baechler's critique of Durkheim is direct and damaging. He argues that Durkheim's correlation between religion and suicide does not hold: the degree of cohesion within a religious community does not determine its protective value, and there is, in Baechler's phrase, no intelligible relationship between the various religions and suicide. The religious element, he contends, is a minor factor compared with the complexity of individual biographies. More broadly, he argues that Durkheim's rates explain nothing about any particular suicide: a rate is an aggregate that conceals the very phenomenon it claims to explain. The criticism does not abolish Durkheim — Baechler keeps the sociological question — but it relocates it: the sociology of suicide must begin from the meaning of the act for the actor, and only then ask how social conditions shape the problems to which suicide is a response.

## Reception and Influence

The book was received as a major contribution to suicidology and to the sociology of action, and it remains the standard reference for the typological approach to suicidal behaviour. Its influence has been strongest in clinical and psychological research: the typology has been used to build instruments measuring the motives for self-harm, and the strategic approach — suicide as a response to a problem — has been taken up in cognitive and behavioural accounts of suicidal behaviour. Critics have argued that the typology is difficult to apply reliably, since the actor's meaning must often be inferred from others after the death, and that the case material, drawn largely from European sources, limits its generality. Others note that the book's focus on meaning leaves the social distribution of suicide — Durkheim's question — unanswered, and that a complete sociology of suicide needs both the meanings Baechler maps and the rates Durkheim measured. That is the book's lasting position: not a replacement for Durkheim but the necessary complement to him, the study that insists that behind every rate there are people solving problems, in the only way they can see, with their own deaths.
