# The Muqaddimah of Ibn Khaldun on the Science of Human Civilization

## The Central Thesis

That history is not the transmission of reports but a **self-standing science**, which Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406) called the **"science of human civilization" ('ilm al-'umran al-bashari)**, and he explicitly claimed that it is new. Its instrument is that every transmitted report is put before the **natures of civilization (taba'i' al-'umran)**: whatever contradicts the nature of human social life is rejected however sound its chain of transmission — and this is an inversion of the criterion of scrutiny from the chain (isnad) to the content (matn).

The pillar of his analysis is **'asabiyya** (group solidarity): cohesion based on lineage or allegiance, which reaches its peak in **Bedouin life** and dissolves in **sedentary civilization**; the state arises through a desert 'asabiyya, then luxury corrupts its solidarity, and it falls in about **three generations**. The word 'asabiyya existed before him, but he gave it its analytic and systematic use in explaining the rise and fall of dynasties.

## Structure of the Work

The Muqaddimah was not originally an independent book, but the **introduction to the *Kitab al-'Ibar wa-Diwan al-Mubtada' wa-l-Khabar fi Ayyam al-'Arab wa-l-'Ajam wa-l-Barbar wa-man 'Asarahum min Dhawi al-Sultan al-Akbar***; it contains the first introduction on the merit of the science of history, then **Book One** on civilization, which has six chapters:

1. Human civilization in general and the earth's share of it (regions and climate).
2. Bedouin civilization, the savage nations, and the tribes.
3. States, caliphate, kingship, and the ranks of authority.
4. Sedentary civilization, towns, and cities.
5. Crafts, livelihood, and means of earning.
6. The sciences, ways of acquiring them, and education.

## Historical Context of Publication

Its draft was written in **779 AH / 1377 CE** at **Qal'at Ibn Salama** (western Algeria) after Ibn Khaldun withdrew from political work, in a short period that he mentions in his autobiographical account. He then revised it and added to it after moving to **Cairo (784 AH / 1382 CE)**, where he took up the Maliki judgeship and teaching posts, and he presented revised copies to the sultans of his time. For that reason there is no "first edition" but **differing copies in the author's own hand**, which makes critical editing a precondition for reading it.

## Reception and Criticism

- **Printing**: the Arabic text was published at **Bulaq (1274 AH / 1857 CE)**, **Étienne Quatremère** issued the Arabic text in Paris in **1858**, and **de Slane** translated it into French (1863–1868).
- **The reference translation into English**: **Franz Rosenthal**, *The Muqaddimah: An Introduction to History*, three volumes, Princeton/Bollingen, **1958** — the accepted scholarly version in Western study.
- **Arab criticism**: **Taha Hussein** in his French thesis at the Sorbonne in **1917** offered a critical reading that questions the coherence of the "science of civilization" and the originality of its independence from earlier philosophy; **Sati' al-Husri** later replied to him in *Dirasat 'an Muqaddimat Ibn Khaldun* (1943 and after) with a reading that establishes the unity of the system.
- **Twentieth-century readings**: **Muhsin Mahdi**, *Ibn Khaldûn's Philosophy of History* (1957); **Yves Lacoste**, *Ibn Khaldoun: Naissance de l'histoire* (1966), with a materialist reading of civilization; **Muhammad Abed al-Jabri**, *Fikr Ibn Khaldun: al-'Asabiyya wa-l-Dawla* (1971); **Aziz al-Azmeh**, *Ibn Khaldun in Modern Scholarship* (1981), a critical review of his reception; and **Ernest Gellner** in *Muslim Society* (1981), who used 'asabiyya as an anthropological tool in explaining tribal societies.

## Arabic Translations

The work was written in Arabic, so the relevant tool is not a translation but a **critical edition**, the most widely relied upon being the edition of **Ali Abd al-Wahid Wafi** (Cairo, Lajnat al-Bayan al-'Arabi, 1957–1962, four volumes), which collated the multiple copies and recorded their differences.

## Psychological and Social Effects in the Book's Chapters

The psychological material of the Muqaddimah is part of its claim and not an ornament in it: the effect of air and climate on people's colors and characters, the effect of abundance or scarcity of food on bodies and minds, the chapter on the crafts and their giving the soul an increase in intellect, and his chapters on dreams, perception, divination, and what befalls the soul when it turns away from the senses. It is a text that connects social conditions with psychological and moral makeup causally, and this is what makes it an early Arabic entry to the question of how civilization makes the human being. Among his best-known sayings is that the human being is the son of his customs and what he is accustomed to, not the son of his nature and temperament, and that human society is necessary because the individual cannot by himself obtain what he needs.

## Criticism of the Book and Its Limits

It is held against the Muqaddimah that its generalizations about Bedouin life and civilization rely on the experience of the Arab Maghreb in the fourteenth century, and that the "three generations" is an approximate rule that does not hold for every state. It is held against his method that he criticizes the report by the criterion of the natures of civilization and then himself relates in places unverified reports. Some have held that his doctrine on the sciences lowers the value of philosophy and logic although he builds his system on logical categories. No continuous scholarly tradition was founded on the Muqaddimah in the Arab world after his death, so it remained an individual effort until European scholarship discovered it in the nineteenth century and Arab scholars then read it in light of modern sociological methods.
