# Patterns of Culture

*Patterns of Culture* (1934) is Ruth Benedict's comparative study of how cultures select and organise human possibilities. Written for a broad readership and published by Houghton Mifflin with an introduction by Franz Boas, it made the anthropological concept of culture familiar to many readers. It argues that traits should be understood within configurations that give them direction and meaning.

## Background

Benedict studied with Boas at Columbia University, where she later taught, and had done fieldwork among Zuni and other Native American peoples. Her colleague Margaret Mead was a close intellectual companion. The book builds on Boasian opposition to racial explanation and to single evolutionary scales, while moving beyond the study of how separate traits spread. Gestalt psychology, historical philosophy, and Nietzsche supplied ideas about wholes and contrasting styles.

## Selection and configuration

Benedict imagines a great arc of possible human interests and behaviour. No society can use them all; each selects some and elaborates them. Over time, borrowed and inherited practices may be reshaped around dominant purposes. A rite of adolescence, warfare, or marriage can therefore carry different meanings in different places. Integration is a tendency and a historical achievement; Benedict noted that some cultures show it only weakly.

## Three cases

The chapters compare the Pueblos of New Mexico, especially Zuni; the people of Dobu, off eastern New Guinea; and the Kwakiutl, or Kwakwaka'wakw, of the Northwest Coast. Her Dobu account relies on Reo Fortune's research, and the Northwest Coast account largely on Boas's.

Drawing on Friedrich Nietzsche, Benedict called the Pueblo emphasis Apollonian: measure, ceremony, restraint, and avoidance of excess and individual prominence. She contrasted it with Dionysian pursuits of intense experience found among other North American peoples, including the Kwakwaka'wakw, whose potlatch rivalry she presented as a striving for self-glorification. Dobu appears as a society pervaded by suspicion, sorcery, and fear of treachery. These descriptions use psychiatric language, including paranoid and megalomaniac, that now reads as particularly reductive.

## Individual and abnormality

Culture provides material from which individual lives are formed, and most people adapt considerably to its emphases. Some temperaments fit them less well. Conduct treated as abnormal or troublesome in one setting may have a valued role in another. Benedict used this comparison to question the assumption that her own society's standards of normality were universal. The book closes with a plea for recognising cultural relativity and for tolerance toward coexisting patterns of life.

## Critiques

The portrait of Zuni attracted early challenge. Li An-che, writing after research there, argued that Benedict understated struggle, individual ambition, and emotion, and later scholarship likewise stressed conflict, history, and differences among Pueblo communities. Representations based partly on outsiders' collecting also raise questions about permission, sacred knowledge, and how the people described understand themselves. For Dobu and the Northwest Coast, dependence on others' records and neglect of historical change, including colonial pressures on exchange, limit the claims.

More broadly, strong integration can hide disagreement and variation by gender, age, or rank, while selected examples are made to fit a theme. Describing a culture as if it had a personality invites stereotypes, a problem that resurfaced in studies of national character. Relativism also leaves open how to judge harm or settle moral disputes within and between societies.

## Influence

The book became a central work of the culture and personality movement and widened public discussion of ethnocentrism. Its comparisons remain useful for asking how institutions and emotional styles connect, provided that the patterns are treated as arguments to be tested and not as complete descriptions of peoples.
