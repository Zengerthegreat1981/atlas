# Finance Capital

**Rudolf Hilferding** published *Das Finanzkapital* in Vienna in 1910. The English title is *Finance Capital: A Study of the Latest Phase of Capitalist Development*. With the later books of Rosa Luxemburg and Lenin, it is a standard reference for classical Marxist discussions of imperialism.

## An empirical observation that began the analysis: the fusion of banks and industry

Hilferding began from a precise empirical observation of the German economy at the start of the twentieth century. Unlike the classical phase of free competition described by Adam Smith and David Ricardo, in which markets operate through a large number of competing small firms and lending banks independent of them, Hilferding observed in Germany an increasing and deep fusion between **large banks** and **giant industrial firms**. Banks were no longer merely lenders to industry through short-term loans; they became direct partners in financing and managing industrial firms, by owning their shares and actively participating in their boards of directors.

## The concept of finance capital

Hilferding applied the term **finance capital** (*Finanzkapital*) to this merged mass of banking and industrial capital, regarding it as a new structural feature that characterizes an advanced phase of capitalist development beyond the classical phase of free competition. In this new phase, the monopolistic concentration of both production and finance becomes a central feature, replacing the competitive dispersion that early political economy had assumed about capitalism.

## From financial monopoly to imperialist expansion

Hilferding draws from his analysis a political conclusion with far-reaching consequences: since the vast monopolistic blocs of finance capital invest enormous sums that are difficult to recover or transfer easily, they need long-term guarantees that open competition cannot provide: customs protection to keep domestic markets safe from foreign competitors, and direct regional expansion to secure protected colonial markets and guaranteed sources of raw materials. From this he directly links the rise of monopolistic finance capital to the political push toward colonial expansion and commercial protectionism in Europe at the start of the twentieth century.

## The joint-stock company and promoter's profit

A distinctive element of the analysis is Hilferding's treatment of the joint-stock company and the stock exchange. He argued that the corporate form lets banks mobilise other people's savings and control far more industrial capital than they own, and he identified a "promoter's profit" (*Gründergewinn*) arising when firms are floated or merged, which gives banks an incentive to promote concentration through cartels and trusts. This institutional analysis underpins the claim that finance capital drives monopolistic concentration.

## The foundation on which Lenin built

Hilferding's analysis of finance capital was one of the basic pillars that **Vladimir Lenin** explicitly relied on in his later pamphlet *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917). Lenin included "the merging of bank capital with industrial capital into a financial oligarchy" among the five features he identified for the imperialist stage, acknowledging his debt to Hilferding's earlier analysis while criticising some of his positions.

## A place within Austromarxism

This book also represents one of the most prominent contributions of the "Austromarxism" current to which Hilferding belonged. This current sought to apply rigorous Marxist analytical tools to specific contemporary economic phenomena (such as the structure of the financial market), rather than limiting itself to restating the general principles of historical materialism in the abstract.

## The importance of the German context specifically

Hilferding chose to study the German economy specifically because, at the start of the twentieth century, it represented the most advanced European industrial model of the fusion of banks with heavy industry (steel, coal and chemicals). This contrasted with the British model, which had retained a relatively greater independence between the banking and industrial sectors until then. This made Germany a more suitable experimental laboratory for observing the phenomenon Hilferding sought to theorize, although his analysis later broadened as a general trend that he expected to spread gradually in other advanced capitalist economies.

## Serious academic reception beyond the Marxist circle

The book was widely discussed beyond socialist circles for its detailed analysis of the structure of the German financial market, and parts of it are read as an early study of the relationship between banks and industry, apart from its broader Marxist conclusions about imperialism.

## A term that persists in contemporary economic analysis

The term "finance capital" coined by Hilferding is still used today in contemporary economic analyses of the "financialization" of advanced capitalist economies, although the historical and technical contexts differ greatly between the German banking system at the start of the twentieth century and the globalized financial system of the twenty-first century. This reflects some conceptual continuity across more than a century of economic change.

## An internal debate on the fate of free competition

The book asks whether monopolistic finance capital ends the competitive phase described by Smith and Ricardo, or whether new forms of competition can return. In *Finance Capital* Hilferding describes a tendency toward cartels and a closer tie between banks, industry and the state. His later formula of "organised capitalism" belongs mainly to writings and speeches of the 1920s, and later writers have disagreed about how far either claim held.
