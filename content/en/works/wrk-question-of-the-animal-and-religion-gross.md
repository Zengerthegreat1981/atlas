# The Question of the Animal and Religion: Theoretical Stakes, Practical Implications

A book by Aaron S. Gross, published in 2015 by Columbia University Press. It is one of the major later works, following *A Communion of Subjects*, in the field of animal and religious studies.

## Definition

Gross argues that the question of "the animal" in religious studies is not a marginal objective addition but a theoretical challenge that reformulates the field's own questions about the boundaries of the religious self and the sacred, and about whether non-human creatures can be an object or even a party in the religious relationship, rather than merely a tool or symbol serving humans.

## Historical Context

The field of "critical animal studies" grew academically since the 1990s, intersecting with environmental ethics and philosophy, and gradually extended into religious studies through questions about the place of the animal in various religious theologies and legislation (such as Jewish kosher dietary laws, Islamic halal slaughter, and Buddhism's position on killing living beings). Gross wrote his work in this growing context, drawing on earlier works such as the volume *A Communion of Subjects*, which gathered multi-religious contributions on the place of the animal in the global religious heritage.

## Main Ideas and Method

Gross builds his theoretical argument through an extended applied case study concerning the Agriprocessors scandal, which concerned the largest kosher meat slaughter facility in the United States. Between the mid-2000s and 2008, serious allegations and findings concerning the treatment of animals and workers at the plant, whose meat was produced under strict Jewish dietary supervision, came to public attention. Gross uses this case to show how the practical question about animals (how they are actually treated, not just how religious texts describe them in theory) reveals deep tensions within the religious tradition itself between formal legitimacy (meeting the apparent jurisprudential conditions) and substantive ethical value (actual compassion toward animals). He suggests that the academic study of religion should take this tension seriously at the theoretical level, rather than treating it as a marginal applied ethical issue.

## Principal Works and Figures

Aaron S. Gross is an American scholar of religious studies and a founder of the non-profit organization Farm Forward, which works on animal welfare. His career thus combines academic research with direct applied activism. His work extends a research tradition begun by Paul Waldau and Kimberley Patton in *A Communion of Subjects: Animals in Religion, Science, and Ethics*, which provided a multi-religious foundation for the field; Gross builds a deeper analysis around a single case.

## Impact and Criticism

The book is a significant contribution to establishing "animals and religion studies" as a mature subfield within religious studies, combining theoretical rigor with clear ethical commitment. Some researchers have noted that the book relies on a single specific Jewish case study (Agriprocessors) to build a general theoretical thesis, and question how far it can be generalized to other religious traditions. Nevertheless, the book's influence in stimulating broader discussions on the place of the animal in comparative theologies remains acknowledged.

## Relation to Arab-Islamic Heritage

The book's main applied focus is the American Jewish case, and it does not offer a parallel detailed study of Islamic halal slaughter. Readers may nevertheless note that the tension it identifies between formal legal validity and actual treatment of animals also arises for religiously certified meat in modern industrial settings generally, including halal.

## Additional Notes on the Agriprocessors Scandal

The Agriprocessors facility, in Postville, Iowa, was a very large kosher meat plant. Investigations by journalists and authorities raised concerns about the treatment of animals during slaughter, alongside violations in the employment of immigrant workers, including minors. Gross analyzes the divergent reactions within the American Jewish community itself. Some regarded the scandal as a purely labor issue separate from the question of kashrut. Others, like Gross himself, saw in it evidence of the need to broaden the concept of "ethical kashrut" to include the treatment of both animal and worker, rather than the formal jurisprudential conditions of slaughter alone.

## Additional Notes on Applied Activism

Gross links his academic work to his activism through Farm Forward, an organization that works on farm-animal welfare in the food system. This combination of theoretical research and practical activism reflects his conviction that the academic study of religion should not limit itself to abstract analysis, but should also bear moral responsibility toward the practical issues that this analysis reveals.
