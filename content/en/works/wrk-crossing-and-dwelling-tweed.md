# Crossing and Dwelling: A Theory of Religion

Thomas Tweed's major theoretical book. Drawing on his earlier ethnographic study of the Cuban shrine in Miami (*Our Lady of the Exile*), it develops a general framework for defining religion itself. Religion is not presented as a fixed belief system bounded by institutional or geographical limits, but as moving, organic cultural **flows** across place and time. The book was published in 2006 by Harvard University Press.

## Historical Context

Tweed wrote this work in a context of rising academic interest in the study of religion and transnational migration. It was shaped by his long field experience studying the Cuban refugee community around the shrine of Our Lady of Charity in Miami. There he observed how this community remained religiously and emotionally tied to Cuba, which it had left, despite the practical impossibility of returning. This led him to question whether traditional definitions of religion, which assume a fixed attachment to a single place, are adequate.

## Main Ideas and Method

Tweed proposes two central, complementary metaphors. "Dwelling" describes how religious people build relatively stable places (homes, bodies, sacred lands) in which they feel belonging and stability. "Crossing" describes how these practices and beliefs move across geographical, temporal and bodily boundaries, so that religious communities never remain confined to a single fixed place. For Tweed, religion is the movement of the constant tension between the two acts together. He defines religions as confluences of organic-cultural flows that intensify joy and confront suffering by drawing on human and suprahuman forces to make homes and cross boundaries. This definition deliberately aims for enough flexibility to accommodate movement and transformation, rather than institutional rigidity.

## Principal Works and Representatives

The book builds directly on Tweed's thesis in *Our Lady of the Exile: Diasporic Religion at a Cuban Catholic Shrine in Miami* (1997). It engages the work of other scholars of migration and religion, such as Peggy Levitt, who studied a parallel phenomenon through the concept of "social remittances" in religion and migration. Together they form an integrated sub-field in the study of religion and transnational migration.

## Impact and Criticism

The book has been influential in the reformulation of the definition of religion within the academic field. It moved from fixed classificatory models (such as Ninian Smart's seven dimensions) to a dynamic model that is meant to account for movement, diaspora and transnational religiosity. Some critics noted that the metaphors of "crossing" and "dwelling", despite their literary appeal, may need more operational specification in order to serve as a criterion distinguishing religion from other culturally mobile phenomena (such as music or popular cuisine).

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The "crossing and dwelling" framework offers a useful tool for understanding Islam as a religion that has been transnational par excellence in its history: from the ancient pilgrimage and trade routes to contemporary Muslim communities in the West, which maintain living religious and cultural ties with their countries of origin through flows similar to those Tweed describes among Cubans. The book itself, however, does not treat Islam in detail.

## A Further Note on Method

Tweed wrote the book in a deliberately literary style that mixes abstract theorising with personal narration of his fieldwork in Miami. This stylistic choice has provoked debate about the limits of scientific objectivity when a general theoretical definition of the whole phenomenon of religion is built on the basis of a single, relatively limited ethnographic case, however deep and rich that case may be.

It was later used as a reference framework for many studies of transnational religiosity that are not limited to the Cuban-American case from which Tweed started.

Tweed holds that a theory of religion aiming at comprehensiveness must explain both stability and movement together, without privileging one over the other.
