# Holy Men and Hunger Artists: Fasting and Asceticism in Rabbinic Culture

A book by Eliezer Diamond, professor of Talmud and rabbinic studies at the Jewish Theological Seminary of America in New York, published by Oxford University Press (dated 2003; some catalogues give 2004).

## Definition

The book addresses a central question in the history of rabbinic Judaism: did the rabbinic sages oppose voluntary physical asceticism (including excessive fasting and sexual abstinence) as contradicting the Talmud's general appreciation of life and of lawful pleasures, or does rabbinic literature contain an internal ascetic current that was recognized, though it remained restricted by controls? Diamond examines rabbinic texts, including the Talmuds, to trace this internal ascetic current, qualifying the common image of rabbinic Judaism as a purely "worldly" religion opposed to all bodily asceticism.

## Historical Context

Diamond places his analysis in an implicit comparison with early Christian asceticism, roughly contemporary with the period of the compilation of rabbinic literature (the second to sixth centuries CE), asking whether rabbinic asceticism was an independent response or partly influenced by the broader ascetic climate of the late Greco-Roman world, which witnessed the rise of Christian monasticism and ascetic Gnostic trends.

## Main Ideas

This is a textual historical study of the position of rabbinic literature (the Mishnah and the Babylonian and Palestinian Talmuds) on fasting and voluntary bodily asceticism among some rabbinic sages. It examines the language of holiness and the language of withdrawal in the texts, and argues that ascetic tendencies were a recognized, though contested, current within rabbinic culture itself, not simply a "deviation" from it, with a careful distinction between valuing asceticism as a spiritual path and caution against its excess, since rabbinic texts remained wary of asceticism that leads to neglect of other religious duties such as procreation and community participation.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

There is no direct historical link between the rabbinic asceticism that is the subject of the book and the Arab-Islamic heritage, since the rabbinic literature studied here precedes the rise of Islam by centuries. However, methodological similarities can be observed between Diamond's question about the place of asceticism within a religious tradition not generally known for asceticism (rabbinic Judaism) and similar questions raised by researchers about the place of asceticism and Sufism within early Islam, which in turn witnessed a jurisprudential debate over the limits of legitimate asceticism, and its criticism by scholars who regarded some extreme ascetic practices as innovation (*bid'a*) contrary to the "natural disposition" (*fitra*) and the moderation of the Sharia.

## Impact

The book challenges a common stereotype in comparative religious studies that used to set "worldly Judaism" against "ascetic Christianity", showing that the boundaries between these two patterns are less rigid than earlier literature had assumed. It is cited in comparative studies of fasting and asceticism.

## Textual Examples Highlighted in the Book

Diamond cites famous rabbinic cases, such as the sage Shimon bar Yohai, who is said to have hidden with his son in a cave for thirteen years to escape the Roman authorities, living only on the fruit of a carob tree and spring water, in a narrative that clearly evokes the language of ascetic holiness. He also discusses the practices of *ta'anit* (voluntary individual fasting), which some sages endorsed as a means of atoning for sin or seeking a vision, in contrast to the warnings of other sages against this trend as an excess that may endanger health and disrupt a person's religious and family duties.

## The Book's Title

The book's title borrows the term "hunger artist" (*Hunger Artist*) from Franz Kafka's famous story *Ein Hungerkünstler* (1922), about a performer who displays his long fast before an audience as a performance art. Diamond uses this modern literary metaphor to raise a question about the ambiguous relationship between sincere religious asceticism and public display, which may drift toward spiritual vanity. This is a problem that the rabbinic sages themselves debated in antiquity, when they warned against asceticism that seeks to attract attention.
