# Psychological Anthropology (ed. Hsu)

*Psychological Anthropology* is a collective volume edited by Francis L. K. Hsu. It first appeared in 1961 with the subtitle *Approaches to Culture and Personality*, and a substantially revised edition was published by Schenkman in 1972. The book surveys research on the relation between culture and psychological processes and helped to establish its title as the name of a field.

## Editor and purpose

Hsu (1909–1999) was born in China, studied anthropology at the London School of Economics, where Malinowski was among his teachers, and made his career in the United States, chiefly at Northwestern University. His comparisons of Chinese, Hindu, and American ways of life shaped his interest in the links among kinship, personality, and social organisation. He argued that the older label "culture and personality" was too narrow for the range of work being done and proposed psychological anthropology in its place.

## Scope

The volume brings together chapters by several specialists. Some review research on particular regions, while others address methods or themes such as child training, mental disorder, projective testing, dreams, cognition, and comparison across societies. The structure lets readers see differences among field observation, cross-cultural statistical comparison, psychological testing, and psychoanalytic interpretation. The 1972 edition revised the earlier material and took account of newer work, including the growing study of cognition.

## Intellectual setting

The earlier culture and personality movement, associated with Ruth Benedict, Margaret Mead, Ralph Linton, and Abram Kardiner, had examined how upbringing and institutions form characteristic dispositions. By the 1950s it faced criticism for treating societies as psychologically uniform, for inferring adult character from isolated infant practices, and for weak tests of its claims, particularly in studies of national character. The handbook reflects efforts to respond with more explicit methods and a wider set of problems.

Hsu's own approach gave weight to kinship. He proposed that the relationship treated as dominant in a family system, such as that between father and son or between husband and wife, carries attributes that influence wider social conduct. This hypothesis was developed further in his other writings and was debated as too schematic.

## Significance and limits

As a collection, the book does not present one theory, and its chapters differ in their assumptions. It is useful as a record of the field during a period of redefinition, between the older configurational studies and later research on cultural models, self, emotion, and embodiment. Much of the work it reviews relies on categories developed in Western psychology, and projective tests used across languages raise difficulties of translation and interpretation. Feminist and political-economic questions that became prominent later receive comparatively little place. Subsequent surveys, such as Philip Bock's histories of the subject, revisit the same lineage with a more critical view of its claims to measure personality across cultures.
