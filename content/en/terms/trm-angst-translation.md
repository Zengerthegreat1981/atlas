# Translating Angst: Anxiety, Dread, or Anguish?

One German word, used by Kierkegaard and Heidegger, changes the way the whole concept is understood depending on how it is translated into English or Arabic — not merely its wording. This entry does not settle which translation is "correct". It documents the dispute itself, as knowledge a reader needs, drawing on the standard translations and the translators' own discussions.

## The origin of the problem

Søren Kierkegaard used the Danish word *Angest* — the counterpart of the German *Angst* — in *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844), published under the pseudonym Vigilius Haufniensis. Martin Heidegger used the same German word with a partly different meaning in *Being and Time* (1927). The standard English rendering, "anxiety", carries modern clinical connotations that may not have been intended at all in the original philosophical text.

The word carries its history in its root: *Angst* goes back to an Indo-European root meaning tightness and constriction, from which come the Latin *angustus* (narrow) and *angor* (choking). The Arabic root is different: *qalaq* means disturbance and lack of settledness — so Arabic foregrounds movement where German foregrounds constriction.

## Kierkegaard: the dizziness of freedom

For Kierkegaard, *Angest* is not fear. Fear (*Furcht* in German) has a definite object; *Angest* has none, or rather has possibility itself as its object. It is what a person feels before freedom: the "dizziness of freedom" that arises when looking down into the abyss of what one might do, including the possibility of doing wrong. In *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844), *Angest* is bound up with innocence and the Fall — Adam's anxiety before the prohibition is the anxiety of possibility, the vertigo of being able to choose — and it is also, paradoxically, the condition of the self's education.

That is why the English title of the book itself became a dispute. Walter Lowrie's translation of 1944 called it *The Concept of Dread*. Reidar Thomte, retranslating it for Princeton University Press in 1980, changed the title to *The Concept of Anxiety* — a deliberate decision that shifted the book's reception: a reader who meets "anxiety" meets a word they already know from the clinic, while a reader who meets "dread" meets a word that belongs to no clinic at all.

## Heidegger: the mood that discloses nothingness

Heidegger's use of *Angst* in *Being and Time* (1927) is different again. For Heidegger, anxiety is a fundamental mood — a *Grundbefindlichkeit* — with no object. Where fear is always fear of something definite, anxiety is anxiety in the face of nothing in particular: what threatens is nowhere, and it is precisely this nowhere that discloses the world as a whole, and discloses *Dasein* to itself as being-in-the-world. In section 40, anxiety reveals the "nothing" and the uncanniness of existence: in anxiety, the familiar world slips away and the person is left face to face with their own finitude and their own freedom.

The English translators John Macquarrie and Edward Robinson, in their 1962 translation of *Being and Time*, rendered *Angst* consistently as "anxiety". Their choice set the terms of the English-language discussion for decades, and it imported the clinical resonance Heidegger's text does not carry: a reader who knows "anxiety" as a symptom meets his most ontological mood through a word that points towards the clinic.

## Direct Comparison with the Danish and German Originals

In Kierkegaard's original Danish text (*Begrebet Angest*, 1844), *Angest* (spelled *angst* in modern Danish) functions both substantively and psychologically as the dialectical pivot of freedom. Kierkegaard explicitly contrasts *Angest* with *Frygt* (fear): while *Frygt* is directed toward a definite, actual object (*noget bestemt*), *Angest* is directed toward "nothing" (*Intet*)—specifically, the ambiguous possibility of being able (*Mulighedens Mulighed*). His famous characterization of anxiety as "the dizziness of freedom" (*Frihedens Svimmelhed*) captures this psychological vertigo as freedom gazes down into its own boundless possibility.

In Heidegger's German text (*Sein und Zeit*, 1927, §40), the distinction between *Angst* and *Furcht* is ontological rather than merely psychological. Heidegger specifies that whereas the *Wovor* (that before which) of fear is an entity within-the-world (*innerweltliches Seiendes*), the *Wovor* of *Angst* is completely indeterminate (*völlig unbestimmt*); what threatens is "nowhere" (*nirgends*), which discloses "being-in-the-world as such" (*das In-der-Welt-sein als solches*). Furthermore, *Angst* reveals the uncanny (*das Unheimliche*, literally "not-at-home-ness") and confronts Dasein with its constitutive nullity (*Nichtigkeit*). Both original Germanic terms share an etymological kinship with narrowing and physical constriction (*Enge*), an affective dimension that Latinate renderings like "anxiety" partially obscure in favor of cognitive or medical associations.

## The contested alternatives

Some translators prefer "dread" because it carries the existential weight without the medical suggestion of "anxiety": dread is weighty, objectless, and archaic enough to signal that something unusual is being said. Lowrie's 1944 title is the classic instance. Others use "anguish" — above all when translating Sartre, whose French *angoisse* names the inescapable consciousness of freedom, rendered "anguish" in Hazel E. Barnes's 1956 translation of *Being and Nothingness* (1943). In Arabic the renderings are distributed between *al-qalaq* (anxiety), *al-dahsha* (amazement or awe), and *al-faza'* (fright or panic), depending on the translator and the context — and *al-faza'* is dangerously close to *Furcht*, fear, which is exactly the word the philosophy distinguishes *Angst* from.

In psychoanalysis the same word took yet another path. Freud's *Angst* was rendered "anxiety" in the Standard Edition, and Bruno Bettelheim criticised that choice in *Freud and Man's Soul* (1983) for giving the text a clinical coldness that does not express the warmth of the ordinary German word. In *The Courage to Be* (1952), Paul Tillich distinguished three forms — the anxiety of death, of meaninglessness, and of guilt — using "anxiety" in a sense at once theological and existential.

## Why this is not a formality

Translating the word as "anxiety" brings the philosophical concept close to the clinical category of generalised anxiety disorder — the diagnostic entry this Atlas keeps structurally separate — while translating it as "dread" or "fright" keeps it closer to the purely philosophical feeling. The choice of translation can therefore push a reader into an unintended confusion between a philosophical mood and a diagnostic category, which is exactly the confusion this Atlas tries to prevent structurally through the separation of concept entries from disorder entries.

The practical rule in careful commentary is to keep the original word alongside the definition at first mention — *Angst* (anxiety, dread) — so the reader knows which word is being translated. "Anxiety" is the most current rendering in Arabic and English existential literature, but it opens the door to confusion with clinical symptoms. Those who prefer "dread" point to the existential dimension, but move away from the letter of the text. There is no rendering that loses nothing: "anxiety" loses the weight and the constriction; "dread" loses the currency; "anguish" carries Sartre's freedom but not Kierkegaard's dizziness.
