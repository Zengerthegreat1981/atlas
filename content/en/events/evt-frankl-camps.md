# Frankl's Concentration Camp Experience

Between 1942 and 1945, the Viennese psychiatrist Viktor Frankl (1905–1997) was imprisoned in a series of Nazi concentration camps, lost his wife, his parents, and his brother, and observed — under conditions of extreme deprivation — the ways in which prisoners held on to, or lost, a sense of meaning. This is the founding event of logotherapy, the meaning-centred approach to psychotherapy, and the raw material of Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning*. It is recorded here as a historical event: what happened, when, and where. The first-person account of how Frankl lived it from the inside belongs to a different entry (exp-frankl-lived-experience).

## What Happened, When and Where

On 25 September 1942, Frankl was deported from Vienna with his wife Tilly Grosser and his parents, Gabriel and Elsa Frankl, to the Theresienstadt ghetto and camp in occupied Czechoslovakia. His father died in Theresienstadt of starvation and pneumonia on 13 February 1943. On 19 October 1944, Frankl and his wife were transported to Auschwitz-Birkenau, where they were separated on arrival; he never saw her again. His mother was murdered in the gas chambers at Auschwitz. 

Frankl himself spent only a few days at Auschwitz before being selected for transfer to forced labour. Camp registers confirm his arrival at Kaufering III, a subcamp of the Dachau concentration camp system, on 25 October 1944, where he was assigned prisoner number 119104 and worked as a slave labourer for five months. In March 1945 he was transferred to the Türkheim subcamp (also in the Dachau network), where he served as an inmate physician treating fellow prisoners suffering from typhus. Türkheim was liberated by the United States Army on 27 April 1945. Meanwhile his wife Tilly had been transferred to Bergen-Belsen, where she died in 1945, and his brother Walter perished at Auschwitz. Of his immediate family, only Frankl himself and his sister Stella — who had emigrated to Australia before the deportations — survived.

The dates matter because the popular telling often compresses the story. Frankl was not in Auschwitz for years; his time there was a transit of days, and most of his imprisonment was spent in Theresienstadt and in the Dachau subcamps of Kaufering and Türkheim.

## What Frankl Observed

Frankl's central observation, made while he was still a prisoner, was that the capacity to endure seemed to depend less on physical strength than on whether a prisoner could hold on to a personal meaning: a person to return to, a task left unfinished, a conviction that suffering could still be met with dignity. He later described prisoners who gave up on such a meaning as dying more quickly, and those who kept one as surviving longer — not because meaning protected the body, but because it organised the will to go on living. He also observed the opposite with equal honesty: that some prisoners with every reason to live died, and that survival depended on luck, health, age, and sheer chance as well as on attitude. The observation is therefore presented here as Frankl's own, drawn from his experience and later developed into a theory, not as an established causal finding. Modern readers should not turn it into a rule — that those who died simply lacked meaning — which would blame the victims for their own murder. Frankl himself resisted that reading.

## What Followed: The Book and the School

Before his deportation, Frankl had sewn the manuscript of his first academic book, *Ärztliche Seelsorge* (*The Doctor and the Soul*), into the lining of his coat; it was confiscated on his arrival at Auschwitz and destroyed. Following his liberation and return to Vienna in the summer of 1945, Frankl learned that his wife, mother, father, and brother had all perished. Over the following months, he reconstructed *Ärztliche Seelsorge* from memory and surviving shorthand notes (published in Vienna in 1946). 

In 1945, he also dictated his personal camp memoir in nine days. It appeared anonymously in German in 1946 under the title *…trotzdem Ja zum Leben sagen: Ein Psychologe erlebt das Konzentrationslager*, and was later published in English in 1959 under the title *From Death-Camp to Existentialism* (subsequently reissued worldwide as *Man's Search for Meaning*).

From the same experience grew logotherapy and existential analysis as an established therapeutic school: the claim that the primary human motivation is the will to meaning, that meaning can be found in creative work, in love and encounter, and in the attitude taken toward unavoidable suffering, and that the therapist's task is to help the patient discover the meaning waiting in their own situation. The Viktor Frankl Institute in Vienna, founded in 1992, later became the institutional home of this teaching.

## Archival Documentation and Historical Corroboration

The chronology of Frankl's imprisonment rests on Frankl's own testimony and on historical scholarship (notably Pytell's biography), which draw on Holocaust-era records such as the Arolsen Archives (International Center on Nazi Persecution), the Yad Vashem database of Shoah victims' names, and camp records for the Dachau subcamps.

## Why an Event, Not a Biography

This entry records the event itself — a dated, located sequence of deportations, transfers, deaths, and liberation — rather than the life of Viktor Frankl as a whole (thk-frankl) or his inner experience of it (exp-frankl-lived-experience). The distinction is deliberate: the event is what happened in the world between 1942 and 1945, verifiable against transport lists, camp records, and the accounts of other survivors, while the experience entry answers a different question, how Frankl lived it from the inside. Keeping the two apart protects both: the history is not softened into a personal story, and the personal story is not flattened into a chronology.

## A Methodological Note

This is a documented historical account, not an inspirational use of Frankl's suffering. The camps were sites of systematic murder and cruelty, and Frankl's survival was exceptional; most of the people deported alongside him did not survive, whatever meaning they held on to. The meaning Frankl found is inseparable from the specific, terrible context in which he found it, and any use of his story that forgets that context misuses it.
