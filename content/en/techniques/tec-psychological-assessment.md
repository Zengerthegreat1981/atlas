# Psychological Assessment

Psychological assessment is the systematic evaluation of a person using interviews, questionnaires, and psychological tests. It provides a baseline understanding and informs treatment planning: before a therapist decides what to do, assessment asks what is actually there — the person's abilities, personality, symptoms, strengths, and the context in which they live. It is the difference between treating a label and treating a person, and between a plan built on a first impression and a plan built on evidence. Done well, it is itself therapeutic: many clients report that a careful assessment was the first time anyone had truly tried to understand them.

## What It Is

Systematic evaluation using interviews, questionnaires, and psychological tests is the method, and its purpose is to provide a baseline understanding and inform treatment planning. The clinical interview is the foundation: a structured or semi-structured conversation that maps the presenting problem, the person's history, their relationships, their work, and their functioning. Questionnaires add standardised self-report — symptom checklists, personality inventories, measures of mood and anxiety — that can be scored and compared with norms. Psychological tests add performance: intelligence tests that measure reasoning and memory, neuropsychological tests that map attention and executive function, and projective measures that invite the person to respond to ambiguous stimuli. No single source is trusted alone; the assessment is the integration of all of them, weighed against each other, into a formulation that says what the problem is, why it is happening, and what is likely to help.

## History

Modern psychological assessment began with Alfred Binet and Théodore Simon, whose scale of 1905 was designed to identify Parisian schoolchildren who needed extra help — the first intelligence test, and the ancestor of a century of testing. Hermann Rorschach's *Psychodiagnostics* (1921) introduced the inkblot test, in which a person's responses to ten ambiguous images were read as a window on perception, thought, and feeling. David Wechsler, dissatisfied with tests built for children, published *The Measurement of Adult Intelligence* in 1939, founding the Wechsler scales that still dominate intelligence testing; the current adult version, the WAIS-IV, appeared in 2008. Starke Hathaway and J. Charnley McKinley published the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory in 1943, a vast true-false questionnaire built empirically, by comparing the answers of patients and non-patients; its revision, the MMPI-2, appeared in 1989. From the 1950s onward, assessment grew into a profession of its own, with training standards, ethical codes, and a literature on validity and bias; Gary Groth-Marnat's *Handbook of Psychological Assessment* (first published 1984; 4th ed. 2003) became a standard reference.

## How It Works

An assessment begins with a referral question: why is this person being assessed, and what decision will the results inform? The assessor then chooses a battery — a set of instruments fitted to the question, not a fixed package — and gathers data across sessions. Intelligence and cognitive testing measure abilities; personality inventories map traits and symptoms; projective tests, used selectively, explore the less accessible parts of experience; collateral information from family, school, or medical records fills in the picture. The assessor scores and interprets, but the crucial step is integration: the numbers and the interview must be brought together into a coherent account, and discrepancies between sources are often the most informative findings of all. The work ends with feedback — a session in which the results are shared with the client in plain language, and a written report that answers the referral question and recommends next steps. A good report is useful to its reader; a bad one is a list of scores in search of a meaning.

## Key Figures

Alfred Binet invented intelligence testing as a practical tool for helping children, not as a ranking of human worth — a distinction his successors have not always honoured. Hermann Rorschach created the most famous and most contested test in psychology, and the debate over its validity continues a century later. David Wechsler made intelligence testing an adult enterprise and defined intelligence as the capacity to act purposefully, think rationally, and deal effectively with the environment. Starke Hathaway and J. Charnley McKinley made personality assessment empirical, building a test from data rather than from theory. Gary Groth-Marnat systematised the field, showing how tests, interviews, and history combine into a competent assessment. Paul Meehl, in *Clinical versus Statistical Prediction* (1954), forced the field to confront an uncomfortable finding: statistical combinations of test data usually outperform unaided clinical judgement — a challenge that reshaped how assessors work.

## Evidence and Influence

The evidence for psychological assessment is strong where tests are used for what they were designed to do. Intelligence tests predict educational and occupational outcomes better than most psychological measures; personality inventories predict behaviour across settings; and structured interviews outperform unstructured ones in diagnosis. The Meyer and colleagues review of 2001, in the *American Psychologist*, found that the validity of psychological tests compares favourably with that of medical tests — a finding the field still quotes. But assessment has also been the site of psychology's most serious ethical failures: tests used to justify racial segregation, to sort immigrants, to label children, and to make decisions their authors never intended. The field's response has been a sustained attention to cultural fairness, to the limits of any single score, and to the principle that a test result is a hypothesis, not a verdict. Assessment's influence is everywhere in modern practice: no treatment plan, no disability determination, no forensic opinion, and no school placement is made without it.
