# Paradoxical Intention

Paradoxical intention is Viktor Frankl's best-known technique: the person is asked deliberately to wish for, or to exaggerate in imagination, the very thing they fear, with the aim of breaking the cycle of anticipatory anxiety. A person who dreads blushing in public is invited to try to blush as intensely as possible; a person who lies awake fearing sleeplessness is asked to try to stay awake all night. The instruction is the opposite of what fear demands, and that reversal is the point. It belongs to logotherapy, Frankl's therapeutic school, and it is one of a small set of logotherapeutic tools, alongside dereflection, that redirect the patient's attention rather than analysing the cause of the symptom.

## What it is

The technique rests on Frankl's account of anticipatory anxiety: fear of a symptom occurring tends to bring the symptom about. Fear and symptom reinforce each other in a closed loop, and ordinary efforts to suppress the symptom feed the loop, because suppression is itself a form of attention to the symptom. Paradoxical intention tries to break the loop from the other side. By intending the feared outcome, the person changes their relationship to it: what was dreaded becomes something willed, and the fear that sustained it loses its footing. Frankl linked this to the human capacity for self-distancing — the ability to take a stance toward one's own symptoms rather than being ruled by them — and to humour, since the deliberate exaggeration of the feared symptom is often faintly comic, and the comic attitude is incompatible with dread.

## History

Frankl introduced the technique in German-language writings beginning in the late 1930s and gave it its first English-language exposition in "Paradoxical Intention: A Logotherapeutic Technique," published in the *American Journal of Psychotherapy* in 1960. It was developed alongside logotherapy itself, which Frankl set out in *Ärztliche Seelsorge* (1946; English *The Doctor and the Soul*, 1955) and *Man's Search for Meaning* (German 1946; English 1959 as *From Death-Camp to Existentialism*, 1962 under its present title). The technique grew out of Frankl's clinical observation that patients' attempts to suppress or avoid a symptom usually intensified it, an observation he connected to his wider claim that human beings retain the capacity for self-transcendence and self-distancing even in the midst of disorder, in contrast to psychoanalytic accounts that stressed the determinism of unconscious drives.

## How it works

In practice, the therapist first establishes that the symptom is maintained by anticipatory anxiety rather than by a medical cause that needs treatment in its own right, and then invites the patient to practise the intention, usually in imagination at first and then in the feared situation. The patient is asked to exaggerate: to wish for the worst blush, the most violent tremor, the longest sleepless night. The humour is not incidental. The technique is also paired with dereflection, its companion tool: where paradoxical intention changes the patient's stance toward the symptom, dereflection redirects attention away from the self toward a task or a person, so that the symptom is no longer the centre of the patient's attention at all.

## Clinical use

Historically the technique was used above all with phobic conditions, including agoraphobia, with obsessive-compulsive presentations, and with sleep-onset insomnia, where the anticipatory loop is clearest. It was also investigated experimentally in insomnia: Ascher and Turner (1979) tested paradoxical intention against other instructions in a controlled study of sleep-onset insomnia and found that instructing patients to stay awake reduced sleep-onset latency, a result that has often been cited as an experimental demonstration of the principle. Reviews of non-drug treatments for insomnia have generally found some support for paradoxical intention, though less than for stimulus control or sleep restriction. Large randomised trials of the technique are lacking, and the evidence for its use with phobic and obsessive-compulsive problems rests mainly on small studies and case reports.

## Limits of use

The technique is not suitable for every case or every kind of anxiety, and it must be applied with specialist training, not as general advice to the reader. It is a description of a documented historical technique, not a guide to self-application. The humour at its centre also requires care: what is liberating for one patient can feel like mockery to another, and the therapist's judgement about when the patient is ready to laugh at the symptom is part of the skill.

## Evidence and influence

While early empirical validation relied on single-case series and the controlled experimental study by Ascher and Turner (1979) in sleep-onset insomnia, subsequent research has evaluated paradoxical intention primarily within the framework of cognitive-behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I). Systematic reviews and clinical practice guidelines by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (Morin et al., 1999, 2006; Morgenthaler et al., 2006) designate paradoxical intention as an empirically supported psychological intervention for chronic primary insomnia. By instructing patients to remain comfortably awake passively in bed and abandon the active effort to sleep, the technique directly counters performance anxiety and sleep-effort syndrome. In contemporary clinical trials, paradoxical intention is typically investigated as a module within multicomponent CBT-I rather than as an isolated monotherapy, though experimental work (such as Broomfield and Espie, 2005) has examined sleep effort as a proposed mechanism of paradoxical instructions. In panic disorder and obsessive-compulsive spectrum conditions, the core mechanism of deliberate symptom provocation has largely been absorbed into modern interoceptive exposure and inhibitory learning protocols. Its wider logic—that the attempt to control a symptom can maintain it—reappears in later work on ironic processes of mental control and in acceptance-based approaches. Paradoxical intention remains the most vivid single illustration of Frankl's therapeutic stance: that the patient is not the symptom, and can take a position toward it.

## Key works

Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning* (German 1946; English 1959/1962) is the best-known text of logotherapy, and his 1960 paper is the first English-language statement of the technique. Ascher and Turner's 1979 study is the key experimental test, and Morin and colleagues' 2006 review summarises the insomnia evidence.
