# Narrative Therapy

Narrative therapy is a therapeutic approach in which the client externalises the problem, identifies alternative narratives, and re-authors their life story. It separates the person from the problem: the client is not an anxious person or a depressed person, but a person who is struggling with anxiety or depression — and that grammatical shift changes everything. If the problem is not who you are, it can be examined, resisted, and rewritten; and if your life story has been told in one narrow, problem-saturated version, another version can be found in the exceptions, the forgotten strengths, and the moments when the problem did not win.

## What It Is

The client externalises the problem, identifies alternative narratives, and reconstructs the life story — that is the method in one sentence. Externalisation is the founding move: the therapist helps the client name the problem as something separate ("the Worry," "the Temper," "the Eating Disorder's voice") and then interviews the problem, asking how it operates, when it is strongest, and what it wants. The client is positioned as the expert on their own life, and the therapist as a curious collaborator rather than an interpreter of hidden depths. The work then turns to "unique outcomes" — moments when the client resisted the problem, even slightly — and builds those moments into an alternative story that the client can inhabit, tell to others, and live by. Documents, letters, and certificates are sometimes used to thicken the new story: a letter from the therapist summarising what was reclaimed, or a certificate marking the defeat of a problem.

## History

Narrative therapy was developed in the 1980s by Michael White, an Australian social worker, and David Epston, a New Zealand family therapist, working across the Tasman Sea and publishing their landmark book *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* in 1990. Their influences were deliberately eclectic: the anthropology of Gregory Bateson, the post-structural philosophy of Michel Foucault — whose idea that power operates through the stories a culture tells about people became the approach's political edge — and the social constructionist insistence that identities are made in language and relationship. From Adelaide and Auckland the approach spread through the English-speaking world, carried by the Dulwich Centre in Adelaide, which White founded, and by training institutes in North America, Europe, and Latin America. Its development was always collaborative and deliberately anti-hierarchical: White and Epston published together, shared authorship, and resisted the idea that the therapist knows best.

## How It Works

A session typically opens with the therapist asking the client to name the problem and describe its effects: on work, on relationships, on sleep, on hope. The therapist maps the influence of the problem on the person's life, and then, crucially, maps the influence of the person on the problem — the times the client stood up to it, ignored it, or outwitted it. These exceptions are investigated in detail, not celebrated in passing: what exactly did you do, who noticed, what does that say about what matters to you? From there the therapist helps the client re-author the story, connecting the exceptions into a coherent alternative narrative and asking who in the client's life would recognise and support this version. The work often ends with a document — a therapeutic letter, a certificate, a story retold to an audience of witnesses — that gives the new narrative a public, durable form. The approach is used with individuals, couples, families, and whole communities, and has been adapted for work with trauma, grief, addiction, and eating difficulties.

## Key Figures

Michael White is the central figure: his *Maps of Narrative Practice* (2007) is the clearest guide to the method's questions and moves, and his papers on externalisation, re-authoring, and re-membering define the practice. David Epston brought the inventive use of letters and documents, and his early *Collected Papers* (1989) show the approach taking shape. Jill Freedman and Gene Combs, in *Narrative Therapy: The Social Construction of Preferred Realities* (1996), gave the approach its most readable North American introduction. Alice Morgan's *What Is Narrative Therapy?* (2000) is a widely used introduction for students. Stephen Madigan, in *Narrative Therapy* (2011), offered a book-length theoretical introduction. Together they built an approach that is as much a political stance as a technique: therapy as the work of helping people reclaim authorship of their lives from the problem stories imposed on them.

## Evidence and Influence

Narrative therapy's evidence base is modest but growing, and it is honest about its own limits. Randomised trials are few, partly because the approach resists manualisation and standardised outcome measures — the very instruments its philosophy distrusts. Qualitative studies and case series report good client satisfaction and positive change, notably with children, where externalisation is often immediately engaging. Systematic reviews conclude that the evidence is promising but insufficient for strong claims, and the approach's own literature acknowledges this. Its influence, meanwhile, is large. Externalisation has entered the common language of family therapy, social work, and counselling; the idea that "the problem is the problem, the person is not the problem" is quoted far beyond the circles that know its source; and narrative methods have shaped community work with refugees, Indigenous communities, and survivors of violence, where reclaiming a story is itself a political act.
