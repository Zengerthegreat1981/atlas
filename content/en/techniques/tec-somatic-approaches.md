# Somatic and Body-Based Methods

Somatic approaches work with the body directly: with breath, posture, tension, sensation and movement. Their premise is that the body carries what the mind cannot yet say — trauma stored as chronic tension, emotion held as a clenched jaw or a shallow breath, wisdom available only as a felt sense in the chest or gut. Talking can circle around such material for years without touching it. The somatic therapist therefore asks a different question: not "what do you think about this?" but "where do you feel it, and what happens if you stay with that feeling?"

## What It Is

Somatic methods form a family rather than a single technique. They include somatic experiencing, developed by Peter Levine; sensorimotor psychotherapy, developed by Pat Ogden; the body-centred work of Babette Rothschild; and Eugene Gendlin's focusing, which trains clients to attend to the vague bodily "felt sense" of a problem. What unites them is a shared conviction: psychological distress is not only a matter of thoughts and memories but of the nervous system, and healing therefore requires working with the body — slowing down, tracking sensation, completing movements that were interrupted, and learning to regulate arousal. This is a different modality from mindfulness, which cultivates awareness of the present moment: somatic work uses the body as the instrument and the site of change, often with touch, movement and breath as active interventions.

## History

The roots of somatic work reach back to Wilhelm Reich's character analysis in the 1930s, which treated chronic muscular tension — "character armour" — as the body's record of repressed feeling, and to the bioenergetics of Alexander Lowen in the 1950s. The modern forms emerged in the 1970s and after. Gendlin published *Focusing* in 1978, describing a teachable method for listening to the body's felt sense. Levine, observing how animals in the wild discharge the energy of a threat once it passes, developed somatic experiencing and set it out in *Waking the Tiger* in 1997. Ogden founded the Sensorimotor Psychotherapy Institute in the 1980s and published the first systematic text, *Trauma and the Body*, in 2006. The field's public breakthrough came with Bessel van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* in 2014, which brought the neurobiology of trauma to a mass audience.

## How It Works

A somatic session moves slowly. The therapist helps the client notice sensation — tightness, heat, numbness, trembling — and to describe it in plain language, without interpreting it. The client is guided to track the sensation as it changes, to breathe into it, and to notice impulses: the urge to push away, to run, to curl up. In somatic experiencing, the therapist uses "titration", touching a small piece of traumatic material at a time so the nervous system is not overwhelmed, and "pendulation", moving attention between distress and a resource — a place of safety or strength in the body. Sensorimotor psychotherapy adds work with posture and movement, helping the client complete a defensive movement that was frozen at the time of the trauma. Throughout, the therapist watches the body as much as the words: the breath, the colour of the face, the tremor in the hands.

## Evidence and Influence

The evidence base is younger and thinner than the enthusiasm. A 2015 review by Payne, Levine and Crane-Godreau in *Frontiers in Psychology* set out the theoretical case for somatic experiencing and reported promising early results for post-traumatic stress, while acknowledging the need for larger controlled trials. Sensorimotor psychotherapy has a growing body of case studies and small trials, and body-based methods are now routinely integrated into trauma treatment alongside EMDR and trauma-focused cognitive therapy. The wider influence is harder to measure but unmistakable: the idea that trauma lives in the body has reshaped how therapists of every orientation listen, pace their work, and think about safety. The concept of the "window of tolerance" — the range of arousal within which a person can think and feel at the same time — has become common clinical language.

## Advantages and Limitations

Somatic work reaches clients for whom talking therapies stall: people who cannot name what happened, whose trauma predates language, or whose bodies carry the story their minds have forgotten. It also gives the client a practical skill — regulation of their own arousal — that outlasts therapy. The risks are real. Working directly with the body can flood a client, trigger dissociation, or retraumatise if the therapist moves too fast. Touch, where it is used, raises ethical and boundary questions that require clear consent and training. And the field has sometimes outrun its evidence, promising more than the trials have shown. Used carefully, within a trauma-informed frame, somatic methods offer something talking alone cannot: a way back into a body that has learned to go numb.
