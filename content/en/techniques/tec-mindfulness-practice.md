# Mindfulness-Based Approaches

Mindfulness is the practice of paying attention to the present moment, on purpose, without judgement. In therapy, it is both a skill the client learns and a stance the therapist brings to the work. A client who has spent years reliving the past or dreading the future is invited, for a few minutes, to notice only what is happening now: the breath, the weight of the body in the chair, the sound of a thought passing. Nothing is fixed in those minutes. But something changes: the client discovers that thoughts are events in the mind rather than facts about the world, and that a feeling can be observed without being obeyed.

## What It Is

Mindfulness-based approaches are a family of structured interventions built around meditation and attention training. The two best-known are mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), an eight-week group programme developed by Jon Kabat-Zinn, and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), which combines MBSR with cognitive therapy to prevent relapse in depression. A session typically includes guided meditation, a body scan — moving attention slowly through the body, noticing sensation without trying to change it — and mindful movement, such as gentle yoga or walking. Between sessions, clients practise daily, often with recordings. The aim is not relaxation, though relaxation often follows. It is a change in the relationship to experience: from being carried away by thoughts and feelings to being able to observe them, and choose a response.

## History

Mindfulness comes from Buddhist meditation, but its clinical form is largely a Western adaptation. Kabat-Zinn, a molecular biologist who had studied Zen and yoga, founded the Stress Reduction Clinic at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center in 1979 and began teaching mindfulness to patients with chronic pain and stress-related illness. He described the programme in *Full Catastrophe Living* in 1990. In the 1990s, Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale, three cognitive researchers working on depression relapse, adapted Kabat-Zinn's programme into MBCT, publishing the first trial in 2000 and the manual in 2002. Their insight was that relapse in depression is driven not by negative thoughts themselves but by the way the mind reacts to them — and that mindfulness could interrupt that reaction. By the 2010s, mindfulness had spread into schools, workplaces, prisons and the military, and into nearly every form of psychotherapy.

## How It Works

MBCT and MBSR follow a similar structure: eight weekly group sessions of roughly two to two and a half hours, plus a day of silent practice, and daily home practice of up to 45 minutes. Early sessions teach the body scan and breath meditation, and introduce the idea of "automatic pilot" — the mind's habit of running on routine while attention is elsewhere. Later sessions teach clients to notice the early signs of a downward spiral: a low mood, a self-critical thought, a tightening in the body. Instead of trying to argue with the thought, the client is taught to pause, breathe, and observe — to "decentre" from the thought, seeing it as a mental event rather than a truth. The programme also teaches a three-minute breathing space, a brief practice for use in moments of difficulty, and encourages a kinder stance toward oneself. The therapist's role is less that of an expert than of a fellow practitioner: most MBCT teachers maintain their own meditation practice.

## Evidence and Influence

The evidence is substantial. Teasdale and colleagues showed in 2000 that MBCT halved the relapse rate in patients who had recovered from three or more episodes of depression, a finding replicated in later trials. A meta-analysis by Khoury and colleagues in 2013, covering 209 studies, found moderate effects of mindfulness-based therapy on anxiety, depression and stress. Mindfulness has since been integrated into dialectical behaviour therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, and many other approaches, and it has changed how therapists think about attention itself. The influence extends beyond the clinic: mindfulness is now taught in schools and recommended in clinical guidelines, including the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, for preventing depressive relapse.

## Advantages and Limitations

Mindfulness-based approaches have real strengths. They are structured, teachable and relatively brief; they give clients a skill they can use for life; and they work for problems — chronic pain, stress, relapse — where other treatments are limited. But they are not for everyone. Meditation can be difficult, even distressing, for people with trauma, psychosis or severe anxiety, and the daily practice demands time and discipline that not every client can give. The popularisation of mindfulness has also brought dilution: a practice stripped of its ethical and communal roots, sold as a productivity tool, is a long way from the programme Kabat-Zinn designed. And the evidence, though strong for depression relapse and stress, is weaker for many of the claims now made on its behalf. Mindfulness here denotes attention training as a clinical practice; it is distinct from somatic methods, which use the body itself as the site of intervention. Used as a clinical practice rather than a fashion, mindfulness remains one of the most useful additions to modern psychotherapy.
