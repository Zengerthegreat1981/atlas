# Trauma-Informed Approaches

Trauma-informed care begins with a recognition that changes everything about how a service should work: a large proportion of the people who seek help — in mental health clinics, hospitals, schools, shelters, prisons — have lived through trauma, and the way services treat them can either help them heal or harm them again. A trauma-informed approach is not a therapy technique. It is a framework for organising care so that it does not repeat the dynamics of trauma: powerlessness, unpredictability, betrayal and silence.

## What It Is

The framework rests on an understanding of trauma's neurobiological impact. Trauma changes how the brain and body respond to threat: the alarm system becomes hypersensitive, the capacity to calm down is weakened, and ordinary stress can trigger survival responses — fight, flight or freeze — that look like defiance, withdrawal or non-compliance. A trauma-informed service therefore organises itself around six principles, set out by the US Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration in 2014: safety; trustworthiness and transparency; peer support; collaboration and mutuality; empowerment, voice and choice; and attention to cultural, historical and gender issues. The approach is distinct from trauma-specific treatment: motivational interviewing, somatic work and other techniques treat trauma, while trauma-informed care changes the conditions under which all treatment happens.

## History

The idea grew out of the women's movement and the trauma literature of the 1970s and 1980s, when clinicians began to notice that many of their patients' symptoms — self-harm, dissociation, substance use — made sense as adaptations to abuse. Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery*, published in 1992, gave the field its classic account of trauma's effects and its three stages of recovery: safety, remembrance and mourning, and reconnection. In 1997, Sandra Bloom published *Creating Sanctuary*, describing how a psychiatric hospital could reorganise itself around an understanding of trauma. In 2001, Maxine Harris and Roger Fallot set out the first systematic account of trauma-informed services, arguing that mental health systems needed to change their culture, not only their treatments. The movement reached official status in 2014, when SAMHSA published its concept paper defining trauma and a trauma-informed approach for the whole US system.

## How It Works

In practice, trauma-informed care shows up in small, concrete decisions. Intake forms ask what happened to a person rather than what is wrong with them. Waiting rooms are calm and predictable. Appointments are kept, explanations are given, and the client is offered choices — about the gender of their therapist, about whether the door stays open, about what happens next — because choice is the opposite of the powerlessness trauma imposed. Staff are trained to recognise survival responses for what they are, rather than labelling the person difficult. Seclusion and restraint are minimised, because they repeat the experience of being overpowered. Peer support is built in, because hearing from someone who has survived similar experiences restores hope in a way that professional reassurance cannot. And the organisation looks after its own staff, because workers exposed to others' trauma every day can develop secondary traumatic stress.

## Evidence and Influence

The evidence for trauma-informed care is of a different kind from the evidence for a drug or a manualised therapy. The framework itself has not been tested in large randomised trials, and critics have pointed this out sharply. What the evidence does show is strong: trauma is extremely common among service users; retraumatisation by services is real and documented; and the specific treatments that trauma-informed systems deliver — trauma-focused cognitive therapy, EMDR, somatic approaches — are effective when the conditions for safety are in place. A 2005 paper by Elliott and colleagues argued that many services were effectively "trauma-denied", organised in ways that ignored or even punished the effects of trauma, and that changing this required leadership, training and new policies rather than a new technique. The framework has since spread from mental health into schools, child welfare, policing, medicine and humanitarian work, and its language — safety, choice, collaboration — has become the common sense of modern care.

## Advantages and Limitations

The strength of the approach is that it changes the question. Instead of asking why a client is non-compliant, it asks what happened to them and what the service is doing that makes compliance hard. It protects clients from harm and gives staff a coherent way of thinking about behaviour that otherwise looks baffling. Its limits are real. As a framework rather than a treatment, it can become a slogan — a poster on the wall while nothing changes in practice. It can also be used to avoid the harder work of providing trauma-specific treatment, as if being kind and safe were enough on their own. And its evidence base, for the framework as a whole, remains thin. The honest claim is narrower: trauma-informed care does not heal trauma by itself, but it creates the conditions under which healing becomes possible.
