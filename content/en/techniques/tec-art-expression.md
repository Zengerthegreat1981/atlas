# Art and Creative Expression

Art and creative expression in therapy is the use of visual media, writing, or creative projects to externalise internal experience. It offers non-verbal access to affect and meaning: when words fail because the feeling is too early, too shameful, or too large for language, a drawing, a collage, a clay figure, or a poem can carry it. The point is not to make good art. The point is to make something outside oneself that can then be looked at, talked about, and changed — a bridge between an inner world that cannot speak and a therapeutic relationship that can listen.

## What It Is

The use of visual media, writing, or creative projects to externalise internal experience is the core of the method, and externalisation is the operative word. A client who draws their anxiety as a dark shape with teeth has done something talking could not do: the anxiety is now on the paper, separate from the person, visible to both client and therapist, and available for discussion. Non-verbal access to affect and meaning is the reason this matters. Trauma, early attachment, and pre-verbal experience are often described as held in images, sensations, and bodily states rather than in sentences; art offers a way in that does not demand that the client translate them into words first. The therapist's task is to witness the making, to receive the image without judgement, and to help the client find the words that the image makes possible — or to accept that some images need no words at all.

## History

Art therapy as a profession in the United States began in the 1940s with two pioneers who disagreed about what it was. Margaret Naumburg, working with children in New York, treated art as a form of symbolic speech: the image was a communication from the unconscious, to be understood through free association, in the psychoanalytic manner. Her *Dynamically Oriented Art Therapy* (1966) set out that position. Edith Kramer, working with children in the same city, took the opposite view: for her, the healing was in the making itself — in the mastery of materials, the sublimation of impulse into form, the satisfaction of creating something. Her *Art as Therapy with Children* (1971) is the classic statement of art as therapy rather than art in therapy. From these two roots the field grew: the American Art Therapy Association was founded in 1969, training programmes spread through North America and Europe, and the method expanded from visual art to writing, drama, music, and movement, becoming the broader field of expressive arts therapy. Cathy Malchiodi's *Handbook of Art Therapy* (2003) mapped that expansion for a new generation.

## How It Works

A session may begin with an invitation — draw how you feel today, make an image of the problem, build a safe place out of clay — or with free choice of materials and no direction at all. The therapist provides the materials, protects the space, and watches the process as much as the product: how the client approaches the blank page, whether they press hard or lightly, whether they destroy what they make, whether they can tolerate mess. After the making comes the looking: the client is invited to describe the image, to give it a title, to say what it needs, or to speak from it. The therapist may reflect, ask questions, or simply stay with the silence the image creates. With children, the work is often wordless play with materials; with trauma survivors, the image can hold what cannot yet be spoken, and can be returned to across sessions as the story becomes tellable; with groups, a shared mural or a collaborative project can make the group's dynamics visible in a way discussion never does. Writing, too, belongs here: a poem, a letter never sent, or a fragment of memoir can do the same work as a drawing.

## Key Figures

Margaret Naumburg founded the psychodynamic strand, insisting that images speak the language of the unconscious. Edith Kramer founded the art-as-therapy strand, insisting that the creative act itself heals. Elinor Ulman edited the field's first journal and argued for art therapy as a distinct discipline. Hanna Yaxa Kwiatkowska, working at the National Institute of Mental Health, pioneered family art evaluation, using joint drawings to assess family dynamics. Shaun McNiff, in *Art as Medicine* (1992), carried the work into expressive arts therapy and argued for the imagination itself as a healing force. Cathy Malchiodi, in her handbook and her work on trauma, brought art therapy into hospitals, schools, and disaster response, and made the case for its use with children who have survived violence.

## Evidence and Influence

The evidence for art therapy is growing but uneven. Reviews find tentative support for its use with trauma, anxiety, and depression, and for its role in helping children and adults who find verbal therapy difficult; but many studies are small, and the field has struggled to standardise an intervention whose power lies partly in its openness. Its influence, however, is wide. Art-based methods are now routine in trauma treatment, in paediatric hospitals, in dementia care, in prisons, and in community work with refugees — settings where words are scarce, dangerous, or simply unavailable. The method has also shaped the wider culture of therapy: the idea that a client might draw, write, or make something in a session is no longer unusual, and many therapists who would not call themselves art therapists keep paper and pastels in the room. By contrast with dream analysis, which interprets a text the sleeping mind has already produced, art work makes a new text — an image, a figure, a page — in the waking presence of another person.
