# Meaning-Centered Psychotherapy

Meaning-centered psychotherapy (MCP) is a structured, manualised psychotherapy developed by the psychiatrist William Breitbart at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center in New York, designed above all for patients with advanced cancer who suffer from existential despair, loss of meaning, and a desire for hastened death. It draws directly on Viktor Frankl's logotherapy, but in a form that can be tested empirically: a fixed number of sessions, set themes, and measurable outcomes, rather than free philosophical reflection. That makes it, within the existential school, an unusual case — a treatment whose claims have been put to randomised trials — and the clearest documented bridge between Frankl's theory and evidence-based clinical practice.

## What it is

MCP is a brief, theme-based intervention. The group version runs for eight weekly sessions of ninety minutes; the individual version runs for seven sessions. Each session has a fixed theme, and the themes lead the patient through a sequence: sources of meaning before the illness; identity and meaning after the diagnosis; historical meaning, the legacy the patient leaves; and creative and experiential meaning in the present. The themes are built directly on Frankl's three sources of meaning — creativity, experience, and the attitude taken toward suffering — and on the idea, central to logotherapy, that meaning can be found even when circumstances cannot be changed.

## History

The early work was a pilot randomised trial of the group format, published by Breitbart and colleagues in *Psycho-Oncology* in 2010, followed by a pilot randomised trial of the individual format in the *Journal of Clinical Oncology* in 2012. The decisive trial was the large randomised controlled trial of the group format, published in the *Journal of Clinical Oncology* in 2015: 253 patients with advanced cancer were randomly assigned to eight sessions of meaning-centered group psychotherapy or supportive group psychotherapy, and those receiving MCP showed significantly greater improvement in spiritual well-being and quality of life and greater reductions in anxiety and desire for hastened death at the end of treatment, with benefits at longer follow-up less consistent. Breitbart and Poppito published treatment manuals for the group format (2010) and the individual format (2014), making the intervention replicable by other teams.

## How it works

The mechanism MCP proposes is straightforward: despair at the end of life is driven not only by pain and symptoms but by the collapse of meaning, and restoring a sense of meaning — through connection, creativity, legacy, and attitude toward suffering — reduces despair even when the disease does not change. The sessions are didactic as well as experiential: patients are taught Frankl's framework explicitly, then asked to apply it to their own lives. The legacy work is often the most affecting part of the protocol: patients are invited to consider what they will leave behind, not as a sentimental exercise but as a way of locating meaning in what has already been lived.

## Key figures

Frankl is the intellectual source: without *Man's Search for Meaning* (German 1946; English 1959 as *From Death-Camp to Existentialism*, 1962 under its current title) there is no MCP, and Breitbart has always presented the treatment as an application of logotherapy rather than a replacement for it.

## Evidence and influence

MCP has among the strongest trial evidence of any treatment in the existential school. The 2015 randomised trial (N = 253) is the centrepiece, supported by the 2010 group pilot and the 2012 individual pilot, both randomised and both showing benefits for spiritual well-being and related outcomes. The authors concluded that the large trial supported the efficacy of meaning-centered group psychotherapy for psychological and existential distress in patients with advanced cancer. The evidence is not without limits: the trials were run by the developing team at a small number of centres, the comparison condition was supportive group therapy, benefits were less consistent at longer follow-up, and the population is specific — advanced cancer — so the results do not automatically generalise to other forms of existential distress. Even so, MCP stands almost alone in its school in having been tested in this way, and it has influenced psycho-oncology and palliative care practice, where meaning-centered approaches are increasingly part of psychosocial care.

## How it differs from supportive group psychotherapy

In the trials, MCP was compared with supportive group psychotherapy. MCP follows a fixed sequence of sessions that targets meaning specifically as the therapeutic variable, whereas supportive therapy offers general emotional support without a prescribed meaning-focused curriculum.

## Criticisms and limits

The structured, manualised form that makes MCP testable is also what some existential therapists criticise: they argue that a fixed sequence of themes risks turning an encounter with meaning into a curriculum, and that the didactic element sits uneasily with the open questioning of the classical existential tradition. The treatment is also demanding of patients who are very ill — eight ninety-minute group sessions is a substantial commitment at the end of life — and the individual format exists partly to answer that objection. Finally, the concept of meaning it teaches is Frankl's, and patients whose meaning is given by a communal or religious framework rather than by individual choice may find the framework less fitting, a cultural limitation shared with much of the existential school.

## Key works

Breitbart and Poppito's treatment manuals (2010 group, 2014 individual) are the practical sources for both formats. The 2015 *Journal of Clinical Oncology* trial is the key evidence. Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning* (German 1946; English 1959/1962) is the theoretical source from which the whole programme derives.
