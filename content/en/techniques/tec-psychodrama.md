# Psychodrama

Psychodrama is a therapeutic method in which the client — the protagonist — directs and enacts a personal situation using group members as auxiliary players, so that an inner conflict, a memory, or a feared future becomes a living scene on a stage rather than a story told from a chair. By stepping into the scene, reversing roles with the people in it, and trying out new responses in action, the protagonist can see a familiar situation from unfamiliar angles, release feelings that words alone cannot reach, and rehearse behaviours that have felt impossible in real life. It is therapy as theatre, but theatre in which the audience is a therapy group and the play is the protagonist's own life.

## What It Is

The client directs and enacts a personal situation using group members as auxiliary egos who embody the significant others of the protagonist's life: a parent, a partner, a boss, a younger self. Externalisation allows new perspectives and behavioural experimentation: what was locked inside as a private torment becomes visible, audible, and moveable. A man who cannot confront his father can meet his father on the stage, played by another group member, and discover what he actually wants to say; a woman dreading a job interview can rehearse it, fail in it, and try again until a different ending becomes imaginable. The method rests on the belief that insight gained in action — in the body, in the voice, in the encounter — reaches deeper than insight gained by talking about action.

## History

Psychodrama was created by Jacob Levy Moreno, a Romanian-born psychiatrist working in Vienna. In 1921 he founded his Theatre of Spontaneity, where actors improvised scenes from the audience's own lives, and in 1925 he emigrated to the United States, settling in New York. There he developed the method systematically, founding the journal *Sociometry* in 1937 and publishing *Psychodrama, Volume 1* in 1946. Moreno's ambition was large: he wanted to replace the talking cure with an acting cure, and to treat the group, not only the individual, as the patient. His ideas spread through Europe and the Americas, and psychodrama became one of the founding methods of group psychotherapy, even as its theatrical form kept it at the margins of mainstream, verbally oriented practice.

## How It Works

A psychodrama session follows a three-part structure: warm-up, action, and sharing. In the warm-up, the director — the therapist — helps the group settle and identifies a protagonist whose concern is ready to be worked on. In the action phase, the protagonist sets the scene with the director's help, chooses group members to play the other figures, and enacts the situation. Core techniques shape the enactment. In role reversal, the protagonist exchanges places with another figure and speaks as that person, discovering how the world looks from the other side. In doubling, another group member stands beside the protagonist and voices feelings the protagonist has not yet spoken, giving words to what is half-felt. In the mirror technique, the protagonist steps out of the scene and watches another member play them, seeing their own behaviour from the outside. The scene may be replayed with a different outcome, or carried forward into a feared future, or rewound into a childhood moment. In the sharing phase, the group members speak not as critics but as witnesses, telling what the scene stirred in their own lives — a discipline that keeps the work from becoming performance and returns it to shared human experience.

## Key Figures

Moreno is the founder, and his concepts — spontaneity, creativity, the role, the encounter, sociometry — still frame the method. Zerka Moreno, his wife and collaborator, carried the work forward after his death in 1974 and trained generations of directors. Adam Blatner, in *Foundations of Psychodrama* (4th ed., 2000), gave the method an accessible modern textbook, connecting Moreno's ideas to contemporary practice. Peter Felix Kellermann, in *Focus on Psychodrama* (1992), clarified its therapeutic mechanisms and its place among the expressive therapies. J. L. Moreno's son Jonathan Moreno later wrote about his father's life and the method's place in the history of group psychotherapy.

## Evidence and Influence

Psychodrama's evidence base is thinner than its clinical reputation. There are no large randomised trials of the kind that anchor cognitive-behavioural treatments, and much of the available evidence is narrative and clinical, drawn from Moreno and his school. Small studies and case reports suggest benefits in a range of settings, and the method is widely used in addiction programmes, veterans' services, and psychodrama institutes across Europe and Latin America. Its influence, however, extends far beyond its own outcome literature. Role-play is now a standard tool in skills training, social skills groups, and family therapy; role reversal appears in couples work; and drama therapy, which drew on psychodrama among other sources, uses scripted and improvised performance with a wider range of clients. Psychodrama also shaped group psychotherapy itself: Moreno was among the first to insist that the group is a therapeutic agent in its own right, not merely an audience for individual work.
