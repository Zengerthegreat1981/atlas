# Cognitive Reappraisal

Cognitive reappraisal is the practice of changing one's interpretation of an emotion-eliciting situation, rather than changing the situation itself, in order to alter the emotional response that follows. A job interview is re-seen not as a trial in which one will be exposed, but as a conversation in which two sides assess a fit; a racing heart before a talk is re-read not as a sign of imminent failure, but as the body preparing for effort. The situation is unchanged. What changes is the appraisal of it, and with the appraisal, the emotion. It is one of the most studied techniques in the science of emotion regulation, and a central component of cognitive behavioural therapy.

## What It Is

Reappraisal is a describable, teachable practice, independent of any one theory or book: the same technique appears in several schools under different names. It works on the meaning assigned to an event, not on the event. That distinction is the whole of the technique. It is not positive thinking — it does not require the new interpretation to be cheerful, only to be plausible and less distressing — and it is not denial, since the situation itself is acknowledged.

## History

The theoretical foundation was laid by Richard Lazarus in his appraisal model of emotion. In *Psychological Stress and the Coping Process* (1966), Lazarus argued that emotion follows from the evaluation of a situation — its relevance to one's goals, its threat or benefit — rather than from the situation itself, and that changing the evaluation is therefore the available point of intervention. Lazarus and Susan Folkman developed the account in *Stress, Appraisal, and Coping* (1984). James Gross then made the principle operational in his process model of emotion regulation: in "Antecedent- and Response-Focused Emotion Regulation" (1998), Gross classified reappraisal as an antecedent-focused strategy, one that acts before the emotion is fully generated, and showed experimentally that it reduces the experience and outward expression of negative emotion without the physiological costs associated with suppression. Kevin Ochsner and Gross (2005) extended the account with neuroimaging evidence, showing that reappraisal engages prefrontal control regions and reduces activity in the amygdala. Gross and John (2003) further reported that people who habitually use reappraisal tend to show better emotional, social and well-being outcomes than those who habitually use suppression.

## How It Works

In practice, reappraisal proceeds in steps. First the person notices the automatic interpretation — "they are judging me," "this will go wrong" — and names it as an interpretation rather than a fact. Then alternative interpretations are generated: what else could this mean, what would a neutral observer see, what will this matter in a year? Then the person tests the alternatives against the evidence and adopts the one that fits best and costs least.

## Key Figures

Lazarus supplies the theory of appraisal; Folkman the account of coping that grew from it; Gross the experimental programme that turned appraisal into a measurable regulation strategy and mapped its neural correlates.

## Evidence and Influence

Reappraisal is among the best-supported techniques in emotion science. Gross's 1998 experiments showed that reappraisal reduced the experience and expression of negative emotion without the physiological costs seen with suppression, which reduced expression but not experience and increased sympathetic activation. Neuroimaging studies have repeatedly found that instructed reappraisal down-regulates amygdala activity through prefrontal control. As a clinical component, reappraisal is central to cognitive behavioural protocols for anxiety and depression, including social anxiety disorder. The evidence, however, is mostly about the mechanism rather than about reappraisal as a standalone protocol: few randomised trials have tested reappraisal alone as a treatment, and the effects documented in the laboratory are smaller and less durable than the effects of full treatment packages in which reappraisal is one element among several. That qualification belongs in the record.

## How It Differs from Defusion

Reappraisal is often contrasted with defusion, the technique from acceptance and commitment therapy. Reappraisal changes the *content* of the interpretation to lessen the emotion; defusion changes the *relationship* to the thought without trying to alter its content or correct it. The difference marks a theoretical divide between the second and third waves of cognitive behavioural therapy: whether distress is best addressed by fixing the thought or by loosening one's grip on it. In practice, skilled therapists use both, and the choice between them depends on the client and the thought in question.

## Criticisms and Limits

Reappraisal has limits that its own literature acknowledges. It works best for emotions that are genuinely driven by interpretation, and poorly for emotions driven by a situation that is objectively bad — reappraising grief, injustice, or danger can become a way of avoiding action that is needed. It requires cognitive resources that are depleted under stress, fatigue, or intense emotion, so the technique is hardest to use exactly when it is most needed. And it can be misused as a form of self-blame, in which the person concludes that their distress is their own fault for "thinking wrong." Used well, reappraisal is a tool for flexibility, not a doctrine that every situation can be thought away.

## Key Works

Lazarus and Folkman's *Stress, Appraisal, and Coping* (1984) is the theoretical source. Gross's 1998 paper is the key experimental statement, and Ochsner and Gross (2005) the standard review of its neural basis. Gross (2002) reviews the affective, cognitive and social consequences of reappraisal and suppression.
