# Empty Chair (Gestalt)

The empty chair is Gestalt therapy's most famous technique, and its most theatrical. The client sits facing an empty chair and speaks aloud to whoever — or whatever part of themselves — is imagined to be sitting there: a dead parent, a former partner, an inner critic, a frightened child. Then the client moves to the other chair and answers, in the voice of the other. The dialogue continues, chair to chair, until something shifts: a feeling long suppressed is finally expressed, a split inside the self is named, or a conversation that could never happen in life happens in the room.

## What It Is

The technique has two main forms. In the first, the empty chair holds another person, and the work addresses "unfinished business" — resentment, grief, guilt or longing left over from a relationship that ended badly or never had its say. In the second, the two-chair dialogue, both chairs hold parts of the self in conflict: the critic and the criticised, the part that wants to change and the part that refuses, the topdog and the underdog, as Fritz Perls called them. The therapist does not interpret the dialogue from outside; the client enacts it, and the understanding comes from the doing. The aim is not catharsis for its own sake but contact — with a feeling, a need, or a part of the self that has been avoided, disowned or silenced.

## History

The technique grew out of psychodrama and was made central to Gestalt therapy by Fritz Perls, who demonstrated it in his workshops at the Esalen Institute in California in the 1960s and published the demonstrations in *Gestalt Therapy Verbatim* in 1969. Perls believed that talking about a problem kept it at a distance, while enacting it in the present brought the person into direct contact with their experience. Erving and Miriam Polster systematised the method in *Gestalt Therapy Integrated* in 1973, describing the experiment as the heart of Gestalt work. In the late 1970s, Leslie Greenberg began to study the technique empirically, and with his colleagues he later built it into emotion-focused therapy, where the two-chair dialogue became a marker-guided intervention for self-criticism and internal conflict.

## How It Works

A session begins when the therapist notices a marker: the client speaks of a split ("part of me wants to leave, part of me is terrified"), or of a relationship left unresolved. The therapist suggests the experiment and sets it up carefully, explaining what will happen and checking that the client is willing. The client addresses the empty chair in the present tense, speaking directly — "you" rather than "he" or "she" — and the therapist coaches the client to stay with feeling rather than explanation, to speak louder, to repeat a sentence, to notice what the body does. When the client switches chairs, the therapist may ask the other side to answer in its own voice. The dialogue is guided toward a softening: the critic is asked what it is afraid of, the wounded part is asked what it needs. The session ends with the client returning to their own chair and reflecting on what was discovered.

## Evidence and Influence

The empty chair is one of the few Gestalt techniques with a substantial research base, much of it from emotion-focused therapy. In a 1979 experiment, Greenberg and Clarke compared the two-chair dialogue with empathic reflection and found that the chair work produced greater depth of experiencing and awareness of the conflict. Paivio and Greenberg showed in 1995 that empty-chair work helped clients resolve unfinished business with a significant other, with gains maintained at follow-up. The technique has since spread far beyond Gestalt therapy: schema therapists use chairwork for modes and unmet needs, cognitive therapists use it to give voice to hot cognitions, and emotion-focused therapists use it as a core intervention for self-criticism. Scott Kellogg's 2004 review traced this migration across several psychotherapies and argued that chairwork survives because it does something talking alone cannot: it turns an internal conflict into a living encounter.

## Advantages and Limitations

The strength of the technique is its immediacy. A client who has talked about anger for months may finally feel it, and name it, in a single exchange with an empty chair. But the same intensity is its risk. The work can overwhelm a fragile client, flood a traumatised one, or produce a dramatic performance that feels powerful in the session and changes nothing outside it. It requires a therapist who can pace the work, contain what arises, and help the client integrate the experience afterwards. Used well, it is not theatre but a structured way of meeting what has been avoided — the person, or the part of oneself, that was never answered.
