# Motivational Interviewing

Motivational interviewing is a way of talking with people about change that does the opposite of what most helpers instinctively do. It does not argue, advise, confront or persuade. Instead, the practitioner listens carefully, reflects what is heard, and helps the person hear their own reasons for changing — in their own words. It was developed for people who are ambivalent about change, most famously problem drinkers, and it rests on a simple observation: people are more likely to be persuaded by what they hear themselves say than by what anyone else tells them.

## What It Is

Motivational interviewing, usually shortened to MI, is a collaborative, person-centred style of conversation designed to strengthen a person's own motivation and commitment to change. Its spirit has four elements: partnership rather than expertise, acceptance rather than judgement, compassion, and evocation — drawing out what is already inside the person rather than installing it from outside. The practitioner uses four core skills, remembered by the acronym OARS: open questions, affirmations, reflective listening and summaries. Beneath the skills lies a stance toward ambivalence: the person is not resistant or in denial, but torn between two sides, and the work is to help both sides be heard so that the person can choose.

## History

MI began with a single paper. In 1983, the American psychologist William R. Miller published "Motivational Interviewing with Problem Drinkers", describing a counselling style he had developed while working with alcohol treatment in New Mexico and on sabbatical in Norway. In 1991, Miller and the British psychologist Stephen Rollnick published the first book, *Motivational Interviewing: Preparing People to Change Addictive Behavior*, which turned a local clinical innovation into an international method. A second edition followed in 2002, and a third in 2013, which reframed MI around four processes — engaging, focusing, evoking and planning — and described it as a style of communication rather than a technique for addiction alone. By then MI had spread from substance use into medicine, criminal justice, education, dentistry and health coaching.

## How It Works

A session begins with engagement: building a working alliance before any talk of change. Then comes focusing, agreeing on what the conversation is about. The heart of the method is evoking, where the practitioner listens for "change talk" — statements of desire, ability, reasons and need to change — and reflects and amplifies them, while responding to "sustain talk", the voice for staying the same, without arguing against it. The practitioner develops discrepancy gently, helping the person notice the gap between their values and their current behaviour, and supports self-efficacy, the belief that change is possible. Only when the person signals readiness does the conversation move to planning, making a concrete, specific plan the person owns. Throughout, the practitioner avoids the "righting reflex" — the urge to fix the problem with advice — because advice, however well meant, usually produces the opposite of what it intends: the person argues for the other side.

## Evidence and Influence

MI is among the most researched counselling methods ever developed. A meta-analysis by Hettema, Steele and Miller in 2005, covering 72 controlled trials, found effects that were modest but consistent across problem areas, with brief MI performing comparably to longer interventions. Lundahl and colleagues, in a 2010 meta-analysis of 119 studies, found small but significant effects of MI compared with weaker comparison conditions, and effects comparable to those of other active treatments. The method has also been adapted: brief motivational interventions in emergency departments, motivational enhancement therapy in Project MATCH, and group MI. Its influence reaches beyond its own trials: the idea that the therapist should evoke rather than install motivation has reshaped addiction treatment, health behaviour change and even the training of doctors and nurses.

## Advantages and Limitations

MI's strength is that it works with ambivalence rather than against it, and that it can be learned by practitioners who are not psychotherapists — nurses, social workers, probation officers. It is brief, respectful and well suited to people who have been lectured at too often. Its limits are real, however. Effects are typically modest rather than dramatic; MI is a way of opening the door to change, not a complete treatment for severe dependence. Fidelity matters: studies show that practitioners who have not been trained and supervised drift back into directing and advising, and the method's effects shrink accordingly. And MI is not a substitute for the structural supports — housing, employment, treatment access — without which motivation alone cannot carry a person very far.
