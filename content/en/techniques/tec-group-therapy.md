# Group Therapy Processes

Group therapy is therapeutic work in a group context. The group becomes an arena for practising authentic relating, receiving feedback, and recognising the universality of human struggles. A person who has always believed that their shame, their anger, or their loneliness is uniquely theirs sits in a circle and discovers that others carry the same things — and that discovery, which no therapist's reassurance can deliver, is itself the beginning of change. The group is not a cheaper substitute for individual therapy. It is a different treatment, with powers individual therapy does not possess: many mirrors instead of one, many relationships instead of one, and a social world small enough to be worked on directly.

## What It Is

Therapeutic work in a group context means that the group itself is the instrument of change, not merely the setting. The group becomes an arena for practising authentic relating: members try out honesty, assertiveness, vulnerability, and conflict in a place where the consequences are real but contained. It becomes a place for receiving feedback — not the therapist's considered interpretation, but the immediate, unfiltered reactions of peers, which are harder to dismiss and easier to believe. And it becomes a place for recognising the universality of human struggles: the relief of learning that one's secret is shared. A typical group has six to ten members and meets weekly for ninety minutes, led by one or two therapists whose task is to keep the group safe, to help members speak to each other rather than to the leader, and to make the group's own process visible to itself.

## History

Group therapy began in 1905, when Joseph Pratt, a Boston physician, gathered tuberculosis patients into classes and discovered that the group itself helped them. Jacob Moreno, in the 1920s and 1930s, made the group a therapeutic agent in its own right through psychodrama and sociometry. In Britain, S. H. Foulkes developed group analysis during the Second World War, treating the group as a matrix in which each member's difficulties appear as disturbances of the whole; his *Introduction to Group-Analytic Psychotherapy* (1948) founded a school. Wilfred Bion, in *Experiences in Groups* (1961), described the unconscious assumptions — dependency, fight-flight, pairing — that pull any group away from its task. The decisive synthesis came from Irvin Yalom, whose *The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy* (1970) identified the therapeutic factors of group work and turned group therapy into a teachable, researchable method. From the 1970s onward, groups proliferated: support groups, skills groups, psychoeducational groups, and groups for specific problems from addiction to bereavement.

## How It Works

Yalom's eleven therapeutic factors are the standard account of how groups heal. Instillation of hope, universality, and the imparting of information come first; altruism, the corrective recapitulation of the primary family group, the development of socialising techniques, imitative behaviour, interpersonal learning, group cohesiveness, catharsis, and existential factors follow. In practice, the work is deceptively simple: members talk to each other, and the therapist helps them notice what is happening between them. A member who dominates is invited to hear how that lands; a member who stays silent is invited to say what the silence costs; a conflict between two members is treated not as a disruption but as the material itself. The therapist's skill lies in holding the frame — time, membership, confidentiality — and in translating the group's here-and-now into learning that members can carry into their lives. Different models emphasise different factors: cognitive-behavioural groups are structured and skills-based, psychodynamic groups are open-ended and process-focused, and support groups are led lightly or not at all.

## Key Figures

Joseph Pratt is often counted among the method's earliest pioneers; he noticed that the group did something his lectures could not. Jacob Moreno made the group a stage and a laboratory. S. H. Foulkes made it a matrix, and his group-analytic tradition remains strong in Britain and Europe. Wilfred Bion gave the field its most penetrating account of group unconscious life. Irvin Yalom gave it its textbook, its research programme, and its humane voice; the sixth edition of his book, with Molyn Leszcz (2020), remains the standard text. Gary Burlingame and his colleagues built the empirical literature, testing which factors predict outcome and which groups work for whom.

## Evidence and Influence

Group therapy has a substantial research base. Meta-analyses, including McRoberts, Burlingame, and Hoag (1998), find that group therapy is about as effective as individual therapy for a range of problems, and it is often considered more economical to deliver. Its influence extends beyond the clinic: the group format is widely used in addiction treatment, including residential programmes (mutual-help fellowships such as Alcoholics Anonymous are related but are not group psychotherapy); it is the standard format for skills training, from dialectical behaviour therapy to anger management; and it shapes organisational life, where group relations conferences and team interventions draw directly on Bion and Foulkes. By contrast with family systems work, which takes an existing family system — real or represented — as its material, the group here is a gathering of strangers who become a therapeutic community, so that healing comes largely from new relationships rather than from reordering old ones.
