# What Is Language?

A central question in philosophy, linguistics, and psychology: is language a system of symbols? Is it a tool for expressing thoughts? Is it a social institution? Is it an innate capacity? The question was raised in Arab thought centuries earlier, in the form of a definition and then in the form of an origin.

## Ibn Jinni's definition and the origin of language among the Arabs

Ibn Jinni (d. 1002) defined language in *al-Khasa'is* as "sounds by which each people expresses its purposes," a definition that combines sound, expression, society, and the communicative aim in a single phrase, and that resembles many modern definitions. Scholars disputed the origin of language between the view of *tawqif* (revelation), namely that God taught Adam the names (Al-Baqara, 31), which is the view of Ibn Faris in *al-Sahibi*, and the view of *istilah* (convention), namely that people agreed upon words. Ibn Jinni wavered between the two positions and allowed that its origin might be inspiration or convention. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddima*, treated language as a craft-like "faculty" (*malaka*) in the tongue, acquired through practice and hearing, just as crafts are acquired, a conception that brings it close to psychology's interest in acquisition. As-Suyuti gathered many of these discussions in *al-Muzhir*.

## Modern dialogues

For Ferdinand de Saussure, in *Course in General Linguistics* (1916, compiled after his death by his students Bally and Sechehaye), language is a social system of signs. The sign is a union of signifier and signified, and the link between them is arbitrary. He distinguishes between language (*langue*) and speech (*parole*). Noam Chomsky, beginning with *Syntactic Structures* (1957) and developing the innateness claim in *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), regards language as an innate human capacity, with a hereditary "universal grammar" proposed to explain the speed with which children acquire it and the open-ended creativity of sentences. Ludwig Wittgenstein, in his *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921), presented the picture theory: the proposition is a picture of reality, and he wrote that the limits of my language mean the limits of my world. He then changed his position radically in *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), toward "language games": meaning is use within a practice. Wilhelm von Humboldt, in his work on the diversity of the structure of human languages (1836), held that language is an activity (*energeia*) rather than a product, and that it carries a distinct "world-view" for each nation.

## Levels of language and psychology

The study of language is divided among phonetics, morphology, syntax, semantics, and pragmatics. The last studies use in context, and is linked to Austin and Searle's view that saying is doing. In psychology, Steven Pinker, in *The Language Instinct* (1994), presented the innateness argument. Eric Lenneberg, in *Biological Foundations of Language* (1967), argued for a critical period for language acquisition, a hypothesis that remains debated. On the question of the relation between language and thought, the hypothesis of linguistic relativity attributed to Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf emerged. *Language, Thought and Reality* (1956) collected Whorf's essays. Its strong form, that language determines thought, has not been confirmed, while its weak form, that language influences some aspects of perception, has received experimental support.

## Contemporary questions

Alan Turing, in his article "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" (*Mind*, 1950), linked language and intelligence through what became known as the Turing Test; his foundational work on computing dates from 1936. Large language models since the last decade have shown the ability to produce coherent text from statistics over texts, and this has revived the debate between those who see language as an innate capacity and those who see it as learned through use. Nevertheless, critics, including Chomsky, ask whether statistical imitation explains understanding.

## Limits

Each approach captures only one aspect: Saussure does not explain creativity, Chomsky underplays the social role, and the late Wittgenstein offers not a theory so much as a method. Classical Arab definitions may carry a normative religious dimension that modern linguistics does not, when language is linked to revelation.
