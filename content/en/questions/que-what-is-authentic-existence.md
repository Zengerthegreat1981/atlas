# What Makes Existence Authentic?

What makes existence authentic? The question has no single settled answer, and that is exactly why it matters. For more than a century and a half, it has functioned as a generative question in existential philosophy: a point of entry that gathers competing answers around itself without being resolved by any one of them. This entry documents the question itself and the network of attempts to answer it. It does not recommend an answer, and it does not declare one correct.

## Why a Question, Not a Concept

The Atlas has a separate entry for the concept of authenticity (con-authenticity), which analyses what the term means. This entry is different: it records the open question as the centre of gravity around which several thinkers organised their work. The link between those thinkers is not agreement but the shared question. Each of them answered it differently, and the differences are the point — they show how much turns on what one takes authenticity to be.

## Kierkegaard: Authenticity as a Leap of Faith

Søren Kierkegaard gave the question its modern form. In *Either/Or* (1843), *Fear and Trembling* (1843), and *The Sickness Unto Death* (1849), he argued that authentic existence requires passing through three stages — the aesthetic, the ethical, and the religious — and ends in a leap of faith: an individual, inward commitment that cannot be justified by reason or by the crowd. For Kierkegaard, the inauthentic life is the life dissolved into "the crowd" (*Mængden*), the anonymous public whose opinions replace personal responsibility. Authenticity, by contrast, is a solitary relation to the absolute, taken up by the single individual. His answer already contains the tension that runs through the whole tradition: authenticity demands standing apart from what everyone else does, yet it cannot be proved to anyone else that one has done so.

## Heidegger: Authenticity as Anticipation of Death

Martin Heidegger reframed the question in *Being and Time* (1927). For Heidegger, everyday human existence (*Dasein*) is mostly inauthentic (*Uneigentlichkeit*): we dissolve into the chatter and ready-made judgements of "the They" (*Das Man*), doing what one does, thinking what one thinks. Authenticity (*Eigentlichkeit*) becomes possible when Dasein anticipates its own death — not as a distant fact, but as its ownmost, unsurpassable possibility — and listens to the "call of conscience" that summons it to take over its own possibilities instead of borrowing them from the crowd. Where Kierkegaard's authentic self leaps towards faith, Heidegger's authentic self turns towards finitude: it is the awareness that my life is mine, and that it ends, that makes it possible to live it as my own.

## Sartre: Authenticity as Refusing Bad Faith

Jean-Paul Sartre attacked the question from the opposite side, by negation. In *Being and Nothingness* (1943), he denied that human beings have a pre-given essence (the formula "existence precedes essence" was popularised in his lecture *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, 1945), and held that we are "condemned to be free." Bad faith (*mauvaise foi*) is the flight from that freedom — pretending to be fixed by a social role, a character trait, or a past, as in his famous example of the waiter who plays at being a waiter, over-identifying with the role until it seems to define him. Authentic existence, for Sartre, is the refusal of that pretence: the frank acknowledgement that every choice is mine, that no excuse relieves me of responsibility, and that I am always more than the role I happen to be playing.

## Camus: Authenticity as Revolt Within the Absurd

Albert Camus, in *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), rejected both Kierkegaard's leap of faith and any metaphysical solution to the human condition. For Camus, the authentic life is lived inside the absurd — the clash between our appetite for meaning and the world's silence — without suicide and without a "leap" into false consolation. Authenticity here is revolt: the lucid, continuing refusal to deny the contradiction or to escape it. Sisyphus, pushing his rock with full knowledge that it will fall, is Camus's image of this stance.

## Frankl: Authenticity as Discovering Meaning

Viktor Frankl, writing from his experience of the concentration camps in *Man's Search for Meaning* (first published in German in 1946), gave the question a different centre again. For Frankl, authenticity is not found in confronting nothingness, freedom, or the absurd as such, but in discovering the unique meaning that a particular situation asks of a particular person — through work, through love, or through the attitude taken towards unavoidable suffering. His question is not "does life have meaning in itself?" but "what does life ask of me, here, now?" The shift matters: authenticity becomes responsiveness to a demand rather than assertion of the self.

## Why the Question Stays Open

Five serious answers to one question, and they do not agree even on whether authenticity requires faith or forbids it, whether it turns towards death or towards meaning, whether it is a solitary leap or a response to what life demands. Kierkegaard anchors it in faith, Heidegger in finitude, Sartre in freedom, Camus in revolt, Frankl in meaning. That disagreement is not a failure of the tradition; it is the tradition. The question remains generative precisely because each answer opens a different path through it, and none has closed it. This entry organises that network. It does not settle it.
