# What Is Forgiveness?

A moral, psychological, and religious question at once: is forgiveness a waiver of a right? A change in feelings? A moral choice? Each tradition has an answer that differs on where the right lies and who has the power to relinquish it.

## Pardon, Forgiving, and Forgiveness in the Islamic Tradition

Arabic distinguishes between closely related terms. *'Afw* is the waiver of the claim to punishment or to a right; *ṣafḥ* is turning away from blame and reproach; and *maghfira*, in origin, is the covering of a sin and the erasing of its trace, and is usually attributed to God. The Qur'an combines the two in the verse: "Let them pardon and overlook. Do you not love that God should forgive you?" (al-Nur 22), praises "those who restrain anger and pardon people" (Al 'Imran 134), and says: "Whoever pardons and makes reconciliation, his reward is with God" (al-Shura 40). In the law of retaliation, the Sharia gives the victim's guardian the right to pardon, thereby waiving retaliation (al-Baqara 178). An important principle emerges: God does not waive the rights of persons by repentance alone; rather, wrongs must be restored or the injured parties must be asked to release the claim, as in the hadith of the bankrupt, transmitted by Muslim. Al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, treated restraint of anger and forbearance as virtues cultivated through training of the soul. Forgiveness here is therefore not merely a feeling, but a three-way relationship between the offender, the injured party, and God.

## The Western Philosophical Tradition

David Konstan, in *Before Forgiveness* (2010), argues that forgiveness as a virtue involving the abandonment of resentment in exchange for repentance was not known in that sense to the Greeks and Romans, but emerged within the biblical tradition. In Augustine's *Confessions* (c. 400), forgiveness is a divine gift that does not originate in the self. In Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morals* (1887), the matter takes two forms: resentment (*ressentiment*) that disguises itself as forgiveness is a repressed revenge, whereas mercy (*Gnade*) is a privilege of the powerful who rise above the law (second essay, section 10), so forgiveness is not for him an absolute weakness. In Derrida's essays on forgiveness (published in French in the late 1990s and in English as *On Cosmopolitanism and Forgiveness*, 2001), true forgiveness concerns what is unforgivable: if we forgive only what is forgivable, that is a calculation, not forgiveness; it is thus both impossible and necessary. In Paul Ricoeur's *Memory, History, Forgetting* (2000), forgiveness is the liberation of the agent from the weight of their act, not the erasure of the act; it is a reconciled memory, not forgetting.

## Psychology

Empirical research on forgiveness began in the 1980s. Robert Enright and his colleagues (beginning with the study of the adolescent as a forgiver, 1989) mapped out a gradual model of the process of forgiveness, passing through stages of uncovering the pain, deciding to forgive, working to understand and empathize with the offender, and finally achieving meaning and liberation. In *Helping Clients Forgive* (2000), Enright and Fitzgibbons presented a systematic treatment. Michael McCullough and his colleagues (1997) defined forgiveness as a motivational transformation away from revenge and avoidance and toward goodwill. Numerous studies link forgiveness to better mental health and lower stress, although the findings on physical health are less conclusive.

## Collective Dimensions

In the political sphere, forgiveness has been raised within transitional justice. South Africa established the Truth and Reconciliation Commission by law in 1995, which offered amnesty in exchange for full disclosure of crimes. Desmond Tutu described the experience in *No Future Without Forgiveness* (1999). Critics argue that forgiveness may become a pressure on the victim, and that justice and forgiveness are not the same thing: they may mix, but neither can substitute for the other. The concepts also need to be kept apart: forgiveness is chiefly a response of the wronged person, reconciliation is the restoration of a relationship and requires two parties, and legal pardon or amnesty is an act of authority that does not by itself involve either.

## Limits

Most psychological models of forgiveness were built on Western samples and individualistic concepts, and need to be examined across cultures. Many measurements rely on self-report. Definitions of forgiveness vary among researchers to such a degree that findings are difficult to pool. Moreover, linking forgiveness to healing may impose an unjust moral obligation on the victim.
