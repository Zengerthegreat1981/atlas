# What Is Consciousness?

What is consciousness in itself? This question is contested by three major answers in modern philosophy, but it was preceded in the Islamic world by important attempts to describe consciousness of oneself, which deserve mention first.

## The Floating Man in Ibn Sina and Presential Knowledge in Suhrawardi

In the book on the soul of *al-Shifa'* (and in *al-Isharat wa al-Tanbihat*), Ibn Sina (d. 1037) presented a famous thought experiment: if a human being were created all at once, complete, suspended in the air, blindfolded, not feeling his limbs and touching nothing, he would still continue to affirm the existence of his own self. The argument aims to show that the soul knows itself by direct acquaintance, unconditioned by the senses or by the body. Some researchers have compared this argument with the cogito, and Jari Kaukua, in his study *Self-Awareness in Islamic Philosophy* (2015), examined the dimensions of this primary, direct self-awareness in Ibn Sina and those who came after him. Suhrawardi (d. 1191) made this direct knowledge of the self "knowledge by presence" (*'ilm huduri*), the root of all knowledge, and in Mulla Sadra (d. 1640) the concept became a pillar of his theory of the union of the knower and the known.

## Consciousness as a Thinking Substance in Descartes (1641)

In *Meditations on First Philosophy* (*Meditationes de Prima Philosophia*, 1641), especially the Second Meditation, Descartes concludes from methodical doubt that he cannot doubt his existence while he is thinking (*Cogito, ergo sum*). For him consciousness is *res cogitans*, a thinking substance distinct from *res extensa*, the extended material substance. It is therefore not a property of matter but an independent substance. This position remains the origin of the dualism that philosophy inherited, and the origin of the question of how mind and body interact.

## Consciousness as Subjective Experience in Nagel (1974)

In the article "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (*The Philosophical Review*, 1974), Thomas Nagel argues against reductive physicalist accounts of the mind: the essence of consciousness is that there is something it is **like** to be that organism. A bat that perceives the world by echolocation has a subjective point of view that no complete objective physical description can reach. On this basis David Chalmers, in "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" (*Journal of Consciousness Studies*, 1995), formulated the distinction between the "easy" problems (explaining attention, reporting, and integration) and "the hard problem": why physical processing is accompanied by subjective experience at all.

## Consciousness as a Narrative Produced by the Brain in Dennett (1991)

In *Consciousness Explained* (1991), Daniel Dennett rejects the "Cartesian theater": there is no point in the brain where conscious experience comes together, only parallel competing processes, and the "multiple drafts" model, which holds that what we call consciousness is a narrative composed retrospectively. There is no entity that watches the experience from the inside.

## Empirical Approaches

By contrast, scientific programs do not claim to solve the hard problem, but they specify what distinguishes the conscious state. The "global workspace" theory, formulated by Bernard Baars in *A Cognitive Theory of Consciousness* (1988), holds that information becomes conscious when it is broadcast widely to specialized brain modules. Giulio Tononi's integrated information theory is another. These theories are tested with experiments on perception below threshold and in comatose states.

## Summary and Limits

Descartes places consciousness in a separate thinking substance; Nagel places it in a subjective perspective that resists reduction; Dennett reduces it to a narrative made by the brain. The Islamic tradition adds that self-awareness is direct presence prior to sense perception. The question has not been settled. Critics of Dennett argue that he explains away the phenomenon itself, and critics of Nagel and Chalmers argue that the "hardness" may result from the limits of our conception rather than from a mystery in nature.
