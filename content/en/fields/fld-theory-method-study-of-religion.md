# Theory and Method in the Study of Religion

## What the field asks

This field examines the concepts, assumptions and procedures by which religion is studied. Where other parts of the module describe traditions, here the question is reflexive: what are we talking about when we say "religion", from what standpoint can it be studied, how far can different traditions be compared, and what counts as an adequate explanation? It overlaps with philosophy of the social sciences, but its problems arise from the specific history of the discipline.

## Main questions

1. **Defining religion.** Is there a stable object of study at all? Edward Tylor proposed "belief in spiritual beings" as a minimal definition (*Primitive Culture*, 1871). Émile Durkheim defined religion through "sacred things" and a moral community (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912). Clifford Geertz defined it as a system of symbols that establishes powerful moods and motivations by formulating conceptions of a general order of existence (1966). Paul Tillich spoke of "ultimate concern". Each definition includes and excludes different cases. Substantive definitions struggle with non-theistic traditions; functional ones risk including nationalism, football or ideology. Some scholars favour "polythetic" or "family resemblance" accounts, in which no single trait is necessary.
2. **Insider and outsider.** Can someone outside a tradition understand it? Can someone inside it study it without bias? The debate over the "emic" and "etic" distinction (Kenneth Pike's terms, borrowed from linguistics) and the writings collected in Russell McCutcheon's *The Insider/Outsider Problem in the Study of Religion* (1999) show that neither position has an automatic advantage. Many scholars aim for methodological agnosticism, bracketing truth claims rather than denying or affirming them.
3. **Explanation and reduction.** Are religious phenomena fully explicable by social, psychological or cognitive causes? Critics of "reductionism" argue that this misses the religious dimension; defenders reply that causal explanation is what scholarship offers and that it does not settle questions of truth. Robert Segal and Donald Wiebe have argued from the explanatory side; Eliade and others warned that religion must be understood as religion. Neither side is unanimous.
4. **Comparison.** Is comparing the sacred in different cultures illuminating or does it impose Western categories? Jonathan Z. Smith's *Drudgery Divine* (1990) and *Imagining Religion* (1982) argued that comparison is an act of scholarly construction that must be justified and made explicit, and that the older Christian-Protestant bias had distorted comparison of "pagan" ritual with early Christianity.
5. **Secularisation.** Did modernity bring decline of religion, privatisation, or only transformation? Theorists such as Max Weber ("disenchantment"), Bryan Wilson, Peter Berger (who first defended and later revised the secularisation thesis), Steve Bruce, José Casanova (*Public Religions in the Modern World*, 1994), Charles Taylor (*A Secular Age*, 2007) and Rodney Stark and others have offered competing accounts, and the evidence is regionally varied.

## Key approaches and their authors

**Phenomenology of religion.** Rudolf Otto's *Das Heilige* (1917, *The Idea of the Holy*) described the "numinous" as a mysterious, awe-inspiring presence. Gerardus van der Leeuw, W. Brede Kristensen and Mircea Eliade extended the method, seeking the structure of religious experience and symbolism through comparison. Critics, including Jonathan Z. Smith and Russell McCutcheon, argue that "the sacred" is a theological category in disguise and that the approach neglects history and power. Defenders respond that careful description of meaning remains necessary.

**Historical and critical methods.** Philology, source criticism and historical contextualisation remain the backbone of work on scriptural traditions. They matter in disputes about authorship, dating and development.

**Critical studies of the category.** Talal Asad's *Genealogies of Religion* (1993) argued that the modern concept of religion as private belief emerged from specific Protestant and Enlightenment histories, and Tomoko Masuzawa's *The Invention of World Religions* (2005) traced how the "world religions" frame arose in nineteenth-century scholarship. Others, such as Brent Nongbri (*Before Religion*, 2013), argue that the ancient world lacked the category. Critics reply that the term, though modern, can still be used analytically if its limits are acknowledged.

**Cognitive and evolutionary approaches.** Scott Atran, Pascal Boyer and Justin Barrett argued that religious ideas are favoured by ordinary cognitive tendencies, such as agency detection. Harvey Whitehouse developed a theory of "modes of religiosity". These approaches test hypotheses experimentally and cross-culturally, and face objections about cultural variation, definitional vagueness and generalisation from limited samples.

## Methods

Scholars use textual analysis, ethnographic fieldwork and participant observation, interviews and surveys, historical-comparative analysis, experimental and statistical methods, and conceptual critique. Each has typical strengths and blind spots. Ethnography gives depth but limited generality; surveys give breadth but depend on question wording; textual analysis tells us what elites wrote, which may differ from lived practice.

## Connections to other disciplines

Anthropology contributed fieldwork and the concept of culture; sociology contributed institutional and comparative analysis; psychology contributed accounts of experience and development; philosophy contributes analysis of concepts and of rational explanation; history supplies contexts and sources; and law raises questions about how states define religion for the purposes of rights, tax and recognition, since legal definitions have practical consequences.

## Debates in brief

Is the field a distinct discipline or a meeting place of disciplines? Should scholars be "advocates" for traditions, critics of them, or neutral describers? Are claims about "the sacred" scholarship or theology? Is "lived religion", the study of ordinary practice (as in work by Robert Orsi and Nancy Ammerman), a better object than doctrine? None of these questions is closed.

## Common misunderstandings and cautions

Methodological neutrality is not relativism. Saying that scholars bracket truth claims does not imply that all claims are equally true. A causal explanation of belief is not a refutation of it, nor a confirmation. Reductionism is a charge as well as a position, so it is worth checking who is claiming what. Finally, keep three things apart: a description of what a community does, that community's own account of why, and a scholar's explanation, which may contradict the second without being hostile to the community.
