# Anthropology

Anthropology is the study of human beings in all their variety: how people live together, make meaning, raise children, exchange goods, worship, govern themselves and change over time. It is distinguished less by a single subject matter than by a way of working. Anthropologists favour long-term, close-up engagement with particular communities, they compare across cases, and they try to understand practices from within before judging them from outside.

## Overview

The discipline is often described as having several subfields, especially in the North American tradition. Cultural (and, in the British tradition, social) anthropology studies the beliefs, institutions and practices of living communities, chiefly through ethnography. Linguistic anthropology studies language as a social and cultural activity. Biological or physical anthropology studies human evolution, variation and the primates. Archaeology studies the human past through material remains. Different national traditions weigh these differently, and many scholars today work across the boundaries or outside the four-field scheme altogether.

## Core commitments

Several habits recur across schools. **Fieldwork** and participant observation place the researcher in daily contact with the people studied. **Holism** asks how economy, kinship, religion and politics fit together rather than treating them in isolation. **Comparison** looks for patterns and differences across societies. **Cultural relativism**, in the methodological sense associated with Franz Boas, asks that practices be understood in their own context; it is distinct from the claim that all practices are equally good. **Reflexivity** asks the researcher to account for their own position and its effects on what is seen and written.

## History and major schools

Nineteenth-century evolutionists such as Lewis Henry Morgan and Edward Burnett Tylor arranged societies in developmental stages and defined culture as a subject of comparison. Boas and his students rejected rigid stage schemes in favour of detailed, historically grounded study of particular cultures. Bronislaw Malinowski made extended participant observation a professional norm, and A. R. Radcliffe-Brown developed structural-functional comparison of social systems. E. E. Evans-Pritchard and his colleagues produced classic accounts of African political and religious life. In France, Marcel Mauss's work on gift exchange and Claude Lévi-Strauss's structural analysis of kinship and myth shaped a distinct tradition. Clifford Geertz later argued for an interpretive anthropology concerned with meaning, summed up in the idea of thick description.

From the later twentieth century, critiques of representation, colonial entanglement and authority reshaped the field. Writing Culture and related work questioned how ethnographic texts are made, postcolonial critics examined the discipline's links to empire, and newer approaches such as the ontological turn ask whether anthropologists should take other ways of understanding the world as serious alternatives rather than as beliefs to be explained.

## Major themes

Kinship and marriage, exchange and reciprocity, ritual and symbol, political order and its alternatives, religion and witchcraft, gender, language, illness and healing, and the effects of colonialism, development and globalisation recur throughout the literature. The three field introductions linked below gather these themes: ethnography and its ethics, kinship and exchange, and power and politics.

## Biological anthropology and primatology

Biological anthropology studies humans as an evolving and variable species. Its areas include palaeoanthropology, which reconstructs human ancestry from fossils; population genetics and the study of human variation; skeletal biology; and human growth, health and adaptation to environments such as high altitude. Sherwood Washburn's call in 1951 for a "new physical anthropology" urged a shift from measuring and classifying types towards studying evolutionary processes and function. Genetic research later reinforced the critique of races as discrete biological units, since most human genetic variation occurs within rather than between conventionally defined groups, as Richard Lewontin showed in 1972.

Primatology compares humans with other primates in order to study behaviour, social organisation, ecology and cognition. Long-term field studies have been central, including Jane Goodall's research on chimpanzees at Gombe, begun in 1960, which documented tool making and use. Other work has examined how female strategies shape primate societies, as in Sarah Blaffer Hrdy's research on langurs. Such comparisons inform questions about human evolution, but no living primate is a human ancestor, and primatologists also address conservation.

## Archaeology

Archaeology investigates past societies through material remains: artefacts, buildings, human and animal bones, plant traces and landscapes. Survey and excavation record objects in context, because their position and association carry much of the evidence. Stratigraphy gives relative sequence, while methods such as radiocarbon dating, developed by Willard Libby and colleagues in the late 1940s, and tree-ring dating help establish age. Theory has shifted over time. Culture-historical archaeology traced the distribution and succession of cultures; the processual or "New Archaeology" associated with Lewis Binford in the 1960s sought testable explanations of cultural systems, drawing on anthropology; post-processual approaches, associated with Ian Hodder and others from the 1980s, stressed meaning, agency and interpretation. Ethnoarchaeology observes living communities to understand how material traces are formed. Archaeology also raises questions of heritage, looting, the treatment of human remains and the rights of descendant communities, for example under the United States Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act of 1990. In North America it is usually taught within anthropology; elsewhere it is often a separate discipline.

## Debates

Long-running questions include the relation of nature and culture, whether cultures can be compared at all, how far the observer can speak for the observed, and what responsibilities researchers owe the communities they study. The debate over positionality and reflexivity, and the critique of the discipline's colonial past, remain active.
