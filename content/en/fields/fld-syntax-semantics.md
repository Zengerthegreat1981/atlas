# Syntax and Semantics

Syntax studies how words are combined into phrases and sentences. Semantics studies what words and sentences mean and how the meaning of a whole depends on its parts and its structure. The two are closely linked, because the meaning of a sentence such as "the dog bit the man" differs from "the man bit the dog" only through structure. Together they address the central fact about language: finite resources let speakers produce and understand an unlimited number of new sentences.

## What the field asks

Speakers know, without being taught, that some word strings are sentences and others are not, that some sentences are ambiguous, and that some pairs mean nearly the same thing. They can understand sentences they have never heard. The field asks what knowledge explains this, how it is represented, and how syntax relates to meaning and to use.

## Main questions

- What are the units of sentence structure, and what constrains how they combine?
- Why do languages with very different word orders share certain limits?
- How is the meaning of a complex expression computed from its parts?
- How do quantifiers, tense, negation, modality and anaphora work?
- Where is the boundary between literal meaning and meaning added by context?
- Is syntax autonomous from meaning, or are they intertwined?

## Key positions in syntax

**Phrase structure and generative syntax.** Noam Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures* (1957) argued that grammar needs more than word-by-word chains and introduced transformations. *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965) distinguished competence from performance. Later stages include Government and Binding Theory (1981), the Principles and Parameters framework, in which languages differ by settings of a few options, and the Minimalist Program (1995), which seeks to reduce grammar to a few simple operations, notably Merge. Related formalisms include X-bar theory, developed by Chomsky and Ray Jackendoff, and Relational Grammar.

**Non-transformational frameworks.** Lexical-Functional Grammar, developed by Joan Bresnan and Ronald Kaplan, separates constituent structure from functional structure. Head-driven Phrase Structure Grammar, by Carl Pollard and Ivan Sag, treats grammar as constraints on feature structures, and Generalised Phrase Structure Grammar preceded it. Tree-Adjoining Grammar, by Aravind Joshi, is a computationally tractable formalism.

**Dependency grammar.** Lucien Tesniere's *Elements of Structural Syntax* (published 1959) treats sentences as networks of head-dependent relations rather than phrase constituents. It is influential in computational work and in the study of languages with free word order.

**Categorial grammar.** Descending from Kazimierz Ajdukiewicz and Yehoshua Bar-Hillel, and developed by Joachim Lambek and later Mark Steedman, it assigns words types that specify how they combine, so that syntax and semantic composition run in parallel.

**Construction Grammar and cognitive approaches.** Charles Fillmore, Paul Kay, Adele Goldberg and William Croft propose that grammar consists of constructions, pairings of form and meaning at every level from morphemes to sentence patterns. Goldberg's *Constructions* (1995) is a prominent statement. Ronald Langacker's Cognitive Grammar treats grammar as symbolic from the start.

**Typological and functional syntax.** Joseph Greenberg's 1963 study of word order universals launched modern typology. Functionalists such as Talmy Givón and Simon Dik argue that structures are shaped by discourse and communicative function.

## Key positions in semantics

**Truth-conditional and formal semantics.** Following Gottlob Frege's distinction between sense and reference (1892) and Alfred Tarski's work on truth, formal semantics takes meaning to be tied to truth conditions. Richard Montague's papers of the early 1970s proposed that natural language can be treated with the same formal precision as logic. Heim and Kratzer's *Semantics in Generative Grammar* (1998) is a standard textbook. Core tools include type theory, lambda calculus, possible worlds and generalised quantifiers.

**Dynamic semantics.** Discourse Representation Theory, by Hans Kamp, and File Change Semantics, by Irene Heim, treat meaning as the way a sentence changes the context, which helps explain pronouns and presupposition.

**Lexical semantics.** Research on word meaning includes componential analysis, Jackendoff's conceptual semantics, Pustejovsky's Generative Lexicon and prototype theory, built on Eleanor Rosch's studies of categories in the 1970s.

**Cognitive semantics.** George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's *Metaphors We Live By* (1980) argued that metaphor structures thought and language, not just style. Frame semantics, by Fillmore, links meanings to background knowledge.

**Pragmatics.** H. P. Grice's work on conversational implicature (William James Lectures, 1967, published in 1989) distinguished what is said from what is implicated. Later neo-Gricean and relevance-theoretic accounts, by Sperber and Wilson, develop this.

## Major debates and objections

**Autonomy of syntax.** Generative theory holds that syntactic knowledge is distinct from meaning and use. Functional and constructionist critics reply that form is motivated by meaning and usage, and that idioms and partly fixed patterns show no sharp line between lexicon and grammar.

**Innateness and learnability.** Chomsky's poverty-of-the-stimulus argument claims that the input does not suffice to explain acquisition without innate structure. Usage-based researchers, among them Michael Tomasello, argue that general learning abilities, with rich input, suffice. The debate remains unresolved and turns on what counts as the input and what is acquired.

**Transformations and derivations versus constraints.** Some frameworks derive sentences stepwise, others state well-formedness conditions on single representations. Many empirical phenomena can be handled either way, which makes comparing frameworks hard.

**Compositionality.** The principle that the meaning of a whole is determined by its parts and their mode of combination is central, but idioms, coercion and context-dependence put pressure on strict versions.

**Semantics versus pragmatics.** How much of apparent meaning belongs to the grammar and how much to inference is disputed, for example in scalar implicatures such as "some" meaning "not all".

## Methods

Syntacticians use acceptability judgments, increasingly with controlled formal experiments, corpora, elicitation in fieldwork and cross-linguistic comparison. Semanticists test entailment, contradiction and truth-value judgments, and use formal modelling. Psycholinguistic methods such as self-paced reading, eye-tracking and event-related potentials test processing. Computational parsing and treebanks, such as the Penn Treebank and the Universal Dependencies project, support data-driven work.

## Connections to other disciplines

Philosophy of language and logic provide the foundations of formal semantics. Psychology examines how sentences are parsed and understood in real time, and how children acquire syntax. Computer science uses formal grammars and parsing. Chomsky's papers of 1956 and 1959 on formal grammars link linguistics to automata theory through what is now called the Chomsky hierarchy. Large language models raise new questions about whether statistical learning can reproduce syntactic competence, and the answers are contested. Anthropology and typology supply cross-linguistic data.

## Cautions

Syntactic trees are analyses, not observations, and competing trees can represent the same facts. Formal semantics models meaning, which does not mean that people literally compute logical formulas. Chomsky's work is not the whole of syntax, and generative grammar is not a single theory but a family. Grammatical sentences can be meaningless, as in Chomsky's "colorless green ideas sleep furiously", which was meant to show that grammaticality is distinct from sense. Finally, classical school terms such as "subject" and "object" do not carry over uniformly across languages.
