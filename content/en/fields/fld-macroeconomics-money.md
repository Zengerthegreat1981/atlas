# Macroeconomics, Money and Finance

## What the field asks

Macroeconomics studies the economy as a whole: total output and its growth, employment and unemployment, the general price level, interest rates, exchange rates, public and private debt, and the banking and financial system. It asks why output fluctuates, what determines long-run growth, what causes inflation, what money is and who creates it, why financial crises occur, and what governments and central banks can and should do about these things. The field developed as a recognisable discipline in response to the Great Depression, though its questions are far older.

## Main questions

- What determines the level of output and employment in the short run: aggregate demand, supply conditions, or both?
- Is the economy self-correcting, or can it remain at high unemployment for long periods?
- What is the source of inflation, and what is the relationship between money, wages, expectations and supply shocks?
- Is money a neutral veil over real activity, or does the monetary and credit system shape real outcomes?
- Why do financial crises recur, and how should banks be regulated?
- How should fiscal and monetary authorities be organised and constrained?

## Key schools and thinkers

**Classical and early monetary theory.** The quantity theory of money has roots in David Hume's essays and later in Irving Fisher's *The Purchasing Power of Money* (1911). Knut Wicksell's *Interest and Prices* (1898) linked the interest rate to price stability through the gap between natural and market rates. The classical presumption, often summarised as Say's law, held that supply creates its own demand in the long run.

**Keynesian economics.** John Maynard Keynes's *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) argued that effective demand determines employment, that investment is volatile because of uncertainty, and that wages and prices need not adjust to restore full employment. John Hicks's IS-LM interpretation (1937) and Paul Samuelson's textbook synthesis shaped the postwar mainstream. The Phillips curve (A. W. Phillips, 1958) became central to policy discussion.

**Monetarism.** Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, in *A Monetary History of the United States, 1867-1960* (1963), argued that monetary contraction was central to the Great Depression, and Friedman's 1968 presidential address to the American Economic Association stressed the natural rate of unemployment and expectations. Monetarism emphasised rules over discretion.

**New classical and new Keynesian macroeconomics.** Robert Lucas's critique (1976) challenged policy evaluation based on stable historical relationships; real business cycle models (Finn Kydland and Edward Prescott, 1982) explained fluctuations by technology shocks; New Keynesians introduced sticky prices and imperfect competition into microfounded models. The dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) framework became standard in central banks, though criticised after 2008.

**Post-Keynesian and Minskyan approaches.** Joan Robinson, Nicholas Kaldor, Paul Davidson and Hyman Minsky stressed fundamental uncertainty, endogenous money and financial fragility. Minsky's financial instability hypothesis, set out in *Stabilizing an Unstable Economy* (1986), holds that stable periods encourage risk-taking that undermines stability. Modern Monetary Theory, associated with Warren Mosler, Randall Wray and Stephanie Kelton, builds on chartalist ideas (Georg Friedrich Knapp, 1905) and sector balances, and is contested among economists.

**Austrian and heterodox perspectives.** Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek offered a credit-cycle theory based on malinvestment. Marxian approaches, including the tradition on profit-rate dynamics and the monetary circuit school, offer alternatives.

**Money and banking history.** Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873) set out the lender-of-last-resort doctrine. Charles Kindleberger's *Manias, Panics and Crashes* (1978) and Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff's *This Time Is Different* (2009) offer historical surveys of crises. Ben Bernanke's research on credit and the Depression, and Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig's bank-run model (1983), are central to the modern literature.

## Major debates and standard objections

*Rules versus discretion.* Should central banks follow rules such as inflation targeting, or exercise judgement? *Fiscal policy.* Do multipliers depend on slack, interest rates and the exchange-rate regime? Do government deficits crowd out private spending? *Inflation.* Is it driven mainly by expectations and money growth, by supply shocks, or by conflict over distribution? The post-2021 inflation episode revived this dispute, and economists differ on the relative weight of demand, supply and policy. *Endogenous money.* Central banks and textbooks now widely acknowledge that bank lending creates deposits, which some argue undermines the older money-multiplier account; the policy implications remain debated. *Microfoundations.* Critics argue that representative-agent models hide distribution, finance and heterogeneity; defenders point to the discipline of explicit models. *Austerity.* The evidence on fiscal consolidation after 2010 was contested, including the Reinhart-Rogoff debt-growth findings, whose spreadsheet errors were identified in 2013 though the broader dispute continued.

## Evidence and methods

Macroeconomists use national accounts, price indices, labour-force surveys, central bank balance sheets and long historical series. Methods include vector autoregressions, structural models, calibration, local projections, narrative identification of policy shocks (Christina and David Romer) and cross-country panel regressions. Macroeconomic data are few relative to the questions: one cannot rerun a recession. Identification is hard, models are sensitive to assumptions, and measurement (output gaps, natural rates) is revised.

## Connections to other disciplines

History supplies crisis episodes and the gold-standard, Bretton Woods and floating-rate regimes. Law shapes central bank mandates, bankruptcy and bank regulation. Sociology and anthropology study money as a social relation (Georg Simmel, *The Philosophy of Money*, 1900; David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, 2011, a work disputed by some historians and economists). Philosophy contributes to the study of uncertainty and causation. Political science examines central bank independence and electoral cycles.

## Cautions

Keep three layers apart. A model's result (for example, that rate rises reduce output in a given framework) is not the same as an empirical finding about a particular episode, and neither determines whether the trade-off between inflation and unemployment is acceptable, which is a political and distributional judgement. Terms such as "money printing", "national debt" and "balanced budget" are used loosely in public debate: a government with its own currency differs from a household or from a member of a monetary union, but that does not mean constraints are absent. Schools often talk past one another because they define money, equilibrium or full employment differently. Treat claims of settled consensus with care, and note that the mainstream itself shifted several times in the twentieth century.
