# Religious Studies

## What the field is

Religious studies is the academic, non-confessional study of religion. It asks what religious beliefs, practices, texts, institutions and experiences are, how they arise and change, how they relate to the societies and individuals that carry them, and how they can be compared. It is practised in departments of religion, theology faculties, area-studies programmes, anthropology, sociology, history and classics, and it draws its methods from all of these. The field is sometimes called the "science of religion" (the older German term *Religionswissenschaft*, associated with Friedrich Max Müller's *Introduction to the Science of Religion*, 1873), "comparative religion", or the "history of religions".

The central discipline-defining commitment is methodological rather than doctrinal. A scholar of religion does not ask whether a tradition's claims are true as a condition of study. Scholars may be believers, sceptics or neither, but the arguments they offer in scholarly work are meant to be assessable by anyone, using shared evidence and standards, regardless of faith.

## What religious studies is and is not

**Versus theology.** Theology, in the classical sense, reasons from within a tradition about its own sources and doctrines, often for the benefit of a community: Christian theology, Islamic *kalam* and *fiqh*, Jewish halakhic and philosophical thought, Buddhist scholastics. Religious studies treats such theology as one of its objects of study and, in many universities, the two coexist in the same faculty. The line between them is a matter of the aim and warrant of an argument, not of the topic. A historical study of how early Christians understood Jesus is religious studies; a constructive argument about what Christians ought to believe is theology.

**Versus philosophy of religion.** Philosophy of religion evaluates arguments about God, evil, religious language, miracles, faith and reason, using the tools of analytic or continental philosophy. It is close to religious studies and often taught alongside it, but it is chiefly normative and conceptual, while religious studies is chiefly descriptive, historical and explanatory. The atlas keeps a separate Philosophy module for the former and cross-links where useful.

**Versus comparative apologetics and polemics.** Defending one tradition against others, or attacking religion in general, is not scholarship in this sense. The atlas aims to be even-handed and respectful across traditions and does not adjudicate truth claims.

**Three registers.** This module distinguishes three things that are easily confused. *Describing* religions: reporting what people believe and do, and what texts say. A tradition's *self-understanding*: how its adherents and authorities explain and justify their own practices, which is itself data. And *scholarly explanation*: accounts that use concepts and theories from history, sociology, psychology or cognitive science, which may be accepted, resisted or ignored by the believers described. Much controversy in the field comes from sliding between these registers.

## Main approaches

- **Historical and philological.** Establishing texts, dates, contexts and transmission; the basis of work on scriptures and institutions. Biblical criticism, Indology, Sinology and Islamic studies all rest heavily on it.
- **Phenomenological and comparative.** Associated with Rudolf Otto (*The Idea of the Holy*, 1917), Gerardus van der Leeuw and Mircea Eliade (*The Sacred and the Profane*, German 1957, English 1959). It seeks to describe religious experience and symbols on their own terms and to compare patterns across cultures. It has been criticised for universalising, for hidden theological assumptions, and for weak historical grounding.
- **Sociological.** Émile Durkheim (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912), Max Weber (*The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, 1904-05, and the sociology of religion in *Economy and Society*) and Ernst Troeltsch examined religion's social functions, forms of organisation and relation to economic and political life.
- **Anthropological.** Edward Burnett Tylor (*Primitive Culture*, 1871), E. E. Evans-Pritchard (*Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic among the Azande*, 1937), Victor Turner, Mary Douglas (*Purity and Danger*, 1966) and Clifford Geertz ("Religion as a Cultural System", 1966) studied ritual, symbol, belief and meaning through fieldwork and cultural interpretation.
- **Psychological and cognitive.** William James (*The Varieties of Religious Experience*, 1902), Sigmund Freud (*The Future of an Illusion*, 1927), Carl Jung, and more recently the cognitive science of religion (for example Pascal Boyer, *Religion Explained*, 2001) study mental processes that bear on religious thought and behaviour.
- **Critical and postcolonial.** Work by Talal Asad (*Genealogies of Religion*, 1993), Jonathan Z. Smith, Edward Said (*Orientalism*, 1978) and others asks how the category "religion" itself was shaped by European Christian and colonial history, and how scholars' categories affect what they see.

## Traditions covered

The atlas aims at breadth, while acknowledging that coverage is uneven. Planned and present areas include:

- **Judaism and Biblical studies**, including the historical-critical study of the Hebrew Bible and New Testament (source, form and redaction criticism; the Documentary Hypothesis and its critics; the quest for the historical Jesus) and the study of rabbinic literature.
- **Christianity**, in its Eastern, Catholic, Protestant and global forms, including early church history and modern movements.
- **Islamic studies**, including the Qur'an and its transmission, hadith and its scholarly assessment (Ignaz Goldziher and later critics and defenders), law, theology, Sufism, Sunni and Shi'i traditions, and modern reform and politics.
- **South Asian traditions**: Vedic and Hindu traditions, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism, with their philosophical schools and ritual practice.
- **East Asian traditions**: Confucianism, Daoism, Chinese and Japanese Buddhism, Shinto, and "popular religion".
- **African and Indigenous traditions**: oral traditions, ritual, ancestors and cosmologies, and the effects of colonialism and missions, including work by Indigenous scholars such as Vine Deloria Jr.
- **Other and newer traditions**: Zoroastrianism, Baha'i, ancient Mediterranean religions, new religious movements, and secular worldviews.

## Debates and standard objections

What is "religion"? Definitions range from substantive (belief in superhuman beings) to functional (what gives ultimate meaning or social cohesion), and some, such as Asad and Smith, argue the category is a modern construction. Can an outsider understand a tradition, or does explanation inevitably "reduce" it? Is a comparative approach illuminating or distorting? Has secularisation been the modern norm, or has it been overstated, a debate associated with Peter Berger's change of mind and with the work of José Casanova and others?

## Methods

Textual criticism, archaeology, ethnography, interviews and surveys, comparison, cognitive experiments, and theoretical critique. No single method defines the field; good work states what its evidence can and cannot show.

## Connections to other disciplines

Religious studies is deeply interdisciplinary: anthropology for ritual and belief, sociology for institutions and change, psychology and cognitive science for experience and cognition, philosophy for concepts and arguments, history and classics for sources, and law and political science for church-state relations, religious freedom and conflict.

## Common misunderstandings

Religious studies is not the same as theology, nor an attack on faith, nor a neutral zone free of assumptions. Traditions are not monoliths; texts do not determine practice automatically; and "world religions" is a convenient but contested framing.

## Limits of this version

Version 1 is a map, not a textbook. Coverage is uneven, with Abrahamic and some South Asian traditions better represented than African, Indigenous and Oceanic ones. Many topics are only introduced, and scholarly disputes are summarised rather than settled. Claims that could not be cleanly sourced are omitted.
