# Social Stratification and Inequality

Social stratification is the study of how societies rank people into unequal positions and how advantages such as income, wealth, power, prestige, education and health are distributed across those positions and passed between generations. It is one of the central fields of sociology, since inequality is both a major outcome that sociologists seek to explain and a cause of many other social patterns.

## What the field asks

Its main questions are: What dimensions of inequality exist, and how do they relate? How are inequalities produced, justified and reproduced? Are they inevitable, functional or avoidable? How much movement is there between positions within a lifetime (intragenerational mobility) and across generations? How do class, status, race, ethnicity, gender, age, caste, religion, region and migration status combine? How do institutions, such as schools, labour markets, states and families, create or reduce inequality? How do people perceive and legitimate their own positions?

## Key concepts

- **Class** usually refers to positions defined by relations to the economy: ownership of property, occupation, employment relations.
- **Status** refers to social honour and prestige, which may follow or diverge from economic position.
- **Power** is the capacity to impose one's will, including through politics and organisations.
- **Social mobility** is movement between positions; **social closure** (Max Weber; Frank Parkin, *Marxism and Class Theory*, 1979) describes how groups restrict access to resources.
- **Ascription and achievement** distinguish positions assigned by birth from those earned.
- **Cumulative advantage** and **opportunity hoarding** (Charles Tilly, *Durable Inequality*, 1998) name processes by which advantages are reproduced.

## Main theories

**Marxian approaches** locate the root of class division in the relation to the means of production, as analysed in Marx's *Capital* (1867). Later neo-Marxists, notably Erik Olin Wright (*Classes*, 1985), reworked the scheme to account for managers and professionals, using the concept of exploitation.

**Weberian approaches** distinguish class, status group and party as separate dimensions of stratification (in *Economy and Society*). John Goldthorpe and Robert Erikson built a widely used class schema on employment relations, applied in comparative mobility studies such as *The Constant Flux* (1992).

**Functionalist theory.** Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore ("Some Principles of Stratification", 1945) argued that unequal rewards attract talented people to important positions. Melvin Tumin (1953) and many others replied that the argument assumes what it must prove, ignores inherited advantage and fails to define functional importance independently of reward.

**Cultural and relational approaches.** Pierre Bourdieu (*Distinction*, 1979) described economic, cultural and social capital, and showed how taste and education convert into advantage. Thorstein Veblen (*The Theory of the Leisure Class*, 1899) had earlier analysed status display. Tilly emphasised categorical pairs, such as insider and outsider, that organise inequality.

**Human-capital and economic perspectives.** Economists emphasise education, skills and returns to them, and, more recently, the distribution of capital and labour income (Thomas Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*, French original 2013, English translation 2014). Sociologists often reply that institutions, discrimination and social networks shape the supposedly market-driven outcomes.

## Race, ethnicity, gender and intersectionality

Race and ethnicity shape life chances through slavery and colonial legacies, segregation, discrimination and unequal institutions. W. E. B. Du Bois (*The Philadelphia Negro*, 1899; *Black Reconstruction in America*, 1935) analysed the "colour line" and the economic roots of racial hierarchy. Gunnar Myrdal's *An American Dilemma* (1944) and William Julius Wilson's *The Truly Disadvantaged* (1987) became reference points in a continuing debate about the relative weight of race and class. Douglas Massey and Nancy Denton (*American Apartheid*, 1993) documented residential segregation, and Michael Omi and Howard Winant (*Racial Formation in the United States*, 1986) argued that racial categories are socially and politically constructed.

Gender stratification covers the division of paid and unpaid labour, occupational segregation and pay gaps. Key contributions include Arlie Hochschild's *The Second Shift* (1989), Joan Acker's analysis of gendered organisations (1990) and Cecilia Ridgeway's *Framed by Gender* (2011).

**Intersectionality**, a term introduced by Kimberlé Crenshaw (1989) and developed by Patricia Hill Collins (*Black Feminist Thought*, 1990), holds that systems of inequality interact, so that people experience combined and not simply additive disadvantages. Researchers debate how to operationalise it empirically and whether it is best treated as a theory, a method or a heuristic.

Other systems of ranking matter in many societies: caste in South Asia, studied by B. R. Ambedkar (*Annihilation of Caste*, 1936) and, from a very different standpoint, Louis Dumont (*Homo Hierarchicus*, 1966); religion and sectarian divisions; and the position of migrants and Indigenous peoples. Global stratification, including the relations of core and periphery (Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems theory, 1974; dependency theory), extends the analysis between countries.

## Measurement

Researchers measure inequality of income, wealth, occupation, education, health and political voice. Common tools include occupational prestige and socioeconomic indices, class schemas, the Gini coefficient and percentile ratios, and mobility tables with indices of relative mobility. Measurement raises problems of comparability across countries, undercounting at the top and bottom of distributions, treatment of households versus individuals and unpaid work, and the difference between inequality of outcome and of opportunity. Blau and Duncan's *The American Occupational Structure* (1967) introduced path models of status attainment and shaped the quantitative study of mobility.

## Major debates and objections

- **Is class still important?** Some argue that class has been displaced by individualisation or identity, while others (for example Bourdieu, Goldthorpe and Wright in different ways) argue that class remains a strong predictor of life chances.
- **Structure versus culture.** Explanations of persistent poverty differ over the roles of labour-market structure, discrimination, neighbourhood and cultural adaptation; the discussion has often been politicised and requires care.
- **Meritocracy.** Critics argue that the idea of rewards earned by talent hides inherited advantage; defenders note real gains in opportunity.
- **Opportunity versus outcome.** Normative disagreement about which inequalities are unjust overlaps with empirical disputes.
- **Trends.** Sociologists and economists debate how far education expansion has or has not equalised opportunities and how inequality has changed over recent decades; claims should be checked against specific datasets and periods.

## Connections to other disciplines

The field draws on economics (income distribution, labour markets), political science and political philosophy (redistribution, justice), history (long-run inequality), anthropology (kinship, hierarchy, caste), psychology (stereotypes and perceived status), geography (segregation) and law (discrimination and property).

## Common misunderstandings

Stratification does not mean only income, nor only individual merit. Social mobility depends on the structure of available positions as well as on individual effort. Describing group differences does not imply that groups are inherently different, and intersectionality is not simply a list of identities. Finally, different societies stratify in different ways, so conclusions from one national context should not be assumed to apply elsewhere.
