# Epistemology

Epistemology, from the Greek *episteme* ("knowledge"), is the philosophical study of knowledge, belief and justification. It asks what knowledge is, how we acquire it, what its sources and limits are, and whether and how we can be certain of anything. Because every inquiry makes claims to know, epistemology is a foundational branch, closely tied to logic, philosophy of science and philosophy of mind.

## What the branch asks

Four questions organise the field. First, **what is knowledge**, and how does it differ from mere true belief or luck? Second, **what is justification**: what makes a belief rationally held? Third, **what are the sources** of knowledge: perception, reason, memory, testimony, introspection? Fourth, **what are the limits**: can we know about the external world, other minds, the past, the future, or morality, and can we answer the skeptic?

## The analysis of knowledge

A traditional proposal, often traced to Plato's *Theaetetus* and *Meno*, holds that knowledge is **justified true belief**: to know that p, one must believe p, p must be true, and one's belief must be justified. In 1963, Edmund Gettier published a three-page paper presenting cases in which someone has a justified true belief that is true only by luck, and so seems not to know. A well-known earlier anticipation, often discussed alongside Gettier cases, was framed by Bertrand Russell in 1948 (*Human Knowledge*): a person looks at a stopped clock that happens to show the correct time. Gettier's own published counterexamples involved inferences derived from justified false premises (such as beliefs about job appointments and coins, or ownership of a Ford). After Gettier, philosophers proposed fourth conditions (no false lemmas, defeasibility, a causal connection), reliability analyses, and virtue-theoretic accounts, such as those of Ernest Sosa and Linda Zagzebski, on which knowledge is true belief arising from intellectual competence. Timothy Williamson, in *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000), argued that knowledge is a basic mental state that should not be analysed in terms of belief plus further conditions. No analysis has won general agreement.

## Rationalism and empiricism

Early modern debate set **rationalists**, such as Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz, who held that reason can yield substantive knowledge independently of experience and that some ideas are innate, against **empiricists**, such as Locke, Berkeley and Hume, who held that all content derives from experience. Locke's *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689) argued the mind begins as a blank slate. Hume's *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-1740) pressed skeptical consequences about causation and induction. Immanuel Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) proposed a synthesis: experience supplies content, but the mind contributes structuring concepts and forms of intuition, making synthetic *a priori* knowledge possible for the world as it appears to us.

The distinctions between *a priori* and *a posteriori* knowledge, and between analytic and synthetic truths, remain central. W. V. O. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) criticised the analytic-synthetic distinction, and Saul Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1980) separated the epistemic category of *a priori* from the metaphysical category of necessity.

## Skepticism

Skeptical arguments claim that we lack knowledge in some domain. Ancient Pyrrhonists, recorded by Sextus Empiricus, recommended suspending judgement, while the Academic skeptics argued that certainty is unattainable. Descartes's *Meditations* (1641) made the dream argument and the evil demon hypothesis central, though he used them to find a secure foundation. Modern versions invoke the brain-in-a-vat scenario. Responses include Moorean appeals to common sense, contextualism (the standards for "knows" shift with context), relevant-alternatives theories, and externalist replies. Whether skepticism is a serious threat or a puzzle about how our concepts work is itself debated. Many traditions have their own skeptical or sceptic-adjacent strands, such as the Indian Madhyamaka and Carvaka critiques and the Zhuangzi's perspectivalism.

## Structure and sources of justification

**Foundationalism** holds that justified beliefs rest on basic beliefs that need no inferential support, as in Descartes and, in different forms, in classical empiricism. **Coherentism**, associated with Laurence BonJour in some writings, holds that beliefs are justified by fitting into a coherent system. **Infinitism** accepts endless chains of reasons. This relates to the **regress problem** on which every reason seems to need another reason.

**Internalism** says that what justifies a belief must be accessible to the believer's reflection. **Externalism** allows factors outside awareness to matter, such as the reliability of the belief-forming process, as in Alvin Goldman's reliabilism. Related positions include evidentialism (Richard Feldman and Earl Conee) and proper functionalism (Alvin Plantinga). Arguments from the "new evil demon" and from clairvoyance cases are standard objections on each side.

## Social and formal epistemology

Much knowledge comes from others, so **social epistemology** studies testimony, expertise, disagreement and group knowledge, with early influences in the work of Goldman and Steve Fuller. The epistemology of **peer disagreement** asks how to respond when equally competent people disagree. Miranda Fricker's *Epistemic Injustice* (2007) examines how prejudice can wrong people as knowers. **Formal epistemology** uses probability, notably Bayesian models of belief and evidence, and epistemic logic. Feminist, decolonial and virtue epistemologies explore the role of social position, power and character in inquiry.

## Connections

Epistemology intersects with philosophy of science (confirmation, evidence), philosophy of mind (perception, belief), logic (inference), metaphysics (truth, realism), and ethics (moral knowledge, epistemic duties). Cognitive science studies how belief formation actually works, and some philosophers pursue a naturalised epistemology, following Quine, that treats it as continuous with empirical psychology.

## Common misunderstandings

"Knowledge" does not mean certainty: most epistemologists accept fallibilism, on which we can know even though we could be wrong. Skeptical arguments are usually presented as challenges to be answered, not as endorsements. Epistemology is not simply a study of science, since it covers everyday knowledge, testimony, memory and moral or mathematical knowledge. And Gettier did not show that justified true belief is irrelevant; it showed that something more or different is needed.
