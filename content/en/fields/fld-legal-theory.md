# Legal Theory

## What legal theory is

Legal theory, often called jurisprudence, is the systematic study of the nature of law and of the reasoning, institutions and values associated with it. Where a lawyer asks what the law requires in a particular case, a legal theorist asks what makes something law at all, how law differs from other normative orders such as morality, etiquette or religion, why law claims authority over those it governs, and whether and when it deserves obedience. The field sits at the meeting point of philosophy, political theory, sociology, economics, history and the comparative study of legal systems.

Three kinds of question recur. Conceptual questions ask what law is and what it has in common across very different societies. Normative questions ask what law ought to be: what justifies coercion, what rights people hold, and what a just legal order would look like. Empirical and interpretive questions ask how law actually works, how judges decide, how legal rules shape behaviour, and how legal meaning is produced and contested. Different traditions weight these differently, and much of the history of the field is a dispute about which question comes first.

## Natural law

Natural law theories hold, in various forms, that law is connected to morality in a way that bears on its validity or its authority. Classical and medieval versions, associated with Aristotle, the Stoics, Cicero and above all Thomas Aquinas (Summa Theologiae, c. 1265-1274), treat law as an ordinance of reason directed to the common good. Early modern writers such as Hugo Grotius (On the Law of War and Peace, 1625) and John Locke (Two Treatises of Government, 1689) recast the tradition in terms of natural rights. In the twentieth century Lon Fuller (The Morality of Law, 1964) argued that law has an internal morality of procedural principles, and John Finnis (Natural Law and Natural Rights, 1980) developed a contemporary account built on basic human goods. Natural law is not a single doctrine: some versions claim that grossly unjust enactments are not law, others only that law is justified by its moral function.

## Legal positivism

Positivism holds that what counts as law depends on social facts, such as enactment or recognition by officials, rather than on moral merit. Jeremy Bentham (Of Laws in General, written c. 1782, published 1970) and John Austin (The Province of Jurisprudence Determined, 1832) offered command theories. H. L. A. Hart (The Concept of Law, 1961) replaced the command model with a union of primary rules of obligation and secondary rules of recognition, change and adjudication, and emphasised the internal point of view of those who accept rules. Hans Kelsen (Pure Theory of Law, 1934; 2nd ed. 1960) built a norm-based science of law resting on a basic norm. Joseph Raz (The Authority of Law, 1979) developed the sources thesis and a service conception of authority. Inclusive and exclusive positivists disagree about whether a legal system may incorporate moral criteria of validity.

The Hart-Fuller debate (1958) and the later Hart-Dworkin debate frame the standard clash. Ronald Dworkin (Taking Rights Seriously, 1977; Law's Empire, 1986) argued that law includes principles and that judges interpret the law in its best moral light, so that law and morality are not separable in the way positivists suppose.

## Legal realism and its heirs

American realists such as Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. ("The Path of the Law", 1897), Karl Llewellyn (The Bramble Bush, 1930) and Jerome Frank (Law and the Modern Mind, 1930) questioned whether legal rules determine outcomes and urged attention to what courts actually do. Scandinavian realists such as Alf Ross (On Law and Justice, 1953) took a different, more philosophical route. Realism's descendants include empirical legal studies and the behavioural study of judicial decisions.

## Critical, feminist and race-conscious approaches

Critical Legal Studies, which emerged in the United States in the late 1970s, argued that legal doctrine is indeterminate and tends to legitimate existing hierarchies; Roberto Unger and Duncan Kennedy are prominent figures. Feminist jurisprudence, represented by Catharine MacKinnon (Toward a Feminist Theory of the State, 1989) and Carol Gilligan's influence on legal debate, among others, examines how law constructs and entrenches gender. Critical race theory, associated with Derrick Bell and Kimberlé Crenshaw, studies the relationship between law and racial hierarchy. These approaches often combine internal critique of doctrine with broader social theory.

## Law and economics

Law and economics applies economic analysis to legal rules. Ronald Coase ("The Problem of Social Cost", 1960), Guido Calabresi (The Costs of Accidents, 1970) and Richard Posner (Economic Analysis of Law, 1973) are central. The field asks how rules affect incentives and whether common law tends towards efficiency, and it faces objections about the normative weight given to efficiency, about distribution, and about idealised assumptions of rational behaviour.

## Sociology of law and legal history

Sociological approaches treat law as a social phenomenon. Emile Durkheim (The Division of Labour in Society, 1893), Max Weber (Economy and Society, posthumous, 1922), Eugen Ehrlich (Fundamental Principles of the Sociology of Law, 1913) and later Niklas Luhmann (Law as a Social System, 1993) each give distinct accounts of how law relates to social order, rationalisation and legitimacy. Legal history and legal anthropology supply the comparative material against which general theories are tested.

## Comparative and Islamic legal thought

Comparative law studies the similarities and differences among legal systems and traditions, such as civil law, common law and religious legal systems. Islamic legal thought has a sophisticated theoretical literature of its own. Usul al-fiqh, the principles of jurisprudence, addresses sources, interpretation and the derivation of rulings; its early systematic statement is usually associated with al-Shafi'i's Risala, and later major works include al-Ghazali's al-Mustasfa and al-Shatibi's al-Muwafaqat on the objectives of the law. The Sunni and Shi'i legal schools (madhahib) differ in methods and conclusions, and modern reform movements debate their authority and adaptation. The atlas treats this literature as legal theory in its own right and in dialogue with the other traditions, without presuming that one framework is the measure of the others.

## How the atlas is organised

This module is organised in fields. This root field maps the whole domain and offers reading paths. Companion fields cover rights, justice and punishment; legal reasoning and interpretation; and comparative and Islamic legal thought. Within them, entries on schools, thinkers, works and concepts are cross-linked, so that a reader can move from, for example, the Hart-Dworkin debate to the theory of rules, to interpretation, to the philosophy of authority. Neighbouring modules on philosophy, political economy, sociology and religious studies provide context and are linked wherever the arguments overlap.

## Methods

Legal theorists use conceptual analysis, argument from hard cases, interpretive reconstruction, genealogical and historical work, comparative study, formal modelling and empirical research. Disagreement about method is itself a theoretical question: whether a theory of law should be descriptive, evaluative or both is contested.

## Limits of version 1 and cautions

This first version is selective rather than exhaustive. It emphasises Anglophone and continental European debates alongside Islamic legal thought, and it under-represents other traditions, including Chinese, Indian, African, Latin American and indigenous legal thought, as well as international law and many specialised areas. Dates given for works are approximate where the publication history is complex.

Legal theory is not legal advice. It describes and evaluates ideas about law; it does not tell anyone what the law is in a specific jurisdiction or how to resolve a particular dispute. Law differs between jurisdictions and changes over time, so anyone facing a legal question should consult a qualified practitioner in the relevant place.
