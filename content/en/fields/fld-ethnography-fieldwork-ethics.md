# Ethnography, Fieldwork and Ethics

Ethnography is the characteristic method of cultural and social anthropology: the in-depth, firsthand study of a community through living among its members, learning its language, observing and taking part in everyday life, and writing the results up. The word names both the activity and the written product. This field introduction follows the method from its consolidation in the early twentieth century through the critiques of authority, representation and colonial complicity that reshaped it.

## Overview

Fieldwork before the twentieth century was often carried out by travellers, missionaries and officials, and then analysed by scholars at home. Franz Boas insisted on learning languages and recording texts in the field, and his methods are described in the Atlas entry on his ethnographic practice. Bronislaw Malinowski's stay in the Trobriand Islands, reported in *Argonauts of the Western Pacific*, became the model of extended participant observation, with its stated aim of grasping the native's point of view. By mid-century, sustained residence, vernacular language competence and attention to the whole of social life were expected of professional anthropologists.

## Key themes

**Participant observation** combines taking part with watching and asking. **Thick description**, a term Clifford Geertz borrowed from the philosopher Gilbert Ryle, calls for ethnography to interpret layers of meaning rather than merely record behaviour; his essay on the Balinese cockfight is the best-known illustration. **Informants and interlocutors** are the people whose knowledge makes the work possible, and the vocabulary reflects shifting ideas about their role. **Fieldnotes**, interviews, genealogies, mapping and archival work supply the data. **Multi-sited ethnography**, advocated by George Marcus, follows people, objects and ideas across places rather than confining study to one village.

## Reflexivity and the crisis of representation

From the 1970s and 1980s, scholars asked who writes ethnography and with what authority. James Clifford and others in the volume *Writing Culture* examined the rhetorical and literary devices through which ethnographic texts persuade readers. Paul Rabinow, Lila Abu-Lughod, Donna Haraway and others emphasised that the observer's gender, nationality and institutional position shape what can be seen and said. The Atlas covers this under the school on the crisis of representation and the debate on positionality and reflexivity.

## Ethics and colonial legacies

Anthropology developed in part under colonial conditions, and critics such as Talal Asad argued that the discipline's access and its categories were bound up with imperial power. Ethical concerns include informed consent, protection of informants from harm, ownership of knowledge, and the use of research by states and militaries. The American Anthropological Association adopted a statement on ethics in 1971 and has revised its professional codes since, while in the United States research with human participants is also overseen by institutional review boards, whose framework grew out of the Belmont Report (1979); anthropologists have often argued that review models designed for biomedical research fit ethnography poorly. Controversy over the work of Napoleon Chagnon among the Yanomami, and the debate over anthropology and militarisation, show how disputed these issues can be. Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies* and related work on Indigenous research and data sovereignty argue that communities should direct research about them, not merely be studied.

## Debates

Positionality and reflexivity: does acknowledging one's position improve ethnography or turn it inward? Insider versus outsider research. Whether ethnographic knowledge can ever be separated from the relations of power in which it is produced.
