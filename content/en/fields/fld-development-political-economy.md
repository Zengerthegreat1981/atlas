# Development and the Global Economy

## What the field asks

Development political economy asks why countries differ so greatly in income and living standards, how poorer societies might raise them, and how the structure of the world economy, including trade, finance, empire, aid and migration, affects those prospects. It combines economics, economic history, political science, sociology and anthropology. It is also normative: "development" can mean income growth, structural transformation, expanded human capabilities or political and social change, and the choice among definitions matters.

## Main questions

- What causes sustained growth, and why did it begin in particular places and times?
- Is development a sequence of stages, a structural transformation, or the outcome of institutions and politics?
- Does integration into the world economy help or hinder poorer countries?
- What is the role of the state, and when does industrial policy succeed or fail?
- Does foreign aid work, and under what conditions?
- Why do natural-resource exporters often perform poorly?
- What distinctive features shape the political economy of the Middle East and North Africa (MENA)?

## Schools and thinkers

**Early and structural theories.** Friedrich List's *The National System of Political Economy* (1841) defended protection for infant industries. Walt Rostow's *The Stages of Economic Growth* (1960) proposed a stage model. W. Arthur Lewis's 1954 dual-sector model described labour moving from traditional agriculture to modern industry. Paul Rosenstein-Rodan, Albert Hirschman (*The Strategy of Economic Development*, 1958) and Ragnar Nurkse analysed balanced and unbalanced growth and coordination problems. Alexander Gerschenkron's *Economic Backwardness in Historical Perspective* (1962) argued that latecomers use different institutions, such as banks and the state, to catch up.

**Structuralism and dependency.** Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer argued that the terms of trade for primary exports tend to deteriorate, supporting import-substituting industrialisation. Dependency theorists, including Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto (*Dependency and Development in Latin America*, Spanish original 1969, English edition 1979), André Gunder Frank, and world-systems analysis (Immanuel Wallerstein), stressed centre-periphery structures. Cardoso and Faletto emphasised that dependency allowed varied outcomes, not stagnation alone.

**Neoclassical and market-oriented views.** Solow's growth model (1956) separates capital accumulation from technical progress and yields convergence only conditional on shared parameters such as saving and population growth, not an unconditional catch-up of poor countries. Critics of import substitution, including Anne Krueger on rent-seeking and Jagdish Bhagwati and I. M. D. Little, argued for openness. The "Washington Consensus", a label John Williamson used in 1989 for a list of reforms he associated with Washington institutions, summarised a market-oriented package. What the list contained, and how far those institutions applied it, is contested.

**Developmental-state literature.** Chalmers Johnson's *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* (1982), Alice Amsden's *Asia's Next Giant* (1989) and Robert Wade's *Governing the Market* (1990) argued that state guidance of credit and trade contributed to East Asian growth. Opponents, including the World Bank's *The East Asian Miracle* (1993), gave greater weight to macroeconomic stability and human capital, with partial acknowledgement of selective interventions.

**Institutions and history.** Douglass North's work, and Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson and James Robinson's "colonial origins" paper (2001) and Acemoglu and Robinson's *Why Nations Fail* (2012), emphasise inclusive versus extractive institutions. Critics question the settler-mortality instrument, the causal weight of institutions versus geography and human capital, and the treatment of non-Western histories. Jared Diamond's *Guns, Germs and Steel* (1997) and Kenneth Pomeranz's *The Great Divergence* (2000) offer other explanations of long-run differences.

**Capabilities and human development.** Amartya Sen's *Development as Freedom* (1999) reframes development as expansion of substantive freedoms, and the UN Development Programme's Human Development Index (from 1990) operationalises a broader measure. Dani Rodrik argues for context-specific policy and the importance of productive transformation.

**Aid and randomised evaluation.** Jeffrey Sachs argued for large-scale aid in *The End of Poverty* (2005); William Easterly (*The White Man's Burden*, 2006) and Dambisa Moyo criticised top-down aid. Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo's randomised trials (*Poor Economics*, 2011) shifted attention to small, testable interventions, drawing in turn the objection that they may not scale or address structural issues.

## Debates and standard objections

Whether trade openness causes growth, or successful countries simply open up later, is disputed; cross-country regressions face endogeneity problems. Whether industrial policy works depends on state capacity and design, and failures are as numerous as successes. The "resource curse" thesis (Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner, 1995) has been challenged for measurement and causal-identification reasons, and some researchers argue the curse operates only given weak institutions. Global-inequality debates ask whether between-country convergence since about 1990, driven by large Asian economies, offsets within-country divergence.

## The MENA case

The region's political economy is often discussed through the rentier state (Mahdavy 1970; Beblawi and Luciani 1987), the postwar "authoritarian bargain" in which states offered jobs and subsidies in exchange for political quiescence, state-led industrialisation under Arab nationalist regimes, the infitah liberalisations from the 1970s, and structural adjustment. Scholars such as Roger Owen, Samir Amin and Timothy Mitchell, among others, give different accounts of colonial legacies and oil. The extent to which these frameworks fit all economies, including resource-poor ones, and how they hold after the 2011 uprisings, remains debated.

## Evidence and methods

Researchers use cross-country growth regressions, long-run historical datasets (Angus Maddison's series and successors), instrumental variables, natural experiments, randomised controlled trials, household surveys, comparative-historical case studies and ethnography. Data quality is uneven in many poor countries, GDP is an incomplete welfare measure, and external validity is a recurring concern.

## Connections to other disciplines

History is indispensable: colonialism, slavery and empire shaped trade patterns. Sociology and anthropology study kinship, markets and local practice (James Ferguson, James Scott). Law and political science treat property rights, state capacity and democratisation. Philosophy contributes theories of justice across borders and the ethics of aid.

## Cautions

Development is not a single ladder every country climbs in order. Empirical claims that a policy "worked" are about specific places and periods, and generalising them is risky. Distinguish a theory (for instance, dependency), an empirical finding (terms-of-trade trends, which are disputed) and a political preference (for protection or openness). Avoid treating the Global South as homogeneous, and avoid reading success stories as proof of a single recipe.
