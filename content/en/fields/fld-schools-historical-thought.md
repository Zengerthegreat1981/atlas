# Schools of Historical Thought

Schools of historical thought represent the major intellectual traditions, theoretical frameworks, and interpretive paradigms that have guided the investigation of the human past. Historians do not merely collect facts; they operate within paradigms that define what constitutes a valid historical problem, which sources are authoritative, and how causation, agency, and change should be conceptualized. From classical antiquity and the medieval Islamic world to modern professional disciplines, the evolution of historical thought reflects ongoing debates over how human societies understand their own trajectories.

## Classical antiquity and early traditions

Western historiography originated in classical Greece, where writers shifted from mythological epic toward empirical inquiry. Herodotus of Halicarnassus (c. 484 – c. 425 BCE) used the word *historiē* (inquiry) as the title concept of his *Histories*, blending geography, ethnography, and oral testimonies to investigate the Persian Wars. Thucydides (c. 460 – c. 400 BCE), in his *History of the Peloponnesian War*, largely set aside divine explanation and focused on political power, state interest, and human psychology, and he is often cited for his emphasis on careful evaluation of testimony.

In the Hellenistic and Roman worlds, historians adapted these models to imperial states. Polybius (c. 200 – c. 118 BCE) formulated pragmatic universal history, explaining how Rome established Mediterranean dominance through constitutional balance and military organization. Roman historians such as Livy and Tacitus combined political narrative with moral instruction, using the past to diagnose political decline, tyranny, and civic virtue.

## The Arab-Islamic historiographical tradition

Classical Arabic and Islamic historiography developed an independent, methodologically rigorous tradition centered on documenting the Islamic community, prophetology, and universal history. Originating in the seventh and eighth centuries CE, historical recording emerged from oral poetry, tribal genealogies (*ansab*), prophetic biography (*sirah*), and military campaigns (*maghazi*).

Islamic historians developed the *isnad* (chain of transmission) and the science of transmitter criticism (*'ilm al-jarh wa-l-ta'dil*) to evaluate reports (*akhbar*). Biographical dictionaries evaluated narrators for moral probity (*'adalah*) and memory accuracy (*dabt*). This methodology enabled Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari (224–310 AH / 839–923 CE) to construct his monumental *Tarikh al-Rusul wa-l-Muluk* (History of the Prophets and Kings). Al-Tabari organized world history annalistically from Creation to the early fourth century AH, systematically recording multiple, parallel transmissions for events to preserve an evidentiary record.

In the fourteenth century CE, 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Khaldun (732–808 AH / 1332–1406 CE) transformed historical theory. In the *Muqaddimah* (779 AH / 1377 CE), Ibn Khaldun critiqued traditional chroniclers for relying solely on transmission (*naql*) without evaluating whether events were physically and sociologically possible. He developed *'ilm al-'umran* (the science of human social organization), arguing that reports must undergo internal critique (*naqd al-akhbar*) based on the laws of society. Analyzing the dynamic between nomadic life (*badawa*) and sedentary civilization (*hadara*), Ibn Khaldun identified *'asabiyyah* (group cohesion and social solidarity) as a central force governing the rise and decay of political dynasties.

## Rankean historicism and the professional turn

Modern professional history took shape in nineteenth-century Germany under Leopold von Ranke (1795–1886). Ranke helped shape professional scholarship through his university research seminar and his insistence that historians base their work on primary archival documents. He famously declared that history should not judge the past or instruct the present, but merely show "as it essentially was" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*).

Ranke's historicism (*Historismus*) held that every historical era possesses its own intrinsic value and must be judged by its own standards. However, Ranke's empiricism focused predominantly on political states, foreign policy, and elite diplomacy. The French Methodical School, codified by Charles-Victor Langlois and Charles Seignobos (1898), further formalized these documentary techniques into systematic rules of external and internal source criticism.

## Historical materialism and Marxist historiography

Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels challenged idealist and state-centric history by formulating the materialist conception of history. Historical materialism posited that the economic mode of production forms the material base conditioning social, political, and intellectual life. Class struggle between exploiting and exploited classes acts as the primary engine of historical transformation.

In the twentieth century, British Marxist historians such as E. P. Thompson (*The Making of the English Working Class*, 1963), Eric Hobsbawm, and Maurice Dobb transformed this tradition. Rejecting crude economic determinism, Thompson helped popularise "history from below," arguing that class is not an abstract structure but an active relationship forged through shared experience, culture, and resistance. This approach recovered the agency and moral economy of working-class communities.

## The Annales School: structural history and the longue durée

Founded in France in 1929 by Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre with the journal *Annales d'histoire économique et sociale*, the Annales School rebelled against traditional political-event history (*histoire événementielle*). The Annalistes advocated "total history" (*histoire totale*), integrating geography, sociology, demography, and economics into historical analysis.

Fernand Braudel (*The Mediterranean*, 1949) redefined the discipline by introducing a tripartite temporality. At its foundation lay the *longue durée*—the slow, structural time of geography, climate, and ecology—followed by medium-term socioeconomic cycles (conjunctures), and finally short-term political events. A third generation of Annalistes, including Jacques Le Goff and Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, expanded into the history of mentalities (*histoire des mentalités*), investigating collective attitudes and emotional structures of past societies.

## Microhistory and the cultural turn

During the 1970s, Italian historians including Carlo Ginzburg and Giovanni Levi developed microhistory (*microstoria*). Rather than analyzing macro-structures or statistical aggregates, microhistorians dramatically reduced the scale of observation to examine a single individual, village, or trial record.

In *The Cheese and the Worms* (1976), Ginzburg analyzed the trial dossiers of Menocchio, a sixteenth-century Friulian miller, reconstructing how popular oral culture interacted with printed texts. Microhistorians used the "exceptional normal" to illuminate the agency and worldviews of common people who left few conventional traces. Concurrently, the wider cultural turn, represented by Natalie Zemon Davis (*The Return of Martin Guerre*, 1983) and historians of emotions such as Barbara Rosenwein, analyzed rituals, symbols, and emotions as central forces in historical life.

## Subaltern, postcolonial, and feminist revisions

From the late twentieth century, postcolonial and feminist scholars challenged Eurocentric and male-dominated assumptions in historical writing:
- **Subaltern Studies:** Formed in South Asia in the 1980s under Ranajit Guha, this collective critiqued colonialist and elite nationalist narratives for depicting Indian history without peasant agency. Scholars such as Dipesh Chakrabarty (*Provincializing Europe*, 2000) interrogated how European historical models were presented as universal benchmarks for all societies.
- **Feminist and Gender History:** Historians such as Joan Wallach Scott ("Gender: A Useful Category of Historical Analysis", 1986) argued that gender is not merely a descriptive category concerning women, but a primary relationship through which power and social hierarchy are articulated.

## Global, connected, and environmental histories

Contemporary historiography increasingly questions methodological nationalism—the assumption that the nation-state is the natural container of history. Historians such as Sanjay Subrahmanyam advance connected histories, tracing trans-regional linkages, maritime networks, and cross-cultural circulations across early modern Eurasia. Simultaneously, environmental historians such as Alfred Crosby (*The Columbian Exchange*, 1972) reintegrate biological agents, pathogens, and climate into historical explanation, arguing that human events are embedded within ecological systems.

## Key debates across schools

The dialogue among these schools centers on enduring epistemological questions:
- **Structure versus agency:** The degree to which human actions are determined by material structures versus conscious choices.
- **Materialism versus culture:** Whether cultural ideas reflect economic conditions or independently shape historical reality.
- **Quantitative cliometrics versus narrative history:** The balance between econometric modeling and qualitative narrative.
- **Empirical detachment versus positionality:** Whether objective neutrality is achievable, or whether historical knowledge inevitably reflects the historian's social location.
