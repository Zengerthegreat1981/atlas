# Labour, Inequality and Distribution

## What the field asks

This field examines how the product of a society is divided among workers, owners of capital and land, and the state; how wages and employment are determined; how class and social position shape economic life; how unequal incomes and wealth are, and why; and what taxation and welfare systems do about it. It sits where economics meets sociology, history, law and moral philosophy, since "who gets what" is inseparable from questions of fairness.

## Main questions

- What determines wages and the share of income going to labour versus capital?
- Is the value of goods rooted in labour, in utility and scarcity, or in something else?
- What is class, and does it still organise politics and life chances?
- How large are inequalities of income and wealth, and what drives their changes over time?
- Does inequality harm growth, democracy or health, or is it a necessary incentive?
- What should taxation and welfare systems aim to do, and what are their limits?

## Schools and thinkers

**Classical economics.** Adam Smith's *Wealth of Nations* (1776) discussed wages, profits and rents as the three incomes, and how bargaining power influenced wages. David Ricardo's *Principles* (1817) made distribution among classes the central problem of political economy and formulated the theory of rent. Thomas Malthus's *Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798) tied wages to population dynamics, a view later weakened by demographic history. John Stuart Mill distinguished the laws of production from the more malleable arrangements of distribution.

**Marxian analysis.** Karl Marx's *Capital* (1867) distinguished labour from labour power, and explained profit through surplus value extracted in production. His account of class built on ownership of the means of production. Later work on labour processes (Harry Braverman, *Labor and Monopoly Capital*, 1974), segmented labour markets, and social reproduction (including Marxist feminist writers) extended the tradition. Critics point to the transformation problem and to the inability of the original value theory to predict prices, while defenders argue that the theory addresses social relations rather than price formation.

**Marginalist theories.** John Bates Clark's marginal-productivity theory, set out in *The Distribution of Wealth* (1899), explained factor incomes as the value of marginal products. The Cambridge capital controversies of the 1950s and 1960s (Joan Robinson, Piero Sraffa's *Production of Commodities by Means of Commodities*, 1960, versus Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow) challenged whether capital can be aggregated coherently; Samuelson conceded some of the technical points. Labour economics developed human capital theory (Gary Becker, Jacob Mincer, 1960s) and search and bargaining models, along with discrimination economics (Becker, 1957).

**Institutional and bargaining views.** Institutionalists (John R. Commons, John Dunlop), the Webbs on trade unions, and later work on minimum wages, unions and monopsony (Alan Manning, *Monopsony in Motion*, 2003; David Card and Alan Krueger's 1994 minimum-wage study) stress institutions and power in wage determination, and these findings are debated against competitive-market predictions.

**Distributive justice.** John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971), Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), Amartya Sen's work on capabilities, and G. A. Cohen's writings give differing philosophical standards for what a just distribution is. Economists generally measure inequality; philosophers argue what level or type is acceptable.

**Class analysis in sociology.** Max Weber's distinction of class, status and party, Pierre Bourdieu's forms of capital (*Distinction*, 1979) and Erik Olin Wright's *Class Counts* (1997) all treat class as more than income.

## Inequality evidence and debates

Simon Kuznets's 1955 hypothesis suggested inequality first rises then falls with development. Thomas Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013), using historical tax data with Emmanuel Saez, Gabriel Zucman and Anthony Atkinson, argued that when the return on capital exceeds growth, wealth concentration tends to rise, and that the twentieth-century compression was historically exceptional. Critics dispute the mechanism, the treatment of capital measurement, and the weight of policy versus structural forces. Branko Milanovic's work on global inequality (the "elephant curve") describes shifting income positions across the world distribution; its interpretation is also debated. Technology, trade, declining union density, tax changes and education have each been proposed as drivers of the rise in inequality in several rich countries since about 1980, and their relative weights are disputed. Measures differ: Gini coefficients, top income shares, wealth versus income, pre-tax versus post-tax; conclusions can vary with the measure and data source (surveys, tax records, national accounts).

## Taxation and welfare

Welfare states are classified in Gøsta Esping-Andersen's *The Three Worlds of Welfare Capitalism* (1990) as liberal, conservative-corporatist and social-democratic, a typology widely used and widely revised. Optimal-tax theory (James Mirrlees, 1971) analyses trade-offs between equity and incentives. Debates concern the elasticity of taxable income, wealth taxes, universal versus targeted benefits, basic income, minimum wages and the effect of the welfare state on work incentives. Evidence from experiments and natural experiments exists but findings depend heavily on context and design.

## Evidence and methods

Researchers use household surveys, administrative tax records, matched employer-employee data, randomised and natural experiments, historical series, ethnographic and qualitative studies of workplaces, and normative argument. Each has blind spots: surveys under-report top incomes, tax data reflect legal avoidance, and income data omit unpaid and household work, which feminist economists (such as Marilyn Waring and Nancy Folbre) have long highlighted.

## Connections to other disciplines

Sociology contributes class, stratification and mobility research; law contributes labour regulation, contract and property; history reveals labour regimes from slavery to industrial unionism; anthropology examines gift, reciprocity and informal work; philosophy supplies justice theories.

## Cautions

Inequality is not a single thing: inequalities of income, wealth, opportunity and outcome are different and need not move together. A finding that inequality has risen is empirical; that it is unjust or tolerable is a value judgement; that a particular tax reform would fix it is a further empirical question. Theories of "fair" pay (marginal product, labour value, bargaining) are competing explanations, not neutral descriptions. Be wary of claims that a single factor explains everything, and of reading a school's policy conclusions as necessarily following from its analytic tools.
