# Arabic Linguistics

Arabic linguistics covers both the long indigenous tradition of Arabic grammatical, rhetorical and lexical scholarship and the modern linguistic study of Arabic in its many varieties. It is one of the world's oldest continuous traditions of language analysis, and it also poses distinctive questions for general linguistics: a root-and-pattern morphology, a classical standard preserved in scripture and literature, and a situation in which spoken dialects and a written standard coexist.

## What the field asks

The tradition began with practical and religious motives: to preserve the correct reading and understanding of the Qur'an and of Arabic poetry as the language spread among non-native speakers in the early Islamic centuries. From this came questions about what counts as correct usage, how to explain case endings and word formation, how meaning is conveyed in speech and writing, and how to compile the vocabulary. Modern work asks how Arabic fits into theories of phonology, morphology and syntax, how the dialects relate to the standard, and how the language should be taught, modernised and managed.

## Main questions

- What are the principles governing word formation and inflection?
- What is the nature of grammatical case, government and sentence structure?
- Whose usage is the authority: poets, Bedouin informants, the Qur'anic readings, or analogy?
- How does eloquence arise, and what distinguishes figurative from literal expression?
- How should the lexicon be arranged and documented?
- How do spoken varieties relate to the standard, and what follows for education and planning?

## The classical grammatical tradition

Arabic grammar took shape in the eighth century CE in the garrison cities of Basra and Kufa in Iraq. Tradition credits Abu al-Aswad al-Duali with early steps, such as marking vowels in the written text, though the details are legendary and historians treat them with caution.

**Al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi** (died around 786 CE) is a founding figure. He is credited with the science of prosody (*arud*), with systematising the analysis of poetic metre, and with beginning lexicography in the *Kitab al-ʿAyn*, the first dictionary of Arabic, arranged by articulatory position of the consonants, though how much of it he completed and how much his students added is discussed by scholars.

**Sibawayh** (died around 796 CE), a student of al-Khalil of Persian origin, composed *al-Kitab*, "The Book", the earliest comprehensive surviving grammar of Arabic. It covers syntax, morphology and phonology with extensive evidence, and it set the model for later work. Its terminology, its notions of government (*ʿamal*) and its treatment of analogy shaped the tradition for a thousand years.

**Basra and Kufa.** Later accounts describe two schools: the Basrans, more committed to analogy and to restricting evidence to trusted sources, and the Kufans, more tolerant of diverse reports. Modern historians, including Kees Versteegh, caution that the sharp opposition is partly a later literary construction, and that a Baghdad school later blended the two. Major later figures include al-Mubarrad, Ibn al-Sarraj, al-Zajjaji, al-Zamakhshari, Ibn Jinni, whose *al-Khasa'is* explored the principles of grammar, and Ibn Malik, whose versified *Alfiyya* became a standard teaching text. Ibn Hisham al-Ansari wrote influential works on syntax and particles, and the Andalusian tradition also contributed.

**Features of the tradition.** It developed a theory of grammatical government and case, a distinction between roots and patterns in the *sarf* (morphology) tradition, and discussion of the sources of evidence (*simaʿ*, hearing from reliable speakers, and *qiyas*, analogy). Ibn Jinni's work on sound and word formation and Sibawayh's phonetic descriptions of the places of articulation are notable for their precision.

## Rhetoric

The study of eloquence (*balagha*) is divided into three branches: *maʿani* (how word order and structure fit the situation), *bayan* (figures such as simile, metaphor and metonymy) and *badiʿ* (embellishment). **ʿAbd al-Qahir al-Jurjani** (died 1078 or 1081 CE) is its central theorist. In *Dala'il al-iʿjaz* he developed the theory of *nazm*, arguing that eloquence lies in the arrangement of words according to the meanings of grammar and not in individual words, and in *Asrar al-balagha* he studied imagery. Later, al-Sakkaki's *Miftah al-ʿulum* systematised the field, and al-Qazwini's summary shaped the teaching tradition. Al-Jahiz's earlier *al-Bayan wa-l-tabyin* was an influential literary and rhetorical work.

## Lexicography

After al-Khalil's *ʿAyn*, Arabic dictionaries developed several organising principles: by articulation, by alphabetical order of the root's last letter, as in al-Jawhari's *al-Sihah* and Ibn Manzur's large *Lisan al-ʿArab* (thirteenth century), and by ordinary alphabetical root order. Al-Fayruzabadi's *al-Qamus al-muhit* became so well known that the word *qamus* came to mean "dictionary" in Arabic. Ibn Faris and others studied word families and meaning, and Ibn Durayd wrote on the roots. Dictionaries of the Qur'an and of dialect and foreign vocabulary also developed.

## Modern descriptive work

From the nineteenth century, European orientalists produced descriptive grammars, including Wright's translation and adaptation of Caspari's *A Grammar of the Arabic Language* (1859-62), and comparative Semitic studies. In the twentieth century, linguists applied structuralist and generative methods. Work on the root-and-pattern system, notably by John McCarthy, whose autosegmental analysis (1981) treated consonantal roots, vowel melodies and templates as separate tiers, became influential in general phonology and morphology. Syntactic research addresses verb-subject and subject-verb order, agreement asymmetries and the clause structure of Arabic, and scholars such as Abdelkader Fassi Fehri have written on it. Arab scholars such as Tammam Hassan in Egypt reread the tradition in the light of modern linguistics, and Michael Carter and others have asked how far Sibawayh's concepts correspond to modern categories. Corpus and computational work now addresses morphological analysis, which is difficult because short vowels are usually omitted from writing.

## Diglossia and dialects

Charles Ferguson's 1959 paper made Arabic a prototypical case of diglossia: a high variety, Classical and Modern Standard Arabic, used in writing, formal speech and education, and low varieties, the spoken dialects, acquired at home as first languages. The dialects differ considerably across regions, commonly grouped into Maghrebi, Egyptian, Levantine, Gulf and others, and they are not always mutually intelligible between distant areas. Scholars debate whether the situation is a clear dichotomy or a continuum with mixed levels, as Said Badawi's proposal of levels in Egypt suggests, and Alan Kaye and Clive Holes among others have discussed the matter. Dialects are the subject of dialectological surveys and of growing sociolinguistic research, including work on urban and Bedouin varieties.

## Academies and planning

Language academies were founded in Damascus (1919), Cairo (1932), Baghdad (1947) and Amman (1976), among others, with the aims of modernising vocabulary, coining technical terms, publishing dictionaries and guarding standard usage. Their coinages meet mixed reception, and rival terms often circulate in different countries. Arabisation policies in education and administration, notably in the Maghreb after independence, raise debates over the status of Berber languages, French and the dialects. Questions of teaching Standard Arabic to children who speak a dialect at home are an active area of research.

## Connections to other disciplines

Psychology and neuroscience study reading in Arabic and the effects of diglossia on literacy. Philosophy and theology intersect with the tradition through questions on the meaning and interpretation of texts, as in jurisprudential theory (*usul al-fiqh*). Anthropology and sociology examine language ideology and identity. Computer science addresses Arabic natural language processing.

## Cautions

Classical Arabic is not simply "Arabic" as spoken today. Dialects are not corrupt Standard Arabic, though they share a common history. The Basra and Kufa division should not be taken as two rigid camps. The modern view of Arabic grammarians as precursors of modern linguistics needs care, because their goals and categories differed. Attributions to early figures, particularly Abu al-Aswad, are semi-legendary.
