# Ethics

Ethics, or moral philosophy, is the systematic study of how we ought to live, what we owe to one another, and what makes actions, persons, institutions and lives good or bad, right or wrong. It is one of the oldest branches of philosophy and also one of the most practical, since every human being makes choices that others can praise or blame. This entry is the root of the Ethics module. It explains what the discipline is, how it is divided, which traditions have shaped it, how the module is organised, and what this first version of the atlas does not yet attempt.

## What ethics is

Ethics asks questions at several levels. At the most concrete level it asks what to do in a particular situation: whether to tell a painful truth, how to allocate scarce medical resources, whether a war is just. At a more general level it asks what makes any act right or wrong, and what kind of character is worth having. At the most abstract level it asks what moral claims mean, whether they can be true, and how anyone could know them. These levels interact, because a view about what moral words mean affects how one argues about cases, and convictions about cases test general theories.

The words "ethics" and "morality" are often used interchangeably. Some writers reserve "morality" for the actual codes and practices of a community and "ethics" for the critical, reflective study of them; others use "ethics" for questions about the good life and "morality" for obligations to others, as Bernard Williams does in *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* (1985). The atlas uses "ethics" for the field and notes the distinction where it matters.

Ethics is also distinct from adjacent enterprises. It is not the same as law, since some legal acts are wrong and some illegal acts may be right. It is not the same as etiquette or custom. It is not identical to religion, although religious traditions supply some of the most developed ethical systems. And it is not simply the empirical study of what people believe, which belongs to psychology, anthropology and sociology; ethics is concerned with what people have reason to believe and do.

## The branches

Philosophers usually divide the field into three overlapping areas.

**Normative ethics** proposes general theories of right action, good character and the good life. Its main families are virtue ethics, deontology, consequentialism, contractualism and care ethics. See *Normative Ethics*.

**Metaethics** studies the nature, meaning and status of moral thought and language: whether there are objective moral facts, how moral knowledge is possible, how moral judgements motivate, and how moral capacities develop. It is closely tied to moral psychology. See *Metaethics and Moral Psychology*.

**Applied ethics** brings these resources to concrete domains, including medicine and the life sciences, research, the environment, business, technology and the professions. See *Applied Ethics*.

Many thinkers add **descriptive ethics**, the empirical study of moral beliefs and behaviour, as a fourth area or as a neighbouring field. The branches are not rigid: a position on abortion draws on normative theory, which in turn presupposes metaethical views.

## The traditions

Moral reflection arose independently in many cultures, and the atlas aims to present them side by side rather than treating Western philosophy as the default.

- **Greek and Hellenistic ethics**: Socrates, Plato, Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* (4th century BCE), the Stoics, Epicureans and Skeptics, centred on flourishing (*eudaimonia*) and virtue.
- **Confucian, Daoist and Buddhist ethics**: the *Analects*, the *Mencius*, the *Zhuangzi*, and the Buddhist emphasis on compassion, non-harm and liberation from suffering.
- **Indian traditions**: discussions of *dharma*, karma and *ahimsa* in the Upanishads, the *Mahabharata*, Jain and Buddhist texts.
- **Jewish, Christian and Islamic ethics**: revelation-based traditions that also developed philosophical and legal reasoning. In the Islamic tradition these include Quranic and prophetic sources, jurisprudence (*usul al-fiqh*) and the purposes of the law (*maqasid*), theological debates between Mu'tazilites and Ash'arites over whether goodness is independently knowable, and philosophical ethics in the work of Miskawayh and al-Ghazali.
- **Modern Western ethics**: Hume, Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785), Bentham and Mill, Sidgwick, and the twentieth-century turn to metaethics beginning with G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903).
- **Contemporary movements**: the revival of virtue ethics, Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971), feminist and care ethics, environmental ethics, and African and Indigenous perspectives such as ubuntu.

## How the atlas is organised

The module is built from field entries, such as this one, that introduce an area and point onward, together with entries on individual thinkers, concepts, works and cases. The three field entries beneath this root cover normative ethics, metaethics with moral psychology, and applied ethics. Each field entry states the central questions, the leading positions with representative thinkers and works, the main debates and objections, the methods used, connections to other disciplines, and common misunderstandings. Entries end with a short list of sources, and cross-references are generated between related entries.

Two reading paths are suggested. A reader new to the subject can move from this root to *Normative Ethics*, then *Applied Ethics*, then *Metaethics and Moral Psychology*. A reader with a specific interest can enter through the applied field and trace the theoretical commitments behind a debate.

## Relation to the Philosophy module

Ethics is a part of philosophy, and the atlas has a separate Philosophy module. That module contains a short entry, *Ethics (Overview)*, which situates ethics within philosophy and points readers here. This module treats ethics at length. Entries about thinkers who belong to both domains, such as Aristotle, Kant, Hume and Mill, are shared: the Ethics module reuses the thinker entries from the Philosophy module rather than duplicating them, and concentrates its own pages on moral questions, theories and applications. Related areas, including political philosophy, philosophy of mind, metaphysics and epistemology, remain in the Philosophy module.

## Methods and neighbouring disciplines

Ethicists reason from principles and cases, aiming at what John Rawls called reflective equilibrium, a state in which general principles and considered judgements about cases are adjusted to cohere. They also use thought experiments, conceptual analysis, comparison of traditions, historical interpretation and, increasingly, empirical results. Psychology and cognitive science inform claims about moral motivation and judgement; law and political economy raise questions of rights, justice and institutional design; religious studies and anthropology document how moral life is practised and justified in different communities.

## Cautions

Ethics is not a list of answers. Moral disagreement is widespread, but it does not by itself show that morality is merely subjective. Describing a tradition's moral beliefs is not endorsing them. Theories are frameworks for reflection, not algorithms. And the atlas presents arguments and objections rather than a verdict: where informed people disagree, the entries try to say why.

## Limits of version 1

This first version is limited. It contains four field entries and does not yet cover every tradition, thinker or applied domain in depth. Coverage of non-Western traditions, including Islamic, Indian, East Asian and African ethics, is introductory and will be extended. Entries summarise only well-established positions and do not report disputed scholarship in detail. Readers should treat the sources listed as starting points, not as complete bibliographies.
