# Sociolinguistics and Language Variation

Sociolinguistics studies the relationship between language and society. It starts from the observation that no language is uniform: speakers differ by region, class, age, gender, ethnicity, context and purpose, and the same speaker shifts style from one situation to another. Rather than treating this variation as noise, the field treats it as structured and meaningful, and asks what it reveals about how language works and how communities are organised.

## What the field asks

Why do people speak differently, and what do those differences signal? How do new forms spread through a community and change a language over time? What happens when several languages share a society or a single mind? How do attitudes, power and policy shape which varieties are valued, taught, written or lost? The field combines the empirical study of speech with the study of social meaning.

## Main questions

- How is linguistic variation distributed across social groups and situations?
- What drives language change, and how does it spread?
- How do speakers use language to construct identity and relationships?
- How do multilingual speakers and communities manage several languages?
- How do languages come into contact, mix, form pidgins and creoles, shift or die?
- How do institutions plan, standardise and regulate language?

## Key positions and scholars

**Variationist sociolinguistics.** William Labov's study of the Lower East Side of New York (*The Social Stratification of English in New York City*, 1966) showed that pronunciation of a feature such as post-vocalic r correlated systematically with social class and style. His earlier work on Martha's Vineyard (1963) tied sound change to local identity. Variation is treated as orderly heterogeneity, modelled by quantitative methods, and *Principles of Linguistic Change* (three volumes, 1994, 2001, 2010) summarises decades of work on change in progress. Uriel Weinreich, Labov and Marvin Herzog's 1968 paper proposed a framework for explaining language change through variation within communities.

**Ethnography of communication.** Dell Hymes argued in the 1960s for studying communicative competence, meaning knowing not only what is grammatical but what is appropriate in context. John Gumperz's work on interactional sociolinguistics showed how cultural differences in cues lead to misunderstanding between groups.

**Language and gender and power.** Robin Lakoff's *Language and Woman's Place* (1975) opened research on gendered speech, followed by Deborah Tannen's popular work and critical responses from scholars such as Penelope Eckert and Sally McConnell-Ginet, who emphasised communities of practice rather than fixed gender differences. Critical Discourse Analysis, associated with Norman Fairclough, examines language and power.

**Diglossia and multilingualism.** Charles Ferguson's 1959 article "Diglossia" described communities with a high and a low variety, as in Arabic-speaking countries, Swiss German, Haiti and Greece. Joshua Fishman extended the idea to bilingual societies. Research on code-switching, by Carol Myers-Scotton, Shana Poplack and others, finds that switching follows grammatical and social constraints rather than reflecting deficiency.

**Contact linguistics.** Sarah Thomason and Terrence Kaufman, Peter Muysken and others study borrowing and convergence. Pidgins and creoles, studied by Derek Bickerton, John McWhorter and Salikoko Mufwene, raise debates over the origin and nature of new languages.

**Language policy and planning.** Heinz Kloss, Einar Haugen and Joshua Fishman developed ideas of standardisation, status planning and corpus planning. Robert Phillipson's *Linguistic Imperialism* (1992) and later work examine the global role of English. Research on language endangerment, by Michael Krauss, Nicholas Evans and others, documents loss of languages and the case for revitalisation.

**Newer approaches.** Penelope Eckert's work on "third wave" variationism studies style and social meaning, with variants as resources for constructing personae. Studies of translanguaging, Jan Blommaert's sociolinguistics of globalisation and research on superdiversity focus on mobile, mixed repertoires.

## Major debates and objections

**Aggregate patterns or individual agency?** Early variationism reported group-level correlations with class, age and sex, which later critics said treated speakers as passive members of fixed categories. Newer work stresses agency, interaction and local categories, while defenders retain the value of large-scale patterns.

**Where is the boundary between languages and dialects?** The old saying that a language is a dialect with an army and a navy, popularised by Max Weinreich, points to the role of politics. Mutual intelligibility is gradient and does not settle the matter, and linguists distinguish languages and varieties on mixed grounds.

**Deficit or difference.** In the 1960s Basil Bernstein's contrast of elaborated and restricted codes was criticised by Labov. "The Logic of Nonstandard English" (1969; reprinted in *Language in the Inner City*, 1972) argued that nonstandard varieties are as rule-governed and expressive as standard ones. The deficit view has little support in linguistics, though debates continue over schooling in standard varieties.

**Creole exceptionalism.** Some claim that creoles are structurally simple or unusual, as John McWhorter argues, while others, such as Michel DeGraff, reject that view as unsupported.

**Prescription and policy.** Linguists generally favour description but disagree about intervention in endangered-language revitalisation, language academies and standardisation.

## Methods

The sociolinguistic interview, devised by Labov to elicit a range of styles, remains central, along with the rapid anonymous survey, participant observation, ethnography, matched-guise tests of attitudes (Wallace Lambert), questionnaires, and large digital corpora, including social media data. Quantitative methods include variable rule analysis and, more recently, mixed-effects regression. Dialectology uses atlases and surveys, such as the Linguistic Atlas projects and the work of Jules Gilliéron in France. Researchers face the observer's paradox, as Labov named it: the aim is to observe how people speak when not observed.

## Connections to other disciplines

Anthropology contributes ethnographic methods and the study of language ideologies. Sociology engages with identity, class, migration and institutions. Psychology adds attitudes, bilingual cognition and accommodation, the tendency of speakers to adjust toward each other, described by Howard Giles. Philosophy of language bears on questions of meaning and community norms. Computer science supplies methods for corpus analysis and raises issues of bias in language technologies. Education, law and political science use the field for policy on literacy, testimony and language rights.

## Cautions

Variation is not error, and nonstandard speech is not lazy speech. Correlations with social categories do not show that a group "has" a particular style by nature. Terms such as "dialect", "accent" and "mother tongue" are slippery and carry political weight. Findings from one community, especially English-speaking Western cities, may not transfer elsewhere. Language shift and endangerment have social and historical causes, and not simply the inferiority of the languages that decline. Finally, the field's vocabulary changes quickly, and older terms may now be regarded as inappropriate.
