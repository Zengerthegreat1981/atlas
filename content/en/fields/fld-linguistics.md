# Linguistics

Linguistics is the scientific study of human language: its sounds, word structure, sentence structure, meaning, use in context, acquisition by children, change over time, variation across communities and representation in the mind and brain. Its basic observation is that every human community has a language, that every ordinary child acquires one without formal instruction, and that these languages differ enormously on the surface while sharing deep regularities. Explaining both the differences and the regularities is the common thread running through the field.

## What linguistics is, and what it is not

Linguistics is descriptive before it is prescriptive. A linguist asks what speakers actually say and judge acceptable, and tries to state the patterns that account for this. This differs from the traditional school grammar that tells people how they ought to speak. Linguists do not deny that standards exist, since standard varieties are social facts worth studying, but they do not treat one variety as intrinsically more logical or correct than another.

It is also not the same as learning many languages. A linguist may know few languages and still study language in general, while a polyglot need not do linguistics. Nor is it the same as philology, the study of texts and their history, although the two overlap in historical and comparative work.

## Levels of analysis

The field is conventionally organised by levels, each studying a different kind of structure.

- **Phonetics** describes the physical production, transmission and perception of speech sounds.
- **Phonology** studies how sounds are organised into systems and patterns within particular languages.
- **Morphology** studies the internal structure of words: roots, affixes, inflection, derivation and compounding.
- **Syntax** studies how words combine into phrases and sentences.
- **Semantics** studies the meaning of words and sentences, and how meanings combine.
- **Pragmatics** studies how context, speaker intention and shared knowledge shape what is communicated beyond literal content.

The levels interact. Morphology and syntax overlap in languages with rich inflection, and semantics and pragmatics divide labour in ways that are themselves debated. The division is a working convenience, not a settled map of the mind.

## Main schools and approaches

**Structuralism** took shape in the early twentieth century. Ferdinand de Saussure's posthumous *Course in General Linguistics* (1916), compiled by his students, treated language as a system of signs defined by their relations to one another, and separated *langue*, the shared system, from *parole*, individual speech. Leonard Bloomfield's *Language* (1933) developed a rigorous American descriptive tradition, and the Prague School advanced the study of phonological oppositions and information structure.

**Generative linguistics** began with Noam Chomsky's *Syntactic Structures* (1957) and *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965). It treats language as a knowledge system in the individual mind, argues that its acquisition is guided by innate constraints, and uses formal rule systems to model it. Later frameworks include Government and Binding, the Principles and Parameters approach and the Minimalist Program. Optimality Theory, introduced by Prince and Smolensky in the 1990s, transformed phonology.

**Functional approaches** explain linguistic structure by reference to communication. The Prague School, Michael Halliday's systemic functional linguistics and the typological work of Joseph Greenberg, Talmy Givón and others hold that grammar is shaped by what speakers do with it.

**Cognitive linguistics**, associated with George Lakoff, Ronald Langacker and Leonard Talmy from the 1980s, treats language as continuous with general cognition. It stresses categorisation, metaphor, embodied experience and the meaningfulness of grammar itself.

**Usage-based approaches**, developed by Joan Bybee, Michael Tomasello, Adele Goldberg and others, hold that grammar emerges from patterns in experience, frequency and general learning mechanisms. Construction Grammar treats form-meaning pairings of many sizes as the basic units.

These schools are not sealed camps. Formal semantics, following Richard Montague in the 1970s, joins logic and linguistics, and many working linguists mix methods.

## Main subfields

- **Historical and comparative linguistics** reconstructs earlier stages and relations among languages, as in the reconstruction of Proto-Indo-European.
- **Linguistic typology** compares structures across the world's languages to find patterns and limits.
- **Sociolinguistics** studies variation, identity, multilingualism and language policy.
- **Psycholinguistics** studies how language is processed, produced and acquired.
- **Neurolinguistics** studies its brain bases and disorders.
- **Computational linguistics** builds models and tools for language data.
- **Applied linguistics** addresses teaching, translation, assessment and language planning.
- **Documentary linguistics** records endangered languages with their communities.
- **Anthropological linguistics** links language to culture and social practice.

## Methods

Linguists use several kinds of evidence. Elicitation and native-speaker judgments of acceptability give data about what is possible in a language. Fieldwork records spontaneous speech and texts. Corpus methods analyse large collections of naturally occurring language for frequency and distribution. Experimental methods, such as reaction-time tasks, eye-tracking and neuroimaging, test processing and acquisition. Acoustic analysis measures speech signals. Comparative methods link languages by regular sound correspondences. Each method has known weaknesses: judgments can be unreliable, corpora do not show what is impossible, and experiments may not generalise beyond the lab.

## Non-European grammatical traditions

Linguistics did not begin in Europe. Panini's *Ashtadhyayi*, a Sanskrit grammar composed probably around the fourth century BCE, gave a remarkably compact system of rules for word and sound formation and later influenced modern linguistics. Arabic grammar developed from the eighth century CE in Basra and Kufa, with Sibawayh's *al-Kitab* as its foundational synthesis, and grew alongside rhetoric, lexicography and phonetics. Hebrew grammarians, Tamil grammatical writing, Chinese phonological and philological scholarship and Japanese national-learning grammar each built their own concepts. The atlas gives the Arabic tradition particular attention because it is central to the project's language and readership, while treating the others as equal contributors to the history of the discipline.

## Connections to other disciplines

Linguistics meets psychology in the study of acquisition and processing, philosophy in debates about meaning, reference and innateness, anthropology in the relation of language to culture and in fieldwork, computer science in natural language processing and formal language theory, and sociology in language policy and inequality. Neuroscience, education, law and literary studies also draw on it.

## Cautions

Several misunderstandings recur. Languages are not primitive or advanced; all are fully capable of expressing the needs of their speakers. Dialects are not corrupted forms of a standard. Grammar rules in linguistics describe patterns, not commands. The claim that language shapes thought, often associated with Sapir and Whorf, exists in strong and weak forms, and the strong form is widely rejected. Statements about universals must be tested against the world's roughly seven thousand languages, most of which are under-described.

## Limits of this version

This first version is selective. Coverage of formal frameworks, signed languages, creoles, language contact, historical reconstruction and many regional traditions is thin or absent, and some subfields are only touched on.
