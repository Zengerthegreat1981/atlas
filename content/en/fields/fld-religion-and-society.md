# Religion and Society

## What the field asks

This field studies how religion and social life shape each other: how religious beliefs and organisations relate to politics, economy, law, family, gender, education and conflict, and how social change affects religion. It sits where the sociology of religion, political science, history, anthropology and law meet. Questions are empirical and comparative: Who believes and practises what? How do religious groups form, grow and decline? How do states manage religious diversity?

## Main questions

- Is religion in decline in modern societies, or is it changing form and location?
- How do religion and politics interact: does religion legitimise power, restrain it, or mobilise opposition to it?
- What explains the rise and fate of new religious movements?
- How should states and legal systems treat religious diversity, freedom and conflicting claims?
- How do religion and gender, race, class and migration intersect?
- What is the relation between religion and violence, and between religion and peace?

## Classical foundations

Émile Durkheim (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912) treated religion as a source of social cohesion. Max Weber argued in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904-05) that certain Protestant ideas of vocation fostered a disposition favourable to modern capitalism, a thesis still debated on historical and causal grounds; his comparative studies (*The Religion of China*, *The Religion of India*, *Ancient Judaism*) and his typology of authority, including charisma, shaped the field. Weber's remark about "disenchantment" is a central reference for secularisation theories. Karl Marx's description of religion as the "opium of the people" (1844) is often quoted without its context: he also called it the "sigh of the oppressed creature". Ernst Troeltsch (*The Social Teaching of the Christian Churches*, 1912) distinguished church, sect and mysticism as types; H. Richard Niebuhr's *The Social Sources of Denominationalism* (1929) developed the church-sect analysis, and Bryan Wilson and others extended it.

## Secularisation and its critics

The classical secularisation thesis held that modernisation (differentiation, rationalisation, pluralism) would reduce religion's social authority and perhaps individual belief. Bryan Wilson, Steve Bruce and, in earlier work, Peter Berger (*The Sacred Canopy*, 1967) defended versions of it. Berger later argued that the world was "as furiously religious as ever" (1999) outside parts of Europe. José Casanova (*Public Religions in the Modern World*, 1994) distinguished decline, differentiation and privatisation, arguing that differentiation does not entail privatisation. Rodney Stark and Roger Finke proposed a "religious economies" model in which pluralism stimulates participation, which critics find less persuasive for Europe. Grace Davie described "believing without belonging" in Britain; Charles Taylor (*A Secular Age*, 2007) offered a historical-philosophical account of the conditions of belief. Pippa Norris and Ronald Inglehart linked secularisation to existential security. Survey data show wide regional variation, and conclusions depend on how religion is measured.

## Religion and politics

Topics include church-state arrangements (establishment, separation, accommodation; French *laïcité*, the US First Amendment, the Ottoman millet system and its successors), civil religion (Robert Bellah, 1967), religion in nationalism, political Islam, liberation theology, the Christian Right in the United States, Hindu nationalism, and religious actors in democratisation and civil rights movements. Scholars disagree about causal claims: whether religious identity drives political behaviour or is a marker of other group interests; whether particular traditions are intrinsically more or less compatible with democracy (a claim most specialists treat sceptically, pointing to variation within each tradition and across time).

## New religious movements and pluralism

Eileen Barker's study of the Unification Church (*The Making of a Moonie*, 1984) challenged simplistic "brainwashing" accounts, finding that many recruits left voluntarily and that recruitment methods were less coercive than public discourse assumed. The "brainwashing" controversy remains contested, with some scholars and former members offering different assessments of coercion in particular groups. Terms such as "cult" are avoided in scholarship because of their pejorative sense. Religious pluralism, immigration and diaspora communities, and the growth of "spiritual but not religious" identification are active research fields.

## Religion and law

States must decide what counts as religion for purposes of rights, exemptions, tax status and recognition. Cases on dress, slaughter, education, blasphemy, conscientious objection and employment raise questions of equality and of freedom of religion and belief, as protected by instruments such as Article 18 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948). Scholars disagree on whether religion deserves special legal protection and how to weigh it against other rights. Religious legal traditions, including halakha, canon law and Islamic *fiqh*, are also studied as normative systems in their own right.

## Gender and religion

Research examines women's roles in religious authority and ritual, interpretation of scriptures, feminist and womanist theologies and critiques, and the politics of dress. Scholars such as Saba Mahmood (*Politics of Piety*, 2005) challenged assumptions that agency can be measured only as resistance to religious norms. Others emphasise institutional exclusion and its consequences. Both views draw on fieldwork and textual study, and neither is universal.

## Violence and peace

Whether religion is especially prone to violence is contested. Mark Juergensmeyer (*Terror in the Mind of God*, 2000) studied religious dimensions of terrorism; William Cavanaugh (*The Myth of Religious Violence*, 2009) argues that the category "religious violence" is unstable because the secular and the religious cannot be cleanly separated. René Girard's theory (*Violence and the Sacred*, 1972) links sacrifice and scapegoating to violence. Studies of interfaith dialogue, nonviolent movements and peacebuilding show religious resources for reconciliation as well. Generalisations should be made cautiously and tied to cases.

## Methods

Surveys and census data, interviews, ethnography, historical and archival work, comparative case study, legal analysis and network analysis. Measurement is a persistent problem: belief, belonging, practice and identity are separate dimensions, and survey categories may reflect a Christian model of religion.

## Connections to other disciplines

Sociology and political science provide the core theories; anthropology offers lived practice; psychology addresses motivation and identity; history supplies long-run change; philosophy treats secularism, toleration and public reason (John Rawls, Jürgen Habermas); law shapes and is shaped by religion.

## Cautions

Distinguish description (what groups do and believe), self-understanding (what traditions say about their own relation to politics or violence), and scholarly explanation (causal accounts that adherents may reject). Avoid treating any religion as a single actor with one position, avoid reading a tradition off from its most visible movements, and avoid equating religion with Christianity in framing the questions.
