# Classification and Diagnosis in Psychiatry

This entry is educational and descriptive. It is not clinical advice and cannot be used to diagnose anyone. Diagnostic categories are conventions with limits, and the entry explains why.

## What the field asks

Classification asks how the huge variety of mental distress and disturbance should be sorted, named and recorded. Diagnosis asks how an individual's problems are assigned to those categories. Both serve several purposes at once: communication between clinicians, research, statistics on illness and services, decisions about treatment, and in many countries eligibility for insurance, benefits, education support or legal provisions. Because the same label is asked to do scientific, practical and administrative work, disagreements about it are frequent.

## Main questions

- Are mental disorders natural kinds with distinct causes, or practical groupings along continua?
- Should classification rest on symptoms and course, on causes, or on treatment response?
- How reliable are diagnoses, meaning will different clinicians agree, and how valid, meaning do the categories track something real?
- How should culture, context and values enter the definition of disorder?
- Where is the boundary between disorder and ordinary variation, including normal sadness, shyness or eccentricity?
- What are the social effects of a label, for the person, family and community?

## A brief history

Early medical writers described conditions such as melancholia and mania, but systematic national statistical classifications of insanity appeared in the nineteenth century. Emil Kraepelin, in successive editions of his textbook from the 1880s to the 1920s, grouped patients by symptom patterns and long-term course, separating dementia praecox from manic-depressive illness. Eugen Bleuler introduced the term schizophrenia in 1908, and set out the concept in his 1911 monograph. This emphasis on course and syndrome set the pattern for later systems.

The American Psychiatric Association published its first *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual* in 1952 and a second edition in 1968, both influenced by psychodynamic and reactive concepts. The World Health Organization added a mental disorders section to the International Classification of Diseases from its sixth revision in 1948. By the 1970s, studies of diagnostic agreement and the debate about homosexuality, which the APA removed as a category in 1973, increased pressure for reform. DSM-III (1980), led by Robert Spitzer, introduced explicit operational criteria and a multiaxial format, largely setting aside causal theory. DSM-IV appeared in 1994, DSM-5 in 2013 and a text revision, DSM-5-TR, in 2022. ICD-11, adopted by the World Health Assembly in 2019 and in effect from 2022, includes a revised mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders chapter. The two systems differ in structure and in some details.

## Key positions

The **categorical** approach, dominant in the manuals, treats disorders as present or absent according to criteria. Its advantages are simplicity and clinical convenience. The **dimensional** approach holds that many problems vary by degree, and that severity and trait profiles are more accurate than binary labels. Hierarchical and transdiagnostic proposals, such as the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology, and the US National Institute of Mental Health's Research Domain Criteria launched around 2009, attempt to build research frameworks on dimensions and neural systems rather than on manual categories. DSM-5 included some dimensional measures, and ICD-11 adopted dimensional severity descriptions for personality disorder.

Philosophers have contributed concepts of disorder. Jerome Wakefield proposed a "harmful dysfunction" analysis, combining a value-laden notion of harm with a factual notion of failure of natural function. Others, such as Christopher Boorse in the philosophy of health generally, defend more purely biostatistical accounts, while some argue that values are unavoidable.

## Debates and standard objections

Reliability was a central concern of DSM-III, but field trials for DSM-5 reported modest agreement for several common diagnoses, and critics noted that some categories remain hard to apply consistently. Validity is harder still. Because most categories were not derived from identified biological markers, critics, including figures involved in the NIMH, argued that symptom-based diagnoses may not map onto underlying mechanisms. Frequent comorbidity, the co-occurrence of several diagnoses in one person, is cited as evidence that categories overlap. Genetic findings that risk factors are shared across several diagnoses point in the same direction.

Another objection is "diagnostic inflation", the claim that thresholds have widened and ordinary experience is increasingly medicalised. Allen Frances, who chaired the DSM-IV task force, has made this argument publicly, notably about DSM-5. The removal of the bereavement exclusion from the depression criteria in DSM-5 was a specific point of contention. Defenders respond that categories help people obtain care, that under-diagnosis also causes harm, and that criteria are revised in light of evidence.

Concerns about labelling include stigma and reduced attention to context, though a name can also bring relief and access to support.

## Evidence and methods

Researchers evaluate classification using inter-rater reliability statistics such as kappa, structured interviews, family and twin studies, longitudinal studies of course, treatment-response studies, factor-analytic and latent-variable modelling of symptoms, and increasingly genetic and neuroimaging data. Each method has limits. For instance, factor analysis depends on the measures included, and imaging findings are generally group-level and not diagnostic for individuals.

## Culture

Cross-cultural research shows that distress is expressed and interpreted in different idioms. DSM-IV introduced an outline for cultural formulation and a glossary of "culture-bound syndromes", and DSM-5 replaced the latter with "cultural concepts of distress". Arthur Kleinman's work argued that applying categories developed in one setting to another risks a "category fallacy". The World Health Organization's multi-country studies of schizophrenia were among the major efforts to compare presentation and outcome across societies. Critics also note that most research samples are drawn from wealthy countries.

## Connections to other fields

**Psychology**: psychometrics, personality theory and clinical assessment overlap with diagnosis, and psychological models offer formulation-based alternatives to labels. **Philosophy**: debates on natural kinds, the concept of disease, and value-ladenness. **Law**: criteria of capacity, criminal responsibility and compulsory treatment use related but distinct legal tests, and a diagnosis alone does not settle them. **Sociology**: labelling theory, medicalisation and the sociology of diagnosis. **Anthropology**: cultural variation and the critique of universal categories.

## Cautions

Reading about classification does not equip anyone to diagnose, and the atlas does not reproduce diagnostic criteria. Diagnoses made in practice rely on training, history and context. Categories are provisional conventions, revised over time and across manuals. Anyone seeking assessment should consult a qualified clinician.
