# Phonetics and Phonology

Phonetics and phonology study the sound side of language. Phonetics describes how speech sounds are made, how they travel as acoustic signals and how listeners perceive them. Phonology asks how a language organises those sounds into a system: which sounds count as different, which combinations are allowed, and how sounds change depending on their neighbours. Put simply, phonetics studies what can be pronounced, and phonology studies what a given language does with it.

## What the field asks

The central puzzle is that speech is physically continuous and variable, yet speakers perceive discrete, stable units. The sound of the vowel in a word differs with every utterance, speaker and speed, yet listeners hear the same word. The field asks what units speakers store, how those units are realised in articulation, and what regularities govern the mapping between abstract forms and physical sound.

## Main questions

- What are the possible speech sounds, and how can they be described and classified?
- What units does the mind use: segments, features, syllables, feet, tones?
- How do sounds pattern in a language, and why do some patterns recur across languages?
- How do stored forms differ from pronounced forms, and by what rules or constraints?
- How do listeners decode a variable signal, and how do children learn a sound system?
- How do sound systems change over time?

## Phonetics

**Articulatory phonetics** classifies sounds by how they are produced: the place of articulation (lips, alveolar ridge, palate, velum, uvula, pharynx, glottis), the manner (stop, fricative, nasal, approximant, trill) and voicing. Vowels are described by tongue height, backness and lip rounding. The International Phonetic Alphabet, first set out in 1888 and maintained, under that later name, by the International Phonetic Association, provides a standard notation. It has been revised several times. Arabic is a useful case, with its pharyngeal and uvular consonants, emphatic (pharyngealised) consonants and contrast of short and long vowels.

**Acoustic phonetics** analyses the speech signal: frequency, intensity and duration, with spectrograms showing formants, which characterise vowels, and bursts and noise, which characterise consonants. Gunnar Fant's *Acoustic Theory of Speech Production* (1960) established the source-filter model, in which the vocal folds provide a source and the vocal tract acts as a filter.

**Auditory and perceptual phonetics** study how listeners categorise sounds. Experiments on categorical perception and on the McGurk effect, reported by McGurk and MacDonald in 1976, show that perception is shaped by learned categories and by vision as well as hearing.

## Phonology

**Phoneme theory.** Early twentieth-century linguists, among them Nikolai Trubetzkoy of the Prague School and the American structuralists, defined the phoneme as the smallest unit that distinguishes meaning, tested by minimal pairs. Variants of one phoneme, such as aspirated and unaspirated stops in English, are allophones. Trubetzkoy's *Principles of Phonology* (published posthumously in 1939) is a classic statement.

**Distinctive features.** Roman Jakobson, Gunnar Fant and Morris Halle proposed in *Preliminaries to Speech Analysis* (1952) that phonemes are bundles of binary features such as voiced or nasal. Features capture natural classes of sounds that behave alike.

**Generative phonology.** Noam Chomsky and Morris Halle's *The Sound Pattern of English* (1968) modelled phonology as ordered rules transforming underlying representations into surface forms. This approach stressed abstract underlying forms and explained alternations through rules.

**Nonlinear phonology.** Work from the 1970s onward, including autosegmental phonology associated with John Goldsmith, metrical phonology by Mark Liberman and Alan Prince, and feature geometry, treats tone, stress and features as separate tiers rather than a linear string.

**Optimality Theory.** Alan Prince and Paul Smolensky circulated their work in 1993, and it was published as *Optimality Theory* (2004). It replaces ordered rules with ranked, violable constraints: a language selects the output that best satisfies a language-specific ranking of universal constraints.

**Articulatory and usage-based phonology.** Articulatory Phonology, developed by Catherine Browman and Louis Goldstein, takes gestures as the basic units. Exemplar and usage-based approaches, associated with Joan Bybee and Janet Pierrehumbert, give weight to frequency and detailed phonetic memory.

## Major debates and objections

**How abstract should representations be?** Rule-based generative accounts posit underlying forms that never surface, which critics regard as too abstract and insufficiently constrained by evidence. Usage-based approaches reply with concrete stored experience, and critics of that view ask how it handles productive generalisation.

**Rules or constraints?** Optimality Theory was offered as a way around some problems of rule ordering. Critics note that it has difficulty with opaque interactions, in which a pattern is obscured by another process, and later work has proposed many variants to address this.

**Phonetics and phonology: separate or continuous?** Traditional views treat phonology as discrete and symbolic, and phonetics as gradient and physical, with a translation step between them. Laboratory phonology, a movement active since the 1980s, argues for closer integration and finds gradient effects inside what looked like categorical patterns.

**Universals and innateness.** Features and constraints are sometimes proposed as innate. Others argue that common patterns reflect articulation, perception and learnability. Evidence from sign language phonology shows that comparable organisation arises in another modality.

**Does phonology need the syllable?** The syllable is widely used, but its status as a primitive and its definition have been contested.

## Methods

Phoneticians use direct observation, palatography, electromagnetic articulography, ultrasound and magnetic resonance imaging of the vocal tract, and acoustic analysis with software such as Praat, created by Paul Boersma and David Weenink. Phonologists use native-speaker judgments, elicitation of paradigms, wug-test style tasks in which speakers apply patterns to invented words, and corpora. Perception studies use identification and discrimination tasks and event-related brain potentials. Typological databases compare inventories across languages, for example the UCLA Phonological Segment Inventory Database and the World Atlas of Language Structures.

## Connections to other disciplines

In psychology, speech perception and phonological development connect with auditory cognition and learning theory, including studies of infants' early sensitivity to contrasts. Philosophy of mind and language engage the question of mental representation. Anthropology and sociolinguistics use sound variation as a marker of identity and community, as in William Labov's study of New York City English (1966). In computer science, speech recognition and synthesis rely on acoustic and phonological models, though modern systems learn statistical representations from data. Speech and hearing sciences apply the field to clinical disorders.

## Cautions

Spelling is not sound: letters and phonemes do not correspond one to one in any writing system, and English orthography in particular hides many patterns. The IPA is a notation, not a claim that every sound is equally well described by its symbol. Phonemes are theoretical constructs, and different analyses of the same language can differ legitimately. Accents are not deviations from a correct pronunciation. Finally, claims of universal sound patterns should be checked against the many languages with limited documentation, and the description of languages with complex phenomena such as clicks, tone systems or pharyngealisation often challenges received categories.
