# Nahda and Modern Arab Thought

The Nahda (Arabic for "rising" or "awakening") is the name usually given to the cultural and intellectual movement of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries in the Arabic-speaking world, centred on Cairo, Beirut, Damascus, Tunis and Istanbul-linked circles. It is associated with the spread of printing and journalism, new schools and translation, state reform, and an intense public argument over how Arab and Muslim societies should respond to European power and to their own institutions. "Modern Arab thought" extends the story through nationalism, liberalism, Islamism and Marxism in the twentieth century, and the self-critical turn after 1967.

## What the field covers

The field studies the ideas, institutions and media of this period: the first modern Arabic newspapers, the literary and scientific societies, mission and state schools, the translation movements, women's journals, and the political essays that debated constitutions and religion. It also studies the reception of European ideas such as liberalism, evolutionary science and socialism, and the ways Arab writers reworked them rather than simply importing them.

## Key figures and works

- **Rifa'a al-Tahtawi (1801-1873)**, an Egyptian scholar who lived in Paris in the 1820s and wrote an account of his stay, *Takhlis al-ibriz fi talkhis Bariz* (1834). He later led translation work in Egypt and wrote on patriotism and education.
- **Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi (c. 1822-1890)**, Tunisian statesman, author of *Aqwam al-masalik* (1867), a case for political and administrative reform grounded in Islamic precedent.
- **Butrus al-Bustani (1819-1883)**, a Lebanese educator and encyclopedist who promoted learning and a broad Syrian patriotism.
- **Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838/39-1897)**, an itinerant activist and writer who advocated pan-Islamic solidarity against European domination and engaged with questions of science and religion.
- **Muhammad Abduh (1849-1905)**, Egyptian jurist and Mufti of Egypt, whose theological treatise *Risalat al-tawhid* (lectures at the Sultaniyya school in Beirut, 1885–1888; first printed at Bulaq in 1315 AH/1897) and educational reforms sought to reconcile Islam with reason and modern life. His disciple **Rashid Rida (1865-1935)** edited the journal *al-Manar* (from 1898). Later accounts often describe him, especially after Abduh's death, as moving toward a more conservative salafi orientation.
- **Qasim Amin (1863-1908)**, author of *Tahrir al-mar'a* (1899) on women's education and status, and the controversy it provoked.
- **Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi (1855-1902)**, whose *Taba'i' al-istibdad wa-masari' al-isti'bad* (Cairo, c. 1900-1902; he died in June 1902) analysed despotism. Early printings are dated variously around his death.
- **Shibli Shumayyil (1850-1917)**, who argued for scientific materialism, and **Farah Antun (1874-1922)**, whose reading of Ibn Rushd was the occasion of a well-known exchange with Abduh.
- **Ali Abd al-Raziq (1888-1966)**, whose *al-Islam wa-usul al-hukm* (*Islam and the Foundations of Governance*, 1925) argued that the caliphate was not a religious obligation and provoked a condemnation from al-Azhar scholars, and **Taha Husayn (1889-1973)**, whose *Fi al-shi'r al-jahili* (*On Pre-Islamic Poetry*, 1926) provoked a separate controversy over the authenticity of pre-Islamic verse. Husayn's later *Mustaqbal al-thaqafa fi Misr* (*The Future of Culture in Egypt*, 1938) argued for Egypt's cultural ties to the Mediterranean.
- **Sati' al-Husri (1880-1968)** and **Michel Aflaq (1910-1989)**, theorists of Arab nationalism; **Hasan al-Banna (1906-1949)**, founder of the Muslim Brotherhood (1928), and **Sayyid Qutb (1906-1966)**, whose *Milestones* (1964) shaped later Islamist thought.
- **Constantine Zurayk (1909-2000)**, whose *Ma'na al-nakba* (*The Meaning of the Disaster*, 1948) responded to the Palestinian catastrophe, and **Sadik Jalal al-Azm (1934-2016)**, whose *al-Naqd al-dhati ba'd al-hazima* (*Self-Criticism after the Defeat*, 1968) belongs to the post-1967 turn.

## Main debates and objections

**Reform and its sources.** Reformers asked whether renewal should proceed by reinterpreting Islamic law and theology, by borrowing European institutions, or by combining the two. Abduh-style Islamic modernism, secular liberalism and early socialism offered different balances. Critics from the religious side charged that borrowing compromised identity; critics from the secular side charged that apologetic reconciliation avoided hard questions.

**Religion and politics.** Abd al-Raziq's argument that the caliphate was not a religious obligation drew a rebuke from al-Azhar scholars and remains a reference point. The later rise of political Islam responded in part to the secular nationalist states that followed independence.

**Nation and identity.** Debates between Arab nationalism, local patriotism (Egyptian, Syrian, Tunisian) and Islamic unity were not simple oppositions, and many writers moved between them.

**Women and society.** Amin's book, women's journals and early feminist activism raised questions about education, the veil and law. Historians note that women writers took part from the start and that the male-reformer story alone is incomplete.

**Historiography.** Albert Hourani's *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age* (1962) gave the period a canonical narrative centred on a "liberal age". Later scholars argue that this story overstated liberalism, underplayed Ottoman context, popular and religious currents and non-elite voices, and imposed a neat arc of rise and decline. The very term "Nahda" has been questioned as a retrospective label that may carry teleological assumptions.

## Connections to other modules

- **Philosophy:** The Nahda recovered classical figures such as Ibn Rushd and Ibn Khaldun as resources, and absorbed European positivism, evolutionism and idealism. See the classical falsafa and kalam entries.
- **Religious Studies:** Abduh, Rida and their critics reinterpreted Quranic exegesis, theology and the role of the ulama.
- **Legal Theory:** Questions of ijtihad, codification, the status of sharia and the adoption of European-style codes link reform thought to usul al-fiqh.
- **Sociology and political economy:** The period raises issues of state-building, education, print capitalism, colonial economies, class formation and the emergence of a public sphere.

## Cautions

The region is not uniform. Egypt and Greater Syria dominate the standard story, while the Maghreb, Iraq, Arabia and Sudan had different timelines and institutions. Christian, Muslim and Jewish writers participated, with differing stakes. Framings of "authentic versus modern" or "West versus Islam" can be useful descriptions of how actors saw themselves, but they are risky as analytic categories, since Arab writers were already internal critics and creative adapters. Labels such as "liberal", "secular" and "salafi" also changed meaning over time and should be read with care.
