# Historiography

Historiography is the systematic study of historical writing, methodology, evidence, and the conceptual frameworks through which societies interpret their past. Rather than chronicling past events directly, historiography investigates how those events have been discovered, recorded, explained, and transmitted across different eras and civilizations. It examines the questions historians formulate, the archives they assemble, the analytical categories they deploy, and the epistemological assumptions that govern historical knowledge.

## What historiography is, and what it is not

Historiography differs from history itself. While history designates the actions, structures, and experiences of past human communities, historiography analyzes the craft, concepts, and texts created by historians. It operates as the critical conscience of historical inquiry, asking not merely what happened, but on what evidentiary grounds claims rest, whose voices were recorded or silenced, and why historical interpretations change across generations.

It is equally distinct from speculative philosophy of history. Metaphysical systems—such as those of Giambattista Vico, G. W. F. Hegel, and Oswald Spengler—sought grand teleological or cyclical patterns governing human destiny. Historiography, by contrast, concentrates on empirical practice and critical philosophy: the verification of surviving traces, the logic of explanation, periodisation, and comparative traditions of historical inquiry. Nor is it antiquarian collecting; where the antiquarian preserves relics for curiosity, the historiographer analyzes how evidence is converted into reasoned argument.

## Source criticism and historical method

At the foundation of historiography lies source criticism: the systematic procedures through which primary traces, documents, inscriptions, and testimonies are tested for authenticity and meaning. Formalized in European scholarship through Jean Mabillon, Leopold von Ranke, and the methodical treatise of Charles-Victor Langlois and Charles Seignobos (1898), source criticism divides into external and internal operations.

External criticism (heuristics) establishes provenance, authorship, physical integrity, and date, using auxiliary disciplines such as paleography, epigraphy, and diplomatics to detect forgeries and corruptions. Internal criticism (hermeneutics) interrogates the author's meaning, competence, bias, and proximity to events. A primary source is not accepted as transparent fact, but as situated testimony whose purpose, audience, and presuppositions must be critically examined before it yields reliable evidence.

## Classical Arabic and Islamic historiography

Arabic and Islamic historiography is among the largest and most methodologically self-conscious historical traditions. Originating in the seventh and eighth centuries CE to record the mission of the Prophet Muhammad and early Islamic conquests, historical inquiry developed through *sirah* (prophetic biography), *maghazi* (campaigns), *futuh* (conquest literature), and *ansab* (genealogies).

To distinguish authentic reports (*akhbar*) from sectarian fabrication, scholars adapted the *isnad* (chain of transmitters) developed in hadith scholarship into a formal instrument of source criticism. Under *'ilm al-jarh wa-l-ta'dil* (impugning and validating transmitters), biographical dictionaries (*kutub al-rijal*) documented the moral integrity (*'adalah*), accuracy (*dabt*), and lifespans of narrators to verify whether continuous contact between transmitters was historically possible.

This documentation culminated in universal chronicles, notably the *Tarikh al-Rusul wa-l-Muluk* by Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari (224–310 AH / 839–923 CE). Al-Tabari arranged history annalistically year by year from Creation to 302 AH, recording multiple, sometimes conflicting *isnads* for the same event without forcibly reconciling them, placing raw evaluated testimonies before the reader.

A major theoretical development came with 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Khaldun (732–808 AH / 1332–1406 CE). In the *Muqaddimah* (779 AH / 1377 CE), Ibn Khaldun observed that transmitter criticism cannot guarantee truth if the reported content (*matn*) violates the inherent laws of human society. He developed *'ilm al-'umran* (the science of human social organization) and *naqd al-akhbar*—the internal critique of reports measured against ecological conditions, modes of production (nomadic *badawa* versus sedentary *hadara*), demographic capacities, and *'asabiyyah* (group cohesion and social solidarity). He argued that political dynasties follow recurring developmental cycles driven by cohesion and state expenditure, an approach often compared with later sociological historiography.

## Major schools and paradigms

Modern historical thought has evolved through successive paradigms:

- **Rankean Empiricism:** Leopold von Ranke (1795–1886) was a leading figure in the rise of professional history in nineteenth-century Germany, championing archival research, philological rigor, and the ideal of recounting the past "as it essentially was" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*).
- **Marxist Historiography:** Grounded in historical materialism, scholars analyzed modes of production, class conflict, and structural change. British Marxist historians such as E. P. Thompson (*The Making of the English Working Class*, 1963) and Eric Hobsbawm helped popularise "history from below," recovering the agency and struggles of ordinary working people.
- **The Annales School:** Founded in 1929 by Marc Bloch and Lucien Febvre, this movement rejected short-term political-event history (*histoire événementielle*). Fernand Braudel introduced a tripartite temporality highlighting the *longue durée*—the slow geographic and environmental structures shaping human activity—alongside conjunctures and events.
- **Microhistory:** Developing in Italy during the 1970s with Carlo Ginzburg (*The Cheese and the Worms*, 1976) and Giovanni Levi, *microstoria* reduced the scale of observation to intensive scrutiny of individuals or local communities, using the "exceptional normal" to illuminate wider cultural mentalities.
- **Subaltern and Postcolonial History:** The Subaltern Studies collective, initiated by Ranajit Guha in the 1980s, critiqued colonial and nationalist archives for ignoring non-elite actors. Dipesh Chakrabarty (*Provincializing Europe*, 2000) argued that European historical categories were universalized while marginalizing non-Western experiences.
- **Global and Connected History:** Contemporary historians such as Sanjay Subrahmanyam explore connected histories that trace trans-regional linkages, maritime networks, and cross-cultural exchanges beyond the nation-state.

## Memory, narrative, and the linguistic turn

The late twentieth century witnessed critical debates on historical knowledge. Maurice Halbwachs and Pierre Nora explored the tension between collective memory and critical history. While memory is present-oriented, emotive, and tied to group identity, history is an analytical discipline that continually tests and dismantles commemorative myths. Nora's *lieux de mémoire* examined how modern societies create institutional sites of remembrance as living traditions fade.

Concurrently, the linguistic turn challenged documentary positivism. In *Metahistory* (1973), Hayden White argued that historical works are narrative structures shaped by literary tropes and modes of emplotment. While historians remain accountable to primary evidence, historical synthesis requires rhetorical composition. Theorists such as Michel-Rolph Trouillot (*Silencing the Past*, 1995) and Ann Laura Stoler (*Along the Archival Grain*, 2009) argued that archives embody state power, creating structural silences that require reading along and against the archival grain.
