# Bridges Between Disciplines

This is the root entry of the Bridges module. It explains what a bridge entry is, how to read one, which families of bridges the module contains, and where its limits lie. Read it before you open any individual bridge.

## What a Bridge Entry Is

Most entries in the atlas describe one thing: a school, a thinker, a concept, a study. A bridge entry describes a relation between two or more of them. It is a structured comparison or link between schools, concepts or disciplines, and it is written to answer three questions. What connects these two things? Why is the link worth drawing? Where does it stop holding?

The third question matters as much as the first. A bridge that lists only similarities is advertising, not analysis. A good bridge entry names the points of real contact, the points of real divergence, and the reasons why the same word or practice may mean different things on each side. It also records the history of contact where this is documented, and says plainly where it is not.

## How to Read a Bridge

Start with the stated reason for the link. Bridges are drawn for different reasons, and the reason tells you what kind of claim you are reading. Some bridges record a documented historical exchange, such as a thinker who read and answered another. Some record a structural parallel, where two traditions reached similar ideas independently. Some record a practical convergence, where clinicians or practitioners combine methods. Some record a tension between incompatible answers to the same question.

Then read the limits: each bridge should say what the comparison does not show. Finally, follow the links to the entries on each side.

## Comparison Is Not Influence

The central discipline of this module is the difference between a comparison and an influence claim. An influence claim says that A shaped B, and it needs evidence: letters, citations, teaching relationships, translations, documented reading. A comparison says only that A and B can be set side by side with illuminating results. Two ideas may resemble each other because of shared sources, shared problems, parallel development, or simply because our own vocabulary makes them look alike.

Quentin Skinner's 1969 essay on meaning and understanding in the history of ideas is a standard statement of the danger of assuming that a later idea was anticipated by an earlier one because the words are similar. Where a bridge in this atlas asserts influence, it should cite the evidence. Where it asserts only comparison, it should say so. If you find a bridge that blurs the two, treat the blur as a flaw of the entry, not as a finding.

## The Main Families of Bridges

The module's contents can be grouped into several families. The grouping is a reading aid, not a classification imposed by the sources, and some bridges belong to more than one family.

### Between psychotherapy schools

This is the densest family. It includes the relation between behaviorism and cognitive psychology, in which the cognitive approach emerged partly from within the behavioral tradition and partly against it; the relation between psychoanalysis and cognitive-behavioral therapy, which are often presented as opposites yet share concerns about how past learning shapes present reaction; the relation between humanistic and positive psychology, which share an interest in flourishing but differ in method and in their attitude to measurement; the relation between existential and phenomenological approaches, which share philosophical roots in the study of lived experience; and the relations among somatic approaches, which work through the body, and systemic approaches, which treat the family or group as the unit of understanding. Further bridges connect therapies to particular problems, such as addiction, where methods from several schools are combined in practice.

### Between psychology, philosophy, religion and culture

A second family connects psychological ideas to older traditions of thought about the person. Examples include comparisons between ancient ethical philosophy and modern accounts of well-being, between contemplative traditions and mindfulness-based clinical methods, and between Western clinical categories and the cultural frameworks in which distress is understood elsewhere. These bridges are especially prone to over-reading, because the vocabularies are far apart and translation always changes meaning.

### Thematic bridges across modules

The module root also anticipates bridges on larger themes that cut across the atlas: mind and language, self and society, power and law, memory and history, culture and diagnosis, justice and responsibility, ritual and the sacred, and evidence and method. These are organising themes, and the bridges under them vary in how fully they are developed.

## Cautions

**Integration claims.** Calling a therapy "integrative" says that it combines elements of different schools. It does not say that the combination works better than its parts, or that the schools' underlying theories are compatible. Combining techniques and reconciling theories are different achievements. The integration literature distinguishes several routes, including technical eclecticism, theoretical integration and common factors, each with its own assumptions.

**Common-factors evidence.** The common-factors idea holds that features shared across therapies, such as the relationship between client and therapist, an agreed rationale and the expectation of improvement, account for much of what helps. The idea has a long history, going back at least to Saul Rosenzweig's 1936 paper and developed by Jerome Frank. Bruce Wampold's later work argues that shared factors matter more than specific techniques. This is a serious position, but it is contested, and the interpretation of the comparative outcome literature is debated. A bridge entry that cites common factors should not present the question as settled in either direction.

**Translation and context.** A concept that moves between a clinical setting and a philosophical or religious tradition changes its function. A bridge should not treat a practice taken from a religious tradition and used in a clinic as the same thing it was in its original setting.

## How the Atlas Is Organised

Entries are of several types: fields, schools, currents, thinkers, concepts, studies and relations. Fields give an overview of a domain. Schools and currents describe bodies of thought. Thinker, concept and study entries give detail. Relation and bridge entries connect these. Each entry has a type, a level, a short path of navigation, and a list of related entries that a build process fills in from the links between them. Entries may also carry a list of known gaps, which are points that need further sourcing or review. Reading these gaps is part of reading the entry honestly.

A suggested path: read the entries for the two sides you want to connect, then the bridge, so that you can judge it instead of taking its description on trust.

## Limits of This Version

This is a first version. Coverage of bridges is uneven: the family of psychotherapy schools is much better developed than the family linking psychology to philosophy, religion and culture, and some of the thematic bridges named above are still thin or missing. Many bridge entries carry open gaps that note claims needing further sourcing. Entries are for orientation and study, not clinical guidance, and do not replace the primary works.
