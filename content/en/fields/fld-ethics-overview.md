# Ethics (Overview)

Ethics, or moral philosophy, is the systematic study of how we ought to live: what is good and bad, right and wrong, virtuous and vicious, and what we owe to one another. This entry is a short overview within the Philosophy module. The atlas treats ethics at length in its own Ethics module, which should be consulted for detailed accounts of theories, thinkers and applied questions. It sits beside philosophy because ethics is historically one of the oldest philosophical branches, and because its questions depend on, and in turn shape, work in metaphysics, epistemology, philosophy of mind and political philosophy.

## What ethics asks

Ethics asks several kinds of question. How should I live, and what makes a life good? What makes actions right or wrong? What kind of person should I be? How do I weigh my interests against others'? Are there moral truths, and how could we know them? It is traditionally divided into three areas, which overlap.

- **Normative ethics** develops general theories of right action and good character.
- **Metaethics** investigates the nature, meaning and status of moral claims.
- **Applied ethics** examines concrete issues such as medicine, war, business, animals, technology and the environment.

## Normative ethics: virtue, duty, consequences

Three families of theory dominate, though many philosophers combine or reject them.

**Virtue ethics** gives priority to character. Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* (4th century BCE) holds that the human good is *eudaimonia*, flourishing, achieved by exercising the virtues, such as courage, temperance and justice, guided by practical wisdom (*phronesis*) and understood as a mean between extremes. Confucian ethics, in the *Analects*, similarly stresses cultivating virtues such as *ren* (humaneness) and ritual propriety, and Buddhist ethics stresses compassion and the path of liberation. Modern revival came with G. E. M. Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958), Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981) and Rosalind Hursthouse. The standard objection is that virtue ethics gives insufficient guidance for action, and that virtues vary across cultures.

**Deontology** holds that some acts are required or forbidden regardless of consequences. Immanuel Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) grounds morality in reason and the **categorical imperative**, one formulation of which commands acting only on maxims one could will as universal law, and another of which commands treating humanity never merely as a means. W. D. Ross's *The Right and the Good* (1930) proposed several irreducible *prima facie* duties. Deontology is criticised for rigidity in conflict cases, for example when lying would save a life, and for how it should settle conflicts among duties.

**Consequentialism** holds that rightness depends on outcomes. Classical **utilitarianism**, advocated by Jeremy Bentham in *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789) and John Stuart Mill in *Utilitarianism* (1863), says to maximise happiness or well-being impartially. Henry Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics* (1874) gave it rigorous form. Peter Singer applied it to animals and global poverty. Objections include that it may permit sacrificing individuals for aggregate benefit, that it is demanding, and that outcomes are hard to measure; rule consequentialism and prioritarian variants answer some of these.

Other approaches include **contractualism** (T. M. Scanlon's *What We Owe to Each Other*, 1998), **care ethics** (Carol Gilligan, Nel Noddings), **natural law**, divine command theories and ethical particularism. Islamic ethics draws on Quranic and prophetic sources, philosophical virtue ethics (Miskawayh, al-Ghazali), and legal-theological discussion of the good. Indian traditions discuss *dharma*, karma, *ahimsa* and the aims of life. African ethics often stresses communal personhood, as in ubuntu.

## Metaethics

Metaethics asks what we are doing when we make moral claims. **Moral realists** hold that there are objective moral facts; **anti-realists** deny this. Prominent options include **error theory** (J. L. Mackie's *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong*, 1977), which says moral claims purport to be objective but are all false; **expressivism** (A. J. Ayer, Charles Stevenson, Simon Blackburn, Allan Gibbard), on which moral statements express attitudes; **constructivism** (Christine Korsgaard); and **relativism**. G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica* (1903) argued that "good" is a simple, indefinable property and that defining it naturalistically commits the "naturalistic fallacy"; his open-question argument remains debated. David Hume's observation that one cannot straightforwardly derive "ought" from "is" frames much of the discussion. Questions about moral epistemology, moral motivation and the evolutionary origins of morality connect metaethics with psychology and biology.

## Applied ethics and further debates

Bioethics, environmental ethics, business ethics, ethics of war, animal ethics, and the ethics of technology and artificial intelligence apply the theories to specific cases. Experimental philosophy and moral psychology, such as Joshua Greene's work on dual-process judgement and Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionism, test claims about how moral thinking works. Free will and moral responsibility link ethics to metaphysics, while questions of justice link it to political philosophy.

Ethics has a very large literature and an equally large range of applications, so the atlas gives it its own space where theories, thinkers and cases can be treated in depth. ## Common misunderstandings

Ethics is not the same as religion or law: acts may be legal but wrong, or illegal but right, and moral philosophy can be pursued without religious premises, though religious ethics is a major tradition. Moral disagreement does not entail that relativism is true. "Utilitarian" does not mean selfish, since it counts everyone's welfare impartially. And ethical theories are not simply recipes: they are frameworks for reflection that practitioners apply with judgement.
