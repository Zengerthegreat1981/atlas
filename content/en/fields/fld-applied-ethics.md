# Applied Ethics

Applied ethics examines concrete moral problems in medicine, research, the environment, business, technology and the professions, using the concepts and arguments of normative ethics and the methods of careful case analysis. It grew rapidly from the 1960s and 1970s, when developments such as organ transplantation, intensive care, human experimentation scandals and environmental concern posed questions that earlier moral philosophy had not addressed.

## What the field asks

Applied ethics asks what should be done, by whom and why, in specific domains. Is it permissible to end a life at a patient's request? What may researchers do to human participants? What do we owe to future generations and to non-human animals? What responsibilities do firms have beyond profit? How should societies govern new technologies such as genetic editing and artificial intelligence? It also asks methodological questions: how general theories bear on cases, who has the authority to decide, and what role institutions, law and public deliberation play.

## Main areas

**Bioethics and medical ethics** address clinical care, reproduction, end of life, organ allocation and public health. Tom Beauchamp and James Childress's *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (first edition 1979) popularised four principles: respect for autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence and justice. Debates include informed consent, abortion, euthanasia and assisted dying, the allocation of scarce resources and the use of genetic technologies. Judith Jarvis Thomson's "A Defense of Abortion" (1971) is a much-discussed argument in the abortion debate, and Don Marquis's "Why Abortion Is Immoral" (1989) offers an influential opposing argument. Islamic bioethics draws on jurisprudence and the objectives of the law (*maqasid al-shari'a*), with fatwas and juristic councils addressing questions such as organ donation, brain death and assisted reproduction, and positions vary among schools and scholars.

**Research ethics** governs the treatment of human and animal subjects. The Nuremberg Code (1947), the Declaration of Helsinki (first adopted 1964) and the Belmont Report (1979) established standards of consent, risk and fair selection, shaped in part by abuses such as the Tuskegee syphilis study. Current issues include data privacy, research integrity and the use of vulnerable populations.

**Environmental ethics** asks whether nature has intrinsic value and what we owe to non-human life and future generations. Aldo Leopold's land ethic in *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), Arne Naess's deep ecology, and Holmes Rolston III's work defend non-anthropocentric views, while others defend anthropocentric or stewardship approaches. Peter Singer's *Animal Liberation* (1975) and Tom Regan's *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983) shaped animal ethics from consequentialist and rights perspectives. Climate ethics concerns responsibility, historical emissions and obligations to the global poor and to future people, a theme in the work of Stephen Gardiner and Henry Shue. Many religious traditions, including Islam, with its idea of humans as stewards (*khalifa*) on earth, have developed environmental ethics.

**Business and technology ethics** includes corporate responsibility, stakeholder theory (R. Edward Freeman, 1984), whistleblowing, fair labour practices and the ethics of markets. Milton Friedman's 1970 essay argued that a firm's social responsibility is to increase its profits within the rules, a claim critics contest. Islamic finance, with its prohibition of *riba* (interest) and emphasis on risk sharing and avoiding excessive uncertainty (*gharar*), offers a distinctive normative framework. Technology ethics addresses privacy, surveillance, bias in algorithms, autonomous systems and the governance of artificial intelligence, drawing on work such as Luciano Floridi's *The Ethics of Information* (2013) and Shannon Vallor's *Technology and the Virtues* (2016).

**Professional ethics** covers codes of conduct and the duties of physicians, lawyers, engineers, journalists and others, including confidentiality, conflicts of interest and public accountability. Other areas include the ethics of war and peace, where just war theory, developed by Augustine and Aquinas and restated by Michael Walzer in *Just and Unjust Wars* (1977), is a major framework, and political and global ethics, including poverty, migration and justice across borders.

## Method of case analysis

Applied ethicists commonly follow a structured process. They establish the facts, identify the stakeholders and the values at stake, state the options, and analyse them through several theoretical lenses, such as consequences, duties and rights, character and relationships. They then weigh considerations and justify a conclusion, while noting what remains uncertain. Principlism, casuistry, which reasons by analogy from paradigm cases (Albert Jonsen and Stephen Toulmin, *The Abuse of Casuistry*, 1988), and approaches based on virtues and care each offer different ways of connecting theory to cases. Clinical ethics consultation and institutional review boards put such methods into practice.

## Debates and objections

One debate concerns method. "Top-down" approaches apply a theory to cases, while "bottom-up" approaches start from considered judgements about cases and build principles. Critics of principlism, such as K. Danner Clouser and Bernard Gert, argue that the four principles lack systematic unity; defenders say they work as a common framework for people with different theories. Another debate concerns whether applied ethics is too tied to Western liberal assumptions, especially the primacy of individual autonomy; feminist, relational and communitarian critiques, and perspectives from non-Western traditions, emphasise family, community and duty. Others ask whether ethicists have any special expertise, and whether their conclusions should bind policy. Within each area there are standard disputes: the moral status of embryos and animals, whether killing differs from letting die, the permissibility of paying research participants or organ donors, the limits of free markets, and how to weigh present against future persons.

## Connections to other disciplines

Applied ethics is interdisciplinary by nature. Medicine, nursing and public health supply the clinical and epidemiological context. Law shapes and is shaped by it through doctrines of consent, liability and rights. Political economy informs debates on markets, distribution and regulation. Psychology and behavioural science describe how people actually decide, including cognitive biases that affect judgement in professional settings. Religious studies and anthropology document how communities frame questions of birth, death, property and nature, and engineering and computer science are essential to technology ethics.

## Common misunderstandings

Applied ethics is not the mechanical application of a theory, and a case does not have a single obvious answer. Ethics is not the same as legal compliance: the law may permit what is wrong or forbid what is right. Ethics committees do not simply vote on morality; they provide structured reasoning and advice within institutions. Different traditions can converge on practical conclusions while disagreeing about reasons, and disagreeing conclusions need not mean that one side is irrational. Finally, a reasoned argument in applied ethics is not a prediction of what a society will accept.
