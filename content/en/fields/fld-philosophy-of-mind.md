# Philosophy of Mind

Philosophy of mind investigates the nature of mental states and processes, such as thoughts, sensations, emotions and intentions, and their relation to the body, the brain and the physical world. It asks what the mind is, how it differs from matter if it does, why there is experience at all, and how minds can be about things.

## What the branch asks

The central issue is the **mind-body problem**: how can conscious, meaningful mental life belong to a physical world? Allied questions include: What is consciousness? How can mental states represent or be "about" things? Can mental states cause behaviour? How do we know other minds? What is the self? Could a machine think or be conscious?

## Dualism

**Substance dualism** holds that mind and body are distinct kinds of thing. Its classic statement is by Rene Descartes in the *Meditations* (1641), who argued that the mind is a thinking, non-extended substance. The main objection, pressed by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia in her correspondence with Descartes (1643), is the **interaction problem**: how can a non-physical thing causally affect a physical one? Descartes's own answers are generally considered unsatisfying. **Property dualism** holds that there is only one kind of substance but that it has both physical and irreducibly mental properties. David Chalmers, in *The Conscious Mind* (1996), defends a form of property dualism, arguing that the existence of experience is not logically entailed by physical facts. Dualist-leaning ideas appear also in Indian Samkhya, which distinguishes consciousness (*purusha*) from matter (*prakriti*), while Buddhist schools reject an enduring conscious self.

## Physicalism

**Physicalism** or materialism holds that everything, including the mind, is physical. **Behaviourism**, in the work of Gilbert Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949), analysed mental talk as talk of dispositions to behave and criticised the "ghost in the machine". **Identity theory**, associated with U. T. Place and J. J. C. Smart in the 1950s, identifies mental states with brain states. Objections include **multiple realisability**, the thought that the same mental state could be realised in different physical systems, as argued by Hilary Putnam in the 1960s. **Eliminative materialism**, defended by Paul and Patricia Churchland, predicts that folk psychology concepts such as belief may be replaced by neuroscience.

## Functionalism

**Functionalism** defines mental states by their causal roles: what causes them, and what they cause in turn, in relation to other states. It allows multiple realisation and underlies much cognitive science, which models the mind as information processing. Objections include John Searle's **Chinese Room** argument (1980), which claims that running a program cannot suffice for understanding; Ned Block's China brain and absent-qualia cases; and worries that functionalism leaves out the subjective character of experience.

## Consciousness

Thomas Nagel's essay "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974) argued that subjective experience resists objective, third-person description. Frank Jackson's knowledge argument (1982), with the story of Mary who knows all the physical facts about colour but has never seen it, and Chalmers's **hard problem** of consciousness (1995) contrast easy problems about function with the problem of why there is experience. Physicalist responses include higher-order theories (David Rosenthal), representationalism (Michael Tye, Fred Dretske), global workspace theories (Bernard Baars and Stanislas Dehaene), and illusionism (Keith Frankish, drawing on Daniel Dennett's *Consciousness Explained*, 1991). Integrated information theory (Giulio Tononi) and panpsychism, defended in recent work by Philip Goff and Galen Strawson, are alternative or controversial views. There is no consensus.

## Intentionality

Franz Brentano revived the medieval idea that mental states are "directed at" objects as a mark of the mental. Theories of **mental content** try to naturalise this: causal-informational accounts (Dretske, Jerry Fodor), teleosemantic accounts (Ruth Millikan), and interpretationist accounts (Dennett, Donald Davidson). Fodor's *language of thought* hypothesis holds that thinking involves a symbolic medium. Connectionism offers a rival picture based on neural-network models. The **extended mind** thesis of Andy Clark and David Chalmers (1998) proposes that cognitive processes can extend into tools and environment. Embodied and enactive approaches likewise emphasise the role of the body.

## Mental causation, other minds and the self

If the physical world is causally closed, how do mental states do any causing? Donald Davidson's anomalous monism and Jaegwon Kim's exclusion argument frame this problem. The **problem of other minds** asks how we justify attributing minds to others, with answers from analogy, inference to the best explanation, and direct perception. Personal identity and the nature of the self are treated in the metaphysics entry; Hume's bundle view, Parfit's reductionism, and Buddhist no-self teachings are central comparisons.

## Artificial intelligence

Alan Turing's 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" proposed an imitation game as a test of machine intelligence. Whether current or future AI systems could understand, think or be conscious is debated: functionalists see no principled barrier to machine minds, while Searle and others argue that syntax is insufficient for semantics. Large language models have renewed interest, but the question of what evidence would settle machine consciousness is unresolved.

## Connections

The field draws on neuroscience, psychology, linguistics and computer science, and informs metaphysics (causation, identity), epistemology (perception, self-knowledge), philosophy of language (meaning), ethics (moral status, animal and machine minds) and philosophy of science (explanation of mental phenomena).

## Common misunderstandings

Dualism is not simply religious belief, and physicalism does not mean consciousness is not real, since most physicalists accept that experiences exist. The Turing test is not a definition of mind. Brain scans correlating activity with experience do not by themselves solve the mind-body problem. Finally, "consciousness" covers several things, such as wakefulness, self-awareness and phenomenal experience, which should be distinguished.
