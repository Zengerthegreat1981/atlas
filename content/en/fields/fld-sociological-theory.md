# Sociological Theory

Sociological theory is the body of concepts, models and explanatory arguments that sociologists use to describe and explain social life. It includes grand frameworks about the nature of society, "middle-range" explanations of particular phenomena, and the metatheoretical debate about what a social explanation should look like. It is both a historical canon and a living research area.

## What the field asks

Theory addresses a cluster of foundational questions. What is a society, and is it more than the sum of individuals? How do structure and agency relate? What is the role of meaning, norms, interests and power in explaining action? What drives social change? How far can social science resemble natural science, and how far must it interpret meanings? How should the researcher's own social position and values bear on knowledge?

## Classical theory

The classical canon typically centres on Marx, Durkheim, Weber and Simmel, although the canon itself is contested.

- **Karl Marx** (1818-1883) explained social order and change through the organisation of production and class relations. *Capital* (1867) analyses capitalist accumulation and exploitation; *The German Ideology* (written 1845-46) sets out historical materialism.
- **Émile Durkheim** (1858-1917) held that social facts exist as external, constraining realities. In *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) he contrasted mechanical and organic solidarity; in *Suicide* (1897) he linked rates of suicide to social integration and regulation; in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) he treated religion as the society's representation of itself.
- **Max Weber** (1864-1920) defined sociology as the interpretive understanding of social action. His work on rationalisation, authority types (traditional, charismatic, legal-rational), and the relation of religious ethics to economic life (*The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism*, 1904-05) shaped later theory. He also separated class, status and party as dimensions of power.
- **Georg Simmel** (1858-1918) analysed "forms of sociation", such as the dyad, the stranger and conflict, and the psychological effects of urban and monetary life.
- **Other founders** include Harriet Martineau, W. E. B. Du Bois, whose idea of "double consciousness" in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903) became central to the study of race, and Ibn Khaldun, whose *Muqaddimah* (1377) offers a theory of group solidarity (*asabiyya*) and political cycles that modern scholars read as an early social theory.

## Mid-century theory

After the Second World War American sociology was shaped by **Talcott Parsons** (*The Structure of Social Action*, 1937; *The Social System*, 1951), who built a systems framework in which institutions meet functional requirements. **Robert K. Merton** (*Social Theory and Social Structure*, 1949) proposed "theories of the middle range" and the distinction between manifest and latent functions. **C. Wright Mills** (*The Power Elite*, 1956; *The Sociological Imagination*, 1959) attacked both abstract grand theory and narrow empiricism. Conflict-oriented responses came from Ralf Dahrendorf (*Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society*, 1959), Lewis Coser and later Randall Collins (*Conflict Sociology*, 1975).

## Interactionism and interpretive approaches

George Herbert Mead (*Mind, Self and Society*, 1934, from lecture notes) argued that the self arises in social interaction. Herbert Blumer named and systematised **symbolic interactionism**. Erving Goffman's dramaturgical studies (*The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, 1959) examined impression management. Harold Garfinkel's **ethnomethodology** (*Studies in Ethnomethodology*, 1967) studied the methods by which people make everyday order accountable. Alfred Schutz's phenomenology and Berger and Luckmann's *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966) tied meaning to institutionalisation.

## Critical, feminist, postcolonial and decolonial theory

Critical theory, associated with the Frankfurt School (Horkheimer, Adorno, Marcuse) and later Jürgen Habermas (*The Theory of Communicative Action*, 1981), links social analysis to critique of domination. Michel Foucault's work on discipline and power (*Discipline and Punish*, 1975) influenced sociology widely, though he was not himself a sociologist. Feminist theory, including Dorothy Smith's standpoint approach and Patricia Hill Collins's *Black Feminist Thought* (1990), argues that knowledge is shaped by social location. Postcolonial and decolonial work, for example by Frantz Fanon, Syed Hussein Alatas, Raewyn Connell (*Southern Theory*, 2007) and Latin American dependency theorists, criticises the universalising claims of a canon formed in imperial centres and proposes alternative histories of the discipline.

## Structure, agency and synthesis

From the 1970s authors tried to overcome the opposition between structure and agency. **Anthony Giddens** (*The Constitution of Society*, 1984) proposed structuration, in which structures are both medium and outcome of action. **Pierre Bourdieu** (*Outline of a Theory of Practice*, 1972; *Distinction*, 1979) used the concepts of habitus, field and forms of capital. James Coleman (*Foundations of Social Theory*, 1990) pursued a rational-choice route, and Margaret Archer's critical realism (*Realist Social Theory*, 1995) argued for analytically separating structure and agency over time. Relational approaches, as in Mustafa Emirbayer's "Manifesto for a Relational Sociology" (1997), treat networks and relations as the basic units.

## Major debates and objections

- **Structure versus agency.** Functionalism and structural Marxism are accused of treating people as bearers of roles; interactionism and rational choice are accused of ignoring constraints that individuals do not choose.
- **Order versus conflict.** Functionalists were criticised for implicit conservatism, and conflict theorists for underplaying cooperation and shared norms.
- **Explanation versus understanding.** Positivist and interpretive traditions disagree about whether social science seeks laws or meaning, and Weber's attempt to combine causal explanation with interpretive understanding remains influential.
- **Micro versus macro.** Collins, Coleman and others have asked how large-scale phenomena emerge from, or are irreducible to, interaction.
- **Methodological individualism.** Whether explanations must ultimately refer to individuals is disputed by holists in the Durkheimian tradition.
- **Universalism and the canon.** Who counts as a classic, and whether theories rooted in Europe travel, are live questions.
- **Value neutrality.** Weber argued for separating empirical analysis from value judgements; critical traditions reply that neutrality is neither possible nor desirable.

## Methods of theorising

Theory is built by conceptual analysis, by constructing ideal types (Weber), by formal and mathematical modelling, by comparative-historical reasoning, by abstracting from case studies (grounded theory, Glaser and Strauss, 1967) and by reinterpretation of the canon. Theories are assessed for logical coherence, empirical adequacy, scope and fruitfulness.

## Connections to other disciplines

Theory borrows from philosophy (action theory, hermeneutics, philosophy of social science), economics (rational choice, institutions), psychology (self, identity and cognition), anthropology (culture, kinship, ritual), history (long-run change) and law (norms and institutions).

## Common misunderstandings

Theories are not simply opinions or political programmes, nor are the classics a single school: Marx, Durkheim and Weber disagreed on basic questions. Functionalism is often caricatured as a defence of the status quo, and interactionism as concerned only with trivia. Reading a classic author as a source of timeless truths ignores the historical contexts in which they wrote and the limits of their evidence.
