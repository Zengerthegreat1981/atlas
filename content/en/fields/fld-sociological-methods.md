# Sociological Methods

Sociological methods are the procedures by which sociologists gather evidence about social life and use it to describe patterns, test explanations and interpret meanings. The field covers data collection (surveys, experiments, observation, interviews, archives, digital traces), data analysis (statistics, comparative logic, coding of texts, network measures) and the methodology that connects them to theory, including debates about measurement, causation, generalisation and ethics.

## What the field asks

Methodological work asks how we can know something reliable about social phenomena when we cannot usually run controlled experiments on whole societies, when the people studied can reinterpret and react to research, and when concepts such as class, trust or discrimination are not directly observable. Main questions include: how are abstract concepts turned into measures? How can a sample represent a population? When can an association be read as causal? What do qualitative studies establish, given that they rest on few cases? What do researchers owe the people they study?

## Foundations

Methodological reflection is as old as the discipline. Harriet Martineau's *How to Observe Manners and Morals* (1838) offered an early guide to systematic observation. Durkheim, in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), argued that social facts should be treated as things and used official statistics in *Suicide* (1897). Weber advocated interpretive understanding (*Verstehen*) together with causal explanation. W. E. B. Du Bois's *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899) combined a house-to-house survey with historical and ethnographic material. In the early twentieth century, Thomas and Znaniecki's *The Polish Peasant in Europe and America* (1918-20) used letters and life documents, and the Chicago School developed urban fieldwork. Paul Lazarsfeld and colleagues built quantitative survey research and panel studies from the 1930s onward, and Samuel Stouffer's wartime *The American Soldier* studies applied it at scale.

## Main approaches

- **Survey research.** Standardised questionnaires administered to probability samples allow estimation of population characteristics. Strengths are breadth and comparability, as in long-running national and cross-national surveys. Weaknesses include non-response, question-wording effects and social desirability bias.
- **Experiments.** Random assignment supports strong causal claims. Sociologists use laboratory experiments (for example, in studies of group status and cooperation), survey experiments such as vignettes, and field and audit experiments, such as sending matched applications to employers to test for discrimination (Bertrand and Mullainathan, 2004; Devah Pager, 2003).
- **Ethnography and participant observation.** Prolonged immersion yields detailed knowledge of practices and meanings. Examples include William Foote Whyte's *Street Corner Society* (1943) and Michael Burawoy's *Manufacturing Consent* (1979). The extended case method uses a case to reconstruct and revise theory.
- **Interviews.** Structured, semi-structured and life-history interviews elicit accounts of experience; focus groups study collective talk. Annette Lareau's *Unequal Childhoods* (2003) combined interviews with home observation.
- **Comparative and historical methods.** Researchers compare a small number of cases using logics such as Mill's methods of agreement and difference (Theda Skocpol, *States and Social Revolutions*, 1979) or set-theoretic approaches (Charles Ragin, *The Comparative Method*, 1987), and use archives and process tracing.
- **Network analysis.** Relations between actors are mapped and measured. Mark Granovetter's "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) is a classic example.
- **Content and discourse analysis, and computational methods.** Texts, images and digital traces are coded or modelled, now often with machine-learning tools, raising new issues of representativeness and privacy.
- **Mixed methods.** Combining approaches can offset the weaknesses of each, for instance by pairing survey patterns with interviews that explain them.

## Measurement

Measurement links concepts to indicators. Researchers work with notions of reliability (consistency) and validity (does the measure capture the concept?), build scales and indices, and confront the problem of comparability across cultures and periods. Debates concern, for example, how to measure social class (occupational schemes, income, wealth, status), race and ethnicity (self-identification, ascription, historically changing categories), and gender. Measurement choices are not neutral: classifications shape what can be seen.

## Causal inference

Sociologists have drawn heavily on the counterfactual framework and graphical causal models (Morgan and Winship, *Counterfactuals and Causal Inference*, 2007; Judea Pearl, *Causality*, 2000), using methods such as matching, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, regression discontinuity, panel models and natural experiments. Critics warn against treating regression coefficients as causal without justification, and some, such as Stanley Lieberson (*Making It Count*, 1985), long stressed the limits of statistical control. A related debate concerns mechanism-based explanation, which seeks to specify the social processes producing an association.

## Major debates and objections

- **Quantitative versus qualitative.** The rivalry is often overdrawn; each addresses different questions, and ongoing work asks for shared standards of rigour, such as those in King, Keohane and Verba's *Designing Social Inquiry* (1994), which was in turn criticised by qualitative researchers.
- **Generalisation.** Large samples allow statistical generalisation; case studies allow theoretical generalisation. Whether either licenses claims across contexts is disputed.
- **Reflexivity and positionality.** Researchers influence the field they study. Feminist and postcolonial methodologists argue for acknowledging power relations between researchers and participants.
- **Reproducibility.** Concerns about selective reporting, small samples and flexible analysis have prompted pre-registration, data sharing and replication projects, in sociology as in other social sciences.
- **Prediction and big data.** Whether new data sources enable better explanation or only better forecasting is debated.

## Ethics

Social research raises issues of informed consent, confidentiality, harm, deception and the protection of vulnerable groups. Modern research ethics draws on the Belmont Report (1979) and institutional review. Controversial past cases, such as Laud Humphreys's *Tearoom Trade* (1970), which involved covert observation, are standard teaching examples. Ethnographers also face dilemmas about anonymising communities and about what to disclose to authorities.

## Connections to other disciplines

Methods overlap with statistics and econometrics, psychology (experimental design and psychometrics), anthropology (fieldwork), history (archival method), political science (comparative methods), computer science (text and network analysis) and law (evidence and discrimination testing).

## Common misunderstandings

Numbers do not speak for themselves: statistical output depends on measurement and modelling choices. Qualitative research is not merely anecdote, since it uses systematic sampling of cases and disciplined analysis, though its claims are different in kind. A significant correlation is not a cause, and a failed prediction does not automatically refute a theory. No method is best in the abstract; fit to the question matters.
