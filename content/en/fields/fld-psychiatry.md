# Psychiatry

Psychiatry is the branch of medicine concerned with the understanding, assessment, prevention and treatment of mental disorders. Its practitioners are physicians who have specialised after medical training, which distinguishes them from psychologists, counsellors and social workers, though all of these professions work with the same human problems. This entry introduces the Psychiatry module. It is educational and descriptive. It is not clinical advice, and nothing in the atlas should be used to diagnose or treat anyone. Anyone worried about their own mental health or that of another person should consult a qualified professional or local emergency services.

## What psychiatry is

Psychiatry deals with conditions in which thought, mood, perception, behaviour or relationships are disturbed in ways that cause distress or impair functioning. Examples named in the standard classifications include depressive and bipolar disorders, schizophrenia and related psychoses, anxiety disorders, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, eating disorders, substance use disorders, neurodevelopmental conditions and dementias. The field sits between neurology, which handles diseases with identifiable structural brain pathology, and psychology, which studies mind and behaviour more broadly. It also borders on public health, law and ethics, because decisions about mental disorder can involve consent, risk and the limits of personal liberty.

## A short history

The word "psychiatry" was coined in the early nineteenth century by Johann Christian Reil. Before that, madness was handled by families, religious institutions and, from the seventeenth century, increasingly by confinement. The late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries saw reformers such as Philippe Pinel in France and William Tuke in England promote "moral treatment" in asylums, which emphasised humane care and routine. The asylum later grew into very large institutions, often overcrowded and custodial.

In the late nineteenth century Emil Kraepelin, in successive editions of his textbook, distinguished dementia praecox, later called schizophrenia, from manic-depressive illness on the basis of course and outcome. This approach to classification is often called neo-Kraepelinian in its modern form. Around the same time Sigmund Freud developed psychoanalysis, notably in *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1899), which treated symptoms as meaningful and shaped psychiatry, especially in the United States, for much of the twentieth century.

Mid-century brought the introduction of chlorpromazine in the early 1950s and other drugs, which changed treatment and contributed, together with policy and cost pressures, to deinstitutionalisation, the movement of care from large hospitals to community settings. The results were mixed and are still debated. From 1980 the third edition of the American Psychiatric Association's *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual* (DSM-III) adopted explicit symptom-based criteria, often called the DSM revolution. The World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases has a parallel mental disorders chapter. Since the 1990s neuroscience, genetics and neuroimaging have grown in influence, alongside renewed attention to psychotherapy research and to patient and survivor perspectives.

## Models of disorder

Psychiatry has no single accepted theory of what mental disorder is. Several models coexist.

- The **biomedical model** treats disorders as conditions linked to brain and body processes, including genes, neurochemistry and circuitry.
- The **psychodynamic** and **cognitive-behavioural** models emphasise meaning, learning, thinking patterns and early experience.
- The **social** model looks at poverty, adversity, discrimination, isolation and culture.
- The **biopsychosocial model**, proposed by George Engel in 1977, argues that these levels interact and should be considered together.

Many contemporary writers prefer pluralism, and some argue that disorders are better described as dimensions than as sharp categories. A related philosophical question is whether disorder is a matter of objective dysfunction, of harm judged by values, or of both.

## Treatments and evidence

Common treatments include psychotherapies such as cognitive-behavioural therapy, medications such as antidepressants, antipsychotics, mood stabilisers and anxiolytics, brain stimulation including electroconvulsive therapy, psychosocial and rehabilitation services, and community support. Evidence comes mainly from randomised controlled trials, systematic reviews and meta-analyses, supplemented by observational and long-term outcome studies. Trials have well-known limits: short duration, selected participants, placebo responses, and publication bias. How much benefit a given treatment gives, and for whom, is often modest on average and varies between individuals. The atlas describes the state of debate and does not recommend treatments.

## Critiques

Psychiatry has attracted serious criticism, and the atlas treats it as part of the subject. Thomas Szasz argued in *The Myth of Mental Illness* (1961) that mental illness is a metaphor and that psychiatry confuses problems in living with disease. R. D. Laing, Michel Foucault in *Madness and Civilization* (1961) and Erving Goffman in *Asylums* (1961) examined power, labelling and institutions. David Rosenhan's 1973 paper "On Being Sane in Insane Places" was influential, though its methods have since been questioned. Other critiques concern pharmaceutical industry influence, expansion of diagnostic boundaries, coercion in treatment, cultural bias, and the neglect of social causes. Defenders reply that mental disorders cause real suffering, that effective help exists, and that rejecting classification entirely would leave people without care. Many contributors to the debate hold positions between the extremes.

The Psychiatry module is designed to be read alongside the Psychology module. Psychology supplies theories of cognition, emotion, development and personality, and research methods, including psychometrics. ## How the atlas is organised

The module groups entries by type, as the other modules do. Field entries, like this one, introduce an area and its debates. Further entries are planned for disorders described in an educational register, for treatments and their evidence, for historical episodes, for thinkers, and for concepts and debates. Each entry carries a "related" list so that readers can move sideways between neighbouring topics, and a short list of sources for further reading.

Two field entries follow this one directly: Classification and Diagnosis in Psychiatry, and Biological, Psychological and Social Models of Mental Disorder. A sensible reading path is to begin here, continue with classification, then models, and then move to individual topics according to interest.

## Cautions and limits of version 1

Diagnostic categories are conventions. They are useful for communication, research, service planning and treatment decisions, but they are revised over time, vary between manuals and cultures, and do not always correspond to distinct underlying causes. Entries must not be used for self-diagnosis or to judge other people. Version 1 of the module is a first, partial map. It is written in English from widely documented sources, it does not cover every condition, it cannot capture all regional and cultural variation in how distress is understood and treated, and it will contain gaps that are recorded in each entry and filled later. Readers who want current clinical guidance should consult national guidelines and qualified clinicians.
