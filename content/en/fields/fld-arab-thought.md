# Arab Thought

Arab thought, as this atlas uses the term, is the intellectual history of writing and argument in Arabic, from the formative centuries of Islamic civilisation to present-day debates in Arab societies and their diasporas. It covers philosophy and theology, law and political theory, history and social analysis, literary and linguistic criticism, and the public essays and journals in which modern Arab societies argued about their own condition. The module is an introduction to a long conversation, not a doctrine, and it treats the conversation's disagreements as part of its subject.

## Scope

The atlas organises the field in three broad layers that overlap.

- **The classical and Islamic heritage.** From the eighth century onward, Arabic became the shared scholarly language of a large region. Its writers produced kalam (rational theology), falsafa (philosophy in the Greek-inspired tradition), usul al-fiqh (legal theory), Sufi thought, historiography and grammar. Important figures include al-Kindi (c. 801-873), al-Farabi (c. 870-950), Ibn Sina (980-1037), al-Ghazali (1058-1111), Ibn Rushd (1126-1198) and Ibn Khaldun (1332-1406), whose *Muqaddima* (1377) is a landmark of social and historical analysis.
- **The Nahda, the nineteenth-century and early twentieth-century "awakening" or renaissance.** Encounters with Europe, state reform in Egypt, Tunisia and the Ottoman provinces, print culture and new schools produced debates on education, constitutional government, women's status, science, language and religion. Names include Rifa'a al-Tahtawi (1801-1873), Muhammad Abduh (1849-1905), Qasim Amin (1863-1908) and Abd al-Rahman al-Kawakibi (1855-1902).
- **Post-colonial and contemporary critique.** After independence and especially after the defeat of 1967, writers reassessed nationalism, development, the state, religion and the relation to the heritage (turath). Thinkers such as Constantine Zurayk (1909-2000), Abdallah Laroui (born 1933), Muhammad Abid al-Jabri (1936-2010), Mohammed Arkoun (1928-2010) and Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd (1943-2010) belong here, alongside Islamist, Marxist, liberal and feminist currents.

"Arabic-language" matters as much as "Arab". Much of the classical corpus was written by Persians, Berbers, Andalusians, Central Asians, Christians and Jews as well as by ethnic Arabs. Maimonides, for example, wrote his *Guide of the Perplexed* in Judeo-Arabic. Conversely, the modern period includes Arab thinkers writing in French or English. The atlas therefore uses language and shared public sphere as its working criterion and does not assume an ethnic or religious definition.

## Why it has its own module

Arab thought could be filed under several other headings, and the atlas links to all of them. It has its own module for three reasons. First, the material has an internal continuity: modern writers constantly argue with the classical authors, and a reader who meets al-Ghazali in the Philosophy module and al-Jabri's reading of him elsewhere benefits from seeing the thread. Second, reading the region only through categories developed for European history can distort it, for example by treating religion as a separable "sphere" or by presuming a single secularisation path. Third, a dedicated module lets the atlas give attention to terms and debates that are specific to this literature, such as turath, tajdid (renewal), islah (reform), asala (authenticity) and mu'asara (contemporaneity).

## Main questions and debates

Several questions recur across periods.

1. **Reason and revelation.** How far can rational argument, including Greek-derived logic, be applied to religious questions? The Mu'tazila, the Ash'ari school, the falsafa tradition and critics such as al-Ghazali and Ibn Taymiyya (1263-1328) each answered differently.
2. **Authenticity and modernity.** Should reform draw on the inherited tradition, on European models, or on some synthesis? Critics of the framing note that it can treat "the West" and "Islam" as fixed blocs.
3. **Reform, secularism and the state.** Debates range from Ali Abd al-Raziq's *Islam and the Foundations of Governance* (1925), which argued that Islam does not prescribe a particular political order, to Islamist programmes associated with Hasan al-Banna (1906-1949) and Sayyid Qutb (1906-1966), to liberal and socialist alternatives. The word "secularism" itself is contested in Arabic usage.
4. **The heritage.** Is turath a resource, a burden, or a construction of the present? Hassan Hanafi, al-Jabri and Arkoun offer quite different answers.
5. **Nation and community.** Arabism, territorial nationalism and Islamic solidarity competed and sometimes combined.

## How the atlas is organised

This module opens with three field introductions: this one, one on the Nahda and modern Arab thought, and one on the critique of Arab reason and contemporary debates. Beneath them the atlas is built from entries on thinkers, works, schools and concepts, joined by links that show influence, disagreement and shared topics. The module connects outward to Philosophy (classical falsafa and kalam, and modern philosophical argument), Religious Studies (Quranic interpretation, theology, Sufism), Legal Theory (usul al-fiqh, sharia and modern codification), and Sociology and political economy (Ibn Khaldun, colonial and post-colonial states, class, development). A suggested reading path for newcomers starts with the Nahda introduction, moves to the critique introduction, and then goes backward into the classical heritage through the thinkers those later writers argue with.

## Limits of this first version

Version 1 is selective. Coverage is uneven: Egypt, the Levant and the Maghreb are better represented than the Gulf, Sudan or Yemen, and canonical male authors are better represented than women writers, though women's thought is part of the field. Literature, poetry and the natural sciences appear mainly where they bear on intellectual debates. The atlas draws on a limited stock of secondary sources, often in English and French, and primary texts in Arabic are not yet fully represented. Readers should treat entries as orientation and follow the cited sources.

## Cautions

The region is diverse in language, confession, politics and history, and no figure speaks for "the Arab mind". Phrases such as "Arab reason" or "the Muslim world" are themselves objects of debate in this literature. Framings of "decline" versus "renewal", or "authentic" versus "imported", are analytic choices with histories, and several thinkers in the module criticise them. The atlas tries to describe positions as their holders understood them and to state criticisms of each without taking a side.

## Naming and transliteration conventions

Arabic names and terms are given in a simplified Latin transliteration without diacritics, using an apostrophe for both hamza and ayn. The definite article is written *al-* and is not capitalised mid-name. Where an established English form exists, it is used: Quran, sharia, Averroes alongside Ibn Rushd, Avicenna alongside Ibn Sina. Dates are Common Era unless marked. Figures with several recognised spellings, such as al-Tahtawi or al-Jabri, are listed under one main form with variants noted.
