# Biological, Psychological and Social Models of Mental Disorder

This entry is educational and descriptive. It is not clinical advice, and it does not tell any reader what causes their own difficulties or what treatment to choose. Diagnostic categories mentioned here are conventions with limits.

## What the field asks

A model of mental disorder is a general account of what such conditions are and what brings them about. The question matters because explanations guide research, treatment, policy and the way people understand themselves. Three families of explanation recur: biological, psychological and social. The central issue is whether they compete or combine, and at what level of description each is most useful.

## Main questions

- To what extent are disorders caused by brain and genetic differences, and to what extent by experience and environment?
- Do causes at different levels interact, and how?
- Does a biological explanation imply a biological treatment, or the reverse?
- Are disorders diseases like others in medicine, or something different?
- How should the meaning and context of symptoms be weighed against their mechanisms?

## The biological family

Biological approaches look to genetics, neurochemistry, brain structure and function, hormones, immune processes and development. Twin and adoption studies indicate that genetic factors contribute to risk for many conditions, with heritability estimates that are substantial for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and autism and generally lower for depression and anxiety. Large genome-wide association studies, including those of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium, have found many genetic variants of small individual effect and considerable overlap between diagnoses, so there is no single "gene for" a disorder. The monoamine hypothesis of depression and the dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia arose partly from observing drug effects. Both have been refined or criticised as incomplete. Neuroimaging has revealed average group differences in some conditions, but findings are not specific enough to diagnose individuals.

Eric Kandel argued in a 1998 paper that psychotherapy and biology are not opposed, because learning changes the brain. Critics of "biological reductionism" warn that explanation at the level of neurons may omit meaning, context and the person.

## The psychological family

Psychological approaches focus on thought, emotion, learning, motivation and relationships. Psychoanalytic and psychodynamic theories, from Sigmund Freud onward, emphasise unconscious conflict and early relationships. Behavioural and cognitive models, associated with figures such as Aaron Beck in *Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders* (1976), stress learned patterns and distorted thinking. Attachment theory, developed by John Bowlby, links early bonds to later emotional development. Trauma research examines how adverse experience affects regulation and symptoms. Psychological models have produced widely used therapies, and meta-analyses support the efficacy of several of them for conditions such as depression and anxiety, although effect sizes and generalisation are debated.

## The social family

Social approaches examine the conditions in which people live. Classic studies by Robert Faris and Warren Dunham in 1939 and by August Hollingshead and Frederick Redlich in *Social Class and Mental Illness* (1958) reported associations between social position and recorded illness. George Brown and Tirril Harris, in *Social Origins of Depression* (1978), studied how life events and social circumstances relate to depression in women in London. Later research on urban living, migration, discrimination, childhood adversity and social isolation has linked these exposures to higher risk, notably for psychosis. Associations are not always causal, and two directions are debated: social causation, where adversity produces illness, and social drift, where illness leads to disadvantage. Both probably operate.

Cultural approaches stress that the experience, expression and meaning of distress differ across societies, and that responses to it, from family care to specialist services, shape outcomes.

## Integration

George Engel proposed the biopsychosocial model in a 1977 paper in *Science*, arguing that medicine should consider biological, psychological and social levels together. It became a standard teaching framework. The diathesis-stress model, in which vulnerability interacts with stressors, is a common specific form. Gene-environment interaction studies, such as those by Avshalom Caspi, Terrie Moffitt and colleagues on the serotonin transporter gene and stress, attracted wide attention, though later replication has been mixed and illustrates the difficulty of this research. Developmental psychopathology, associated with Michael Rutter and Dante Cicchetti among others, offers a lifespan framework in which risk and protection interact.

## Debates and objections

Critics of the biopsychosocial model, including Nassir Ghaemi, argue that it can become an eclectic list without guidance on priority, offering no clear way to decide which level matters in a given case. Critics of biomedical dominance, including some clinicians and patient advocates, argue that "chemical imbalance" explanations were oversimplified and that attention to social adversity has been neglected. Defenders of biological psychiatry point to the evidence for genetic and neural contributions and to effective medications. A further debate concerns whether biological explanations reduce stigma: studies suggest they may reduce blame but can increase perceptions of permanence or difference. Network and systems models, and predictive-processing accounts, offer newer ways of thinking about how factors interact. They remain areas of active research and not settled theory.

## Evidence and methods

Methods include twin, family and adoption designs, genome-wide association, neuroimaging, animal and cellular models, randomised trials of drugs and therapies, epidemiological cohort studies, and qualitative research on lived experience. Each can answer some questions and not others. Heritability is a population statistic and does not mean a trait is fixed. Associations in observational data may be confounded. Animal models have limits for conditions defined by human experience.

## Treatment evidence

Randomised trials support specific psychotherapies and medications for particular conditions, with average benefits that are real but vary, and social interventions have their own evidence base. The atlas describes this evidence and does not recommend treatment.

## Connections to other fields

**Psychology** supplies cognitive, developmental and personality theory and methods. **Philosophy** examines explanation, reductionism, mind-body questions and the concept of disorder. **Law** engages with responsibility and capacity, where causal explanation does not by itself settle legal questions. **Sociology** studies inequality, labelling and the organisation of services. **Anthropology** documents cultural variation in distress and healing.

## Cautions

No model explains every case, and individual cases cannot be derived from general models. This entry is not a guide to causes of any person's difficulties. Readers needing help should consult a qualified professional.
