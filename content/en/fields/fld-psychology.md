# Psychology

Psychology is the scientific study of mind and behaviour. It asks how people perceive, remember, learn, feel, decide, develop, and relate to one another, and why individuals differ. It sits at an unusual crossroads: part biological science, part social science, part humanistic inquiry. That position explains both its breadth and the persistent arguments about what counts as good evidence in it.

## What psychology is

The discipline took recognisable institutional form in the late nineteenth century, when Wilhelm Wundt opened an experimental laboratory in Leipzig in 1879 and William James published *The Principles of Psychology* in 1890. Before that, questions about the mind belonged to philosophy and physiology. Early psychology split almost at once into rival programmes: introspective structuralism, functionalism, psychoanalysis, and, in the early twentieth century, behaviourism, which insisted that only observable behaviour could be studied scientifically. The "cognitive revolution" of the mid-twentieth century restored mental processes to respectable study, using the computer as a model, and more recently neuroscience and genetics have added further levels of explanation.

Psychology is best understood as a family of questions and methods rather than a single theory. No unified paradigm commands agreement the way evolution does in biology, and a student quickly learns that most claims come with a qualifier about population, measure and context.

## Main subfields

- **Cognitive psychology** studies attention, perception, memory, language, reasoning and decision making.
- **Developmental psychology** traces change across the lifespan, from infancy through ageing, and asks how biology and experience interact.
- **Social psychology** examines how thoughts, feelings and actions are shaped by other people, groups and situations.
- **Personality and individual differences** study stable patterns of thought and behaviour and how to measure them, including intelligence.
- **Clinical and counselling psychology** concern the assessment, understanding and treatment of mental distress and disorder.
- **Biological psychology and neuroscience** link behaviour to brain, hormones and genes.
- **Learning and behavioural psychology** study how experience changes behaviour.
- **Applied fields** such as educational, organisational, health and forensic psychology use these findings in practice.

## Methods

Psychologists use controlled experiments, in which a researcher manipulates one variable and randomly assigns participants to conditions, to support causal claims. They use correlational and survey research when manipulation is impossible or unethical, longitudinal studies to follow people over time, case studies for rare phenomena, observation, twin and adoption designs, and neuroimaging and physiological recording. Measurement itself is a field of study: psychometrics asks whether a test is consistent and whether it measures what it claims to measure. Statistics are central, and the discipline has had a prominent public debate about their misuse, discussed in the entry on the replication crisis. Research with people also carries ethical obligations, including informed consent and protection from harm, which developed partly in response to past abuses.

## Limits and cautions

Several cautions apply throughout. First, much classic research drew on small samples of university students in wealthy Western societies, a pattern that Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan described with the acronym WEIRD; findings may not generalise to other populations. Second, many headline results have proved harder to reproduce than expected, so single studies deserve modest confidence. Third, psychological constructs such as intelligence or personality are measured indirectly, and the measure is not the thing itself. Fourth, correlation does not establish causation, and effects that are statistically detectable may be small in practice. Fifth, theories differ in how scientific they are: some, such as psychoanalysis, have been criticised as hard to falsify, while others make narrow, testable claims. Finally, popular psychology often simplifies or distorts findings, and a term borrowed into everyday speech may not mean what researchers meant.

## Not a substitute for clinical advice

This atlas is educational. Entries on disorders or therapies describe how the field understands them and cannot diagnose, treat or replace professional assessment. Anyone worried about their own mental health, or someone else's, should consult a qualified clinician, or local emergency services if there is risk of harm.
