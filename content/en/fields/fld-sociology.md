# Sociology

Sociology is the systematic study of human social life: how people form relationships, groups, organisations and institutions, how those structures shape what individuals think and do, and how individuals in turn reproduce or change them. The word was coined by Auguste Comte in the 1830s, but there is no single agreed definition of the discipline. A workable description is that sociology explains patterns in collective life, such as inequality, religion, crime, family forms, work, politics and cultural taste, by pointing to social relations and conditions and not only to individual character or biology.

## What sociology asks

Sociology starts from an observation that is easy to state and hard to take seriously: much of what looks like private choice or personal trouble has social regularities. Rates of suicide, marriage, voting, educational attainment and illness differ systematically between groups and across time, and these differences call for explanation. C. Wright Mills called the habit of seeing this connection between private troubles and public issues "the sociological imagination" (1959).

Its central questions recur in every subfield. What holds societies together, and what divides them? How do structures (rules, roles, institutions, class positions) constrain and enable action? How do shared meanings arise, and how are they enforced? Who gets what, and why? How do societies change, and who drives the change? How can social claims be tested against evidence?

## Origins

Reflection on society is old and not confined to Europe. The Tunisian-born scholar Ibn Khaldun (1332-1406) set out in the *Muqaddimah* (1377) an explanatory science of human civilisation, including the concept of *asabiyya*, group solidarity, and a theory of the rise and decline of dynasties. Many historians of the field regard it as an important precursor, though it did not found a continuous research tradition in the modern sense.

Modern sociology took shape in nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Europe and North America, in response to industrialisation, urbanisation, revolution and colonial expansion. Key figures include:

- **Auguste Comte** (1798-1857) proposed a "positive" science of society and named it sociology.
- **Harriet Martineau** (1802-1876) wrote *Society in America* (1837) and *How to Observe Manners and Morals* (1838), an early methodological treatise, and translated Comte into English (1853).
- **Karl Marx** (1818-1883) analysed capitalism, class and historical change, notably in *Capital* (1867).
- **Émile Durkheim** (1858-1917) argued in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) that social facts are real and can be studied empirically, and applied the idea in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893), *Suicide* (1897) and *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912).
- **Max Weber** (1864-1920) developed interpretive sociology, centred on the subjective meaning of action, in *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904-05) and the posthumous *Economy and Society*.
- **Georg Simmel** (1858-1918) studied forms of social interaction, the metropolis and money, as in *The Philosophy of Money* (1900) and *Sociology* (1908).
- **W. E. B. Du Bois** (1868-1963) combined empirical fieldwork, as in *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899), with analysis of race and the "colour line" in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903). His contribution was long under-recognised in disciplinary histories.

Sociology became institutionalised through university departments, notably Bordeaux under Durkheim and Chicago from 1892, and through journals and professional associations. The twentieth century added important traditions from Latin America (such as dependency theory, associated with Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto, 1969), from the Arab world, South Asia and Africa, and critical work on the discipline's own Eurocentrism, for example by Syed Farid Alatas and Raewyn Connell (*Southern Theory*, 2007).

## Main paradigms

Sociologists usually distinguish a few broad theoretical orientations. They are best treated as lenses, not as rival sects, and many works combine them.

- **Functionalism** treats society as a system of interdependent parts whose institutions serve needs of the whole. Durkheim is an ancestor; Talcott Parsons (*The Social System*, 1951) and Robert K. Merton (*Social Theory and Social Structure*, 1949) are its mid-century representatives. Critics charge it with downplaying conflict and change.
- **Conflict theories** stress domination, scarcity and struggle between groups. They draw on Marx and Weber and include later work by Ralf Dahrendorf, Randall Collins and many critical and feminist authors.
- **Interactionism and interpretive approaches** focus on how people create and negotiate meaning in everyday encounters: George Herbert Mead (*Mind, Self and Society*, 1934), Herbert Blumer, Erving Goffman (*The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*, 1959), and Berger and Luckmann (*The Social Construction of Reality*, 1966). Critics note that they can neglect large-scale structure.
- **Other currents** include rational-choice sociology (James Coleman), critical theory, feminist and postcolonial theory, structuration (Anthony Giddens) and practice theory (Pierre Bourdieu).

## Subfields

Typical subfields include stratification and inequality, race and ethnicity, gender and sexuality, family, education, religion, economic sociology, political sociology, organisations, work, crime and deviance, health, demography, urban and rural sociology, culture, science and technology, migration, social movements and globalisation. Each combines general theory with its own empirical literature.

## Methods

Sociologists use quantitative and qualitative methods, often together. Surveys and administrative data support statistical description and modelling; experiments, including field and audit experiments, support causal claims; ethnography and interviews capture meanings and practices from the inside; comparative-historical analysis examines revolutions, states and economic change across cases; network analysis maps relations; and computational methods use large digital datasets. Debates continue about causal inference, measurement, reproducibility and the ethics of studying human subjects.

## Connections to other disciplines

Sociology overlaps with anthropology (which shares ethnographic methods and the concept of culture), economics (markets, inequality and the limits of rational-choice assumptions), psychology (social psychology sits at the border), history (historical sociology), political science, law (the sociology of law) and philosophy, from which many of its concepts and its dispute about explanation and interpretation derive. The boundaries are institutional conventions more than natural divisions.

## A suggested reading path

A newcomer might begin with Sociological Theory, to meet the classical authors and the main paradigms, then Sociological Methods, to see how claims are tested, and then Social Stratification and Inequality, a core substantive field that draws on both. Readers can then follow thinkers and concepts chronologically or move into the subfields that interest them.

## Limits of this version

This is a first version and coverage is uneven. Classical European and North American theory is better represented than sociology from Africa, Asia, the Arab world and Latin America, which reflects the sources used and not the importance of those traditions. Entries are introductions, not substitutes for primary texts or specialist reviews, and any short summary of a major author is simplified.

## Common misunderstandings

Sociology is not the same as social work or socialism, and it is not merely common sense restated in jargon: it often finds that intuitive explanations fail when tested. It does not deny individual agency; most traditions try to explain how structure and agency interact. Describing a pattern, such as a difference between groups, is not justifying it, and a sociological explanation of behaviour is not an excuse for it. Finally, no single paradigm is "the" sociology: the field is plural, and much of its value lies in its internal disagreements.
