# Ritual, Myth and Symbol

## What the field asks

This field studies the expressive core of religious life: what people do (ritual), what they tell (myth and narrative) and what they use to convey meaning (symbol, image, object, space). Scholars ask how these forms work, what they mean for participants, what social or psychological functions they serve, and how they are transmitted and change. The field draws chiefly on anthropology, history of religions, sociology, literary study and cognitive science.

## Main questions

- Is ritual primarily expressive of belief, or does belief follow practice? Is ritual action meaningful, or is its force in being formal and repetitive?
- What is myth: a primitive explanation of nature, a charter for social order, a structure of thought, or a sacred narrative of origins whose truth is of a different kind from history?
- What distinguishes "sacred" from "profane", and is that distinction found across cultures or imposed by scholars?
- How do symbols carry meaning, and does their interpretation belong to participants, to scholars, or to both?

## Theories of ritual

Early theorists were often evolutionary. Edward Tylor and James Frazer (*The Golden Bough*, 1890 and later editions) treated magic and ritual as mistaken early science or a stage in cultural development. This framework has been widely rejected for its ethnocentrism and its speculative history, though Frazer remains historically important.

William Robertson Smith (*Lectures on the Religion of the Semites*, 1889) argued that ritual preceded belief and that sacrifice created communal bonds. Émile Durkheim (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912) saw ritual as generating "collective effervescence" and renewing social solidarity. Bronisław Malinowski emphasised the functions of magic and ritual in managing anxiety in uncertain tasks. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown criticised this and stressed social structure.

Arnold van Gennep's *Les rites de passage* (1909) described rites of transition as having three phases: separation, margin (liminality) and aggregation. Victor Turner developed the liminal phase in *The Ritual Process* (1969), introducing "communitas" as the experience of anti-structure among participants in rites and pilgrimage; later studies of pilgrimage have contested its universality. Mary Douglas (*Purity and Danger*, 1966; *Natural Symbols*, 1970) analysed purity rules and bodily symbolism as expressions of social classification and boundary.

Clifford Geertz's interpretive approach read ritual as a "model of" and "model for" reality. Catherine Bell (*Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice*, 1992) criticised the ritual/belief and thought/action dichotomies and argued for treating "ritualisation" as a way of acting. Roy Rappaport (*Ritual and Religion in the Making of Humanity*, 1999) offered an ambitious account linking ritual to conventions, commitments and the sacred. Cognitive approaches (E. Thomas Lawson and Robert McCauley; Harvey Whitehouse's "modes of religiosity") ask which features of ritual are memorable and how they are transmitted.

## Theories of myth

Max Müller read myth as a "disease of language" arising from nature worship, a view long abandoned. Frazer treated myth as a rationale for ritual. Malinowski's *Myth in Primitive Psychology* (1926) held that myth is a living "charter" justifying social institutions. Claude Lévi-Strauss, in *Structural Anthropology* (1958) and the four volumes of *Mythologiques* (1964-71), analysed myth as structures of binary oppositions mediated by narrative, comparing variants across cultures. Mircea Eliade, in *Myth and Reality* (1963) and *The Myth of the Eternal Return* (1949), described sacred myths as accounts of origins that give meaning by recalling "sacred time". Critics noted his sweeping generalisation. Bruce Lincoln (*Theorizing Myth*, 1999) located myth in claims to authority and the ideological work narrative does. Carl Jung and Joseph Campbell proposed archetypal readings, which many scholars regard as speculative.

A note on terms: in everyday speech "myth" often means a falsehood. In religious studies it is a technical term for a traditional narrative of significance to a community, with no implication about its historicity.

## Theories of symbol and the sacred

Rudolf Otto's "numinous" and Durkheim's sacred/profane contrast framed much of the discussion. Eliade proposed "hierophany", the manifestation of the sacred in ordinary objects. Critics, including Jonathan Z. Smith (*To Take Place*, 1987), argued that "sacredness" is a relation of attention and place created by human acts, not an essence in things. Paul Ricoeur (*The Symbolism of Evil*, 1960) developed a hermeneutics of religious symbols. Anthropologists such as Victor Turner (*The Forest of Symbols*, 1967) analysed symbols' multiple meanings and "dominant" symbols in Ndembu ritual, based on fieldwork and the explanations of ritual specialists, an instructive case of combining participant exegesis with the analyst's interpretation.

## Key debates and objections

**Meaning versus action.** Frits Staal argued that Vedic ritual is essentially rule-governed activity without semantic meaning, a provocative claim that many scholars dispute, pointing to participants' own interpretations.

**Universality.** Does "ritual" name a cross-cultural category, or does it project a Protestant opposition between inner belief and outward form? Catherine Bell and Talal Asad pressed this.

**Function versus meaning.** Functional explanations (social cohesion, anxiety reduction) may not capture what practitioners intend; interpretive accounts may lack testability.

**Efficacy.** Studies of ritual healing and trance ask how rites can be effective; Lévi-Strauss on shamanic cures and later medical-anthropological research offer different explanations, without settling the question of the participants' own claims.

## Methods

Ethnographic observation and interview, textual and philological study of liturgies and myths, comparative and structural analysis, iconographic and archaeological analysis of objects and spaces, and experimental work on memory, emotion and synchrony in ritual. Each yields a different kind of evidence, and the atlas encourages noting which is in use.

## Connections to other disciplines

Anthropology (fieldwork, kinship, structure), sociology (solidarity, institutions), psychology (emotion, development, Freud's and Jung's interpretations), philosophy (hermeneutics, language, meaning), history and classics (liturgies, cults, mythography), and literary study (narratology, folklore). Law touches ritual where religious practices, such as slaughter, dress or burial, are regulated or protected.

## Cautions

Description, self-understanding and explanation must be distinguished. A myth's account of itself (as true history, revelation or story) is evidence about the tradition; the scholar's analysis of its structure or function is a separate claim. Avoid treating "primitive" and "modern" as stages, or Western categories (ritual, myth, magic, religion) as neutral. Avoid assuming that ancient or non-Western practices are static; rituals are revised continually.
