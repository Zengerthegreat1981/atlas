# Power, State and Politics in Anthropology

Political anthropology asks how order is made and unmade in human societies: who decides, how disputes are settled, how authority is justified, and how people resist. It treats politics as embedded in kinship, ritual, economy and belief, rather than as a separate sphere confined to formal government. This field introduction follows the subject from classic studies of societies without centralised states to later work on colonialism, violence, development and displacement.

## Overview

The earliest comparative question was how societies hold together without a state. Fortes and Evans-Pritchard's collection *African Political Systems* distinguished societies with centralised authority from those organised through lineages and other kin-based groupings. Evans-Pritchard's study of the Nuer described a segmentary lineage system in which groups divide and combine according to the scale of a conflict, giving order without rulers. Such accounts have been influential and also criticised for portraying societies as static and bounded, and for neglecting the effects of colonial rule.

## Key themes

**Acephalous (leaderless) societies** and **chiefdoms** are contrasted with **states**, and anthropologists have studied how states form and how they are experienced from below. Edmund Leach's *Political Systems of Highland Burma* argued that Kachin communities oscillated between different political forms, rejecting the idea of fixed, self-contained systems. Max Gluckman and the Manchester School studied conflict, ritual and social change, notably in colonial southern Africa, emphasising that conflict can sustain as well as threaten social order. Victor Turner's analysis of social drama and the processes of schism and reconciliation grew from the same milieu.

## From order to power

Later generations turned to history and inequality. Eric Wolf placed small communities within global systems of trade, empire and capital, challenging the picture of isolated societies. Jean and John Comaroff examined colonialism, missionary encounters and consciousness in southern Africa. Concepts such as hegemony, developed in the Marxist tradition, helped analysts consider rule by consent as well as coercion, and studies of cultural resistance examined how subordinate groups contest domination.

## Violence, health and development

Anthropologists have documented war, genocide and everyday violence, and have argued that harm is often structural, built into social and economic arrangements rather than inflicted by identifiable perpetrators alone. Paul Farmer, working in Haiti, helped popularise the idea of structural violence in medicine and public health. Applied and development anthropology study aid, planning and projects, and have also drawn sharp criticism; the Atlas covers the critique of development anthropology and a case study of dam-induced displacement. Research on asylum and borders examines how states classify and control movement.

## Debates

How far can early studies of political systems be trusted given their colonial setting? Is the state the right unit of analysis? Should anthropologists work with military and development agencies, or is such work incompatible with their ethical commitments? The debate over militarisation and the critique of development take up the last two questions.

## How the Atlas covers this field

Coverage runs from founding works on African political systems through to violence, development and migration. It is stronger on British structural-functionalist and later critical approaches than on anarchist or anti-state theory, flagged in the gaps above.

## Suggested reading order

Start with the political anthropology subfield entry, then *African Political Systems* and the Nuer. Move to Leach and the Manchester School, then Wolf and the Comaroffs, and finish with structural violence and the debates on development and militarisation.
