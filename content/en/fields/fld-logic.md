# Logic

Logic is the study of correct reasoning. It asks what makes an argument good, what follows from what, and how the form of a statement determines which inferences are safe. It is a branch of philosophy, but also of mathematics and computer science, and its results are used far beyond philosophy.

## What logic asks

The central question is what it means for a conclusion to follow from premises. In a **deductive** argument, the premises are intended to guarantee the conclusion: if they are true, the conclusion cannot be false. In an **inductive** argument, the premises make the conclusion probable without guaranteeing it, as when we generalise from observed cases. A third kind, **abductive** reasoning or inference to the best explanation, selects the hypothesis that best accounts for the evidence.

Two terms must be kept apart. An argument is **valid** when it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false. It is **sound** when it is valid and its premises are in fact true. A valid argument can have false premises, and an argument with true premises can be invalid. Validity concerns form and logical relation, not the truth of the content.

## Main questions

- What makes an inference valid, and how is validity to be defined, by truth-preservation across all interpretations or by proof?
- Which logical constants (such as "and", "or", "not", "if... then", "all", "some") belong to logic, and how should they be understood?
- How should conditionals and negation be treated, given that natural-language "if" behaves in puzzling ways?
- Is there one correct logic, or several?
- What are the limits of formal systems?

## Key positions and developments

**Aristotle** (384-322 BCE) founded formal logic in the *Prior Analytics*, with his theory of the syllogism, an argument pattern such as "All humans are mortal; all Greeks are humans; therefore all Greeks are mortal". The Stoics, notably Chrysippus, developed a logic of propositions, which anticipated modern propositional logic. Medieval logicians in the Latin and Islamic worlds, including Avicenna, Abelard and Ockham, refined theories of consequence, modality and reference.

Indian logic developed independently in the Nyaya school, whose members analysed inference (*anumana*) using a five-part argument pattern, and in Buddhist logic, where Dignaga and Dharmakirti worked on the theory of the "reason" or mark that licenses an inference. Chinese logic was pursued by the Mohists and the School of Names, with attention to disputation and language.

The modern era began with **Gottlob Frege**, whose *Begriffsschrift* (1879) introduced quantificational logic with a precise formal language. **Bertrand Russell** and **Alfred North Whitehead** produced *Principia Mathematica* (1910-1913), attempting to derive mathematics from logic. **David Hilbert** pursued formal axiomatisation, and **Kurt Gödel** showed in 1931 that any consistent formal system strong enough for arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and cannot prove its own consistency. Alfred Tarski gave a rigorous account of truth for formal languages, and Alonzo Church and Alan Turing demonstrated in 1936 that first-order logic is undecidable. Gödel's completeness theorem (1929) shows that first-order logic proves all its valid formulas.

## Formal logic and its extensions

**Propositional logic** treats whole statements and the connectives joining them. **First-order (predicate) logic** adds quantifiers and predicates, and became the standard framework of mathematical logic. **Modal logic** adds operators for necessity and possibility, and has been developed with possible-worlds semantics, associated with Saul Kripke in the 1950s and 1960s. Higher-order, temporal, deontic and epistemic logics extend the toolkit.

**Non-classical logics** reject some classical principles. **Intuitionistic logic**, inspired by L. E. J. Brouwer, drops the law of excluded middle because it ties truth to constructive proof. **Paraconsistent logics** allow contradictions without trivialising everything, a view defended by Graham Priest. **Many-valued** and **fuzzy** logics admit more than two truth values, and **relevance logics** require premises to be relevant to the conclusion. **Dialetheism** holds that some contradictions are true. Whether these are rivals to classical logic or tools for different purposes is a live dispute known as logical pluralism versus monism.

## Fallacies

A fallacy is a pattern of reasoning that seems good but is not. Formal fallacies, such as affirming the consequent ("If it rains the ground is wet; the ground is wet; so it rained"), are invalid by form. Informal fallacies depend on content or context. Examples include the *ad hominem* (attacking the arguer rather than the argument), equivocation, begging the question and the straw man. Some alleged fallacies, such as appeals to authority, are sometimes reasonable, so context matters.

## Major debates and objections

Disputes about the **paradoxes of material implication** concern whether the classical reading of "if" captures ordinary conditionals. The **liar paradox** and **Russell's paradox** show that naive truth and set comprehension lead to contradiction, motivating hierarchies and revisions. W. V. O. Quine argued that logic is continuous with empirical theory and that deviant logics change the subject, whereas others hold that logic is analytic or conventional. Hilary Putnam suggested that quantum mechanics might motivate revising logic, though this remains contested. The problem of **induction**, raised by David Hume, asks whether inductive reasoning can be justified without circularity.

## Connections

Logic underlies mathematics (set theory, proof theory, model theory, computability) and computer science (programming languages, verification, databases, artificial intelligence). In philosophy, it supports metaphysics through modality, epistemology through theories of justification and probability, and philosophy of language through formal semantics. Philosophy of science draws on probability and confirmation theory, sometimes called inductive logic.

## Common misunderstandings

Logic is not the same as being sensible or unemotional, and "logical" in ordinary speech often means merely "reasonable". Validity does not guarantee truth. A formal system's limits, as shown by Godel, do not show that human reasoning is irrational or that mathematics is unreliable. Finally, there is not simply one fixed logic that all reasoning must obey, though classical logic remains the default for much of mathematics and science.
