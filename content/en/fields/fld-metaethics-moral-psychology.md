# Metaethics and Moral Psychology

Metaethics steps back from questions about what is right or wrong and asks what moral thought and talk are: what moral words mean, whether moral claims can be true, how we could know them, and why they move us to act. Moral psychology studies the mental capacities involved in moral life, including judgement, emotion, motivation, responsibility and moral development. The two are treated together here because many metaethical disputes turn on psychological claims, and because empirical work on morality has raised new philosophical questions.

## What the field asks

The main questions are these. Are there objective moral facts, independent of what anyone thinks, or are moral claims expressions of attitude, convention or culture? What is the semantic function of "good" and "ought"? How, if at all, can we have moral knowledge? Do moral judgements necessarily motivate? Can a person judge that something is right and feel no pull to do it? How do moral capacities develop in children and cultures? What is the role of emotion and reason in moral judgement? What do responsibility and free will require?

## Key positions

**Moral realism** holds that there are mind-independent moral facts. Non-naturalists such as G. E. Moore (*Principia Ethica*, 1903) argue that goodness is a simple, indefinable property not reducible to natural ones, an argument supported by his open-question argument. Naturalist realists, such as Richard Boyd and those in the Cornell school, treat moral properties as natural properties identified through the explanatory role they play. Derek Parfit's *On What Matters* (2011) defends a non-naturalist realism about reasons.

**Error theory**, defended by J. L. Mackie in *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977), claims that moral judgements purport to describe objective, action-guiding facts, that no such facts exist, and that all positive moral claims are therefore false. Mackie's supporting arguments are the argument from queerness and the argument from relativity.

**Expressivism** denies that moral judgements primarily describe facts. Early forms include A. J. Ayer's emotivism in *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936) and Charles Stevenson's *Ethics and Language* (1944). Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism try to explain how moral talk can behave like ordinary truth-apt discourse while still expressing attitudes.

**Constructivism** holds that moral truths are the outcome of an idealised procedure of reasoning or agreement. Christine Korsgaard's *The Sources of Normativity* (1996) derives moral authority from the structure of rational agency, and John Rawls's work offers a political version.

**Relativism** says that moral truth or justification is relative to a framework, such as a culture. Its descriptive premise, that moral codes differ, is widely accepted, but whether this supports the metaethical thesis is disputed. **Divine command views** hold that morality depends on God's will, which links metaethics to the theological debates about whether goodness is independent of God's commands; in Islamic thought this was a central dispute between the Mu'tazilites, who defended rational access to goodness, and the Ash'arites, who stressed divine will.

## Moral epistemology

How do we know moral truths, if there are any? Intuitionists such as W. D. Ross and, more recently, Michael Huemer hold that some moral truths are known by intuition. Coherentists appeal to reflective equilibrium. Naturalists look to experience and explanation. Evolutionary debunking arguments, advanced by Sharon Street in "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (2006) and Richard Joyce in *The Evolution of Morality* (2006), claim that if our moral faculties were shaped by natural selection without tracking moral truth, realists face a challenge. Replies deny that the debunking premise applies or argue that evolution is compatible with tracking truth. Related is the broader problem that David Hume's observation about the gap between "is" and "ought" poses for any attempt to derive moral conclusions from facts.

## Moral motivation

Internalists hold that moral judgement necessarily gives some motivation; externalists deny this. Hume's claim in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-40) that reason alone is inert and that "reason is the slave of the passions" supports the Humean theory of motivation, while Kantians, such as Korsgaard, argue that practical reason can itself motivate. Michael Smith's *The Moral Problem* (1994) clarifies how these views, together with realism, form an apparently inconsistent set. The amoralist and the weak-willed person (*akrasia*, discussed since Aristotle) are standard test cases.

## Moral psychology and development

Classical and recent theories divide over the roles of reason and sentiment. Hume and Adam Smith (*The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, 1759) emphasised sympathy; Kant emphasised reason. Developmental psychology includes Jean Piaget's *The Moral Judgment of the Child* (1932) and Lawrence Kohlberg's stage theory, criticised by Carol Gilligan in *In a Different Voice* (1982) for neglecting an ethic of care. Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model (2001) argues that moral judgements usually arise from rapid intuitions followed by post hoc reasoning, while Joshua Greene's dual-process theory links deontological responses to emotional processing and consequentialist ones to controlled reasoning. Both are contested on empirical and philosophical grounds. Work on psychopathy, empathy and moral emotions such as guilt, shame and disgust informs the discussion, as do cross-cultural studies. Islamic and other traditions have their own moral psychologies, including al-Ghazali's account of the soul's faculties and the cultivation of character, and Buddhist analyses of mental states.

Responsibility is a related theme. P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" (1962) argued that reactive attitudes such as resentment are central to our practice of holding people responsible, while debates about determinism, free will and moral luck (Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel, 1976) ask what responsibility requires.

## Debates and objections

Realists face the queerness objection and the question of how non-natural facts could be known or motivate. Anti-realists face the Frege-Geach problem, how moral sentences embedded in logical contexts get their meaning, and the charge that they cannot account for moral disagreement or moral progress. Relativism is accused of incoherence when it condemns intolerance. Experimental philosophy raises the question of whether intuitions are reliable guides, given evidence of framing and order effects, a claim that its critics dispute.

## Methods

Metaethics uses conceptual and linguistic analysis, theory of meaning, metaphysics and epistemology of normativity, and arguments from parsimony and explanation. Moral psychology adds experiments, neuroimaging, developmental studies and cross-cultural surveys, as well as phenomenological and historical work.

## Connections to other disciplines

The field links to linguistics and philosophy of language, cognitive science, evolutionary biology and anthropology, which study the origins and variety of moral norms. Law and political economy use assumptions about motivation and responsibility. Religious studies examine how theological views of divine will, revelation and natural reason shape moral epistemology.

## Common misunderstandings

Metaethics does not tell people what to do, and holding an anti-realist view does not imply that one should be less committed to moral values. Moral disagreement alone does not entail relativism. "Naturalistic fallacy" is used inconsistently: Moore's use differs from the simple is-ought point. Psychological findings about how people judge do not by themselves settle how they ought to judge.
