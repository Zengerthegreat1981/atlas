# Metaphysics

Metaphysics is the branch of philosophy that investigates the most general features of reality: what exists, what kinds of things there are, and how they are related. The name comes from the title given by later editors to Aristotle's treatise placed "after the *Physics*", and Aristotle himself described the subject as the study of being *qua* being. Questions in metaphysics are not settled simply by laboratory experiment, yet they bear on how we interpret science and everyday life.

## What the branch asks

Metaphysics divides roughly into **ontology**, the study of what exists and what it is to exist, and the study of the fundamental structure of the world, including causation, time, space, modality and identity. Typical questions: Are there abstract objects, such as numbers? What is a substance, and what is a property? Does the future exist? What does it mean to say something could have been otherwise? What makes you the same person over time? Do we have free will?

## Being and substance

Aristotle's *Categories* and *Metaphysics* (4th century BCE) treat **substance** as the primary kind of being: individual things such as a particular horse or person, which are the subjects of which other things (qualities, quantities, relations) are predicated. Plato's theory of **Forms** located true reality in abstract, unchanging entities. In the medieval period, Avicenna distinguished essence from existence, and Thomas Aquinas developed a related view. Descartes took mind and matter to be two substances, Spinoza argued for a single infinite substance, and Leibniz proposed an infinity of simple substances called monads. Hume and later empiricists questioned whether we have any idea of substance beyond bundles of qualities. Indian philosophy offers rival views too, such as the Nyaya-Vaisheshika realism about substances and universals and the Buddhist doctrine that there are no enduring substances, only momentary events.

## Universals and particulars

The **problem of universals** asks what, if anything, makes different things alike. **Realists** hold that universals, such as redness, exist; Platonic realists say they exist independently of instances, Aristotelian realists say only in instances. **Nominalists** deny universals, holding that only particulars exist. **Trope theory** posits particular property-instances. Medieval debates between realists and nominalists, notably involving Abelard, Scotus and Ockham, anticipate contemporary arguments by D. M. Armstrong and others.

## Causation

Aristotle distinguished four kinds of cause (material, formal, efficient, final). David Hume, in the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), argued that we observe regular succession but never a necessary connection, prompting regularity and counterfactual analyses; David Lewis's later counterfactual account is influential. Others defend causal powers or dispositions. Kant treated causality as a necessary structure of experience. Islamic thinkers such as al-Ghazali, in the *Incoherence of the Philosophers*, argued that God is the only true cause, a view often compared with occasionalism, while Ibn Rushd defended natural causation.

## Time

Debate about time was sharpened by J. M. E. McTaggart's 1908 argument that time is unreal. The **A-theory** takes the passage of time and a privileged present to be real; the **B-theory** holds that time consists of earlier-than and later-than relations among events, all equally real, a view often paired with eternalism, the claim that past and future things are as real as present ones. **Presentism** claims only the present exists. Physics, especially relativity, is often held to favour the B-theory because it denies absolute simultaneity, though the matter remains disputed.

## Modality and possible worlds

Metaphysicians distinguish what is actual from what is possible or necessary. Saul Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1980) revived the idea that some truths, for instance that water is H2O, are necessary though discovered empirically. David Lewis in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986) defended modal realism, the view that other possible worlds exist as concretely as ours, which most philosophers find extravagant. Rivals treat worlds as abstract representations, or reject worlds altogether.

## Personal identity and free will

What makes a person at one time the same as a person at another? John Locke proposed psychological continuity grounded in memory, Joseph Butler and Thomas Reid objected to circularity, and Derek Parfit in *Reasons and Persons* (1984) argued that identity is not what matters. Animalists hold that we are organisms. Buddhist thinkers deny a persisting self altogether.

The **free will** debate concerns whether freedom is compatible with determinism. **Compatibilists**, such as Hume and Harry Frankfurt, say yes; **libertarians** say we are free and that freedom requires indeterminism, with some also appealing to agent causation; **hard determinists** deny freedom. Peter van Inwagen's consequence argument and Galen Strawson's basic argument are prominent challenges to freedom.

## Connections and method

Metaphysics supports and is challenged by philosophy of science, which asks which entities our best theories commit us to, a method defended by Quine in "On What There Is" (1948). It connects to philosophy of mind (mental causation, consciousness), logic (modal and temporal logic), philosophy of language (reference, essence), and ethics (agency and responsibility). Logical positivists such as Rudolf Carnap rejected much metaphysics as meaningless; Kant limited it to what experience can support; recent analytic work is more confident, as in naturalised and neo-Aristotelian approaches. Process, Buddhist, Daoist and African traditions offer further alternatives, such as treating events or relations as fundamental.

## Common misunderstandings

Metaphysics does not mean the occult or the supernatural, as bookshops sometimes suggest. It is not necessarily speculation divorced from evidence: many metaphysical views are developed with close attention to physics and logic. And disagreement does not imply that the questions are meaningless; it shows that they are difficult and that theory choice depends on weighing costs and benefits.
