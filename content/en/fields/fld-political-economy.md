# Political Economy

## What the field is

Political economy studies how production, exchange, money, property and distribution are shaped by political power, law and institutions, and how in turn economic arrangements shape politics. The term is older than "economics". In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries "political economy" simply meant the science of managing the wealth of nations. When economists in the late nineteenth century began to call their subject "economics", they signalled a narrower, more technical and, in their view, less political discipline. Since the 1970s the older label has returned in several senses: as the name of a Marxian and heterodox tradition, as the name of the formal "public choice" and positive political economy literatures, and as a general label for work that places markets inside states, classes and histories.

This module of the atlas treats political economy as a family of questions rather than one method. Who gets what, and why? What do markets do well and badly? What can states do, and what do they do in practice? Why do some societies grow and others stagnate? How do money and finance work, and why do they produce crises? The answers differ sharply between schools, and the atlas aims to present each school on its own terms before presenting the objections made to it.

## The main strands

**Classical political economy.** Adam Smith's *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776) placed the division of labour, market exchange and the accumulation of capital at the centre of the subject. David Ricardo's *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817) developed theories of rent, wages, profit and comparative advantage. Thomas Malthus, John Stuart Mill (*Principles of Political Economy*, 1848) and others extended the framework. The classical writers were concerned with long-run growth, class income shares and the role of the state.

**Marxian political economy.** Karl Marx's *Capital* (volume I, 1867; later volumes edited by Friedrich Engels, 1885 and 1894) offered a critique of classical economy, a labour theory of value, a theory of exploitation and an account of capitalism's historical dynamics and crises. Later contributors include Rosa Luxemburg, Rudolf Hilferding, Vladimir Lenin on imperialism, and, in the twentieth century, Paul Baran and Paul Sweezy, the French regulation school and analytical Marxists such as John Roemer. Whether the labour theory of value survives the "transformation problem" is a long-standing dispute within and outside the tradition.

**Marginalist and neoclassical economics.** In the 1870s William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger and Léon Walras independently shifted attention to marginal utility and individual choice; Alfred Marshall's *Principles of Economics* (1890) consolidated the approach. Neoclassical theory models agents optimising under constraints, markets clearing through prices, and welfare properties of equilibria. The Austrian school (Menger, Ludwig von Mises, Friedrich Hayek) emphasised dispersed knowledge, entrepreneurship and the limits of planning, and diverges from mainstream formalism in method.

**Keynesian and post-Keynesian economics.** John Maynard Keynes's *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936) argued that aggregate demand can leave an economy at persistent unemployment and that fiscal policy can respond. Postwar "neoclassical synthesis" Keynesianism, monetarism (Milton Friedman), new classical macroeconomics, new Keynesian models and post-Keynesian schools (Joan Robinson, Nicholas Kaldor, Hyman Minsky) continue to debate the role of expectations, money and stabilisation policy.

**Institutionalist traditions.** Thorstein Veblen, John R. Commons, the German historical school (Gustav Schmoller), Karl Polanyi (*The Great Transformation*, 1944) and, later, the new institutional economics of Ronald Coase, Oliver Williamson and Douglass North examine how rules, norms and organisations structure markets. Polanyi's claim that markets are embedded in social relations and North's emphasis on property rights and transaction costs are different, and sometimes opposed, contributions.

**Development and international political economy.** Debates over industrialisation, trade and dependency include Friedrich List's national system, Alexander Gerschenkron on late industrialisation, Raúl Prebisch and the structuralists, dependency theory (Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto, André Gunder Frank), the East Asian "developmental state" literature (Chalmers Johnson, Alice Amsden, Robert Wade), and later work by Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, Dani Rodrik and Amartya Sen's capability approach.

**Contemporary strands.** These include public choice (James Buchanan, Gordon Tullock, Anthony Downs), positive political economy and formal models of institutions, behavioural and experimental economics, comparative political economy and "varieties of capitalism" (Peter Hall and David Soskice, 2001), feminist and ecological economics, and large-scale empirical work on inequality such as Thomas Piketty's *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013).

## Arab and Islamic economic thought

Ibn Khaldun's *Muqaddimah* (1377) is widely read as an early analysis of how labour, taxation, urban life, group solidarity ('asabiyya) and dynastic cycles interact. He observed that excessive taxation can reduce incentives and revenue, a point later invoked in supply-side debates; historians caution against reading modern theory back into him. Other medieval writers, including al-Ghazali, Ibn Taymiyya and al-Maqrizi on money and price disturbances, discussed markets, money and the duties of rulers. In the twentieth century, "Islamic economics" developed as a modern project (Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr's *Iqtisaduna*, 1961, and later scholarship on Islamic banking), and critics and sympathisers still disagree on whether it is a distinct science or an ethical and legal overlay on conventional economics. The rentier-state literature, associated with Hossein Mahdavy (1970), Hazem Beblawi and Giacomo Luciani (1987) and later debated critically, asks how states financed by external rents rather than domestic taxation relate to their citizens. Whether oil rents explain authoritarian durability remains contested, as comparative evidence is mixed.

## Methods and evidence

The field uses formal modelling, econometrics and natural experiments, archival and historical analysis, comparative case studies, ethnography, input-output and national-accounts data, and normative argument. These are not interchangeable. A mathematical model shows what follows from assumptions; an empirical estimate shows what a dataset suggests under identification assumptions; a normative claim states what ought to be done. A recurring caution of this module is to keep these layers apart.

## Common misunderstandings

Political economy is not one ideology. Schools range from market liberal to Marxist, and most contain internal splits. "Economic theory" is not the same as "economic findings", and neither settles value questions such as how to trade off equality and efficiency. Disagreement among schools is sometimes empirical, sometimes about method and sometimes about values, and it is useful to ask which.

## How the atlas is organised

This module opens with field introductions: this root, then Macroeconomics, Money and Finance; Development and the Global Economy; and Labour, Inequality and Distribution. Each introduction points toward thinkers, works, schools, concepts and debates, linked through the atlas's relation system. Suggested paths: begin with this page and the classical authors, then choose a thread (money and crises, development, or labour and distribution) and read the matching field page.

## Limits of this version

Version 1 is a map, not a textbook. Coverage is uneven: Anglophone and European traditions are better represented than Latin American, South Asian, African and East Asian ones, and the Arab and Islamic material is introductory. Political science, law and sociology are touched only where they bear on economic questions. Quantitative claims are deliberately avoided where they could not be sourced cleanly.
