# Normative Ethics

Normative ethics is the branch of moral philosophy that tries to say, in general terms, what makes actions right or wrong, what makes character good, and what makes a life go well. It differs from metaethics, which asks what moral claims are, and from applied ethics, which addresses specific problems, although the three constantly inform one another.

## What the field asks

The central questions are these. What makes an act right: its consequences, its conformity to duty, the character it expresses, or its justifiability to others? What is the good life, and what is the relation between my own flourishing and the claims of other people? Are there absolute prohibitions, or can anything be justified if the stakes are high enough? Whose interests count, and how much? How do we handle conflicts between values? Normative theories offer systematic answers, and their rivalry has structured the field for centuries.

## Main positions

**Virtue ethics** makes character the primary subject of evaluation. Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* (4th century BCE) takes the human good to be flourishing (*eudaimonia*), achieved by exercising virtues such as courage, temperance and justice under the guidance of practical wisdom (*phronesis*). Confucian ethics, as in the *Analects*, centres on cultivating *ren* (humaneness) and ritual propriety. G. E. M. Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) prompted a modern revival, followed by Philippa Foot, Alasdair MacIntyre's *After Virtue* (1981) and Rosalind Hursthouse's *On Virtue Ethics* (1999). In the Islamic tradition Miskawayh's *Tahdhib al-akhlaq* (Refinement of Character) combines Greek virtue theory with Islamic thought, and al-Ghazali's *Ihya' ulum al-din* (Revival of the Religious Sciences) treats the cultivation of the soul at length.

**Deontology** holds that some acts are required or forbidden independently of their results. Immanuel Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) grounds duty in reason and the categorical imperative, which has several formulations, including acting only on maxims one could will as universal law and treating humanity never merely as a means. W. D. Ross's *The Right and the Good* (1930) defends plural, irreducible *prima facie* duties. Divine command theories, in which morality rests on God's commands, are a theological relative; their Islamic counterpart in the debates between Ash'arite and Mu'tazilite theologians asks whether acts are good because God commands them or whether God commands them because they are good.

**Consequentialism** holds that the rightness of acts depends on the goodness of outcomes. Classical utilitarianism, in Jeremy Bentham's *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789) and John Stuart Mill's *Utilitarianism* (1863), requires maximising overall well-being impartially. Henry Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics* (1874) gave it its most rigorous classical statement. Later developments include rule consequentialism (Brad Hooker's *Ideal Code, Real World*, 2000), preference utilitarianism and R. M. Hare's two-level view in *Moral Thinking* (1981). Peter Singer's *Animal Liberation* (1975) and "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) applied the approach to animals and global poverty.

**Contractualism** grounds morality in what could be justified to others. T. M. Scanlon's *What We Owe to Each Other* (1998) says that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject. The tradition draws on Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau and Kant and, in political form, on John Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971).

**Care ethics** begins from relationships of dependence and responsiveness. Carol Gilligan's *In a Different Voice* (1982) and Nel Noddings's *Caring* (1984) challenged models of morality built on impartial rules, and later work by Virginia Held and Joan Tronto developed care as a political and social value.

Other approaches include natural law (Aquinas), ethical particularism (Jonathan Dancy), the capabilities approach (Amartya Sen, Martha Nussbaum), and African communitarian ethics such as ubuntu, which understands personhood through relations with others.

## Major debates and standard objections

Against consequentialism, critics argue that it can license sacrificing an innocent person for aggregate benefit, that it is excessively demanding, and that it ignores the separateness of persons, a point pressed by Rawls and Bernard Williams (in the "integrity" objection, *Utilitarianism: For and Against*, 1973). Defenders respond with rule-based and satisficing variants, or argue that intuitions against the verdicts are unreliable.

Against deontology, critics point to rigidity when duties conflict or catastrophe looms, the problem of how to rank duties, and the charge of rule worship. Defenders appeal to thresholds, to the distinction between doing and allowing, and to the doctrine of double effect.

Against virtue ethics, critics claim that it gives insufficient action guidance, that virtues differ across cultures, and that empirical "situationist" psychology casts doubt on stable character traits, a debate involving John Doris and Gilbert Harman. Defenders reply that virtues include practical wisdom that yields guidance, and that the empirical findings are contested.

Against contractualism, objections concern those who cannot take part in agreements, such as animals and future generations, and the vagueness of "reasonably reject". Against care ethics, critics worry about partiality and parochialism; defenders argue that care and justice are complementary.

Further debates concern moral pluralism versus monism, whether morality is demanding or leaves room for personal projects, agent-relative reasons, and the relation between moral and prudential reasons. Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) and *On What Matters* (2011) explored population ethics and attempted a convergence of Kantian, contractualist and consequentialist views.

## Methods

Normative ethicists test theories against considered judgements about cases, often using thought experiments such as the trolley problem introduced by Philippa Foot (1967) and developed by Judith Jarvis Thomson. They seek reflective equilibrium between principle and judgement, analyse concepts such as harm, consent and responsibility, and interpret historical texts. Some work builds formal models, for instance in welfare economics and decision theory. Others examine moral traditions through textual and comparative study.

## Connections to other disciplines

Psychology and cognitive science address how people actually judge and decide, for example in Joshua Greene's dual-process work and Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionism, which are sometimes used to challenge the evidential weight of intuitions. Law and political philosophy overlap in theories of rights, punishment and justice. Political economy and welfare economics use consequentialist and contractarian ideas in assessing policy. Religious studies and comparative ethics examine the moral systems of Islam, Judaism, Christianity, Buddhism, Hinduism and Confucianism, which often blend features of the three main families.

## Common misunderstandings

The three main theories are not simply "rules versus results versus character": every theory discusses all three, and differs in what it treats as fundamental. Utilitarianism is not selfishness, since it counts everyone's welfare. Kant's ethics is not simple rule-following; it asks whether a maxim can be universalised. Virtue ethics is not mere good habit, and it is not opposed to principles. Religious ethics is not necessarily divine command theory. And a theory's difficulty with a hard case does not show it false; every theory faces such cases and philosophers debate how to weigh them.
