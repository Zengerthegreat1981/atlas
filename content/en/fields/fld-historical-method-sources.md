# Historical Method and Sources

Historical method designates the systematic body of principles, rules, and analytical procedures by which historians locate, authenticate, interpret, and synthesize surviving traces of the past into reasoned historical accounts. Because the past cannot be directly observed or replicated experimentally, historical knowledge is necessarily indirect, resting on the critical examination of evidence. Methodological rigor distinguishes historical scholarship from myth, polemic, fiction, and unverified memory.

## The nature of historical traces and sources

Historical inquiry begins with surviving traces (*vestigia*). Historians divide these into primary sources—materials generated during or immediately adjacent to the period under investigation—and secondary literature, which comprises later historical interpretations and scholarly reconstructions.

Primary sources encompass diverse human artifacts:
- **Written and administrative records:** State charters, treaties, statutes, legal transcripts, tax registers, diplomatic correspondence, and private diaries.
- **Literary and narrative accounts:** Chronicles, annals, biographies, travelogues, and polemical treatises.
- **Material culture and archaeological remains:** Architecture, coins (numismatics), tools, and physical monuments.
- **Inscriptions and epigraphic texts:** Royal decrees, tomb inscriptions, stelae, and papyrological fragments.

Historians distinguish between intentional records—documents created explicitly to inform posterity or shape opinion—and unintentional traces, such as fiscal tallies or parish registers. While intentional texts reveal mentalities, ideologies, and propaganda, unintentional traces frequently provide more reliable data on socioeconomic structures, demography, and material life because they were not composed to persuade a future audience.

## Source criticism: external and internal

The foundation of historical technique is source criticism, systematised from the work of Jean Mabillon (*De re diplomatica*, 1681) and set out in the manual of Charles-Victor Langlois and Charles Seignobos in their 1898 manual. Source criticism operates across two reciprocal axes: external criticism and internal criticism.

External criticism (heuristics) interrogates the physical and provenance status of the document:
- **Authenticity:** Auxiliary sciences such as paleography (the study of ancient handwriting), diplomatics (the analysis of document formulas), and epigraphy establish whether script, material, and vocabulary match the claimed date and location.
- **Provenance and transmission:** Determining where the document was preserved, through what copies it survived, and what scribal errors or interpolations entered during transmission.

Internal criticism (hermeneutics) interrogates semantic content, credibility, and authorial perspective:
- **Meaning:** What the words signified in their specific linguistic and cultural milieu.
- **Authorial competence:** Whether the author was an eyewitness or relied on hearsay, and whether they had the competence to comprehend what they witnessed.
- **Bias and motive:** What religious, political, or institutional interests shaped the account, and what was omitted or distorted.
- **Corroboration:** Verifying claims through independent, non-collusive testimonies.

A comparable tradition developed in classical Islamic scholarship. To verify reports of prophetic traditions and early conquests, scholars instituted *isnad* (chain of transmission) criticism alongside *'ilm al-jarh wa-l-ta'dil* (biographical evaluation of transmitters), assessing moral probity (*'adalah*), mnemonic accuracy (*dabt*), and chronological possibility of meeting teachers. In the fourteenth century CE, Ibn Khaldun (732–808 AH / 1332–1406 CE) expanded internal criticism (*naqd al-akhbar*), arguing that transmitter verification cannot establish truth if the reported content (*matn*) contradicts the physical, ecological, and sociological laws of human civilization (*'ilm al-'umran*).

## Archives and archival methodology

The modern archive, emerging alongside the bureaucratic nation-state in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, is not a neutral repository of facts, but an institutional site structured by state power, administrative classification, and imperial governance.

Working in archives requires specialized methods. Ann Laura Stoler (*Along the Archival Grain*, 2009) emphasized reading archives not merely as extraction sites for raw data, but as artifacts of statecraft that reveal the anxieties and epistemological assumptions of administrators. Simultaneously, historians read "against the grain," as urged by Subaltern Studies scholars and Michel-Rolph Trouillot (*Silencing the Past*, 1995), identifying systemic silences where the perspectives of enslaved people, peasants, women, and colonized subjects were omitted from official records.

## Oral history and non-textual sources

In societies with oral cultures, or among groups excluded from official writing, oral sources constitute essential evidence. Oral history comprises recorded interviews with living participants reflecting on their lived experiences. Oral tradition, by contrast, denotes the verbal transmission of narratives, genealogies, poetry, and laws across generations without writing, as exemplified by West African Mandinka *griots* preserving *The Epic of Sundiata*.

Africanist historian Jan Vansina (*Oral Tradition as History*, 1985) developed influential methods for evaluating oral traditions, analyzing performance contexts, mnemonic formulas, and political uses of memory. Historians of oral testimony, such as Paul Thompson (*The Voice of the Past*, 1978), confront challenges of retrospective bias and shifting memory, yet oral evidence offers valuable insight into subjective experience and popular attitudes that textual archives overlook.

## Periodisation, time, and scale

Historical time is constructed through periodisation. Dividing the past into eras—such as Antiquity, the Middle Ages, the Early Modern, and the Modern—provides necessary organizational frameworks, yet all periodisations are interpretive conventions that often impose European timelines onto global societies whose historical rhythms followed distinct trajectories.

Fernand Braudel restructured the treatment of historical time by proposing three distinct scales:
1. **The *longue durée*:** Geographical and environmental time, moving almost imperceptibly, shaping human possibilities over centuries through climate, geography, and ecology.
2. **Conjunctures:** Socioeconomic cycles spanning decades, such as demographic fluctuations, trade trends, and price waves.
3. **Events (*histoire événementielle*):** The rapid time of political and diplomatic incidents, which Braudel described as surface agitation.

German theorist Reinhart Koselleck (*Futures Past*, 1979) enriched temporal theory by identifying *Zeitschichten* (sediments of time), arguing that multiple historical temporalities coexist simultaneously within any given society.

## Causation, explanation, and quantification

Historians analyze causation as an interplay between long-term structural preconditions, intermediate institutional factors, and immediate triggers, negotiating the tension between structural determinism and individual human agency.

Debates over historical explanation have divided philosophers. Positivists such as Carl Hempel (1942) proposed the "covering law model," arguing that historical explanation must subsume individual events under universal empirical laws. This was opposed by William Dray (*Laws and Explanation in History*, 1957), who drew on R. G. Collingwood's (*The Idea of History*, 1946) view, and argued that history requires empathetic reconstruction of rational human intentions rather than natural laws.

In the 1960s and 1970s, cliometrics introduced econometric modeling and quantitative methods. Scholars such as Robert Fogel analyzed massive demographic, agricultural, and transportation datasets. While quantification resolved specific empirical debates, historians recognized its limitations: cultural values, emotional life, and political ideology cannot be reduced to statistical aggregates.

## Narrative, memory, and the objectivity debate

The late twentieth century destabilized the nineteenth-century belief in absolute historical detachment. In *What Is History?* (1961), E. H. Carr argued that facts never reach the historian pure, but are selected and refracted through the historian's own social context. Conversely, G. R. Elton (*The Practice of History*, 1967) defended traditional documentary independence. Peter Novick (*That Noble Dream*, 1988) traced how the ideal of objectivity shifted within the American historical profession from a confident scientific credo to a contested professional norm.

Meanwhile, Hayden White (*Metahistory*, 1973) analyzed the narrative dimension of historical writing, arguing that historians employ poetic tropes and narrative emplotments to transform archival fragments into coherent narratives. Furthermore, collective memory studies (Maurice Halbwachs, Pierre Nora) highlighted the divergence between living commemoration and critical historical investigation. The contemporary consensus rejects naive positivism without lapsing into radical relativism: historical claims must remain accountable to verified empirical traces while acknowledging the interpretive position of the historian.
