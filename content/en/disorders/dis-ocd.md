# Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder

A mental disorder characterized by recurrent obsessions (thoughts, mental images, urges) that cause severe anxiety or distress, and compulsions (behaviors or mental acts) aimed at relieving this distress. The person usually knows that the obsessions are irrational (in adults) but resists them with difficulty, and satisfying them consumes a long time (usually more than an hour a day) or causes marked functional distress.

## Clinical Ceiling

This entry, like all entries of the type 'disorder/clinical condition' in this atlas, is committed to describing the diagnostic category descriptively as it appears in DSM-5-TR and ICD-11, without offering any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual treatment guidance. It describes; it neither diagnoses nor treats.

## Relation to the Corresponding Philosophical Concept

Obsessions in OCD must not be confused with 'methodical doubt' as Descartes formulated it in his *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641; see con-cartesian-doubt-method). Cartesian doubt is a voluntary cognitive tool that begins and ends by the philosopher's will and serves the aim of establishing a first certainty (the cogito), whereas the obsession in OCD is an involuntary compulsive intrusion that consumes time and causes distress, and whose sufferer seeks to stop it, not to exploit it cognitively.

## Notes on the Diagnostic Criteria

DSM-5-TR requires the presence of obsessions and/or compulsions that are time-consuming (for example more than an hour a day) or cause marked distress or functional impairment. DSM-5-TR distinguishes several dimensions of obsessions: contamination, forbidden thoughts, harm-related symptoms, symmetry symptoms, and others. It also specifies the level of insight (good or fair insight / poor insight / absent insight or delusional beliefs) and whether the disorder is tic-related.

ICD-11 groups OCD under "Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders" (6B20), with a broadly similar description of obsessions and compulsions and with insight specifiers (fair to good insight; poor to absent insight).

## Core Symptoms

- **Obsessions**: recurrent thoughts, mental images, or urges considered intrusive and unwanted. The person knows they are the product of his own mind. They differ from excessive worry in being specific and recurrent.
- **Compulsions**: repetitive behaviors or mental acts that the person performs to prevent or reduce the anxiety associated with the obsessions. They are not connected in a realistic way to what they aim to prevent.
- **Resistance**: the attempt to resist the obsessions or compulsions.
- **Functional distress**: a clear effect on work, relationships, and daily activities.

## Stepwise Clinical Management

### Stage 1: Comprehensive Assessment

- Assessing the severity of obsessions and compulsions (Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale, Y-BOCS).
- Assessing insight into the irrationality of the obsessions.
- Excluding comorbid psychotic disorders (symptoms may overlap).
- Assessing suicidal thoughts (suicidal obsessions or the avoidance of frightening situations may provoke despair).

### Stage 2: Psychotherapy, First Line

Exposure and response prevention therapy (ERP) is the best-supported first-line psychological treatment:

- Building an exposure hierarchy from least to most anxiety-provoking.
- Repeated exposure to the triggering situations while refraining from compulsions.
- Weekly sessions, usually for 12-20 weeks.
- Daily homework between sessions.

### Stage 3: Pharmacotherapy (When Needed)

Serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the usual first-line medication; response is judged over a longer period than in depression, and decisions about dosing and duration belong to a prescribing clinician. Clomipramine (a tricyclic antidepressant) is a further option.

### Stage 4: Complex or Resistant Treatment

- Combining ERP + SSRI.
- Neuromodulation: deep brain stimulation (DBS) is reserved for severe treatment-resistant cases, and transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) is a more recent option.

## Associated Techniques by School and Stage

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): The Accepted First Line

- **In vivo exposure with response prevention (ERP)**: the cornerstone of OCD treatment. It builds new learning that anxiety will decrease without performing compulsions.
- **Building a graded exposure hierarchy**: it prepares graduated situations from least to most difficult, carried out before ERP begins.
- **Cognitive restructuring**: useful for obsessions of excessive responsibility (for example: "I am responsible for everyone's safety"). Used together with ERP.
- **The downward arrow technique**: it reveals the core beliefs that feed the obsessions ("What if the worst happens?" → "I am a corrupt person who deserves punishment").
- **Delayed response strategy**: delaying the compulsive act by 5-15 minutes, gradually, to test the capacity to tolerate anxiety.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): An Alternative for Those Who Do Not Respond to ERP

- **Leaves on a stream**: detaching from the content of the obsessions as passing notes on the surface of the water.
- **Labeling thoughts**: "I notice a thought that I will become contaminated" instead of believing it literally.
- **Willingness vs. willfulness**: opening up to the inevitable discomfort instead of fighting it through compulsive cleanliness.
- **Thanking your mind**: treating the obsessions as an overactive guard who deserves thanks, not conflict.

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT): For Complex Cases with Comorbid Disorders

- **Radical acceptance**: accepting the existence of obsessions and embarrassing thoughts without trying to suppress them (important in OCD with forbidden thoughts).
- **STOP**: a crisis technique to halt the chain of compulsive behavior when it escalates.
- **IMPROVE**: improving the capacity to tolerate anxiety between sessions.
- **Opposite action**: doing the opposite of the compulsive urge when the opposite behavior is more consistent with values.

## Common Comorbid Disorders

- **Depression**: very common among people with OCD.
- **Other anxiety disorders**: GAD, social phobia.
- **Avoidant personality disorder**.
- **Tourette's disorder**: genetic and clinical overlap.
- **Eating disorders**: especially in OCD with food obsessions.

## Cultural Notes

- Cultural variation is observed in the content of obsessions (for example: religious obsessions in Islamic and Christian contexts).
- The transition between "normal religiosity" and "religious obsession" requires sensitivity.
- The language used in treatment must respect the patient's cultural framework.

## When to Seek Specialized Help

- Obsessions or compulsions that consume more than an hour a day.
- Marked functional distress (work, relationships, hygiene).
- Thoughts of harming oneself or others.
- Drug resistance to the first treatment.
- Complex comorbid disorders.

## Limits and Risks

- ERP is difficult and prone to dropout; adherence to homework is essential.
- When ERP is not tolerated or not effective, other approaches (such as ACT-based methods) may be considered.
- Severe cases may call for pharmacological intervention or even deep brain stimulation.
- Compulsions in OCD do not mean "enjoyment," but a means of relieving anxiety.
