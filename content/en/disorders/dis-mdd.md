# Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

Major depressive disorder is a formal diagnostic category for a sustained pattern of low mood and loss of interest or pleasure that causes functional impairment. It is fundamentally different from sadness as a passing human experience: sadness is a response to a specific loss, while major depression is a pattern defined by duration, severity, and functional impact, with explicit diagnostic thresholds. The disorder is among the most common and most studied conditions in psychiatry, and also one of the most contested — its boundaries, its causes, and the relative merits of psychotherapy and medication are all subjects of live debate. This entry is a general informational description of the category, not a diagnostic tool and not a substitute for clinical assessment.

## What it is

The defining feature is a major depressive episode: a period of at least two weeks in which depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure is present most of the day, nearly every day, together with other symptoms from a defined list, and in which the symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning. The episode is distinguished from ordinary sadness by its persistence, its breadth — it touches sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, and the sense of self — and its resistance to the ordinary consolations that lift a passing low mood. The category is descriptive: it groups a pattern of symptoms without claiming that all cases share one cause.

## Diagnostic features

DSM-5-TR requires five or more of nine symptoms during the same two-week period, with at least one being depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure: depressed mood, markedly diminished interest or pleasure, significant weight or appetite change, insomnia or hypersomnia, psychomotor agitation or retardation, fatigue, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, diminished concentration or indecisiveness, and recurrent thoughts of death or suicidal ideation. The symptoms must cause significant impairment and must not be attributable to a substance or another medical condition. ICD-11 (code 6A70 for a single depressive episode, 6A71 for recurrent disorder) describes the same picture in similar terms. DSM-5 removed the bereavement exclusion that DSM-IV had applied: a major depressive episode following the death of a loved one can now be diagnosed if the full criteria are met, with the clinician distinguishing it from prolonged grief disorder. 

## History

Depression as a clinical concept descends from melancholia, a term in use since Hippocratic medicine, but the modern category is a twentieth-century construction. Emil Kraepelin's distinction between manic-depressive illness and dementia praecox, set out in the successive editions of his textbook from 1899, first separated mood disorder from schizophrenia as a diagnostic entity. The decisive step toward the present category was the operational criteria movement of the 1970s: the Research Diagnostic Criteria of Spitzer and colleagues (1978) and the DSM-III (1980) defined major depression by explicit symptom counts and durations, replacing clinical impression with checkable thresholds. Aaron Beck's cognitive theory, first set out in *Depression: Clinical, Experimental, and Theoretical Aspects* (1967) and developed into a treatment in *Cognitive Therapy of Depression* (1979, with Rush, Shaw, and Emery), gave the field its dominant psychological model alongside the biological one. 

## Causes and mechanisms

No single cause accounts for major depression, and the contemporary picture is deliberately plural. Beck's cognitive triad describes a stable negative view of the self, the world, and the future that maintains low mood and feeds itself: the person interprets setbacks as confirmation of worthlessness, and the interpretation deepens the mood that produced it. The biological account points to dysregulation in monoamine neurotransmission and, more recently, to changes in neuroplasticity, stress-axis regulation, and inflammation; the SSRI drugs were built on the monoamine hypothesis, though the hypothesis in its simple form — depression as a chemical deficiency — is now regarded as inadequate by most researchers. Johann Hari's *Lost Connections* (2018) popularised the argument that social and environmental disconnection is a major driver, a claim that is debated but that has pushed the literature toward a broader causal picture than the purely neurochemical one.

## Treatment

The two principal treatments are psychotherapy, above all cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy, and antidepressant medication, above all SSRIs. Both have substantial trial support, and the debate over their relative efficacy — whether psychotherapy, medication, or their combination works best, and for whom — is one of the most studied questions in psychiatry, with the combination generally showing a modest advantage for severe depression. The choice among these is a clinical decision that depends on severity, history, preference, and risk, and it belongs to a clinician who has assessed the person.

## The existential dimension

Some existential readings link an aspect of depression to the loss of meaning — existential meaninglessness — but this must not be confused with the claim that all clinical depression is a crisis of meaning, which would be a methodological error stated plainly in the category's own notes. A person can be depressed for reasons that have nothing to do with meaning, and a person in a crisis of meaning need not meet the criteria for a depressive episode. The existential dimension is a partial reading of some cases, not a theory of the disorder. 

## Key works

Beck's *Depression* (1967) and *Cognitive Therapy of Depression* (1979) are the founding texts of the cognitive model and its treatment. DSM-5-TR (2022) is the current diagnostic source. Hari's *Lost Connections* (2018) is the most influential recent popular account of the social causes debate. Styron's *Darkness Visible* (1990) is the classic first-person account.
