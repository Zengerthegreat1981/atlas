# Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

Post-traumatic stress disorder is a persistent response to a traumatic event, characterised by intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, negative changes in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal. The person relives the event in memories, nightmares, or flashbacks; avoids thoughts, feelings, and reminders associated with it; thinks and feels differently about themselves and the world; and lives in a state of heightened vigilance and reactivity. The disorder is the point where the normal aftermath of trauma — which is itself painful and disruptive — does not resolve, and instead becomes a lasting reorganisation of the person's inner life. This entry is a general informational description of a diagnostic category, not a diagnostic tool and not a guide to coping with trauma individually.

## What it is

The defining feature is the persistence of the response. Most people exposed to a traumatic event experience acute stress reactions that fade over weeks; in PTSD the response continues for more than a month and causes clinically significant distress or functional impairment. The intrusive symptoms are the most distinctive: the event returns unbidden, in the daytime as intrusive memories and at night as nightmares, and in flashbacks the person may feel or act as if the event were recurring. Avoidance is the second pillar: the person avoids the thoughts and feelings connected to the event, and avoids the people, places, and situations that remind them of it, which narrows their life progressively. The third and fourth clusters — negative alterations in cognition and mood, and alterations in arousal and reactivity — complete the picture: persistent negative beliefs about the self and the world, blame, emotional numbing, loss of interest, irritability, hypervigilance, an exaggerated startle response, and disturbed sleep.

## Diagnostic features

DSM-5-TR requires exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence, directly or indirectly, together with symptoms from four clusters: intrusion (at least one symptom), avoidance (at least one), negative alterations in cognition and mood (at least two), and alterations in arousal and reactivity (at least two), lasting more than one month and causing significant distress or impairment. ICD-11 (code 6B40) uses a narrower definition, requiring only three core features — re-experiencing, avoidance, and a persistent sense of current threat — and places the broader symptoms, including emotional numbing and negative self-concept, in a separate category, complex PTSD (code 6B41), which is used when the trauma was prolonged or repeated. 

## History

The history of PTSD is the history of trauma being recognised, forgotten, and recognised again. Nineteenth-century railway accidents produced "railway spine"; the First World War produced "shell shock"; the Second World War produced the KZ syndrome among concentration camp survivors. Judith Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) reconstructed this history and argued that the recognition of trauma has always depended on political movements — the anti-war movement, the feminist movement — that made the suffering of particular groups visible. The diagnosis itself entered DSM-III in 1980, largely as a result of advocacy by Vietnam veterans and the clinicians who treated them, and it was revised through DSM-IV (1994) and DSM-5 (2013), which moved it from the anxiety disorders to a new chapter on trauma- and stressor-related disorders. 

## Causes and mechanisms

The dominant psychological account is the cognitive model of Ehlers and Clark (2000): PTSD persists when the traumatic event is processed in a way that produces a sense of serious current threat, through negative appraisals of the trauma and its consequences and through a trauma memory that is poorly elaborated and easily triggered by matching cues. Neurobiologically, the disorder involves hyperreactivity of the amygdala, reduced top-down control from the medial prefrontal cortex, and a smaller hippocampal volume, findings that van der Kolk (2014) synthesised for a general readership. Comorbidity is high, above all with major depression and substance use disorders.

## Treatment

The treatments with the strongest evidence are trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapies: prolonged exposure, in which the person revisits the traumatic memory in a controlled way until its power diminishes, and cognitive processing therapy, which targets the distorted beliefs about the event and its meaning. Eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR) has comparable support. SSRIs, particularly sertraline and paroxetine, are the medications with the best evidence, though their effects are generally smaller than those of the psychological treatments. Treatment decisions belong to a clinician who has assessed the person, and they should never be undertaken without safety and support in place.

## The existential reading

Some existential frameworks read trauma as a collapse in the basic existential structures through which a person understands the world and the self — not merely a failure of information processing, as some cognitive-behavioural accounts describe it — and this is a genuine theoretical dispute between frameworks. Trauma often confronts the person directly with existential givens (death, the fragility of existence, the loss of basic safety) whatever therapeutic framework is later used. 

## Key works

Herman's *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) is the founding historical and clinical text. Van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* (2014) is the standard synthesis of the neurobiology. Ehlers and Clark's 2000 paper is the key statement of the cognitive model. DSM-5-TR (2022) is the current diagnostic source.
