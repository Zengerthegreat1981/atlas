# Dissociative Amnesia

A disorder characterized by gaps in memory that cannot be explained by ordinary forgetting, involving an inability to recall important personal information (most often localized or selective), not explained by a neurological medical condition or the effect of a substance, and causing marked distress or functional impairment.

## The clinical ceiling

This entry is committed to describing dissociative amnesia descriptively as a diagnostic category documented in DSM-5-TR and ICD-11, without offering any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual therapeutic guidance: description, not diagnosis or treatment. Determining whether a particular memory gap meets this diagnosis requires a specialized clinical assessment that rules out organic causes and malingering.

## Relation to the corresponding philosophical concept

Dissociative amnesia as a clinically bounded diagnostic category must not be confused with memory (con-memory) as a broader philosophical and cognitive concept of the continuity of identity and consciousness through time, as treated by Bergson in *Matter and Memory* (referred to within con-memory), where memory is a continuous existential construction of the self, not merely a storage mechanism liable to damage. Dissociative amnesia disorder is a clinical interruption, defined by criteria, in this function, and not a philosophical reflection on the nature of memory itself or its relation to time and identity.

## Notes on the diagnostic criteria

DSM-5-TR requires an inability to recall important autobiographical information, usually of a traumatic or stressful nature, that is inconsistent with ordinary forgetting and causes clinically significant distress or impairment. The amnesia must not be attributable to a substance or a neurological or other medical condition, and is not better explained by another dissociative disorder, PTSD, acute stress disorder, or a somatic symptom disorder. Localized or selective amnesia is the most common pattern; generalized amnesia is rare. The specifier "with dissociative fugue" (F44.1 in ICD-10-CM) applies when there is apparently purposeful travel or bewildered wandering associated with the amnesia. The clinician must also differentiate it from organic amnesia and from feigned amnesia.

ICD-11 classifies dissociative amnesia under 6B66, describing an inability to recall important autobiographical memories, typically of recent traumatic or stressful events, that is not explained by ordinary forgetting, a medical condition, or a substance, and that causes significant impairment. Dissociative fugue is a specifier.

## Core symptoms

- **Localized amnesia**: inability to recall events of a specific period (such as the hours after an accident).
- **Selective amnesia**: forgetting certain events within a period while remembering others.
- **Generalized amnesia**: wide loss of personal memory (place, name, date).
- **Continuous amnesia**: a rare pattern in which the person cannot form memories from a particular point onward.
- **Loss of identity**: rarely, forgetting one's name and personal information, sometimes with travel away from home (dissociative fugue).
- **Identity confusion**: difficulty determining "who am I" after memories are recovered.

## Gradual clinical management

### Phase 1: Comprehensive assessment and ruling out organic causes

- Comprehensive neurological assessment: ruling out epilepsy, head injuries, other neurological conditions.
- Ruling out substance effects (alcohol, drugs).
- Assessing trauma history: were there childhood or more recent traumas?
- Assessing current safety: is the person at risk?
- Assessing co-occurring disorders (PTSD, depression, anxiety).

### Phase 2: Stabilization and building safety

- Building a safe and stable therapeutic relationship.
- Teaching mental grounding techniques.
- Calming the nervous system before any attempt at recovery.
- Making sure the client's environment is safe enough for recovering memories.

### Phase 3: Gradual work with memory

- Memories often return spontaneously once the person is safe; therapy does not aim to force recovery.
- Treatment generally follows the phase-oriented model used for trauma-related disorders (safety and stabilization first), and the evidence base for specific techniques in dissociative amnesia is limited.
- Approaches sometimes used in trauma-focused work include EMDR, sensorimotor or body-oriented approaches, and parts-based therapies such as IFS; their effectiveness specifically for dissociative amnesia has not been established.

### Phase 4: Processing the recovered memories and integration

- Reprocessing the recovered memories in a safe therapeutic context.
- Linking the memories to the client's overall identity.
- Processing any strong emotions (guilt, shame, sadness, anger) connected to the memories.
- Developing a new meaning for the experience.

### Phase 5: Reconstruction and relapse prevention

- Treating co-occurring personality or relationship disorders.
- Developing healthy coping strategies.
- Building a social support network.
- Long-term follow-up to prevent relapse.

## Related techniques by school and phase

### Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT): for cognitive support

- **Grounding techniques**: calming the nervous system before recovery.
- **Cognitive restructuring**: processing guilt and shame around the memories.
- **Reflective writing**: recovering memories and linking them to identity.
- **Self-compassion exercises**: especially after recovering painful memories.
- **Building a graded exposure hierarchy**: gradual preparation for difficult memories.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): for changing the relationship with loss

- **Leaves on a stream**: detaching from intrusive memories.
- **Labeling thoughts**: "I notice the thought that I have forgotten something important" instead of worry.
- **Observer self meditation**: developing gentle observation.
- **Radical acceptance**: accepting the current loss without forced attempts.

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT): for emotion regulation

- **Radical acceptance**: accepting the loss.
- **Self-soothing with the five senses**: self-soothing at times of anxiety.
- **The "observe" skill**: watching the experience calmly.

### Other specialized schools

Several trauma-focused approaches (EMDR, IFS, Sensorimotor Psychotherapy, Somatic Experiencing) are used in clinical practice with trauma-related dissociation. Their use is mostly supported by clinical experience, and controlled evidence specifically for dissociative amnesia is limited.

## Common co-occurring disorders

- **Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)**: often overlaps. Amnesia for part of the trauma is itself a PTSD symptom, so dissociative amnesia is diagnosed separately only when it is not better explained by PTSD.
- **Dissociative identity disorder (DID)**: involves recurrent gaps in everyday memory together with identity disruption, which distinguishes it from dissociative amnesia alone.
- **Complex post-traumatic stress disorder (C-PTSD, ICD-11)**: overlaps in trauma history, but amnesia is not one of its core diagnostic features.
- **Depressive disorders**.
- **Anxiety disorders**.
- **Eating disorders** and addiction.

## Cultural notes

- Complete loss of identity with travel away from home (fugue) is rare in all settings.
- In some cultures, amnesia may be attributed to spiritual or social causes.
- Awareness that some cases of "amnesia" may result from collective traumas (wars, displacement).
- Careful differentiation between dissociative memory loss and feigned amnesia is needed.

## When to seek specialized help

- Memory gaps for significant periods of time.
- Sudden travel away from home without remembering.
- Inability to recall basic personal information.
- Co-occurring symptoms: severe anxiety, depression, nightmares.
- The appearance of behaviors or skills that the person does not know how they learned.
- A history of trauma whose details the person has not been able to recall.

## Limits and risks

- Forced recovery of memories using hypnosis or drugs creates the risk of "false memories" and should be avoided.
- Direct confrontation approaches are not helpful and may be harmful.
- The therapist needs specialized training in dissociative disorders.
- Some reported amnesia may be feigned for secondary gain (such as escaping legal responsibility).
- Rushing recovery before the client is stable can be distressing and destabilizing.
