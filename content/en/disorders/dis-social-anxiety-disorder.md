# Social Anxiety Disorder

An anxiety disorder characterized by excessive and persistent fear of social situations in which the person may be exposed to the evaluation of others (whether interactional situations such as conversations or performance situations such as giving a speech). The person fears acting in an embarrassing way or appearing anxious. In children, DSM-5-TR requires that the anxiety occur in peer settings and not only in interactions with adults.

## The Clinical Ceiling

This entry, like all entries of this type, commits to describing the diagnostic category descriptively without offering any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual treatment guidance: description, not diagnosis and not treatment.

## Relation to the Corresponding Philosophical Concept

Social anxiety disorder as an official diagnostic category must not be confused with the concept of "status anxiety" (con-status-anxiety-concept), a socio-philosophical analysis of the tension arising from class comparison and the fear of losing status. The latter is a widespread human condition tied to the very structure of society, not an individual pathological category; fear of social evaluation is one thing, and meeting the criteria of a disorder that causes clinical distress is another.

## Notes on the Diagnostic Criteria

DSM-5-TR requires fear of one or more social situations in which the person may be exposed to the evaluation of others, with anxiety appearing immediately upon exposure or anticipation. The fear, avoidance or intense anxiety is out of proportion to the actual threat and persists, typically for 6 months or more. DSM-5-TR offers a **performance only** specifier, for fear restricted to speaking or performing in public (DSM-IV's "generalized" subtype was replaced by this specifier).

ICD-11 classifies social anxiety disorder under 6B04, describing marked and excessive fear or anxiety in social situations in which the person may be scrutinized by others, with fear of acting in a way that will be negatively evaluated, avoidance or intense distress, and persistence over at least several months.

## Core Symptoms

- **Fear of negative evaluation**: excessive anxiety about judgment, embarrassment or rejection.
- **Social avoidance**: avoiding gatherings, conversations and public occasions, sometimes stopping study or work.
- **Physical anxiety**: blushing, trembling, sweating, rapid heartbeat, dry mouth, confusion in speech.
- **Fear of visible signs of anxiety**: worry that others will notice the symptoms of anxiety themselves.
- **Functional distress**: an effect on relationships, study, work and opportunities for advancement.

## Gradual Clinical Management

### Stage 1: Comprehensive Assessment

- Assessing the pattern of avoidance and fear of evaluation (Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale).
- Distinguishing between social anxiety disorder and normal shyness and personality disorders (avoidant personality, dependent personality).
- Assessing performance in performance situations (public speaking, singing) versus everyday social interaction.
- Screening for comorbid disorders (depression, substance use, anxiety about speaking in front of people).

### Stage 2: Psychotherapy, the Accepted First Line

- **CBT with exposure (Exposure + Cognitive Restructuring)**: the best-supported psychological treatment.
- **Graded exposure**: from the least threatening situations (greeting a colleague) to the most severe (giving a presentation).
- **Cognitive restructuring**: challenging thoughts such as "I will be judged", "I will look foolish", "everyone will hate me".
- **Social skills training**: assertiveness, starting conversations, maintaining eye contact.
- **Training in exposure to bodily sensations**: accustoming the body to the symptoms of anxiety instead of fearing them.
- **Group therapy protocols**: especially effective in social anxiety because they provide a natural exposure environment.

### Stage 3: Pharmacological Treatment (When Needed)

- **SSRIs** (Sertraline, Paroxetine, Escitalopram; trial results for Fluoxetine are mixed): the first-line medication.
- **SNRIs** (Venlafaxine): an effective alternative; the efficacy of Duloxetine for this disorder is not established.
- **Benzodiazepines**: may be used short-term, but the risk of dependence is high.
- **Beta-Blockers** (Propranolol): sometimes used for performance anxiety (for example fear of public speaking), with supporting evidence mainly from small single-dose studies.
- **MAOIs** (Phenelzine): effective but of limited use because of side effects.

### Stage 4: Treating Resistant Cases

- Combining CBT + SSRI.
- **Intensive CBT**: condensed treatment programs.
- **Virtual reality (VR) exposure**: a modern alternative for cases where in vivo exposure is difficult.
- **Treating comorbid personality disorders** (avoidant personality).

## Associated Techniques by School and Stage

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): the Accepted First Line

- **Building the graded exposure hierarchy**: a hierarchy of 10-20 situations from the least to the most severe.
- **In vivo exposure**: confronting social situations with daily homework.
- **Cognitive restructuring**: dismantling thoughts such as "I will be judged" and "everyone will reject me".
- **Socratic questioning**: "What is the evidence that people will judge me? What is the alternative?".
- **The downward arrow technique**: uncovering core beliefs ("I am incompetent", "I am weak").
- **Challenging cognitive distortions**: identifying mind-reading and catastrophizing.
- **Assertiveness training**: learning to say "no", to express an opinion, to confront unreasonable demands.
- **Active listening skills**: developing confidence in interaction before delving into difficult situations.
- **Role-playing**: acting out difficult social situations in sessions.
- **Behavioral rehearsal**: repeating specific social skills.
- **Deep breathing**: immediate calming before and during social situations.
- **Self-compassion exercises**: confronting the voice of the inner judge.
- **Interoceptive awareness training**: accepting the symptoms of anxiety instead of fearing them.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): an Effective Alternative

- **Labeling thoughts**: "I notice the thought that I will look foolish".
- **Willingness versus willfulness**: accepting anxiety as a natural presence while continuing social action.
- **Perspective-taking**: "How would my friend see this situation?", breaking self-centeredness.

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT): for Cases with Severe Avoidance

- **Opposite action**: doing what runs counter to the urge to avoid.
- **DEAR MAN**: an effective skill for asking for things clearly and tolerating refusal.

## Common Comorbid Disorders

- **Other anxiety disorders**: GAD, panic disorder, specific phobia.
- **Depression**: common among people with social anxiety.
- **Personality disorders**: avoidant personality (the greatest overlap), dependent personality.
- **Substance use**: alcohol as a social sedative, use of other substances.
- **Obsessive-compulsive disorder**: overlap in some cases.

## Cultural and Epidemiological Context

- Global epidemiological surveys indicate an average lifetime prevalence of social anxiety disorder of roughly 4%, with considerable variation between countries: rates were lowest in low and lower-middle income countries and in the African and Eastern Mediterranean regions, and highest in high-income countries and in the Americas and Western Pacific regions (Stein et al., 2017).
- In Arab and Middle Eastern contexts, community and student studies report widely varying rates, reflecting differences in instruments and cut-offs; regional data remain limited.
- Culturally, normative values around modesty (*hayaa'*), respect for hierarchy, and family reputation can intersect with social anxiety: behaviors that might be viewed as avoidant in individualistic contexts may be perceived as appropriate reserve or deference in traditional settings. Consequently, diagnosis requires careful differentiation between socially sanctioned modesty and excessive fear that impairs functioning or causes severe personal distress.
- DSM-5-TR notes culture-related presentations such as *taijin kyofusho* (in Japan and Korea), in which the fear concerns offending or embarrassing others rather than being embarrassed oneself.
- Notions of "social face" and honor can shape how social fear is experienced and expressed.
- Virtual reality therapy is less available in low-resource contexts.

## When to Seek Specialized Help

- Persistent avoidance of social situations with an effect on daily life.
- Severe anxiety that interferes with work, study or relationships.
- Use of substances or medications as a sedative for social anxiety.
- Comorbid depression.
- Failure of prolonged self-confrontation attempts.

## Limits and Risks

- Chronic social anxiety overlaps considerably with avoidant personality disorder.
- Antidepressant treatment may take several weeks to show its full effect.
- Cases with comorbid personality disorders are harder to treat.
- Some patients prefer to rely on medication over exposure therapy, which limits long-term outcomes.
- Worry about "whether the treatment will work" may itself need addressing.
