# Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Generalized anxiety disorder is a formal diagnostic category describing a persistent pattern of excessive worry that causes distress or functional impairment. The worry is not tied to a single object, as in a specific phobia, nor to a past event, as in post-traumatic stress: it is free-floating and general, shifting from health to money to work to relationships, and it is experienced as difficult or impossible to control. The distinction from anxiety as a feeling is deliberate and central to this Atlas: a disorder is a diagnostic category with defined criteria of duration, severity, and functional impact, while a feeling is a passing human experience. This entry is a general informational description of the category, not a diagnostic tool and not a substitute for clinical assessment.

## What it is

The core of the condition is chronic, excessive worry — worry that is more frequent, more intense, and longer-lasting than the situation warrants, and that the person cannot easily set aside. The worry is accompanied by physical symptoms: restlessness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, and disturbed sleep. The person worries about worrying, and the attempt to control the worry by seeking reassurance, checking, or over-preparing usually maintains it, because each act of control confirms the belief that the worry was necessary. 

## Diagnostic features

DSM-5-TR requires excessive anxiety and worry, occurring more days than not for at least six months, about a number of events or activities, which the person finds difficult to control, together with three or more of six symptoms — restlessness, easy fatigability, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, and sleep disturbance — and requires that the anxiety cause clinically significant distress or impairment and not be attributable to a substance or another medical condition. ICD-11 (code 6B00) describes the condition in similar terms, with an emphasis on general apprehension and the associated physical symptoms. 

## History

Generalised anxiety has a long history under other names. Nineteenth-century physicians described neurasthenia, a state of nervous exhaustion that included chronic worry among its symptoms, and early psychoanalytic writing treated free-floating anxiety as a central clinical problem. The modern category was created by DSM-III in 1980, which defined generalised anxiety disorder as a residual category for anxiety that did not fit the other anxiety disorders, and it was substantially revised in DSM-III-R (1987), which made excessive, uncontrollable worry the central feature and set the six-month duration, and in DSM-IV (1994). Thomas Borkovec and colleagues, beginning with a 1983 study of the characteristics of worry and developing the account over later decades, gave the field its dominant model of worry itself: worry is a form of cognitive avoidance, an attempt to think one's way out of threat that prevents the emotional processing of the feared material and thereby maintains the anxiety. DSM-5 (2013) and DSM-5-TR (2022) retained the category with minor changes, while ICD-11 (2019) adopted it as code 6B00 and also recognised mixed anxiety and depressive disorder (6A73) as a separate category for presentations that combine symptoms of both without meeting the full threshold of either.

## Causes and mechanisms

The causes of GAD are best described as a combination of temperament, learning, and circumstance. Genetically, the condition shares a substantial heritable component with other anxiety disorders and with depression. Psychologically, the cognitive model centres on intolerance of uncertainty: the person experiences uncertainty itself as threatening, and worry is the attempt to eliminate it by anticipating every possible outcome. Borkovec's avoidance model adds the maintaining mechanism: worry is verbal and abstract, and by keeping the feared material in words rather than images, it suppresses the emotional arousal that would allow the fear to be processed and extinguished. 

## Treatment

The interventions with the broadest evidence support are cognitive behavioural therapy — in particular cognitive reappraisal and imaginal exposure to the worried-about future — and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, according to reviews by NICE and the American Psychiatric Association. Medication is effective for many patients, and the combination of medication and CBT is sometimes used for severe or chronic presentations. This is a summary of the literature, not a recommendation for any individual; treatment decisions belong to a clinician who has assessed the person.

## The philosophical distinction

Generalised anxiety disorder as a clinical condition must not be confused with existential anxiety as Kierkegaard, Heidegger, or Rollo May discussed it. Existential anxiety is a philosophical concept about the human condition — the apprehension that belongs to freedom, finitude, and the absence of a given meaning — and it is not a pathological category. A person can suffer from GAD without ever reflecting on the existential questions, and a person can be seized by existential anxiety without meeting any diagnostic criterion. The confusion of the two is exactly the methodological error this Atlas was designed to avoid, and the distinction is stated here because the two share a word and almost nothing else. 

## Key works

Borkovec and colleagues' 1983 paper is an early empirical study of worry, and Borkovec, Alcaine and Behar (2004) set out the avoidance theory of worry. Stein and Sareen's 2015 review in the *New England Journal of Medicine* is the standard clinical summary. May's *The Meaning of Anxiety* (1977) is the classic existential treatment. DSM-5-TR (2022) is the current diagnostic source.

## Epidemiological and Cultural Context

Epidemiological surveys indicate that generalized anxiety disorder has a lifetime prevalence of approximately 3.7% to 5.7% in high-income Western countries (Kessler et al., 2005; Stein & Sareen, 2015). In the Arab world and Middle Eastern populations, epidemiological studies (such as the WHO World Mental Health Surveys in Lebanon and national surveys in the Gulf) report 12-month prevalence rates between 2% and 5% in community and primary health care settings (Karam et al., 2006). Cultural presentation in Arab contexts frequently emphasizes somatic manifestations of anxiety—such as muscle tension, gastrointestinal complaints, palpitations, and chronic headaches—rather than purely psychological worry. As a result, individuals with GAD often seek care initially in general medical or primary care clinics rather than mental health settings.
