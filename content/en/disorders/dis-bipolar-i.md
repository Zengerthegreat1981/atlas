# Bipolar I Disorder

A chronic mood disorder characterized by alternating cycles of manic episodes (Mania) and depressive episodes, with the possibility of periods of normal mood between them. What distinguishes BD-I from BD-II is the presence of at least one manic episode over the course of the illness (which may or may not be accompanied by depressive episodes). Mania is not merely an elevated mood but a defined period of abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable mood with increased activity or energy, lasting at least a week and causing severe functional distress or requiring hospitalization.

## Notes on the Diagnostic Criteria

DSM-5-TR requires at least one manic episode (lasting at least a week, or any duration if hospitalization is required) for diagnosis. Mania is characterized by an abnormally and persistently elevated, irritable or expansive mood together with increased activity or energy, with 3 or more of the following symptoms (4 if the mood is only irritable): inflated self-esteem or grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, talkativeness, flight of ideas, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity, and involvement in activities with painful consequences. The episode may include psychotic features (delusions or hallucinations), and DSM-5-TR uses the specifier "with mixed features" when depressive symptoms co-occur with the manic episode. Other DSM-5-TR specifiers include with anxious distress, with rapid cycling, with melancholic or atypical features, with catatonia, with peripartum onset, and with seasonal pattern; severity is rated as mild, moderate or severe. In the lifetime prevalence figures of the World Mental Health surveys, BD-I affects about 0.6% of adults (Merikangas et al., 2011).

ICD-11 classifies bipolar type I disorder under 6A60 (bipolar type II is 6A61) and adopts a similar definition of mania. Additional coding is provided for the current episode (manic, hypomanic, depressive, mixed, or in remission) and for the presence or absence of psychotic symptoms.

## Core Symptoms

### Manic Symptoms

- **Abnormally elevated, irritable or expansive mood**.
- **Inflated self-esteem or grandiosity**: a feeling of extraordinary abilities, unrealistically ambitious projects.
- **Decreased need for sleep**: sleeping only 2-3 hours without fatigue.
- **Talkativeness**: rapid and continuous speech, difficulty interrupting the person.
- **Flight of ideas**: a feeling of a continuous stream of racing thoughts.
- **Distractibility**: jumping from one topic to another, inability to concentrate.
- **Increased goal-directed activity**: multiple projects, high energy, fast driving, reckless spending.
- **Involvement in activities with painful consequences**: excessive spending, disastrous financial decisions, risky sexual relations.

### Depressive Symptoms (in a Depressive Episode)

- Depressed mood most of the day, loss of interest, changes in sleep and appetite, exhaustion, difficulty concentrating, suicidal thoughts.

## The Relation to the Corresponding Philosophical Concept

One must not confuse the manic and depressive episodes in BD-I, as diagnostic categories, with 'sadness' (con-sadness) as a transient human feeling connected to a response to a perceived loss. Ordinary sadness is limited to a real event and does not continue for a whole week by defined criteria of severity and function, whereas a depressive episode in BD-I is an official diagnostic category, and it has no direct philosophical counterpart on the side of mania itself: the elevation of mood and the grandiosity in mania exceed any philosophical description currently available in this atlas of joy or natural ecstasy.

## Graded Clinical Treatment

### Stage 1: Assessment and Differential Diagnosis

- Confirm the criteria for mania (and not merely a "high-energy phase").
- Distinguish from ADHD, personality disorders, and substance use (cocaine and stimulants may mimic mania).
- Assess suicide risk (very high in BD-I, especially in the periods of early recovery from mania).
- Assess medication history and family history (BD-I has a strong hereditary component).

### Stage 2: Acute Treatment of a Manic Episode

- **Mood stabilizers** (Lithium, Valproate, Carbamazepine) and atypical antipsychotics.
- Lithium is a long-established first-line option, particularly in recurrent mood episodes.
- In acute mania with psychotic features: antipsychotics plus a mood stabilizer.
- Hospital admission may be necessary in acute mania.

### Stage 3: Long-Term Maintenance Treatment

- Maintenance treatment with a mood stabilizer is often long term, with periodic monitoring of blood levels where relevant.
- Combining pharmacological and psychological treatment gives the best results.
- Psychoeducation for the patient and family about early warning signs of relapse.
- Lifestyle regulation: regular sleep, avoiding substances (alcohol, drugs), reducing stress.

### Stage 4: Adjunctive Psychotherapy

- **CBT for BD**: learning to recognize early indicators of mania or depression.
- **IPSRT (Interpersonal and Social Rhythm Therapy)**: regulating the daily rhythm.
- **Family-Focused Therapy (FFT)**: family therapy that reduces relapses.

## Related Techniques by School and Stage

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), Adapted for BD

- **Pleasant activity scheduling**: caution in BD; it is not used during mania because it may trigger escalation.
- **Behavioral activation**: useful in depressive episodes (with attention to triggers of mixed mania).
- **Graded task assignment**: breaking down large tasks.
- **Behavioral experiments**: testing pessimistic thoughts in depression and grandiose thoughts in mania.
- **Cognitive restructuring**: challenging "all-or-nothing" thinking (which appears in both mania and depression).
- **Challenging cognitive distortions**: especially "mind reading," "fortune telling," and "labeling."
- **Downward arrow technique**: reaching core beliefs ("I am special," "I am a failure").
- **Decisional balance sheet**: delaying major decisions (financial, professional, personal) until after mood has stabilized.
- **Anger management techniques**: in irritable mania.
- **Gratitude practices**: in depression.
- **Self-compassion exercises**: to confront the guilt that follows mania.
- **Mindfulness meditation**: in the period between episodes.
- **Reflective journaling**: recording mood, sleep and triggers.
- **Self-monitoring**: recognizing early patterns.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), for the Experience of Fluctuating Mood

- **Urge surfing**: dealing with the urge for reckless impulsivity in hypomania.
- **Leaves on a stream**: dealing with racing thoughts in mania as passing leaves.
- **Labeling thoughts**: "I notice a grandiose thought" instead of obeying it.
- **Radical acceptance**: accepting mood fluctuation without desperate attempts to suppress it.
- **Bullseye exercise**: ordering priorities according to values, not according to the impulse of mania.

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), for Emotional Regulation

- **TIPP**: dealing with the emotional peak in mixed mania or severe depression.
- **STOP**: breaking the chain of reckless decisions.
- **Self-soothing with the five senses**: calming during the escalation of mania.
- **IMPROVE**: improving the ability to tolerate intense emotions.
- **Radical acceptance**: accepting fluctuating mood without resignation.
- **PLEASE / ABC PLEASE**: protecting emotional vulnerability by managing sleep, eating and exercise.
- **Check the facts**: objective evaluation of grandiose thoughts in mania.
- **Opposite action**: doing the opposite of the reckless impulse.
- **Labeling emotions**: precise differentiation between emotions.
- **DBT diary card**: tracking mood, sleep, medications and triggers.
- **Wise mind**: finding a balance between emotional response and wisdom.
- **Coping ahead for relapse triggers**: planning in advance how to deal with the onset of mania or depression.

## Common Comorbid Disorders

- **Anxiety disorders**: very common among people with BD-I.
- **Substance use**: especially alcohol and cocaine, as an attempt at "self-medication" of mania.
- **ADHD**: high overlap, especially in BD-I with early onset.
- **Eating disorders**: especially in females.
- **Personality disorders**: especially borderline personality (a known diagnostic overlap).
- **Medical disorders**: obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disease (partly related to lifestyle and to some long-term medications).

## Cultural Notes

- Reported rates of BD-I vary across cultures, which may reflect actual differences or differences in access to diagnosis.
- In Arab contexts, many BD-I patients are interpreted as "nerves," "possession," or "the evil eye," which delays the correct diagnosis.
- The psychotic symptoms accompanying mania may be interpreted culturally as "spiritual experiences," especially in religious contexts, which impedes treatment.
- Women are usually diagnosed with more depressive episodes than men, although BD-I is equally prevalent between the sexes.

## When to Seek Specialized Help

- The appearance of the first signs of mania (lack of sleep, reckless spending, grandiosity).
- Suicidal thoughts or behavior.
- The collapse of relationships or jobs because of mood fluctuations.
- Substance use to cope with mood.
- A family history of BD-I with the appearance of similar symptoms.
- The failure of self-help attempts (regulating sleep, avoiding substances).

## Limits and Risks

- **Very high suicide risk**: suicide risk in bipolar disorder is many times higher than in the general population. Older estimates of 15-20% lifetime mortality from suicide are now considered too high, although more recent estimates remain substantially elevated. Periods of depression and the weeks after discharge are often cited as high-risk times.
- **Repeated admissions**: BD-I often involves repeated hospital admissions.
- **Severe social and financial losses**: reckless decisions in mania can destroy lifetime savings.
- **Lithium has medical risks**: potential toxicity, requiring periodic monitoring of kidney and thyroid function.
- **Adherence is a common difficulty**: many patients stop medication during periods of stability.
- **Stigma**: BD-I still carries great stigma ("crazy," "unstable") even within health circles.
