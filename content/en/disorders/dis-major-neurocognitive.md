# Major Neurocognitive Disorder (Dementia)

A disorder characterized by a marked decline in cognitive performance in one or more cognitive domains (memory, attention, executive function, language, visual-spatial perception, social cognition) compared with the previous level, with an effect on independence in daily activities. It was historically known as "dementia". Care combines medical management of the underlying cause with psychosocial interventions that support the patient and the caregiver.

## Notes on the diagnostic criteria

DSM-5-TR requires: (1) significant cognitive decline from a previous level in one or more cognitive domains, based on (a) concern of the individual, an informed observer, or the clinician, and (b) substantial impairment documented by standardized neuropsychological testing or an equivalent clinical assessment; (2) the deficits interfere with independence in everyday activities (at a minimum, requiring assistance with complex activities such as managing finances or medications); (3) the deficits do not occur exclusively in the context of delirium; and (4) they are not better explained by another mental disorder. "Major" and "mild" neurocognitive disorder are distinguished by whether the deficits interfere with independence. The etiological subtype is specified: Alzheimer's disease, vascular disease, Lewy bodies, frontotemporal degeneration, Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, traumatic brain injury, substance/medication use, HIV infection, prion disease, another medical condition, multiple etiologies, or unspecified. Severity is rated as mild, moderate or severe.

ICD-11 classifies major neurocognitive disorder (dementia) under the block 6D80-6D8Z. It offers a similar structural classification, with a focus on the cause: 6D80 dementia due to Alzheimer disease, 6D81 vascular dementia, 6D82 dementia due to Lewy body disease, 6D83 frontotemporal dementia, 6D84 dementia due to psychoactive substances, and 6D85 dementia due to diseases classified elsewhere.

## Clinical context

**Medical management.** Treatment begins with addressing any treatable cause or contributor (such as vascular risk factors in vascular dementia), and may include licensed medications (cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil and rivastigmine, or memantine), which can provide modest symptomatic benefit in some types (especially Alzheimer's disease and dementia with Lewy bodies) without altering the underlying course. The behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) may call for additional medications with great caution (atypical antipsychotics raise the risk of stroke and death in dementia).

**When do psychological interventions come in?** Primarily with the caregiver and the environment, and with the patient in the early stages:

- **Supportive psychotherapy**: in the early stages, to help the patient cope with the diagnosis and the gradual loss of independence.
- **Psychological counseling for the family / caregiver**: essential for reducing the burden and preventing caregiver burnout and for learning to manage difficult behavior.
- **Emotion-focused coping**: with the caregiver in dealing with the loss of the patient.
- **Self-monitoring / behavioral monitoring**: tracking cognitive and behavioral changes, especially in the early stages.
- **Pleasant activity scheduling**: preserving the daily rhythm and social activity for as long as possible.

**Other non-pharmacological approaches.** Cognitive stimulation therapy (group activities designed to stimulate thinking and social engagement) has evidence of benefit in mild to moderate dementia and is recommended in NICE guidance. Evidence for individual cognitive training and rehabilitation is more modest, and classic reality orientation has been largely superseded by these approaches. Caregiver programs that combine education, skills training for managing behavioral and psychological symptoms (BPSD), and support have been shown to reduce caregiver burden and distress. Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine have limited effects on BPSD, so non-drug approaches are generally tried first.

## Related techniques (complementary, not primary)

- **Supportive psychotherapy**: coping with the diagnosis and the loss of abilities.
- **Psychological counseling for the family**: essential for caregivers, to manage difficult behavior and reduce burnout.
- **Self-monitoring**: tracking cognitive and behavioral changes, especially in the early stages (by the patient or the caregiver).
- **Pleasant activity scheduling**: preserving activity and the daily rhythm.
- **Emotion-focused coping**: dealing with the accompanying anxiety and depression (in the patient and the caregiver).

## Limits and risks

- **Licensed medications have limited effectiveness**: they may ease symptoms in certain types, but do not stop or reverse the underlying disease.
- **Antipsychotics in dementia**: linked to an increased risk of stroke and death, especially in dementia with Lewy bodies. They are used with great caution.
- **Falls and accidents**: an increased risk due to impaired spatial cognition and memory.
- **The burden on the caregiver**: very high, especially in the late stages. Many caregivers develop depression and anxiety.
- **Stigma**: dementia is still linked to "losing one's mind" in many cultures, which hinders help-seeking.
- **Specialized services**: limited in many regions, including low-resource settings; dementia units, day services, and caregiver support groups are available in few places.

## Relation to the corresponding philosophical concept

Major neurocognitive disorder, as a diagnostic category describing an objective, measurable decline in specific cognitive domains (memory, language, executive function) according to the DSM-5-TR/ICD-11 criteria, should not be confused with "memory" (con-memory) as a general cognitive-philosophical concept describing the mind's capacity for encoding, storage, and retrieval and for forming continuity of identity over time. The fundamental difference: the first is a clinical condition diagnosed by objective impairment affecting functional independence, while the second is a concept that explains a natural cognitive mechanism present in every person, healthy or affected, and carries in itself no pathological implication.
