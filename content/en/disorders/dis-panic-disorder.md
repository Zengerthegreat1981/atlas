# Panic Disorder

Panic disorder is a condition defined by recurrent, unexpected panic attacks together with persistent worry about further attacks, or a significant change in behaviour because of them. A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within minutes, accompanied by physical symptoms such as a pounding heart, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, and a fear of losing control or dying. The disorder is not the attack itself, but the pattern that grows around it: the dread of the next attack, and the shrinking of life to avoid places where escape or help might be difficult. This entry is a general informational description of a diagnostic category, not a diagnostic tool and not a substitute for clinical assessment.

## What it is

The central distinction is between a panic attack and panic disorder. A panic attack is a symptom — an episode that can occur in many conditions, or in isolation, without constituting a disorder. Panic disorder requires the attacks to be recurrent and unexpected, and requires at least one month of persistent concern about having another attack, worry about its consequences, or a significant maladaptive change in behaviour related to the attacks, such as avoiding exercise, crowds, or travel. The attacks themselves often occur without an obvious external trigger, which is the feature that most separates the condition from fear as a response to a perceived threat. 

## Diagnostic features

DSM-5-TR defines a panic attack as an abrupt surge of intense fear or discomfort reaching a peak within minutes, with four or more symptoms from a list that includes palpitations, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, choking, chest pain, nausea, dizziness, chills or heat, numbness or tingling, derealisation or depersonalisation, fear of losing control or "going crazy," and fear of dying. For the disorder, the attacks must be recurrent and unexpected, with at least a month of persistent worry or behavioural change, and the picture must not be better explained by another mental disorder, a substance, or a medical condition. ICD-11 (code 6B01) describes the same condition in similar terms. 

## History

Panic as a clinical problem was recognised long before it had a name: nineteenth-century physicians described "effort syndrome" and "soldier's heart," and early twentieth-century accounts of anxiety neurosis included attacks of the same kind. The modern category was created by Donald Klein, whose 1964 paper delineated two drug-responsive anxiety syndromes and showed that imipramine blocked panic attacks while leaving general anxiety untouched — evidence that panic was a distinct condition with its own mechanism. The category entered DSM-III in 1980 as panic disorder, was refined through DSM-IV (1994) and DSM-5 (2013), and is retained in DSM-5-TR (2022). David Clark's cognitive model, published in 1986, gave the field its dominant psychological account: panic arises when normal bodily sensations are catastrophically misinterpreted — a racing heart read as a heart attack, breathlessness read as suffocation — and the misinterpretation amplifies the arousal it fears.

## Causes and mechanisms

No single cause explains panic disorder, and the models are best read as complementary. Biologically, the condition involves a hypersensitive alarm system: the amygdala and the brainstem circuits that monitor suffocation and arousal respond to small changes in bodily state as though they signalled danger. Cognitively, Clark's model describes a vicious circle in which a sensation is catastrophically misinterpreted, the interpretation produces fear, the fear intensifies the sensation, and the intensified sensation confirms the interpretation. Behaviourally, avoidance maintains the disorder: each avoided situation prevents the person from learning that the feared catastrophe does not occur, and the relief of avoidance reinforces the avoidance. Conditioning also plays a role: places and situations present during an attack can become triggers for further attacks through association. 

## Treatment

The treatments with the strongest empirical support are cognitive behavioural therapy built around interoceptive exposure — the deliberate, graded induction of the feared bodily sensations, such as spinning to produce dizziness or breathing through a straw to produce breathlessness, so that the person learns the sensations are harmless — and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Barlow's programme, set out in *Anxiety and Its Disorders* (2002) and in the treatment manual *Mastery of Your Anxiety and Panic* (2007, with Michelle Craske), combines psychoeducation, cognitive restructuring of catastrophic interpretations, interoceptive exposure, and in vivo exposure to avoided situations. This is a summary of the literature, not a recommendation for any individual; treatment decisions belong to a clinician who has assessed the person.

## The existential connection

Panic disorder is of particular interest to the existential tradition because the attack confronts the person with death directly: the fear of dying is among the most common symptoms listed in the criteria, and the dread of the next attack is a dread of finitude experienced in the body. The existential reading does not replace the clinical one; it adds a dimension, the observation that what is feared in panic is not only a sensation but what the sensation is taken to mean about one's mortality.

## Key works

Clark's 1986 paper gave the field its cognitive model. Barlow's *Anxiety and Its Disorders* (2002) is the standard reference on the nature and treatment of anxiety and panic, and the Craske and Barlow manual (2007) is the standard treatment text. DSM-5-TR (2022) is the current diagnostic source.
