# Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by persistent, developmentally inappropriate patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that interfere with social, academic, or occupational functioning. While symptoms originate during the developmental period in childhood, longitudinal research confirms that ADHD frequently persists into adolescence and adulthood, often shifting in clinical presentation from overt motor hyperactivity to internal restlessness and executive dysfunction.

## Clinical Ceiling

This entry describes the clinical features, diagnostic taxonomy, and evidence-based management of ADHD descriptively based on DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 standards. It provides no personal diagnostic appraisal or individualized therapeutic guidance; clinical diagnosis requires comprehensive multimodal assessment by a qualified physician, psychologist, or psychiatrist.

## Conceptual Differentiation: Diagnostic Category vs. Cognitive Attention

ADHD as a diagnostic syndrome must be rigorously distinguished from the general cognitive mechanism of attention (see con-attention). While all human beings experience transient fluctuations in attentional focus, mental fatigue, or distractibility under stress, ADHD represents a chronic, genetically influenced neurodevelopmental deficit in executive control and dopaminergic/noradrenergic signaling. The clinical diagnosis requires meeting rigorous criteria regarding the number of symptoms, early age of onset, cross-situational pervasiveness, and functional impairment.

## Diagnostic Criteria: DSM-5-TR and ICD-11

Both international classification systems conceptualize ADHD as a neurodevelopmental disorder:

### DSM-5-TR (F90.x)
- **Symptom Thresholds**: Requires at least 6 of 9 symptoms of inattention and/or 6 of 9 symptoms of hyperactivity-impulsivity for children up to age 16; for individuals aged 17 and older, a minimum of 5 symptoms in either domain suffices.
- **Duration and Onset**: Symptoms must have persisted for at least 6 months and several symptoms must have been present prior to age 12.
- **Cross-Situational Impairment**: Symptoms must be evident across two or more settings (e.g., home, school, work, or social environments).
- **Presentations**:
  - *Predominantly inattentive presentation* (F90.0)
  - *Predominantly hyperactive/impulsive presentation* (F90.1)
  - *Combined presentation* (F90.2)

### ICD-11 (6A05)
ICD-11 classifies ADHD under neurodevelopmental disorders using the same three presentations: predominantly inattentive (6A05.0), predominantly hyperactive-impulsive (6A05.1), and combined (6A05.2). It emphasizes a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity that has direct negative impacts on academic, occupational, or social functioning, with onset during the developmental period (typically early to mid-childhood).

## Epidemiological Context and Regional Prevalence in the Arab World

Global epidemiological meta-analyses (Polanczyk et al., 2007, 2014) estimate the worldwide childhood prevalence of ADHD at approximately 5% to 7%, and adult prevalence at approximately 2.5%. 

In the Arab world and Middle East region, systematic reviews (e.g., Alqahtani, 2010; Farah et al., 2009; Jenabi et al., 2021) report pooled pediatric prevalence rates ranging between 4% and 9%, broadly consistent with global benchmarks. Regional studies demonstrate substantial variation attributable to differing diagnostic methodologies (e.g., teacher-completed rating scales vs. structured clinical parent-child interviews) and cultural expectations surrounding child behavior. High rates of consanguinity in some regional populations and underdiagnosis among females—who more frequently present with inattentive symptoms—remain active areas of epidemiological study.

## Neurobiology and Multimodal Clinical Management

Quantitative genetics and genome-wide association studies estimate the heritability of ADHD at approximately 70% to 80% (Faraone & Larsson, 2019), implicating polygenic pathways regulating prefrontal-striatal circuits and catecholaminergic neurotransmission.

### Evidence-Based Interventions
- **Pharmacotherapy**: Pharmacological intervention represents a premier first-line treatment for moderate to severe ADHD in school-age children, adolescents, and adults:
  - *Stimulants*: Methylphenidate and amphetamine derivatives (e.g., lisdexamfetamine) increase synaptic dopamine and norepinephrine availability, demonstrating substantial effect sizes in symptom reduction.
  - *Non-Stimulants*: Atomoxetine (a selective norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor) and alpha-2 adrenergic agonists (guanfacine, clonidine) serve as effective alternatives when stimulants are poorly tolerated, contraindicated, or where substance use comorbidity is present.
- **Psychosocial and Behavioral Interventions**:
  - *Parent Training and Classroom Interventions*: For preschool children (under age 6) and mild presentations, behavioral parent training (e.g., Triple P, Incredible Years) and behavioral classroom management are recommended as initial interventions before medication.
  - *Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Adults*: Manualized adult CBT protocols (e.g., Safren et al., 2005) effectively target residual executive dysfunction, time management, organizational skills, and emotional regulation.
  - *Executive Function Coaching*: Task decomposition, environmental scaffolding, and externalized organizational aids.

## Clinical Cautions and Differential Diagnosis

- **Controlled Substances**: Psychostimulants carry risks of misuse and diversion; careful clinical monitoring, baseline cardiovascular screening, and ongoing prescription auditing are essential.
- **Differential Diagnosis**: Clinicians must rule out or identify comorbid mood disorders, anxiety, specific learning disorders, sleep apnea, trauma-related arousal, and autism spectrum disorder.
- **Adult Underdiagnosis vs. Childhood Overdiagnosis**: While high natural activity levels in early childhood can lead to diagnostic inflation if strict impairment criteria are not applied, adults who grew up without diagnosis often struggle with severe unrecognized functional difficulties and secondary depression or substance misuse.
