# Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

A neurodevelopmental disorder that begins in childhood, characterized by a persistent pattern of inattention and/or hyperactivity and impulsivity that interferes with functioning or development. DSM-5-TR continues to distinguish three presentations: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive/impulsive, or combined.

## Clinical boundary

This item, like all items of this type, is committed to describing the diagnostic category descriptively, without offering any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual therapeutic guidance: description, not diagnosis and not treatment.

## Relation to the corresponding philosophical concept

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder as a clinical diagnostic category must not be confused with 'attention' (the item con-attention already in this atlas) as a cognitive/philosophical concept of the mechanism of directing awareness toward one stimulus rather than another. The latter is a general ability or process that every human possesses in varying degrees, whereas ADHD is a stable, functionally impairing pattern of difficulty in regulating this process, meeting specific duration, severity and impact criteria in recognized diagnostic manuals.

## Notes on the diagnostic criteria

DSM-5-TR requires: (1) a persistent display (≥6 months) of at least 6 symptoms of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity (at least 5 for adolescents aged 17 and older and adults), inconsistent with the developmental level; (2) the onset of several symptoms before age 12; (3) the symptoms appearing in at least two settings (such as home and school); (4) a clear impact on social/academic/occupational functioning; (5) the symptoms not being explained by another disorder. **Note**: since DSM-5 (2013), ADHD is classified among the neurodevelopmental disorders rather than among disorders "usually first diagnosed in infancy, childhood, or adolescence," which reflects its developmental character.

ICD-11 (code 6A05) uses the designation "attention deficit hyperactivity disorder" with the same three presentations (predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, combined) and requires a persistent pattern with onset during the developmental period and clear functional impact.

## Clinical context

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental disorder with a strong neurobiological basis (genetic factors, differences in dopamine/norepinephrine signaling, differences in the prefrontal cortex). Twin studies estimate heritability at roughly 70-80%, and symptoms persist into adulthood in a substantial proportion of those diagnosed in childhood. Worldwide prevalence in children is estimated at about 5% (Polanczyk et al., 2007). **Medication is a well-established treatment** and a first-line option for moderate to severe ADHD in school-age children and adults, whereas for preschool children and milder impairment, parent training and behavioral interventions are usually preferred first:

- **Stimulants** (methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine): effective for a majority of patients, with individual variation in response and tolerability.
- **Non-stimulants** (atomoxetine, guanfacine, clonidine): alternatives when stimulants are not tolerated or are contraindicated.

Psychological interventions are not a substitute for medication in most cases, but are an **effective complement**, especially:
- For adults (CBT for Adult ADHD) for most of the residual symptoms despite medication.
- For children who are not candidates for medication, or as additional support.
- For treating common comorbidities: anxiety, depression, learning difficulties, sleep disorders, oppositional defiant disorder.

The intervention proposed for children is often multimodal: medication + parent training + school intervention + individual therapy.

## Related techniques (limited, complementary)

The following techniques complement medication treatment, **and are not a substitute for it**, in most cases:

- **Graded Task Assignment**: dividing large tasks into small achievable steps, addressing organizational difficulties.
- **Behavioral Activation**: increasing participation in rewarding activities, especially in comorbid depression.
- **Mindful Breathing**: quick calming in moments of impulsivity.
- **One Mindfully**: from DBT skills, directly counters the fragmentation of attention.
- **Labeling Thoughts**: from ACT, helps develop distance from distracting thoughts.
- **Social Skills Training**: to treat difficulties in social interaction (impulsivity, interrupting others, waiting one's turn).

## Limits and risks

- **Medication is generally considered a central part of treatment in moderate and severe cases**; decisions about it belong to a qualified clinician. Psychotherapy alone improves some functional difficulties but has smaller effects on core symptoms.
- Stimulants are controlled substances. Over-prescribing them or misusing them (for academic or recreational purposes) poses a real danger.
- ADHD in adults is underdiagnosed: many of them were not diagnosed in childhood and suffer from comorbid disorders (anxiety, depression, addiction).
- Hyperactivity lessens in adulthood, but inattention and impulsivity often continue.
- Risks of overdiagnosis: many normal behaviors in early childhood (high activity, short attention span) are not ADHD.
