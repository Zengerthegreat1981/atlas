# Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)

A personality disorder characterized by a pervasive pattern of instability in interpersonal relationships, self-image, emotions, and behavior, with marked impulsivity. It usually begins in early adulthood and appears in multiple contexts. In DSM-5-TR it is diagnosed when five of nine criteria are met; in ICD-11 it is described as a "borderline pattern" qualifier applied to a dimensional diagnosis of personality disorder that varies in severity.

## The clinical ceiling

This entry, like all entries of this type, is committed to describing the diagnostic category descriptively without providing any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual therapeutic guidance: description, not diagnosis and not treatment.

## Relation to the corresponding philosophical concept

Borderline personality disorder as a clinical diagnostic category should not be confused with the duality of the "false self versus the true self" (con-false-self-vs-true-self) as treated by Winnicott and other existential and analytic thinkers. The latter is a philosophical/psychological concept about the structure of the self and its development in relation to the other, whereas identity disturbance in BPD is a specific clinical category defined by criteria of duration, severity, and functional impact in accepted diagnostic manuals (DSM-5-TR and ICD-11). Not every fluctuation in the sense of self, or every search for a "true" self, means borderline personality disorder.

## Notes on the diagnostic criteria

DSM-5-TR requires 5 of 9 criteria (unchanged from DSM-IV; DSM-5 also offers an alternative model for personality disorders in Section III): frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment, a pattern of unstable and intense relationships, identity disturbance, emotional instability, chronic emptiness, inappropriate anger, impulsive self-damaging behavior (in at least two areas), recurrent suicidal behavior, gestures or threats or self-mutilating behavior, and transient stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms.

ICD-11 uses a dimensional model: a single diagnosis of personality disorder (6D10) rated for severity (mild, moderate, severe), with optional trait domain qualifiers (Negative Affectivity, Detachment, Dissociality, Disinhibition, Anankastia) and a "Borderline pattern" qualifier, coded 6D11.5, which corresponds approximately to DSM-5-TR borderline personality disorder.

## Core symptoms

- **Instability in relationships**: swinging between idealization and devaluation (splitting).
- **Identity disturbance**: an unstable self-image, a feeling of chronic emptiness.
- **Emotional instability**: rapid and intense fluctuations (hours to days).
- **Impulsivity**: in multiple areas (spending, sex, addiction, driving).
- **Fear of abandonment**: an exaggerated response to any perception of abandonment.
- **Self-harm**: as a means of regulating emotions.
- **Suicidal thoughts**: repeated threats, serious attempts.

## Graduated clinical management

### Phase 1: Stabilizing the crisis (in the first weeks)

- Building a strong therapeutic relationship (the foundation of all treatments).
- Assessing suicide risk.
- Addressing dangerous impulsive behaviors (self-harm, suicide attempts).
- Crisis management (TIPP, STOP, self-soothing).

### Phase 2: Psychotherapy, the first line

- **DBT (Dialectical Behavior Therapy)**: the best-studied psychotherapy for BPD (developed by Marsha Linehan). A comprehensive program (individual therapy + skills group + phone coaching + a therapist consultation team) typically lasting about a year.
- **MBT (Mentalization-Based Treatment)**: an effective alternative, focusing on the capacity for mentalization.
- **TFP (Transference-Focused Psychotherapy)**: a psychodynamic option, typically longer in duration.
- **Schema Therapy**: for pattern-based personality disorders.
- **STEPPS**: a structured group program of about 20 weeks.

### Phase 3: Addressing comorbid disorders

- Mood and anxiety disorders.
- Eating disorders.
- Substance use disorders.
- Post-traumatic stress disorder (very common in BPD).

### Phase 4: Medication support (supplementary, of limited effectiveness)

- There is no approved medication for BPD.
- Medications to treat targeted symptoms: antidepressants, mood stabilizers, antipsychotics at low doses.

### Phase 5: Autonomy and long-term recovery

- Building a social support network.
- Finding meaning and purpose.
- Moving from "the patient" to "the person who lived through an experience and learned from it".

## Techniques linked by school and phase

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)

DBT is the best-studied treatment for BPD and includes four skill modules:

#### Mindfulness module
- **Wise Mind**: balancing the rational mind and the emotional mind.
- **Observe, Describe, Participate**: the "What" skills.
- **Non-judgmental, One-mindfully, Effectiveness**: the "How" skills.
- **Mindfulness of current emotion**: dealing with emotional fluctuations.

#### Distress Tolerance module
- **TIPP**: physiological calming in crises.
- **STOP**: breaking impulsivity.
- **ACCEPTS**: distraction during a crisis.
- **Self-soothing with the five senses**: gentle care.
- **IMPROVE**: improving the moment.
- **Radical acceptance**: accepting what cannot be changed.
- **Urge surfing**: tolerating compulsive urges.

#### Emotion Regulation module
- **Identifying and describing emotions**: a first step in dealing with them.
- **Checking the facts**: examining whether the intensity of the emotion fits.
- **Opposite action**: reversing the emotional urge.
- **Dialectical thinking**: holding contradictions.
- **ABC PLEASE**: preventing escalation.
- **Behavioral chain analysis**: understanding the triggers.
- **Self-validation**: acknowledging feelings without needing approval.

#### Interpersonal Effectiveness module
- **DEAR MAN**: asking effectively for what one needs, or saying no.
- **GIVE**: maintaining relationships.
- **FAST**: protecting self-respect.
- **THINK**: building empathy.
- **Levels of validation**: deepening the quality of validation.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT): an alternative or complement to DBT

- **Willingness versus willfulness**: distinguishing between openness and resistance.
- **Radical acceptance**: accepting difficult emotions.
- **Pain versus suffering**: distinguishing between unavoidable pain and additional suffering.
- **Leaves on a stream**: detaching from the content of thoughts.
- **Labeling thoughts**: "I notice the thought that...".
- **Noticing "who is noticing"**: distinguishing the self from the content.
- **The life compass**: values as a compass in unstable relationships.

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT): an aid for targeted symptoms

- **Socratic questioning**: challenging extreme borderline beliefs ("I am completely bad" / "I am great").
- **Cognitive restructuring**: modifying extreme interpretations.
- **Identifying core beliefs**: reaching deeper layers.
- **Assertiveness training**: expressing needs.
- **Boundary setting**: protecting oneself in relationships.
- **Self-compassion exercises**: confronting excessive self-criticism.
- **Anger management techniques**: dealing with anger outbursts.
- **Graded task assignment**: confronting social withdrawal.

## Common comorbid disorders

- **Mood disorders**: major depression is very common.
- **Anxiety disorders**: GAD, Panic, OCD.
- **Eating disorders**: especially Bulimia.
- **Substance use disorders**: common.
- **PTSD**: very common.
- **Other personality disorders**: frequently co-occur.

## Cultural notes

- Reported rates of BPD vary across cultures, which may reflect differences in presentation or in access to assessment.
- Accuracy in diagnosis requires cultural sensitivity (avoiding overdiagnosis in women).
- Family and kinship styles may protect against or aggravate symptoms.

## When to seek specialized help

- Repeated self-harming behavior.
- Suicidal thoughts or attempts.
- Severe instability in relationships.
- Destructive impulsivity (spending, driving, behaviors).
- Excessive substance use.
- Depressive symptoms with detachment from reality.

## Limits and risks

- DBT is intensive (typically about a year) and requires commitment from the patient and the treatment team.
- Premature dropout from treatment is common in BPD.
- Other treatments (TFP, MBT) often last a year or more.
- Medications have limited effectiveness.
- Relapse is common; recovery is possible but takes time.
