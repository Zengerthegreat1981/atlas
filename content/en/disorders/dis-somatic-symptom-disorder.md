# Somatic Symptom Disorder

A disorder characterized by real or perceived somatic symptoms (pain, fatigue, functional impairment) accompanied by excessive and disproportionate thoughts, feelings and behaviors related to these symptoms, usually persisting for more than 6 months.

## The clinical ceiling

This element, like all elements of this type, is committed to describing the diagnostic category descriptively without offering any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual treatment guidance: description, not diagnosis and not treatment.

## Relation to the corresponding philosophical concept

Somatic symptom disorder as an official diagnostic category must not be confused with the concept of the 'lived body' (con-lived-body, the phenomenological distinction between Leib and Körper), which describes how the body is experienced from within, independently of any pathological judgment; the latter is a philosophical/phenomenological concept about the structure of bodily experience in itself, not a diagnostic category; experiencing the body intensely or being preoccupied with it does not necessarily mean meeting the criteria of the disorder.

## Notes on the diagnostic criteria

DSM-5 (2013) introduced a radical change, carried into DSM-5-TR (2022): it replaced the categories of somatization disorder, undifferentiated somatoform disorder, pain disorder, and most cases of hypochondriasis with the unified category "Somatic Symptom Disorder." It requires one or more distressing somatic symptoms or significant disruption of daily life, together with excessive thoughts, feelings or behaviors related to the symptoms (disproportionate and persistent thoughts about their seriousness, persistently high anxiety, or excessive time and energy devoted to them), with the symptomatic state persistent, typically for more than 6 months. The essential point: the symptoms may be medically real or only perceived; what matters is the person's excessive response to them.

ICD-11 has no category named "somatic symptom disorder"; the closest equivalent is bodily distress disorder (6C20), which is defined by distressing bodily symptoms together with excessive attention to them, and which is graded as mild (6C20.0), moderate (6C20.1) or severe (6C20.2). It does not require the symptoms to be medically unexplained.

## Core symptoms

- **Somatic symptoms**: pain, fatigue, functional impairment, or other symptoms (which may or may not be accompanied by a medical condition).
- **Excessive thinking about the symptoms**: persistent worry, catastrophic thoughts, or intense absorption in the symptoms.
- **Excessive anxiety**: a level of anxiety disproportionate to the severity of the symptoms.
- **Excessive behavior**: repeated medical consultations, avoidance of activity, continual search for a diagnosis.
- **Time and energy consumed**: a great drain on daily life because of the symptoms.

## Staged clinical management

### Stage 1: Comprehensive medical and psychological assessment

- **Comprehensive medical examinations** to rule out serious organic causes.
- Assessing the nature of the somatic symptoms (actually present or perceived?).
- Assessing the anxiety associated with the symptoms.
- Assessing health beliefs (illness representations and fears about illness).
- Assessing trauma history (physical trauma, for example, may be related).

### Stage 2: Building the therapeutic relationship and normalization

- The therapeutic relationship is decisive, especially since patients may be suspicious of being referred to psychotherapy.
- Acknowledging the patient's bodily suffering without denial.
- Clarifying that the somatic symptoms are real (or perceived) and known, and that psychotherapy helps in dealing with them.
- Avoiding "it's all in your head" — this is very harmful and undermines the relationship.

### Stage 3: Education about the symptoms and the response

- Educating the patient about the relationship between stress and somatic symptoms.
- Teaching the "excessive response" model to symptoms.
- Focusing on gradual return to function instead of eliminating the symptoms.

### Stage 4: Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) — first line

- Cognitive reframing: dealing with catastrophic beliefs about the symptoms.
- Behavioral experiments: testing beliefs about activity and symptoms.
- Gradual exposure to avoided activities.
- Relaxation training and stress management.
- Active life planning (return to work, to activities).

### Stage 5: Additional techniques (ACT, mindfulness, DBT)

- ACT: accepting chronic symptoms while focusing on values.
- Mindfulness: restoring connection with the body in a non-evaluative way.
- DBT: emotion regulation skills.

### Stage 6: Pharmacological treatment (when needed)

- Antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs), especially when depression or anxiety co-occur.
- Medications for chronic pain if a medical condition is present.
- Painkillers with caution (avoiding addiction).

### Stage 7: Functional rehabilitation

- Reintegration into work and relationships.
- Developing healthy habits (sleep, nutrition, exercise).
- Building a social support network.

## Related techniques by school and stage

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) — the approved first line

- **Cognitive reframing**: with catastrophic beliefs.
- **Behavioral experiments**: testing beliefs about activity.
- **Graded tasks**: gradual return to activities.
- **Pleasant activity scheduling**: countering pathological inertia.
- **Deep breathing and progressive relaxation**: calming stress.
- **Reflective writing**: tracking symptoms and patterns.
- **Communication skills training**: expressing needs.
- **Assertiveness training**: dealing with medical and social circles.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) — for chronic symptoms

- **Leaves on a stream**: detaching from catastrophic thoughts.
- **Radical acceptance**: accepting the symptoms while focusing on values.
- **Life compass**: finding what is worth living for.
- **Body scan**: restoring connection with the body without judgment.

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) — for emotion regulation

- **Radical acceptance**: accepting the symptoms.
- **TIPP**: acute calming during episodes.
- **ABC PLEASE**: strengthening biological stability.
- **Mindfulness of the current emotion**: restoring connection with feelings.

### Other schools

- **MBSR**: mindfulness-based stress reduction, which has been studied for stress and somatic symptoms.
- **Behavioral medicine**: integration between medicine and psychotherapy.

## Common comorbid disorders

- **Depressive disorders**: with wide prevalence.
- **Anxiety disorders** and panic attacks.
- **Insomnia**.
- **Addiction**: especially to painkillers.
- **Chronic medical illnesses**: may accumulate alongside somatic symptom disorder.

## Cultural notes

- Bodily expression of distress is common across cultures, and whether symptoms are read as psychological or bodily signs is greatly affected by culture (Kirmayer & Sartorius, 2007).
- Psychological treatment may carry more stigma than treatment for bodily illness in some settings.
- Careful distinction is needed from locally meaningful explanations of illness (for example humoral or spiritual concepts), without dismissing them.
- Gender differences should be taken into account: women are more often diagnosed, but this may partly reflect diagnostic or help-seeking bias.

## When to seek specialized help

- Chronic somatic symptoms (6 months+) with excessive anxiety.
- Pathological behavior: repeated medical visits, severe avoidance.
- A major effect on work and relationships.
- Failure to relieve anxiety despite medical examinations.
- Persistent catastrophic beliefs about health.
- Excessive use of painkillers or fear of becoming addicted to them.

## Limits and risks

- Adequate medical examinations must be carried out before diagnosis to avoid overlooking a genuine illness.
- The "it's all in your head" explanation is harmful and undermines the therapeutic relationship.
- Excessive medical examinations may entrench the pathological behavior.
- Sedative medications may cause addiction.
- Do not try to convince the patient that the symptoms are "not real" — focus on his response to them.
- Misdiagnosing local medical folklore may delay treatment.

## Historical development in the DSM

The categories of somatization disorder, undifferentiated somatoform disorder, pain disorder and most cases of hypochondriasis were merged in DSM-5 into a unified diagnosis, "Somatic Symptom Disorder," shifting the diagnostic focus from the absence of a medical explanation for the symptoms to the presence of disproportionate and excessive thoughts, feelings and behaviors toward them.

This change has been debated. Critics argued that, because medically unexplained symptoms are no longer required, people with serious medical illness and understandable worry may be diagnosed too readily, and that the criteria are not specific enough; supporters held that the new approach avoids the mind-body dualism implicit in "unexplained" symptoms (see Rief & Martin, 2014).
