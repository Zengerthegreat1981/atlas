# Alcohol Use Disorder

A chronic disorder characterized by a pattern of alcohol use that causes marked clinical distress or impairment. It ranges from mild to severe, and in its unified definition it includes what used to be called "alcoholism" and "alcohol abuse." It centers on loss of control over use, intense craving (Craving), continued use despite consequences, and the appearance of withdrawal symptoms. It is distinguished from "hazardous use" (a risk pattern that does not by itself constitute a disorder) by the presence of clinically significant impairment or distress.

## The Clinical Ceiling

This entry, like all entries of the type "Disorder/Clinical Condition" in this atlas, is committed to describing the diagnostic category descriptively, namely its official criteria, its development in the classification manuals, and the documented intervention techniques concerning it, without offering any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual treatment guidance. Description, not diagnosis or treatment.

## The Relation to the Corresponding Philosophical Concept

One must not confuse alcohol use disorder as a clinical diagnostic category with con-addiction ("Addiction and the Distorted Spiritual Quest") as a psychoanalytic/philosophical concept that reads addiction as a distorted expression of a spiritual search for completeness. The first is a measurable clinical criterion (the number of DSM-5-TR criteria met, severity, course), while the second is a moral interpretation of the motive of addiction that is used neither as a diagnostic tool nor as a substitute for one.

## Notes on the Diagnostic Criteria

DSM-5 (2013) merged "alcohol dependence" and "alcohol abuse" into a single entity, "alcohol use disorder" (AUD). DSM-5-TR requires that at least two of eleven criteria be met within a 12-month period, organized in four groups: (1) impaired control (drinking larger amounts, failed attempts to cut down, craving), (2) social impairment, (3) risky use (use in physically hazardous situations, use despite known problems), (4) pharmacological criteria (tolerance, withdrawal). DSM-5 removed the earlier "legal problems" criterion and added craving. The ICD-10-CM codes are F10.10 (mild) and F10.20 (moderate or severe). Severity is determined by the number of criteria: mild (2-3), moderate (4-5), severe (6+).

ICD-11 classifies alcohol-related disorders under category 6C40 (Disorders due to use of alcohol), with distinct entities including episode of harmful use (6C40.0), harmful pattern of use (6C40.1), alcohol dependence (6C40.2), intoxication, and withdrawal. "Hazardous alcohol use" is not a disorder in ICD-11 but is listed among factors influencing health status. DSM-5-TR differs from ICD-11 in being a single entity graded by severity, whereas ICD-11 keeps the harmful-pattern and dependence categories separate; the ICD-11 codes given here (6C40.1 and 6C40.2) correspond only approximately to DSM-5-TR severity levels.

## Core Symptoms

- **Drinking larger amounts or for a longer period** than intended.
- **A persistent desire or failed attempts** to cut down on or control drinking.
- **Spending a great deal of time** obtaining alcohol, drinking it, or recovering from it.
- **Craving**: a strong desire to drink alcohol.
- **Failure to fulfill major obligations** (work, family, study) because of recurrent use.
- **Continued use despite recurrent social or personal problems**.
- **Giving up important activities** because of use.
- **Use in physically hazardous situations** (driving, operating machinery).
- **Continued use despite known physical or psychological problems**.
- **Tolerance**: the need for larger amounts for the same effect.
- **Withdrawal**: withdrawal symptoms when stopping or reducing the dose.

## Graded Clinical Treatment

### Stage 1: Assessment and Intervention in Withdrawal

- Assess the severity of use (screening instruments such as the AUDIT or the CAGE questions) and the risk of withdrawal (CIWA-Ar).
- Medical monitoring of withdrawal: it can be life-threatening (seizures, delirium tremens, DTs).
- Benzodiazepines are the standard medical treatment for moderate to severe withdrawal and are given only under medical supervision.
- Electrolyte correction, nutrition, and thiamine supplementation (to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy).
- Assess comorbid disorders (depression, anxiety, PTSD, personality disorders).

### Stage 2: Motivating the Patient to Change

- Motivational interviewing (MI) is especially effective at this stage.
- Strengthening self-motivation, exploring discrepancies between values and behavior.
- Avoiding stigma, respecting the patient's pace.

### Stage 3: Psychotherapy: First Line After Stabilization

**Cognitive behavioral therapy for alcohol use disorder** has an established evidence base:

- Building alcohol refusal skills.
- Managing triggers and cue associations.
- Planning alternative activities.
- Techniques for tolerating craving.

The **12-step program (AA)** and 12-step facilitation are among the most widely researched group interventions. They are not for everyone, but they are an available and tested option.

**Motivational enhancement therapy (MET)** and **modified relapse prevention (RP)** are effective complementary elements.

**Contingency management** and **couple/family therapy** add value in specific contexts.

### Stage 4: Pharmacological Treatment

Medication decisions belong to a qualified clinician. Medications used in AUD include:

- **Naltrexone**: an opioid antagonist that reduces heavy drinking and craving.
- **Acamprosate**: used to support abstinence after withdrawal.
- **Disulfiram**: produces an unpleasant reaction when alcohol is consumed; used with supervision in adherent patients.
- **Long-acting injectable naltrexone**: a monthly formulation that can improve adherence.
- **Baclofen**: evidence is mixed, and it is not approved for AUD in many jurisdictions.

### Stage 5: Treatment of Comorbid Disorders

- Treating PTSD, depression and anxiety concurrently with addiction treatment.
- An "integrated treatment" model is better than sequential treatment.
- Monitoring medications such as benzodiazepines for the potential of misuse.

## Related Techniques by School and Stage

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), for Relapse Prevention

- **Decisional balance sheet**: an immediate weighing of the consequences of drinking against the consequences of abstinence.
- **Downward arrow technique**: revealing core beliefs ("I don't deserve a good life," "without alcohol I can't bear reality").
- **Cognitive restructuring**: challenging thoughts such as "one reward won't hurt," "I am an addict, there is no use trying."
- **Behavioral experiments**: testing the expectation "I will become anxious without alcohol" through an actual experiment (in a therapeutic context).
- **Coping statements**: preparing ready-made sentences to recall at moments of craving ("this is a craving that will subside, I was not always like this").
- **Distress tolerance**: alternatives to drinking at moments of emotion.
- **Graded task assignment**: rebuilding a pleasant activity emptied of alcohol.
- **Self-compassion**: confronting "relapse = failure = collapse" with compassion.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), an Effective Alternative

- **Labeling thoughts / leaves on a stream**: detaching from thoughts such as "I need a drink," "I won't be able to."
- **Values clarification**: linking abstinence to vital values (relationships, health, freedom).
- **Urge surfing**: tolerating the urge to drink as a passing wave without responding.
- **Radical acceptance**: accepting that relapse is a possible part of the journey, without interpreting it as an identity catastrophe.

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), for Regulating Emotions and Impulsivity

- **TIPP**: reducing the impulse to drink at the emotional peak.
- **Urge surfing**: tolerating craving.
- **STOP / ACCEPTS**: managing moments of crisis.
- **Pros and cons**: a conscious decision at the moment of temptation.
- **Opposite action**: calling a friend, going to a meeting, practicing a hobby, when there is a desire for isolation and drinking.
- **Coping ahead (Cope Ahead)**: preparing a plan for high-risk situations before they occur.

### Additional Interventions

- **Motivational interviewing (MI)**: in the early stages, to raise motivation without confrontation.
- **Self-help groups (AA)**: 12 steps, SMART Recovery, or cultural alternatives.
- **Family and couple therapy**: especially when relationships are affected.

## Common Comorbid Disorders

- **Other substance use disorders** (tobacco, cannabis, sedatives): very common.
- **Depression**: common, and often co-occurring with AUD.
- **Anxiety disorders**: especially GAD, social phobia, PTSD.
- **Personality disorders**: especially borderline, avoidant and antisocial.
- **Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)**: a common episode.
- **Sleep disorders**.
- **Eating disorders**.
- **Suicidal thoughts and suicide attempts**: the risk is much higher in AUD.

## Cultural Notes

- The "alcoholic" in the popular imagination often overlooks hazardous use in social and occupational contexts.
- In cultural contexts that prohibit alcohol (such as some Arab and Islamic contexts), stigma is harsher, use is secret, and it is hard to seek help.
- Moderate "social drinking" that turns into excess is diagnosed years late.
- The cultural normalization of alcohol use as a tool for celebration or coping impedes awareness of the problem.
- In Arab contexts, the shift from "celebratory use" to "daily coping use" is an early warning sign.

## When to Seek Specialized Help

- Repeated failed attempts to stop or cut down.
- A strong, irresistible desire to drink.
- Withdrawal symptoms (tremor, anxiety, insomnia, sweating, hallucinations).
- Continued drinking despite health, social or legal problems.
- Drinking in the morning to relieve withdrawal.
- Suicidal thoughts or self-harm.
- Accidents or injuries connected to drinking.
- Concern from family or partner about the drinking pattern.

## Limits and Risks

- **Acute withdrawal**: can be life-threatening (DTs, seizures). It requires medical supervision.
- **Wernicke-Korsakoff encephalopathy**: brain damage caused by thiamine deficiency. It can often be prevented with thiamine.
- **Relapse is common**: AUD is often a chronic disorder; relapse is part of the journey, not a failure within it.
- **Stigma**: the greatest barrier to seeking help, especially in conservative contexts.
- **Compulsory treatment**: in some legal contexts, it may be necessary.
- **Pharmacological treatment needs supervision**: interactions, side effects, and adherence.
- **Self-treatment**: many patients try to "stop on their own" before seeking help, with the risks of withdrawal.
