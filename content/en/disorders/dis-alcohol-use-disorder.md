# Alcohol Use Disorder

Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD) is a chronic, relapsing neurobehavioral disorder characterized by a problematic pattern of alcohol consumption leading to clinically significant distress or impairment. It is defined by impaired control over drinking, escalating physiological tolerance, craving, continued use despite adverse physical, psychological, and interpersonal consequences, and the onset of physical withdrawal symptoms upon cessation. In contemporary nosology, AUD represents a unified diagnostic continuum encompassing conditions previously termed alcohol abuse and alcohol dependence.

## Clinical Ceiling

This entry outlines the diagnostic criteria, neurobiological mechanisms, and evidence-based interventions for Alcohol Use Disorder descriptively based on DSM-5-TR and ICD-11. It does not provide medical advice or individualized treatment plans; severe alcohol withdrawal can be life-threatening and requires immediate professional medical management.

## Conceptual Differentiation: Clinical Disorder vs. Philosophical Concepts of Addiction

Alcohol Use Disorder as an operational medical diagnosis must be differentiated from philosophical and psychoanalytic interpretations of addiction (such as formulations viewing addiction as an existential or spiritual void; see con-addiction). Clinical AUD is diagnosed strictly through standardized behavioral, physiological, and functional impairment criteria established in psychiatric taxonomies.

## Diagnostic Classifications: DSM-5-TR vs. ICD-11

### DSM-5-TR (F10.10 / F10.20)
DSM-5-TR integrates alcohol abuse and dependence into a single, unified disorder graded by severity across 11 diagnostic criteria occurring within a 12-month period:
1. Drinking larger amounts or for longer than intended.
2. Persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down.
3. Excessive time spent obtaining, using, or recovering from alcohol.
4. Craving (intense urge to drink).
5. Recurrent use resulting in failure to fulfill major role obligations.
6. Continued use despite persistent social or interpersonal problems.
7. Giving up important social, occupational, or recreational activities.
8. Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations (e.g., driving).
9. Continued use despite knowledge of physical or psychological problems caused or exacerbated by alcohol.
10. Tolerance (need for increased amounts to achieve intoxication, or diminished effect with continued use of the same amount).
11. Withdrawal (characteristic withdrawal syndrome, or drinking to avoid withdrawal).

**Severity Grading**:
- *Mild* (F10.10): 2–3 criteria present.
- *Moderate* (F10.20): 4–5 criteria present.
- *Severe* (F10.20): 6 or more criteria present.

### ICD-11 (6C40)
ICD-11 maintains distinct categorical distinctions under *Disorders due to use of alcohol*:
- **Harmful Pattern of Use of Alcohol (6C40.1)**: A pattern of drinking that causes clinically significant harm to physical or mental health, or behavior leading to harm to others, without meeting dependence criteria.
- **Alcohol Dependence (6C40.2)**: A neuroadaptation characterized by impaired control over alcohol use, priority of alcohol over other activities, and physiological features (tolerance, withdrawal, or use to prevent withdrawal).

## Global Epidemiology and Cross-Cultural Disparities

According to the World Health Organization's global status reports on alcohol and health (WHO, 2018, 2024), alcohol use contributes to more than 2.6 million deaths annually and accounts for a substantial proportion of the global burden of disease:
- **Global Prevalence**: Worldwide, alcohol use disorders affect an estimated 100 to 110 million people (approximately 2.6% to 5.1% of the global adult population), with higher rates among men than women (roughly 2:1 to 3:1).
- **Geographic Disparities**: Prevalence is highest in the WHO European Region and the Region of the Americas, where per capita alcohol consumption is elevated.
- **Arab World and Conservative Cultural Contexts**: In the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR), per capita alcohol consumption is the lowest globally due to religious, cultural, and legal prohibitions. However, individuals who develop AUD in these settings encounter severe social stigma, legal jeopardy, and profound barriers to seeking care. Clinical presentations in conservative environments are often marked by covert use, high risks from adulterated or toxic illicit alcohol (such as methanol toxicity), and late presentation with advanced cirrhosis, Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome, or severe delirium tremens.

## Multimodal Clinical Management

### Phase 1: Medically Supervised Detoxification and Withdrawal Management
- **Clinical Assessment**: Stratification of withdrawal severity using validated instruments such as the Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, Revised (CIWA-Ar).
- **Medical Stabilization**: Acute alcohol withdrawal can produce life-threatening complications, including generalized tonic-clonic seizures and delirium tremens (DTs). Medically monitored benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam, lorazepam, or chlordiazepoxide) represent the standard protocol for preventing withdrawal complications.
- **Thiamine Prophylaxis**: High-dose parenteral thiamine is administered before any carbohydrate intake to prevent the development of Wernicke's encephalopathy and Korsakoff syndrome.

### Phase 2: Evidence-Based Pharmacotherapy
Medication for AUD is prescribed by qualified physicians to support abstinence or reduce heavy drinking:
- **Naltrexone**: An opioid receptor antagonist that attenuates the rewarding, euphoric effects of alcohol and diminishes cravings (available in daily oral and monthly extended-release injectable formulations).
- **Acamprosate**: Modulates NMDA and GABAergic neurotransmission, reducing protracted withdrawal symptoms and helping maintain abstinence.
- **Disulfiram**: An aldehyde dehydrogenase inhibitor that produces unpleasant physiological reactions (flushing, nausea, tachycardia) if alcohol is consumed; utilized primarily with motivated, supervised patients.

### Phase 3: Psychosocial and Behavioral Interventions
- **Motivational Interviewing (MI)**: Explores ambivalence, resolves resistance, and strengthens intrinsic motivation for behavioral change.
- **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) and Relapse Prevention**: Identifies internal and external drinking triggers, teaches cognitive restructuring, and develops coping and refusal skills (Marlatt & Gordon).
- **Mutual-Help Peer Groups**: Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) and Twelve-Step Facilitation (TSF), as well as secular alternatives (SMART Recovery), offer structured long-term peer recovery support.
