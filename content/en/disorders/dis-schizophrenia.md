# Schizophrenia

A chronic disorder within the psychosis spectrum characterized by fundamental distortions in perception (hallucinations), mental content (delusions), thought organization (formal thought disorder), motivation and emotional expression (negative symptoms), and psychosocial function. Antipsychotic medication (typical and atypical) is a cornerstone of treatment, and psychological and psychosocial interventions are **complementary** and not a substitute.

## The clinical ceiling

This entry, like all elements of the type 'disorder / clinical condition' in this Atlas, is committed to describing the diagnostic category descriptively as it appears in DSM-5-TR and ICD-11, without offering any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual treatment guidance: description, not diagnosis and not treatment.

## Relation to the corresponding philosophical concept

'Schizophrenia' as a precise clinical diagnostic category (positive and negative symptoms specified in time) must not be confused with 'schizoanalysis' (con-schizoanalysis-desiring-machines) as put forward by Deleuze and Guattari in *Anti-Oedipus* (1972); the latter is a philosophical-political metaphor for criticizing the capitalist organization of desire, not a clinical description of a disorder, and it proposes no therapeutic alternative to antipsychotics.

## Notes on the diagnostic criteria

DSM-5-TR requires two or more of five symptoms, each present for a significant portion of a one-month period (or less if successfully treated), with at least one being (1) delusions, (2) hallucinations, or (3) disorganized speech; the others are (4) grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior and (5) negative symptoms (diminished emotional expression or avolition). There must be marked functional decline, and continuous signs of the disturbance (including prodromal or residual periods) must persist for at least six months. Schizoaffective and mood disorders with psychotic features, and substance or medical causes, must be excluded. DSM-5 removed the classical subtypes (paranoid, disorganized, catatonic and others); course specifiers (first episode, multiple episodes, continuous) are used, and symptom severity is rated on dimensional scales (Clinician-Rated Dimensions of Psychosis Symptom Severity, in Section III).

ICD-11 classifies schizophrenia under 6A20 and uses a similar symptom description (positive, negative, depressive, manic, psychomotor and cognitive symptom qualifiers) with a duration of at least one month, and it likewise dropped the classical subtypes. Course is coded as first episode (6A20.0), multiple episodes (6A20.1), or continuous (6A20.2), with specification of the current symptom severity and of partial or full remission.

## Clinical context

**Role of medication.** Schizophrenia is widely understood as a neurodevelopmental disorder with strong genetic and biological contributions. Antipsychotic medications (including atypical agents such as risperidone, olanzapine and aripiprazole) reduce positive symptoms and lower the risk of relapse, and by stabilizing symptoms they allow psychological interventions to work. Prescribing decisions belong to a qualified clinician.

**When does psychotherapy come in?** After acute symptoms have stabilized on medication, complementary psychological therapies can be introduced, especially:

- **CBTp (CBT for psychosis)**: to modify beliefs about hallucinations and delusions (not to deny them), and to reduce the distress associated with them.
- **Family Psychoeducation (family counseling)**: reducing relapse by educating the family about the illness, improving communication, and reducing high Expressed Emotion in the home.
- **Social Skills Training**: for negative symptoms and functional deterioration.
- **Supported Employment**: for return to work.

**Limits of psychological interventions on their own.** They are generally insufficient during acute psychosis or when medication is not being taken.

## Related techniques (complementary, not primary)

- **CBTp (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Psychosis)**: a psychological approach with good evidential support and recommended in NICE guidance, working on delusional beliefs (Delusional beliefs) and voice experiences in a collaborative, non-confrontational style.
- **Family psychoeducation**: reducing relapse by educating the family about early warning signs.
- **Supportive psychotherapy**: building a stable therapeutic relationship as a base for any intervention.
- **Self-monitoring**: tracking early warning signs of relapse (sleep pattern, stress, initial symptoms).
- **Cognitive reframing**: in its specialized form for schizophrenia experiences (normalizing hallucination without believing its content).
- **Metacognitive training (MCT)**: a group-based program that targets cognitive biases (such as jumping to conclusions) associated with psychosis.

## Limits and risks

- **Psychological intervention is not a substitute for medication** in schizophrenia. CBTp and others mainly address residual symptoms, distress and quality of life.
- **Suicide risk is high**: lifetime estimates are around 5%, with the risk concentrated in the early years after diagnosis and during depressive episodes (Palmer et al., 2005).
- **Structural stigma**: schizophrenia is among the most stigmatized mental disorders, which delays seeking treatment and reduces adherence.
- **Side effects of antipsychotics**: metabolic syndrome, tardive dyskinesia, weight gain. They call for regular medical monitoring.
- **Non-adherence is common**: many patients stop medication, and psychological interventions alone do not solve this problem.
