# Prolonged Grief Disorder

Prolonged grief disorder (PGD) is a relatively new diagnostic category describing a grief response that remains intense and functionally impairing well beyond what is expected culturally after the death of a person close to the bereaved. The core of the picture is persistent, pervasive yearning for the deceased, or preoccupation with thoughts and memories of them, accompanied by intense emotional pain and by difficulty re-engaging with life. The category is recent — it entered DSM-5-TR in March 2022 and is included in ICD-11 — and it is partly contested in the literature, because it risks classifying a long but natural grief as a pathological condition. This entry is a general informational description of the category, not a diagnostic tool.

## What it is

Grief is a normal human response to loss, and its duration varies widely between people and cultures. Prolonged grief disorder is defined not by the presence of grief but by its persistence, intensity, and functional cost beyond the period in which grief is culturally expected. The grief must exceed what the person's social, cultural, and religious norms would anticipate, must cause clinically significant distress or impairment, and must not be better explained by another mental disorder or medical condition. 

## Diagnostic features

The two manuals differ in detail. DSM-5-TR requires the death of a person close to the bereaved at least 12 months ago (6 months for children and adolescents), with intense yearning or preoccupation present nearly every day for at least the last month, plus at least three of eight further symptoms: identity disruption, marked disbelief about the death, avoidance of reminders, intense emotional pain, difficulty reintegrating into relationships and activities, emotional numbness, a feeling that life is meaningless, and intense loneliness. ICD-11 (code 6B42) requires a grief response that has lasted an atypically long time — at least six months at a minimum — with longing or preoccupation accompanied by intense emotional pain, and it uses a typological rather than algorithmic approach, without a fixed count of accessory symptoms. Both manuals require the clinician to take the cultural and religious norms of mourning into account before judging that a grief response exceeds them.

## History

The category has a long prehistory as "complicated grief." Holly Prigerson and colleagues proposed diagnostic criteria for prolonged grief disorder in *PLoS Medicine* in 2009, arguing that a distinct syndrome could be separated from depression and normal bereavement on the basis of yearning and its correlates. ICD-11 then included prolonged grief disorder as a new diagnosis, and the American Psychiatric Association approved its inclusion in Section II of DSM-5-TR (2022), superseding the earlier provisional category of persistent complex bereavement disorder, which DSM-5 had placed in Section III as a condition for further study. 

## Causes and mechanisms

The mechanisms proposed for prolonged grief centre on the failure of the bereaved person to integrate the reality of the loss. Attachment theory supplies the first strand: the yearning and preoccupation are the attachment system continuing to seek a person who is gone, and the avoidance of reminders prevents the updating of the internal model of the relationship. The dual process model of Stroebe and Schut (1999) supplies a second: healthy grieving oscillates between loss-oriented coping (feeling the grief, remembering) and restoration-oriented coping (rebuilding a life, taking on new roles), and prolonged grief is marked by a failure of that oscillation — the person is stuck in loss-orientation, or avoids it entirely. Neurobiological work has pointed to reward circuitry: yearning for the deceased activates reward-related regions, suggesting that the attachment bond continues to be rewarding in a way that maintains the grief. Risk factors identified in the literature include the suddenness or violence of the death, a close or dependent relationship with the deceased, prior depression or anxiety, and a lack of social support.

## Treatment

The treatment with the strongest evidence is complicated grief treatment, a structured therapy developed by Katherine Shear and colleagues, which combines elements of exposure — revisiting the story of the death and the avoided reminders — with work on restoring a life and goals. Shear and colleagues' randomised trial, published in *JAMA* in 2005, found that complicated grief treatment produced substantially better outcomes than interpersonal psychotherapy in patients with complicated grief. 

## The controversy

The controversy over the category should be recorded plainly, since it is part of the subject. Critics argue that setting a time limit on grief — twelve months in DSM-5-TR, six in ICD-11 — medicalises a human experience whose normal duration varies enormously across cultures and religions, and risks turning ordinary mourning into a diagnosis with a prescription attached. Defenders reply that the criteria do not pathologise grief as such, but identify a minority whose grief is genuinely impairing and who benefit from a specific treatment that generic support does not provide, and that the cultural-exceedance clause in both manuals is a real safeguard rather than a formality. The debate is a live one in the literature.

## Key works

Prigerson and colleagues' 2009 proposal is the founding statement of the criteria. The 2021 *World Psychiatry* validation is the key source for the DSM-5-TR criteria and the PG-13-R. Stroebe and Schut's 1999 dual process model is the standard theoretical account of grieving. Shear and colleagues' 2005 trial is the key treatment study.
