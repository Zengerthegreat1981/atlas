# Autism Spectrum Disorder

A neurodevelopmental disorder centered on differences in social communication and interaction, with restricted and repetitive patterns of behavior and interests, beginning in the early developmental period (symptoms must be present in the early developmental period). DSM-5-TR describes autism spectrum disorder under "neurodevelopmental disorders," and reflects the principle of the spectrum, that is, variation in severity and needs from one person to another.

## The clinical ceiling

This entry, like all entries of the 'disorder/clinical condition' type in this atlas, is committed to describing the diagnostic category descriptively, without offering any direct diagnostic assessment to the reader or individual therapeutic guidance: description, not diagnosis or treatment.

## Relation to the corresponding philosophical concept

Autism spectrum disorder as a medical diagnostic category (with criteria of impairment and severity as set by DSM-5-TR/ICD-11) must not be confused with the social model of disability (con-social-model-disability) as a philosophical framework: the latter locates disability in environmental/social barriers rather than in individual deficit, whereas the clinical classification here is a diagnostic standard aimed at determining the need for support and intervention, not a philosophical position on the nature of disability or neurodifference.

## Notes on the diagnostic criteria

DSM-5-TR requires two main criteria: (1) **persistent deficits in social communication and social interaction** across multiple contexts (such as deficits in social-emotional reciprocity, deficits in nonverbal communicative behaviors, difficulty in developing and maintaining relationships); (2) **restricted/repetitive patterns of behavior, interests or activities** (such as stereotyped motor movements, insistence on routine, highly restricted interests, hyper- or hypo-reactivity to sensory input). Severity varies across three levels (1: requiring support, 2: requiring substantial support, 3: requiring very substantial support).

ICD-11 (6A02) uses the label "autism spectrum disorder" with a similar two-domain description (social communication, and restricted/repetitive behavior), and instead of severity levels adds specifiers for the presence or absence of disorder of intellectual development and for the degree of functional language impairment.

## The clinical context

Autism spectrum disorder is a neurodevelopmental disorder; **no medications treat the core of the spectrum**. Pharmacological treatment (such as atypical antipsychotics for irritability, SSRIs for co-occurring anxiety/depression) targets only co-occurring symptoms.

The core evidence-based interventions:
- **Early structured education** (Early Intensive Behavioral Intervention, EIBI, built on ABA principles): the most evidence-supported approach for preschool-age children.
- **Social Skills Training**: especially at the milder levels and in older ages.
- **Speech and language therapy**: to treat co-occurring language delay/disorder.
- **Occupational therapy (OT)**: to treat sensory processing difficulties and fine motor skills.
- **Family therapy and parent support**: a basic pillar of long-term success.

Psychological interventions (CBT, ACT, DBT) come in as support for co-occurring anxiety/depression/irritability, **not as treatment for autism spectrum disorder itself**.

## Related techniques (limited, supportive)

The following techniques are **supportive and not stand-alone treatments** for autism spectrum disorder:

- **Social Skills Training**: teaching interaction skills (eye contact, understanding social cues, starting/ending conversations) using video modeling, role play, and teaching in small groups.
- **Behavioral Rehearsal**: repeating miniature social situations to acquire flexible alternative behaviors.
- **Mindful Breathing**: adapted for those who are anxious or facing sensory loads, as a simple calming strategy.
- **Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA)**: mentioned here as a reference concept and not a traditional psychological intervention (it has its conceptual reference in con-aba-autism).

## Limits and risks

- **No CBT/ACT/DBT as a stand-alone treatment** for autism spectrum disorder; this is a common fallacy. Cognitive intervention alone does not treat social communication deficits or sensory patterns.
- Early identification and intervention are generally associated with better developmental outcomes, though responses vary widely between individuals.
- Attention should be paid to co-occurring conditions: anxiety (very common; roughly 40% of children and youth with ASD meet criteria for an anxiety disorder in meta-analytic estimates, van Steensel et al., 2011), depression, ADHD, sleep difficulties, feeding difficulties.
- Pseudo-treatments (vitamin therapy, chelation therapies, MMS) are harmful and ineffective.
- The diversity of the spectrum means that "one prescription" does not suit everyone. The plan must be individual.
- Autistic people must be protected from the stigma of a "hopeless disability"; many of them live independent and productive lives with appropriate support.

## Historical development in the DSM

DSM-IV listed several separate pervasive developmental disorders, including autistic disorder, Asperger's disorder, childhood disintegrative disorder (Heller's syndrome), and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified (PDD-NOS). DSM-5 (2013) merged these into a single category, autism spectrum disorder, with specification of the level of support required.
