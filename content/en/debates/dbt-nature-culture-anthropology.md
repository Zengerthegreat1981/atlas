# Nature vs. Culture

## Description of the Debate

The nature/culture dichotomy is one of the classificatory pillars on which modern Western thought rests: nature as the domain of necessity, instinct, and physical laws, and culture as the domain of meaning, intention, and human diversity. **Claude Lévi-Strauss** made this dichotomy central to his structuralism. He saw in the incest prohibition (*prohibition de l'inceste*) the point of transition from nature to culture: a universal rule (which is why it appears natural), yet at the same time a social rule whose content varies (which is why it is cultural). He described this as a logical "scandal" that exposes the falsity of a sharp separation between the two fields (*Les Structures élémentaires de la parenté*, 1949).

## The First Position: The Dichotomy as an Analytical Tool

Structuralist and pre-structuralist anthropologists held that the distinction between nature and culture, although Western in origin, is a valid analytical tool for studying all societies. In their view, every human society draws boundaries between what it regards as "given" and what it regards as "made," even if the location of this line differs from one culture to another. Lévi-Strauss's structural anthropology used this assumption to build cross-cultural comparisons of kinship, myth, and cooking (opposing the "raw" to the "cooked" in *The Raw and the Cooked*, French original 1964).

## The Second Position: Critiquing the Universality of the Dichotomy

Since the 1990s, anthropologists such as **Philippe Descola** have launched a radical critique of this dichotomy; Descola's synthesis is his book *Par-delà nature et culture* (2005; English translation 2013 as *Beyond Nature and Culture*). In his view, the dichotomy is not a universal truth but a specific historical product of the modern Western ontology he calls "naturalism," which assumes continuity of physicality (all beings share the same material nature) and discontinuity of interiority (only humans have minds and culture). Descola compared this pattern with three other ontological modes he identified among different peoples: totemism, animism (in which spirit and intentionality are attributed to animals and plants), and analogism.

In the same direction, **Eduardo Viveiros de Castro** developed the concept of "Amerindian perspectivism," the idea that several Amazonian peoples conceive of animals as subjects who have a human perspective from within. Thus the European nature/culture dichotomy turns into a completely different system that does not rest on "one nature and many cultures," but on the reverse: "one culture (shared intentionality) and many natures" (multinaturalism). He showed this in his landmark article "Cosmological Deixis and Amerindian Perspectivism" (*Journal of the Royal Anthropological Institute*, 1998).

## Current Situation

Today the field, under the label of the "Ontological Turn" and posthumanist anthropology, tends to treat the nature/culture dichotomy as an object of ethnographic study rather than a neutral framework for it. The question is no longer "how is the relationship between nature and culture organized in a given society," but "does this dichotomy itself exist in that society's conceptions at all." The works of **Bruno Latour** also contributed to this turn, in his critique of the "modern constitution" that separates the world of things from the world of humans (*Nous n'avons jamais été modernes*, 1991), as did studies in multispecies anthropology, which trace networks of relations between humans and other beings without assuming a fundamental rupture between them. The debate remains open as to whether generalizing concepts such as "perspectivism" falls in turn into the trap of generalizing a local (Amazonian) framework to the whole world.
