# Thick Description vs. Phenomenological Description: Geertz and Eliade Compared

An apparent methodological convergence between two major approaches to studying religion, both contemporary: the interpretive "thick description" of Clifford Geertz and the "phenomenological description" of Mircea Eliade. Beneath it lies a fundamental disagreement about the nature of what is described.

## Apparent Convergence: Rejecting Causal Reduction

Both approaches share a rejection of explaining religion and symbolic action by reducing them to a purely external cause, such as a social function (as in Durkheim) or a psychological-defensive mechanism (as in Freud). Both require the researcher to stay close to the phenomenon as it is lived or practiced, rather than leaping directly to a predetermined causal explanation.

## The Essential Difference: Does Religion Have an "Essence"?

Eliade assumes there is a **real religious structure or essence** (the sacred, *sui generis*) that can be uncovered by comparing phenomena from multiple cultures. For him, religious experience is a reality that can manifest (hierophany) in recurring patterns throughout history. Geertz, however, does not assume a transcendent essence of this kind. For him, religion is a **system of symbols** produced by a specific cultural context and read within that context alone. He does not assume a religious truth "outside" the symbolic system itself, waiting to be discovered through comprehensive comparison. In other words, Eliade is "phenomenological" in a philosophical sense (he presupposes something that appears), whereas Geertz is "interpretive" in a semiotic sense (he reads a cultural text without presupposing a transcendent referent for it).

## Impact on the Comparative Method

This difference directly affects each one's position on comparison across cultures. Eliade practices comprehensive comparison in search of the common pattern, which Smith critiqued in `dbt-comparativism-vs-contextualism-eliade-smith`. Geertz tends toward local ethnographic depth. His limited comparative study of Islam in Morocco and Indonesia in *Islam Observed* compares two carefully defined contexts, not a universal abstract pattern. This makes Geertz's position methodologically closer to Smith's contextual caution than to Eliade's comprehensiveness, even though both Geertz and Eliade reject causal reductionism.

## An Application to the Study of Islam: Geertz in Morocco and Indonesia

Geertz's book *Islam Observed: Religious Development in Morocco and Indonesia* (1968) provides a practical example of his method. Rather than searching for a single "essence" of Islam that manifests in recurring patterns (as Eliade does with the sacred), Geertz describes how Islamic piety in Morocco took shape around the charismatic model of the active saint (*wali*), with Sidi Lahsen Lyusi as an example. In Java, Indonesia, by contrast, it took shape around an inward, contemplative piety, blended with an earlier Hindu-Buddhist and Javanese mystical heritage. For Geertz, then, Islam is read through two distinct cultural versions, not through a single fixed essence manifesting in multiple forms, as the Eliadean method presupposes.

## Other Voices on Each Side

The two labels cover varied positions. Among phenomenologists, **Rudolf Otto** (*The Idea of the Holy*, 1917) centred description on the experience of the "numinous", at once overwhelming and attracting, and held that it cannot be reduced to rational or moral concepts. **Joachim Wach** joined interpretive understanding to the study of religious experience and its expression in thought, action and community, giving social forms more systematic attention than Eliade did. **Ninian Smart** moved furthest from essentialism: he advocated withholding judgement about the truth of what is described, entering the believer's view through informed empathy, and comparing traditions across several dimensions, such as ritual, myth, doctrine and institution, without requiring a single sacred essence.

Among interpretive anthropologists, **Victor Turner** read symbols as carrying many meanings and studied them in ritual process and social conflict, combining participants' explanations with observed use. **Mary Douglas** (*Purity and Danger*, 1966; *Natural Symbols*, 1970) linked classifications of purity and bodily symbolism to patterns of social organization, and was readier than Geertz to propose cross-cultural comparative schemes. The contrast is therefore less a two-way split than a range of answers to two questions: what lies behind the symbols, and how far description may travel across cultures.

## A Third Party: Talal Asad

Talal Asad's critique of Geertz's definition of religion in *Genealogies of Religion* (1993) is relevant to both approaches: he argues that treating religion as a universal domain of symbolic meaning, separable from power and from historical disciplines, is itself a historical product of Christian and modern Western thought.

## Why the Difference Matters Methodologically

This contrast explains why proponents of thick description criticize Eliadean phenomenology as a metaphysics disguised in the language of descriptive science. When Eliade presupposes the existence of "the sacred" as a universal category prior to any cultural context, he anticipates the conclusion that empirical research itself should uncover. Geertz's critics, for their part, respond that his strict cultural relativism may prevent any meaningful comparison between religions. It becomes difficult, on that view, to speak of a shared "religious phenomenon" worth comparative study at all.
