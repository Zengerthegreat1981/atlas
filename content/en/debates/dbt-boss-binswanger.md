# Who Read Heidegger Right?

Binswanger or Boss? This is the founding dispute of existential therapy, and its question is wider than either man: what happens when a philosophy is carried into the clinic? Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss were the two Swiss psychiatrists who first built a therapy on Martin Heidegger's philosophy, but they read Heidegger in incompatible ways, and Heidegger himself is reported to have taken sides. The dispute is not a quarrel about biography; it is about what counts as a faithful transfer of an idea from one field to another, and its echoes are still felt in differences among training traditions in existential analysis. This entry is about the debate between the two readings; the Daseinsanalysis current itself, as a branch, is a separate subject.

## Where the Dispute Lies

Binswanger read Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927) and built on it an analysis of patients' worlds. In *Basic Forms and the Knowledge of Human Existence* (1942) he set out his own account of human existence; in later studies he described forms of failed existence (Verstiegenheit, Verschrobenheit and Manieriertheit, collected in a book of 1956) and analysed the worlds of melancholia, mania and schizophrenia as constrictions of the possibilities open to a person. Heidegger, as reported by Boss and others, judged that Binswanger had turned his ontological analysis into a theory of the subject, thereby returning it to exactly what Heidegger had tried to move beyond. The charge was precise: *Being and Time* analyses the structure of being, not the psychology of individuals, and a theory of "worlds" belonging to subjects reintroduces the Cartesian subject Heidegger had dismantled.

## The Position of Boss

Medard Boss took the other path. He wrote to Heidegger in 1947 asking for a sounder philosophical basis for his clinical work, and the correspondence led to the Zollikon Seminars, held in Boss's own house from 1959 to 1969, in which Heidegger discussed Boss's clinical material and worked with psychiatrists on his philosophy. Boss's *Psychoanalysis and Daseinsanalysis* (1957; English translation, 1963) and his *Outline of Medicine and Psychology* (1971) present a version closer to the Heideggerian original: no psychic structures, no theory of the subject, only a description of the openness of the world — *Daseinsanalyse* as the study of how a person's being-in-the-world shows itself, including how it narrows in illness. The *Zollikon Seminars* themselves were published in German in 1987, edited by Boss (English translation 2001), and they remain the documentary record of the collaboration.

## The Arguments

Binswanger's defenders argue that any transfer from philosophy to clinic necessarily involves transformation. Literal fidelity to the text produces philosophical commentary, not a practice that helps anyone; Binswanger's "worlds" gave psychiatrists a usable language for what they actually saw in patients, and a bridge to the diagnostic categories of traditional psychiatry. Boss's defenders reply that a transformation which changes the meaning is not a transfer but a borrowing of the name: whoever does that should declare that he is building something new, not applying Heidegger. The dispute has never been settled because it is not a disagreement about a fact but about a standard — what makes a transfer from one field to another legitimate — and no such standard is agreed. It is a debate about criteria, and criteria are precisely what the two sides do not share.

## Its Effect Today

Every time a textbook says "existential therapy is built on Heidegger", this dispute is present in the sentence without being named. In practice, the choice between the two readings still matters. A therapist who follows Binswanger works within existing diagnostic frames, speaks a vocabulary close to that of the ordinary clinic, and can converse with traditional psychiatry and teach inside institutions with relative ease — at the risk of letting Heidegger's phenomenology slide into psychological classification dressed in philosophical language. A therapist who follows Boss refuses diagnostic classification as a distortion of the phenomenon and stays with the description of the patient's experience as it discloses itself in being-in-the-world — more faithful to Heidegger, but harder to teach and harder to communicate with health institutions that require a clear diagnosis. The divide between these readings continues to shape contemporary training in existential analysis rather than being a mere historical curiosity.

The dispute also illustrates a wider problem in the history of ideas: the founder's authority. Heidegger's reported disapproval of Binswanger is often invoked as if it settled the matter, but much of the evidence passes through Boss, who recorded and edited the seminars and reported what Heidegger said, and Heidegger's sustained collaboration was with Boss. Those who side with Binswanger note that Heidegger's explicit criticism reached print in the record Boss edited, after Binswanger's death, and that a philosopher's preference for one disciple is not a philosophical argument. Those who side with Boss reply that the seminars, which include Heidegger's direct criticism of Binswanger (notably in November 1965), are the closest thing to a verdict the record offers, and that Heidegger's sustained engagement with Boss speaks for itself. The appeal to Heidegger's authority is thus part of the dispute, not a way out of it.
