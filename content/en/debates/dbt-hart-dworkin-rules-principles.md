# The Hart–Dworkin Debate (Rules vs. Principles)

A debate running from 1967 until after the death of **Hart** (1992), conducted by **Ronald Dworkin**, who succeeded Hart in the chair of jurisprudence at Oxford, as he criticized Hart's central theses in *The Concept of Law*.

## The First Objection: Rules Versus Principles

In his early essay "The Model of Rules" (1967), Dworkin argues that Hart's description of the legal system as a system of **rules**, applied in an all-or-nothing manner, ignores the role of **principles** (such as the principle that "no one may profit from his own wrong", invoked by the New York Court of Appeals in *Riggs v. Palmer*, 1889), which:

- Do not have their validity determined by a clear official source criterion (such as legislation or precedent) in the way that the validity of rules is determined through the **rule of recognition**.
- Have **weight**: in hard cases they are weighed against one another, rather than being applied or set aside entirely like rules.

Dworkin concluded that this undermines the idea of the "rule of recognition" as a comprehensive criterion for the validity of everything legal in a system.

## The Second Objection: No Single Right Answer?

Dworkin later extended the argument in a deeper thesis, in *A Matter of Principle* (1985) and *Law's Empire* (1986): every hard legal case has **one right answer** (One Right Answer) that optimal constructive interpretation discovers. It does not have a set of possible answers from which the judge chooses with discretionary power, as Hart had assumed.

## Hart's Reply (Postscript, 1994, After His Death)

In the Postscript added to the second edition of *The Concept of Law*, based on a draft Hart left behind, he defended a position he called **soft positivism**, often discussed as **inclusive positivism**. The rule of recognition may **incorporate a moral criterion** (such as principles of justice) among the conditions of a law's validity in a given system, without this overturning the general positivist separation between law and morality. The incorporation itself is a conventional social choice, not a conceptual necessity as Dworkin claims.

## The Importance of the Debate

This debate is the axis around which analytical philosophy of law split into two camps after Hart. The first is **exclusive positivism** (Raz), which insists that the validity of law cannot rest on moral criteria at all. The second is **inclusive positivism** (Coleman), which accepts that possibility without abandoning the general positivist framework.
