# Fusha vs. ʿAmmiyya: Which Language for Education and the State?

A linguistic, political and educational debate that has continued since the late 19th century over which linguistic form should be used in the institutions of education, the state and the media in the Arab world: standard Modern Standard Arabic (*al-ʿArabiyya al-Fuṣḥā*), a form that is not acquired as a mother tongue but learned formally, or a colloquial *ʿammiyya* that takes one of the regional dialects as its written basis.

## The origin of the debate: Orientalist proposals and early reform

From the late 19th century and the early 20th century, European Orientalists and reformers made explicit proposals for adopting Egyptian colloquial as the basis for writing and literature. Wilhelm Spitta published a grammar of Egyptian colloquial in 1880, and the engineer William Willcocks argued in 1893 that Egyptian colloquial should replace the classical language in writing. They argued that the wide gap between written Fuṣḥā and the everyday spoken colloquial slows the eradication of illiteracy and makes primary education difficult for children who effectively learn a "foreign language" within their schools. These proposals met with broad rejection from the intellectuals of the Nahda (Arab Renaissance), who saw in Fuṣḥā the bond of cultural, religious and national unity among the Arab countries and the means of connecting with the Islamic and literary heritage spanning centuries.

## The two sides of the contemporary debate

- **The position of adherence to Fuṣḥā**: its proponents argue that Fuṣḥā, despite being a language "acquired" through education rather than a mother tongue, is the only bond that unites readers, writers and viewers across regional dialects that may find direct mutual understanding difficult (such as the Moroccan and Gulf dialects, for example). They argue that abandoning it would threaten the unity of Arab cultural identity and cut generations off from the written heritage.
- **The position favoring colloquial**: its proponents argue that primary education in a language closer to what the child actually speaks at home improves literacy outcomes and reduces school dropout. They cite evidence from psycholinguistics about the difficulty of learning to read in a language the learner does not use in actual daily life, and they also cite the experience of colloquial varieties written in literary form (such as theatre and popular song), which have gained a partially recognized cultural standing.

## The present situation: practical bilingualism without resolution

No Arab state has officially adopted replacing Fuṣḥā with colloquial in education or administration at present. However, actual practice shows a growing overlap between the two forms, especially in satellite media and written social media platforms, where many users mix Fuṣḥā with colloquial or openly write in colloquial. This makes the practical boundaries between the two forms more fluid than the classical theory of linguistic diglossia by Ferguson describes.

## The empirical educational dimension

The educational debate over the effect of the linguistic gap on educational outcomes remains empirically open. Some studies point to real difficulties in acquiring early reading among children who do not speak Fuṣḥā at home, while other studies warn that comparing educational outcomes across different countries and educational systems mixes many factors (overall quality of education, resources and training) with the effect of language itself, making it difficult to isolate the effect of diglossia alone with decisive confidence.

## The modern media and technical dimension

Modern media have added a new layer to this debate. Since the late 20th century, an intermediate form known sometimes as "simplified Modern Standard Arabic" or "media language" has emerged. It is used by news anchors and talk show hosts. It is related to what Badawi (1973) described as intermediate levels between classical and colloquial Egyptian Arabic, and what is often called "Educated Spoken Arabic." It preserves much of Fuṣḥā grammar and vocabulary but often drops case endings and reduces the old complex rhetorical and lexical forms in favor of vocabulary closer to broad public comprehension. Some researchers see it as a practical middle ground between pure classical Fuṣḥā and spoken colloquial, while others see it as a gradual deterioration of precise Fuṣḥā standards rather than a real solution to the dilemma.

## Comparisons with other linguistic situations

This debate often cites the experiences of other countries that faced a similar dilemma between a historical standard language and spoken vernaculars derived from it. One example is modern Greek, which resolved a similar debate between the official formal "Katharevousa" and the spoken "Dimotiki," gradually in favor of the latter during the 20th century. Another is Swiss German, which maintains a stable practical diglossia between written standard German and the Swiss dialects spoken daily, without intense conflict over which of the two is preferable. These comparisons are sometimes used as an argument for the possibility of a similar gradual development in the Arab case, while others question whether these experiences can be generalized given the particular depth of the religious and historical bond between Fuṣḥā and the Qur'anic text, a bond that is not present with the same strength in the other compared cases.

## The impact of social media on the debate

Social media platforms have reshaped the field of this debate in practice since the first decade of the 21st century. They provided, for the first time, a wide space for everyday colloquial writing on an unprecedented mass scale (comments, posts and messages). Many users now write in colloquial directly in contexts where colloquial was not traditionally written (such as public political debate). Some linguistic researchers see this as a new "written" diglossia that did not previously exist, when colloquial writing was limited to theatrical dialogue and popular song. Meanwhile, formal, journalistic and academic writing remains almost exclusively Fuṣḥā at present.
