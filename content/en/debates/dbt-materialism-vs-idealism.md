# Materialism versus Idealism

Do material conditions drive history, or do ideas? The debate between materialism and idealism is one of the oldest in social thought, and it runs through the whole of sociology. Materialists argue that the economy, technology, and the physical environment set the terms of social life, and that ideas — religion, law, philosophy — ultimately reflect those conditions. Idealists argue that ideas have a power of their own: beliefs, values, and meanings shape institutions, motivate action, and can transform the material world. This entry treats the dispute as a debate about the direction of causation in social life, distinct from the narrower concept of materialism as a philosophical doctrine: the question is whether ideas are an effect of material conditions or a cause in their own right.

## The Materialist Position: Conditions First

The classic materialist statement is Marx's. In *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859), he wrote that the mode of production of material life conditions the social, political, and intellectual life process in general, and that it is not the consciousness of people that determines their being but their social being that determines their consciousness. The argument was worked out with Friedrich Engels in *The German Ideology* (written 1845–46, first published in 1932), where ideas are treated as expressions of the material activity and intercourse of real people, and in *Capital* (1867), where the commodity form is shown to shape how people perceive their own social relations. Twentieth-century materialists developed the position in several directions. Marvin Harris, in *Cultural Materialism* (1979), argued that cultural practices — from food taboos to religious rituals — can be explained by their material costs and benefits, above all by the requirements of production and reproduction. The materialist case rests on a simple observation: people must eat, shelter, and reproduce before they can philosophise, and the ways they organise those necessities constrain everything else. Its strongest evidence is correlation — the close fit between economic structure and political and cultural forms across societies — and its strongest argument is that idealist explanations leave the origins of ideas themselves unexplained.

## The Idealist Position: Ideas Have Consequences

The idealist reply begins with Max Weber. In *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1904–05), Weber argued that a religious ethic — Calvinist anxiety about salvation and the duty of disciplined work in a calling — helped create the spirit of modern capitalism, a case in which an idea was not a mere reflection of economic conditions but an independent cause of economic transformation. Weber himself disclaimed any intention of replacing a one-sided materialist account with an equally one-sided spiritualist one, and his wider work gives weight to both interests and ideas. Émile Durkheim made a parallel argument in *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912): religious categories such as the sacred and the profane are not illusions floating above social life but the very framework through which people organise their experience, and they exert a real force on conduct. Marshall Sahlins, in *Culture and Practical Reason* (1976), argued against materialist explanations of cultural practice, showing that what counts as useful or edible depends on cultural categories — the same animal is food in one culture and taboo in another, so culture, not biology or economics, decides. Raymond Williams, in *Marxism and Literature* (1977), developed a Marxist cultural materialism that rejects the crude base–superstructure model: culture is not a superstructure floating above the economy but itself a material practice, a form of production in which meanings are made. For idealists, the decisive point is that material conditions never speak for themselves — they must be interpreted, and interpretation is the work of ideas.

## Arguments and Counter-Arguments

Each side accuses the other of a one-sided causal story. Materialists charge idealists with ignoring the brute constraints of survival: ideas do not feed people, and a sociology that begins with beliefs while ignoring who owns what will mistake the rhetoric of the powerful for the reality of social life. They also argue that idealist explanations are circular — if capitalism was caused by the Protestant ethic, what caused the Protestant ethic? Idealists reply that materialism is equally circular: if ideas are merely reflections of material conditions, then the materialist theory itself is merely a reflection of nineteenth-century industrial conditions, and it cannot claim to be true. They also point out that material conditions underdetermine outcomes: societies with similar economies develop very different politics and cultures, which suggests that ideas do independent work. The sharpest form of the dispute concerns cases of rapid transformation — revolutions, religious conversions, nationalist movements — where changes in belief seem to precede and produce changes in material organisation, rather than following them.

## Contemporary Status

Contemporary sociology has largely abandoned the question in its stark form — material conditions or ideas, first cause or last? — and asks instead how the two interact in specific historical processes. Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann, in *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966), showed how ideas become material: institutions, once created by human action, confront people as objective realities that constrain them, so that the products of consciousness acquire material force. Bourdieu's theory of practice similarly treats culture and economy as interconvertible forms of capital. The debate survives in a transformed form in research on topics such as climate change, where material facts and cultural meanings interact, and in the study of economic life, where the "cultural turn" in economic sociology has shown that markets depend on shared meanings, trust, and narratives. The lasting lesson of the debate is methodological: any explanation that treats either material conditions or ideas as the sole cause is incomplete, and the task is to trace their mutual constitution in particular cases.
