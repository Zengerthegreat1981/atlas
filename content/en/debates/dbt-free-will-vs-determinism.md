# The Debate of Free Will versus Determinism and Fatalism

The founding formulation of the oldest metaphysical and ethical debate in philosophy: is every event, including human decisions, a necessary result of a chain of prior causes, or is there in the cosmos a real margin of contingency that opens the door to an undetermined choice? This file documents the classical formulation of the debate from its Hellenistic origin to its early modern crisis; the contemporary analytic formulations (compatibilism, metaphysical libertarianism, and neuroscience) are documented in separate files linked below to avoid repetition.

## The Stoic origin: Chrysippus and universal fate (Heimarmene)

Chrysippus of Soli (279–206 BCE), the third head of the Stoic school and the most precise theorist of its logical doctrine, defended a universal cosmic causal determinism (heimarmene): every event, including the voluntary acts of human beings, is part of a coherent chain of causes with no break. Chrysippus's original text on fate is lost; what has reached us is the report of his adversary Cicero in his work *On Fate* (De Fato, 44 BCE), supplemented by later reports such as Aulus Gellius and Alexander of Aphrodisias. Chrysippus tried to reconcile cosmic determinism with the agent's responsibility through his distinction between "perfect and principal" causes and "auxiliary and proximate" causes, illustrated by the image of a cylinder that rolls by its own shape once pushed (the agent's own constitution as the principal cause of assent, not a mere mechanical conveyor).

## The Epicurean reply: the atomic swerve (Clinamen)

In contrast, Epicurus of Samos (341–270 BCE) rejected the strict Democritean atomic determinism because, in his view, it empties ethics of meaning if there is no real margin for an undetermined action. He proposed that the atoms, in their parallel fall through the void, swerve slightly and unpredictably (clinamen), breaking the strict causal chain. The swerve is not found in Epicurus's surviving letters; the fullest surviving exposition comes from the Roman poet Lucretius (c. 99–c. 55 BCE) in his philosophical poem *On the Nature of Things* (De Rerum Natura, Book II, mid-first century BCE, lines 216–293), which links the atomic swerve to the possibility of a "will wrested from fate" (*fatis avolsa voluntas*).

## Early modern continuation: Spinoza and Kant

Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) reformulated universal determinism rationally in the *Ethics* (published posthumously in 1677; see Part I, Proposition 29 and the Appendix to Part I): everything in nature follows necessarily from the nature of God/substance, and there is no "free" will in an undetermined sense; what we call freedom is ignorance of the causes that move us. Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781, the Third Antinomy) and the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), attempted a solution different from the Stoic one: he separated the world of appearances, governed by strict causal determinism, from the intelligible standpoint of the "thing in itself", where the rational agent can be a free cause in a transcendental sense that, Kant argued, does not contradict natural determinism, and he treated freedom as the condition of the moral law.
