# The Wealth Tax Debate

Thomas Piketty proposed, in his widely discussed book *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (French original 2013, English translation 2014), a radical political remedy for the rising global concentration of wealth. This is a coordinated, ideally global, progressive annual tax on net personal wealth (not annual income alone), applied to the highest wealth brackets. The idea moved from an academic proposal to a hot political issue in the United States and Europe after prominent politicians adopted it, including Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Sanders in their American presidential campaigns. It provoked a sharp economic debate about its practical value.

## The Rationale: Breaking the r > g Cycle

Piketty drew on his central observation that the return on capital (r) has historically exceeded the growth rate of the economy as a whole (g) in most periods, except for the exceptional mid-twentieth century. This means accumulated wealth grows automatically faster than general economic income, and becomes increasingly concentrated across generations through inheritance rather than current productive effort. Piketty held that an annual wealth tax is the only instrument capable of slowing this structural accumulation directly at its source, namely capital itself. An income tax, by contrast, does not touch inherited idle wealth. He sketched progressive rates, low for moderate fortunes and rising to several percent for the very largest, with emphasis on the need for international coordination to prevent evasion through the transfer of wealth to jurisdictions with lower taxes.

## Criticism of Practical Feasibility: Difficult Valuation and Likely Flight

The proposal faced serious practical objections from economists, including Lawrence Summers and Natasha Sarin. In an influential article in 2019 they argued that valuing illiquid assets every year (such as private companies, art and unique real estate) is very difficult in practice and administratively costly. They pointed out that earlier European experiments with wealth taxes (in France, Sweden, Germany and others) yielded modest revenue and were vulnerable to evasion through emigration or legal restructuring of assets. Most of these countries abandoned them over the past decades; the number of OECD countries levying a net wealth tax fell sharply after 1990. France replaced its general wealth tax (the ISF) in 2017 with a tax on real estate wealth only (the IFI), a reform defended as a response to capital flight and weak yield and criticised as a gift to the wealthy.

## The Defence: Evasion Can Be Blocked by Proper Design

Emmanuel Saez and Gabriel Zucman, the principal economic advisers to the Warren and Sanders proposals, defended the view that the failure of earlier European experiments stems from poor design. These included many exemptions, very low rates that did not justify the administrative cost, and the absence of effective international tax information exchange at the time. In their view the failure does not stem from an intrinsic flaw in the idea of a wealth tax itself. They argued that recent developments make a well-designed wealth tax far more applicable than the earlier twentieth-century experiments. Among these developments are the spread of automatic international exchange of banking information between states in the years following the financial crisis and leaks such as the "Panama Papers", and the improved ability of tax authorities to trace true ownership across borders. A well-designed wealth tax, with higher rates, strict exit penalties and broader international cooperation, could therefore work far more effectively.

## A Practical Alternative: A Tax on Unrealised Gains for Billionaires

Given the general practical difficulties of a comprehensive wealth tax, later political debate moved toward a narrower formula. It targets only a very small top group, billionaires, who are counted in the hundreds rather than the millions, which makes valuation and enforcement relatively easier. The formula proposes an annual tax on the increase in value of their publicly traded assets (such as shares in their companies), even before those assets are actually sold. This addresses a known problem: many technology wealth holders borrow against shares whose value keeps rising, without ever selling them, and so avoid the conventional income tax on realised capital gains indefinitely. A version of this approach appeared in the Biden administration's 2022 budget proposal for a minimum tax on very wealthy households, which counted unrealised gains, and in a related proposal by Senator Ron Wyden; neither was enacted.

## The Current Position

None of these radical national proposals has yet been applied on a broad scale despite their political momentum. A modest international advance has been achieved in a parallel direction: the OECD-led global minimum corporate tax agreement of 2021 (a 15% rate). The debate about the usefulness of an individual wealth tax remains open. On one side are those who see it as a necessity for restraining structural inequality. On the other are those who see it as an administratively costly instrument of limited net revenue value.

## An Additional Dimension: Switzerland as a Rare Example of Continuity

Switzerland is cited as a striking exception amid the decline of European wealth taxes. It has kept an annual tax on net wealth, levied by the cantons, for a very long time without abolishing it, at comparatively low rates. Its tax system relies on extensive third-party reporting and a fiscal culture in which wealth declarations are routine, which supporters say limits evasion. Advocates of the wealth tax use this as evidence that its continuation is genuinely possible when combined with sufficient administrative capacity and low rates, while critics note that Switzerland's low rates and particular institutions make it hard to generalise.
