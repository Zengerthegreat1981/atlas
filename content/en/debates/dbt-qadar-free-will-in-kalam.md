# The Debate over Qadar, Compulsion and Choice in Islamic Kalam

The founding theological dispute over the relation of the human being to his acts: does the servant create his act himself and so deserve reward and punishment by divine justice, or is God alone the creator of every act and the servant "acquires" it without originating it? The debate arose in the second century AH (eighth century CE) and has remained a pivot of kalam to this day.

## Early background: Qadariyya and Jabriyya

The question arose in the Umayyad period. Early figures such as Ma'bad al-Juhani (executed c. 80 AH / 699 CE) and Ghaylan al-Dimashqi affirmed human power over action and were called Qadariyya, while Jahm ibn Safwan (d. 128 AH / 746 CE) defended strict determinism (jabr), denying that human beings have real power over their acts. The Mu'tazila and the Ash'ariyya later took positions in the space between these poles.

## The position of the Mu'tazila: the human creates his acts (qadariyya in the positive sense)

Wasil ibn Ata (d. 131 AH / 748 CE) founded the school of i'tizal partly on the question of the "position between the two positions", and the later Mu'tazila inherited his position in affirming the servant's power to create his acts. Al-Qadi Abd al-Jabbar ibn Ahmad al-Hamadhani (d. 415 AH / 1025 CE) formulated in his book *al-Mughni fi abwab al-tawhid wa-l-adl* the most detailed argument: divine justice requires that the servant be the real agent of his acts, otherwise it would be impossible to charge him or to hold him accountable for what he has no power over. Power in the human being is "originated" and precedes the act immediately, and the human creates his act by it, independently of God's direct power over that very act.

## The position of the Ash'ariyya: the theory of acquisition

Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. c. 324 AH / 936 CE) replied to his Mu'tazili teacher Abu Ali al-Jubba'i with a position midway between pure determinism and i'tizal, whose theory of acquisition (kasb) is developed in his *al-Luma'* (while *al-Ibana an usul al-diyana* represents his traditionalist creed): God is the sole creator of every act, including the acts of servants, but the servant "acquires" the act when his originated power is joined to the created act, so that he becomes legally responsible for it without being truly its originator. This theory of "acquisition" (kasb) became the dominant official Sunni position after al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH / 1111 CE) adopted it in *al-Iqtisad fi al-i'tiqad*, while stressing that the relation between power and act is a constant divine "habit" and not a necessary rational causality.

## Why this dispute matters

The question is not abstract: on it is built the legitimacy of obligation, reward and punishment in Islamic jurisprudence, and it is the historical precedent of the discussion of determinism and libertarianism in modern philosophy of action. Ash'arism still represents the most widespread position in traditional Sunni institutions, while the arguments of the Mu'tazila are revived in contemporary reformist readings that tie divine justice to human freedom of choice.
