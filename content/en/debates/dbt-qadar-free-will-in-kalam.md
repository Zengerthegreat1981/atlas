# The Debate over Qadar, Compulsion and Choice in Islamic Kalam

The founding theological dispute over the relation of the human being to his acts: does the servant create his act himself and so deserve reward and punishment by divine justice, or is God alone the creator of every act and the servant "acquires" it without originating it? The debate arose in the late first and second centuries AH (late seventh and eighth centuries CE) and has remained a pivot of kalam to this day.

## Early Background: Qadariyya and Jabriyya

The controversy over divine foreordination (*qadar*) and human agency emerged during the late Umayyad era. Early theologians such as Ma'bad al-Juhani (executed c. 80 AH / 699 CE) and Ghaylan al-Dimashqi (executed under the caliph Hisham, r. 105–125 AH / 724–743 CE) affirmed real human power over voluntary actions; their detractors labelled them the *Qadariyya* (those who assert human agency over *qadar*). In contrast, Jahm ibn Safwan (executed 128 AH / 746 CE) championed absolute determinism (*jabr*), asserting that human beings possess no genuine causal power and that acts are attributed to humans only metaphorically, just as movement is attributed to a tree swaying in the wind. The subsequent schools of the Mu'tazila and the Ash'ariyya developed structured dialectical positions between these initial poles.

## The Position of the Mu'tazila: The Agent Creates His Acts

Wasil ibn Ata (d. 131 AH / 748 CE) and early Mu'tazili masters grounded their doctrine of divine justice (*al-'adl*) on human moral responsibility. Later systematizers, preeminently al-Qadi Abd al-Jabbar (d. 415 AH / 1025 CE) in *al-Mughni fi abwab al-tawhid wa-l-'adl*, argued that moral obligation (*taklif*) and divine justice rationally necessitate that humans be the true creators (*khaliqun*) and authors of their voluntary deeds. If God created an act of disobedience in a human and subsequently punished the agent for it, God would be tyrannical (*zalim*), which is impossible for a just Deity. For the Mu'tazila, God endows the agent with an originated causal capacity (*qudra*) prior to the act, by which the agent freely originates the deed.

## The Position of the Ash'ariyya: The Doctrine of Acquisition (Kasb)

Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 324 AH / 935–936 CE), reacting against Mu'tazili rationalism after leaving the circle of Abu Ali al-Jubba'i, sought a middle path affirming divine omnipotence without entirely abandoning human moral culpability. In *Kitab al-Luma'*, al-Ash'ari formulated the doctrine of acquisition (*kasb*): God is the sole creator (*khaliq*) of all substances and accidents, including every human action and volition. However, God creates in the human subject an originated power (*qudra haditha*) simultaneously with the act. The act is said to be "acquired" (*muktasab*) by the servant through its concurrence with this originated capacity, thereby establishing legal accountability without according the human real ontological causality. Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH / 1111 CE) defended this synthesis in *al-Iqtisad fi al-i'tiqad*, framing regularities between volition and bodily action as habitual divine custom (*sunnat Allah* / *'ada*) rather than necessary intrinsic physical causality.

## Textual Traditions and Critical Editions

The core texts of this dispute were preserved in distinct textual transmissions. Al-Qadi Abd al-Jabbar's encyclopedic *al-Mughni fi abwab al-tawhid wa-l-'adl*, rediscovered in Yemen in 1951, was critically edited under the general supervision of Taha Hussein and Ibrahim Madkour and published in Cairo (al-Mu'assasa al-Misriyya al-'Amma, 1960–1969). Key volumes addressing action, justice, and responsibility include Volume 6, Part 1 (*al-Ta'dil wa-l-tajwir*, ed. Ahmad Fu'ad al-Ahwani, 1962), Volume 8 (*al-Makhluq*, ed. Tawfiq al-Tawil and Sa'id Zayid, 1965), and Volume 11 (*al-Taklif*, 1965).

For the Ash'ari school, Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari's primary polemical work, *Kitab al-Luma' fi al-radd 'ala ahl al-zaygh wa-l-bida'*, was critically edited with an English translation and analysis by Richard J. McCarthy in *The Theology of al-Ash'ari* (Beirut: Imprimerie Catholique, 1953). His traditionalist creed, *al-Ibana 'an usul al-diyana*, has seen multiple scholarly printings, notably edited by Fawqiyya Husayn Mahmud (Cairo: Dar al-Ansar, 1977).

## Why This Dispute Matters

The debate directly underpins Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*) regarding intention, legal capacity (*ahliyya*), and moral culpability. Historically, it anticipates contemporary discussions in philosophy of action concerning compatibilism, libertarian free will, and hard determinism. Ash'ari *kasb* remains influential in classical Sunni theology, while Mu'tazili arguments continue to inform modern Islamic reformist discourses linking ethical autonomy to social and political accountability.
