# Ritual Competence Theory vs. Modes of Religiosity (McCauley & Lawson vs. Whitehouse)

Whitehouse's doctrinal/imagistic dichotomy (`con-modes-of-religiosity-theory-whitehouse`) has a close parallel within the cognitive science of religion: the ritual form (or ritual competence) theory developed by E. Thomas Lawson and Robert McCauley (*Rethinking Religion*, 1990; *Bringing Ritual to Mind*, 2002). The two frameworks were developed in parallel and are often compared.

## The Point of Disagreement

Both sides accept the shared assumption that religious ritual can be analyzed cognitively, with a structure that can be classified across different cultures. However, they differ in emphasis. McCauley and Lawson's *ritual form hypothesis* distinguishes rituals by the role of the superhuman agent in the representation of the ritual action: in "special agent" rituals the gods act through a human agent (such as the Eucharist), and the ritual can be repeated; in "special patient" rituals the gods act on the participant (such as initiation or baptism), and the effect is non-repeatable. They predict that special patient rituals tend to be performed rarely with high sensory pageantry, while repeated rituals tend to have lower sensory pageantry. This overlaps with Whitehouse's pairing of frequent, low-arousal ritual (doctrinal mode) and rare, high-arousal ritual (imagistic mode), but the theories explain it differently: McCauley and Lawson by the cognitive representation of ritual action, Whitehouse by memory systems (semantic versus episodic) and their social consequences.

## Assessment

The two frameworks are often cited together as complementary rather than as mutually exclusive: the ritual form theory focuses on the internal structure of ritual action, whereas Whitehouse's theory focuses on the collective social effects (cohesion versus institutional spread). Neither has definitively displaced the other.
