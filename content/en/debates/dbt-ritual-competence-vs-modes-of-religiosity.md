# Ritual Competence Theory vs. Modes of Religiosity (McCauley & Lawson vs. Whitehouse)

Whitehouse's doctrinal/imagistic dichotomy (`con-modes-of-religiosity-theory-whitehouse`) has a close parallel within the cognitive science of religion: the ritual form (or ritual competence) theory developed by E. Thomas Lawson and Robert McCauley (*Rethinking Religion*, 1990; *Bringing Ritual to Mind*, 2002). The two frameworks were developed in parallel and are often compared.

## The Point of Disagreement

Both sides accept the shared assumption that religious ritual can be analyzed cognitively, with a structure that can be classified across different cultures. However, they differ in emphasis. McCauley and Lawson's *ritual form hypothesis* distinguishes rituals by the role of the superhuman agent in the representation of the ritual action. In special-agent rituals the most direct link to the god is in the agent role, as in rites such as baptism in which a ritual official acts on a participant; these are treated as having lasting effects and as carrying relatively high sensory pageantry. Rituals in which the god is implicated instead as patient or instrument, such as the Eucharist, are more readily repeated and are predicted to show lower sensory pageantry. Malley and Barrett (2003) summarize the claim as one about repeatability, reversibility, and relative sensory pageantry. This overlaps with Whitehouse's pairing of frequent, low-arousal ritual (doctrinal mode) and rare, high-arousal ritual (imagistic mode), but the theories explain it differently: McCauley and Lawson by the cognitive representation of ritual action, Whitehouse by memory systems (semantic versus episodic) and their social consequences.

## Assessment

The two frameworks are often cited together as complementary rather than as mutually exclusive: the ritual form theory focuses on the internal structure of ritual action, whereas Whitehouse's theory focuses on the collective social effects (cohesion versus institutional spread). Neither has definitively displaced the other.

## Later comparison of the two frameworks

In *Bringing Ritual to Mind* (2002), McCauley and Lawson set their ritual form hypothesis beside what they call a ritual frequency hypothesis, taking Whitehouse's work as its strongest statement. They note that the two accounts often predict the same pairing of rare, high-arousal ritual with frequent, low-arousal ritual, and that the interesting cases are those in which the predictions diverge. Their argument is that ritual form is the more basic variable, because the way a superhuman agent is represented helps explain how often a ritual is performed, which the frequency account leaves as an independent fact. They also report that the two lines of work had already been seen to make some incompatible predictions. The joint volume *Mind and Religion* (Whitehouse and McCauley, eds., 2005) gathers tests and criticisms of modes theory, including Lawson's chapter on form and frequency. Atkinson and Whitehouse (2011) then tested the modes prediction across cultures and reported a negative correlation between ritual frequency and rated arousal, while listing the role given to supernatural agency in McCauley and Lawson as one among several accounts of why ritual features cluster. The later literature therefore compares, tests, and sometimes nests the two claims. It does not replace either framework with a single combined theory.
