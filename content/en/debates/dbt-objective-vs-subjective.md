# Objective versus Subjective

Few disagreements have shaped sociology as deeply as the one between the objective and the subjective. At its simplest, the debate asks what social reality is made of: external facts that exist independently of the people who live among them, or meanings that people create, interpret, and carry in their minds. The question is not merely philosophical. It decides what sociologists should study, how they should study it, and what counts as a valid explanation. An objectivist treats suicide rates, wage structures, and legal codes as things that can be measured and compared; a subjectivist insists that no rate or rule means anything until we understand how the people involved experience and interpret it. Most sociologists today refuse to choose one side outright, yet the tension still organises textbooks, methods courses, and research design.

## The Objectivist Position: Society as External Fact

The classic objectivist statement comes from Émile Durkheim, who argued in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) that social facts are "things": ways of acting, thinking, and feeling that exist outside the individual and exert a coercive power over them. A language, a currency, a legal system, or a suicide rate confronts each person as a ready-made reality. Durkheim pressed the point in *Suicide* (1897), arguing that suicide rates vary systematically with levels of social integration and regulation, even though each individual suicide feels intensely personal. The objectivist case rests on three claims. First, social patterns are real and relatively stable across individuals, so they can be observed, counted, and compared. Second, these patterns constrain action whether or not people notice them: unemployment, class position, and institutional rules shape lives from the outside. Third, explanation should therefore work like explanation in the natural sciences, identifying causes that operate independently of actors' intentions. Later objectivists, including structural Marxists and many quantitative sociologists, kept this emphasis on structures, institutions, and distributions that exist before any particular person acts.

## The Subjectivist Position: Society as Meaning

The subjectivist reply begins with Max Weber, who insisted that sociology must grasp the subjective meaning actors attach to their conduct. In *Economy and Society* (1922), Weber defined sociology as the interpretive understanding of social action, and his method of *verstehen* demanded that researchers reconstruct the motives and meanings behind behaviour. A crowd is not the same phenomenon as a queue, even if both look similar from the outside, because the meanings differ. Alfred Schutz developed this position in *The Phenomenology of the Social World* (1932), arguing that the social world is first of all a world of everyday, taken-for-granted meanings, and that scientific concepts must be built on the common-sense categories people already use. Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann carried the argument further in *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966): institutions feel objective and solid, but they are the products of human action that has been habitualised and passed on, and they must be continually reaffirmed in interaction to survive. For subjectivists, ignoring meaning produces explanations that are technically precise but sociologically empty.

## Arguments and Counter-Arguments

Each side accuses the other of a characteristic blindness. Objectivists charge subjectivists with reducing sociology to psychology: if everything depends on individual interpretation, then stable patterns such as class inequality or the persistence of institutions become hard to explain, and the discipline loses its claim to explain anything beyond what people say about themselves. They also warn that meanings can be mistaken or ideological, so taking actors' accounts at face value risks reproducing the very illusions sociology should expose. Subjectivists return the charge: objectivism risks what Alfred North Whitehead called the fallacy of misplaced concreteness, treating statistical aggregates and reified concepts as if they were real agents. A suicide rate does not kill anyone; only people do, acting on reasons, despair, and circumstances that a rate cannot capture. C. Wright Mills, in *The Sociological Imagination* (1959), sharpened the dispute by arguing that good sociology must connect personal troubles to public issues, which requires both the measurement of structures and the interpretation of experience.

## Syntheses and Contemporary Status

The most influential work since the 1970s has tried to dissolve the opposition rather than win it. Pierre Bourdieu's theory of practice, set out in *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1977), treats objective structures and subjective dispositions as two sides of one process: the *habitus* is a set of embodied dispositions produced by objective conditions, which then generates practices that reproduce those conditions. Anthony Giddens's structuration theory, in *The Constitution of Society* (1984), argues that structure is both the medium and the outcome of action: rules and resources only exist insofar as they are drawn upon in conduct, yet they also constrain that conduct. Margaret Archer, in *Realist Social Theory* (1995), objected that such syntheses blur the distinction too far; her morphogenetic approach insists that structure and agency operate over different time scales and must be analysed separately before their interplay can be understood. Today the debate lives on mainly as a methodological division of labour: quantitative research maps objective distributions, qualitative research recovers subjective meaning, and mixed-methods designs try to hold both in view. The question is no longer which side is right, but how to combine them without losing either the weight of social structure or the reality of lived experience.
