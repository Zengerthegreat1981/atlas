# Determinism versus Voluntarism

The debate between determinism and voluntarism asks how far human conduct is produced by conditions that actors do not control, and how far it results from their choices, purposes and efforts. In sociology it is usually less a metaphysical dispute about free will than a disagreement about what an adequate explanation of social life must contain. It is closely related to the debate over structure and agency.

## The Determinist Position

Determinist accounts explain conduct chiefly through causes external to individual decision, such as economic organisation, social norms, institutions or position in a hierarchy. Émile Durkheim's *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) described social facts as external to individuals and exercising constraint over them, and *Suicide* (1897) related differences in suicide rates to social integration and regulation. Some readings of Marx, especially of the 1859 preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, make the economic base the determinant of political and intellectual life, and the structural Marxism associated with Louis Althusser treated individuals chiefly as bearers of structural positions. Determinism in this field need not mean that every act is predictable; many versions claim only that social conditions strongly shape the distribution of outcomes.

## The Voluntarist Position

Voluntarist accounts hold that action cannot be explained without the ends, interpretations and efforts of actors. Max Weber defined sociology around the interpretive understanding of meaningful social action. Talcott Parsons, in *The Structure of Social Action* (1937), set out a "voluntaristic theory of action" in which actors pursue ends under conditions and normative standards, in opposition to both utilitarian and purely positivist explanations. Interactionist and phenomenological sociologists later stressed that people interpret situations and negotiate their meanings, so that social order is continually accomplished.

## Representative Arguments

Dennis Wrong's "The Oversocialized Conception of Man in Modern Sociology" (1961) argued that theories emphasising internalised norms, including those influenced by Parsons, left too little room for conflict between individual drives and social demands. Alan Dawe, in "The Two Sociologies" (1970), distinguished a sociology of social system, concerned with order, from a sociology of social action, concerned with control by actors over their situations. Determinists reply that appeals to choice explain little unless one accounts for why choices differ systematically between social groups. Voluntarists answer that regularities do not remove the actor, since structures operate through what people understand and do. Marx's remark in *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852) that people make their own history, but not under circumstances of their own choosing, is often cited as a statement of the middle ground.

## Contemporary Status

Most later theory tries to combine the two. Anthony Giddens's structuration theory, set out in *The Constitution of Society* (1984), treats structure as both medium and outcome of action. Pierre Bourdieu's concept of habitus describes dispositions shaped by social position that nonetheless allow improvisation. Margaret Archer, in *Realist Social Theory* (1995), argued against merging structure and agency and proposed analysing their interplay over time. Disagreement persists over whether such syntheses resolve the problem or restate it, and over how much weight each side should receive in particular cases, a question that empirical research on inequality, the life course and social movements addresses case by case.
