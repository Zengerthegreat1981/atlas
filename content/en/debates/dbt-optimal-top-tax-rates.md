# The Optimal Top Tax Rates Debate

What is the highest tax rate that can be imposed on the wealthiest income bracket before tax revenues themselves begin to fall, because of reduced incentives to work, invest and report income honestly? This is the core of a long-standing economic and political debate that has swung between a well-known economic argument warning against excessive progressive taxation, and more recent empirical research suggesting that optimal rates may be much higher than was widely believed in recent decades.

## From Mirrlees's optimum to a formula for the top rate

The modern theory of the optimal income-tax schedule begins with James Mirrlees, "An Exploration in the Theory of Optimum Income Taxation" (*Review of Economic Studies*, 1971). The government observes income, not ability, so a more progressive schedule changes how much high-ability people choose to earn. Emmanuel Saez, "Using Elasticities to Derive Optimal Income Tax Rates" (*Review of Economic Studies*, 2001), wrote the revenue-maximizing top marginal rate in terms of the elasticity of taxable income and the shape of the top of the income distribution. Diamond and Saez (2011) is a calibration of that approach, not the start of the literature.

## The Laffer curve: a warning against excessive taxation

In the 1970s Arthur Laffer popularised a simple diagram, reportedly sketched for US officials in 1974, that influenced the Reagan tax cuts. Tax revenue is zero at a rate of zero and zero at a rate of 100 percent, and positive in between, so there is some rate beyond which a further increase lowers revenue because people work less, invest less, shift income or evade tax. The shape alone does not say where current rates sit. The diagram is sometimes loosely compared with Ibn Khaldun's remarks in the *Muqaddimah* on excessively heavy taxation; that is an analogy drawn by later writers, not a shared model. In the United States the top statutory federal rate on ordinary income was 70 percent before the Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981, which reduced it to 50 percent; a 50 percent maximum already applied to much earned income. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 set a top statutory rate of 28 percent, with a 33 percent rate where lower brackets and exemptions were phased out. Advocates treated pre-1981 rates as being past the revenue peak. That claim is an empirical assertion about elasticities, not a consequence of the diagram.

## Diamond and Saez's critique: optimal rates far higher than assumed

In an influential 2011 paper, the economists Peter Diamond and Emmanuel Saez presented a more precise theoretical framework based on the empirically measured "elasticity of taxable income" rather than abstract theoretical guesswork. They concluded that the revenue-maximizing top marginal tax rate in the United States could be around 73%, well above the rates prevailing in recent decades. Their calculation depends on assumptions about the elasticity of taxable income, and they argued that much of the response of high earners to higher taxes reflects tax avoidance and income shifting (for example into capital gains or deferred compensation) rather than a genuine decline in work. This means that a large part of the apparent "tax avoidance" can be addressed by closing legal loopholes rather than by reducing the nominal rate itself.

## The moral progressivity argument: breaking the concentration of income at the top

Piketty, Saez and Stantcheva (2014) added a further argument, estimating a revenue-maximizing top rate of around 83% under certain assumptions: a large part of the enormous rise in incomes of the top 1% in Anglo-Saxon economies since the 1980s does not necessarily reflect a parallel increase in their real marginal productivity, but partly reflects a growing capacity to extract bargaining rent (for example, chief executives who set their own compensation through sympathetic boards of directors). In this case, a high progressive tax on the top does not weaken economic productivity but only limits the incentive for this type of rent extraction. This would make optimal rates higher than estimates built on the assumption that high income always reflects an equivalent genuine productivity.

## The counter-critique: risks of tax flight and investment

Other economists, particularly from the classical liberal tradition, warn that these theoretical estimates understate two important channels: the migration of the wealthy and business owners to jurisdictions with lower taxes in an integrated global economy (a flexibility that is difficult to measure domestically but is real across borders), and the effect of high taxation on investment decisions and long-term entrepreneurship that may not appear in short-run data. They argue that the Diamond–Saez models may overstate the optimal rate when applied to a small open economy rather than a theoretically closed one.

## Its effect on contemporary political debate

This research has fuelled actual political campaigns to raise taxes on upper brackets in the United States and Europe in the last decade, and has been directly linked to Piketty's broader debate on inequality. However, practical implementation has remained relatively limited, owing to international tax competition and the difficulty of coordinating a common global minimum tax on high income, unlike the partial coordination that was later achieved on a global minimum corporate tax.
