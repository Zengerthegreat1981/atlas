# Authenticity versus Constructedness

The debate between authenticity and constructedness asks whether things that people value as genuine, such as a tradition, an identity, a style of music or a tourist site, possess qualities of their own that make them real, or whether their genuineness is a status conferred through social processes. It matters because claims to authenticity carry consequences: they affect who may speak for a group, which cultural goods command respect and higher prices, and which practices are protected as heritage.

## The Case for Authenticity

In everyday usage, and in some older traditions of folklore and cultural criticism, the authentic is what is original, continuous with its source and free of calculation or commercial staging. On this view a practice handed down within a community differs in kind from an imitation designed for outsiders. Lionel Trilling's *Sincerity and Authenticity* (1972) traced how authenticity became a demanding moral ideal in Western literature and thought, distinct from the older ideal of sincerity. Dean MacCannell argued in 1973, and in *The Tourist* (1976), that modern tourists seek authentic experience but often encounter "staged authenticity", settings arranged to look like the private back regions of social life.

## The Constructionist Position

Constructionists hold that authenticity is not a property that can simply be read off an object. Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann's *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966) supplied a general account of how human products come to be experienced as objective facts. In *The Invention of Tradition* (1983), edited by Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger, historians showed that some traditions presented as ancient were recent creations. Benedict Anderson's *Imagined Communities* (1983) analysed the nation as an imagined political community. Richard Handler and Jocelyn Linnekin (1984) argued that tradition is an interpretive process, so that the contrast between genuine and spurious tradition is misleading. Richard Peterson's *Creating Country Music: Fabricating Authenticity* (1997) showed how performers and the music industry worked out what would count as authentic country music, and David Grazian's *Blue Chicago* (2003) examined how authenticity is sought and performed in blues clubs.

## Points of Dispute

Critics of constructionism argue that showing a thing to have a history does not show it to be false, and that calling identities "constructed" can seem to dismiss the experience of those who live them or to weaken claims by minorities and Indigenous peoples to land and heritage. Constructionists reply that construction is not the same as fabrication: what is socially made can be durable, binding and consequential. Ian Hacking's *The Social Construction of What?* (1999) pointed out that the label is applied to very different things and that one should ask what exactly is said to be constructed, and to what critical purpose. A further objection is that analysts who expose invented traditions may keep an unstated standard of the truly old or spontaneous against which the invented is measured.

## Contemporary Status

Much current research sets aside the question of whether something is really authentic and asks instead how authenticity is claimed, judged and contested: who has the authority to certify it, which cues audiences accept, and what rewards or exclusions follow. Studies of food, music, tourism, heritage, branding and online self-presentation take this approach. The philosophical disagreement remains open, since the sociological account of how judgements are made does not by itself settle whether some judgements are better founded than others.
