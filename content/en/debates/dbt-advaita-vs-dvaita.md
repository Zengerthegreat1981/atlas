# Advaita Vedanta versus Dvaita Vedanta: Is the World Illusion or Reality?

One of the great debates in Indian philosophy, between the Advaita school associated with Shankara and the Dvaita school founded by Madhva. Shankara is traditionally dated 788-820 CE, though modern scholarship generally places him in the eighth century (roughly 700-750 CE); he is credited with the summary "Brahman is real, the world is mithya (not ultimately real), the self is Brahman". Madhva is dated 1238-1317 CE by most modern scholars (the tradition gives 1199-1278 CE); he affirmed that the world is real, that differences are real and eternal, and that Vishnu is the supreme, independent reality.

## The First Position: Advaita (Shankara)

### The Thesis
- **Brahma satyam jagan mithya**: Brahman is the ultimate reality and the world is mithya, that is, not ultimately real. This formula is traditionally attributed to Shankara's school, and it is a summary rather than a quotation from his commentaries.
- **Maya**: neither simply real nor unreal, which later Advaitins call "anirvacanīya" (indefinable).
- **Liberation (moksha)**: the realization that the individual self (jiva) is Brahman.

### The Arguments
- Scripture: the great sayings (mahavakyas) of the Upanishads, such as "That thou art" (*tat tvam asi*, Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7) and "I am Brahman" (*aham brahmasmi*, Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.4.10), are read as statements of non-difference.
- Experience: dreams appear real while one dreams but are sublated on waking, and Advaita holds that the ordinary world is similarly sublated by liberating knowledge.
- Dialectic: later Advaitins such as Sriharsha (*Khandanakhandakhadya*, 12th century) argued that the notion of difference, and the categories used to define it, cannot be defended without contradiction.

### The Analogies
- The world is superimposed (adhyasa) on Brahman, as a rope seen in dim light is taken for a snake: the error does not touch the rope itself.
- After liberating knowledge (jnana) the appearance of diversity is seen to be mithya.

## The Second Position: Dvaita (Madhva)

### The Thesis
- **Reality of three principles**: Vishnu (God, the sole independent reality), jivas (individual souls) and jada (matter, prakriti), all dependent on God.
- **Pancha-bheda**: five eternal differences (God and soul, God and matter, soul and soul, soul and matter, matter and matter).
- **Liberation**: moksha is eternal bliss in the presence of Vishnu while preserving individual identity.

### The Arguments
- Scripture and perception: Madhva held that perception and the plain sense of scripture show difference, and he read the Upanishadic sayings against the Advaitin reading (for example as *atat tvam asi*, "you are not that").
- Ethics and theology: if the world and the self are not ultimately real, devotion and moral effort lose their basis. This is a recurring objection to Advaita, which Advaitins answer by distinguishing levels of truth.
- Logic: Madhva and his followers argued in works such as the *Mayavada-khandana* that the Advaitin notion of an indefinable illusion is incoherent.

## The Position of Ramanuja (Viśiṣṭādvaita)

- Ramanuja (traditionally 1017-1137 CE) offered a "qualified non-dualism": Brahman is one, but the world and souls are His body (sharira). The world is real and not independent of God.
- For Ramanuja, maya refers to God's creative power and not to illusion, so he rejects Shankara's doctrine of illusion (see con-maya-vedanta).
- Madhva rejected Ramanuja's qualified non-difference as well, and held that God and souls are fully distinct.

## Modern Scholarly Literature on Advaita Ethics

A persistent issue in modern comparative scholarship is whether Advaita's non-dualism can sustain a normative ethics, given its doctrine that the world of plurality is *mithya* (not ultimately real):
- **Paul Hacker and the "Tat Tvam Asi" Ethic**: Paul Hacker (1978) argued that modern interpreters (beginning with Swami Vivekananda and Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan, partly influenced by Arthur Schopenhauer and Paul Deussen) formulated an explicit Advaitic social ethic around the Chandogya Upanishad dictum "That thou art" (*tat tvam asi*)—the idea that one should love others because they are identical with one's own true self. Hacker argued that this universal altruism was a modern neo-Vedantic development rather than a feature of classical Advaita, which focused on ascetic renunciation (*samnyasa*).
- **Wilhelm Halbfass on Dharma and Levels of Truth**: Wilhelm Halbfass (*India and Europe*, 1988) refined this critique by clarifying how classical Advaita integrates ethics through the two levels of truth (*satya-dvaya*): the empirical/practical level (*vyavahara*) and the ultimate level (*paramartha*). On the empirical level, moral duties (*dharma*), social responsibilities, and karma are fully valid and binding; they serve as indispensable preliminary disciplines for purifying the mind (*citta-shuddhi*), even though all distinctions are sublated upon attaining liberating knowledge (*jnana*).
- **Eliot Deutsch and Anantanand Rambachan**: Deutsch (1969) and Rambachan (2006) argue that non-dual realization does not obliterate moral concern, but provides an experiential ground for spontaneous compassion, as the liberated person (*jivanmukta*) sees all beings in the Self and the Self in all beings.

## Why the Debate Matters

- **For theology**: the debate over the relation of God and world (manifestation, body, or independent creation) and the means of liberation (knowledge, devotion and grace, or eternal nearness).
- **For ethics**: how moral action and devotion fit with the claim that the world is mithya, and how Advaita's levels of truth answer the objection.
- **For comparison with Western philosophy**: the debate is sometimes compared with Neoplatonic and Christian discussions of the relation between God and creation (Plotinus, Augustine, Nicholas of Cusa), though the contexts differ.

## The Philosophical Limit

- The debate is sometimes presented as "does the world have reality?", but it is more complex. Shankara does not say the world is nothing, but that it is not the final reality. Madhva does not say the world is God, but that it is God's dependent creation with a real existence.
- The debate was carried on through centuries of polemical literature. On the Advaita side, thinkers such as Vachaspati Mishra, Prakasatman, Sriharsha and Madhusudana Sarasvati developed and defended the system (Madhusudana's *Advaitasiddhi* answers Vyasatirtha's *Nyayamrita*). On the Dvaita side, Jayatirtha (14th century) systematized Madhva's thought, and Vyasatirtha (c. 1460-1539) pressed the critique of Advaita.
