# The Reductionism vs Non-Reductionism Debate in Religious Studies

Can the religious phenomenon be explained by reducing it to non-religious factors (cognitive, social or psychological) as an adequate explanation? Or does religion possess an essence (*sui generis*) that cannot be reduced to anything else and requires understanding from within? This methodological debate has run through comparative religious studies since its foundation. It is the clearest dividing line between the phenomenological current (Otto, Eliade) and the cognitive and social explanatory current (Durkheim, and later Boyer and Barrett).

## The Non-Reductive Position (Sui Generis)

- **Rudolf Otto** described the "numinous" as a primary experience that cannot be reduced to anything else, nor derived from an emotion or concept prior to it.
- **Mircea Eliade** argued that the sacred is a category specific to the consciousness of religious man (*homo religiosus*), which cannot be understood by reducing it to society, economy or psychology. He held that the researcher must study the religious symbol "on its own plane".
- **The methodological argument**: reduction necessarily drops what makes the phenomenon specifically religious. When belief in gods is explained as a by-product of a hyperactive agency-detection mechanism, precisely what matters both to the believer and to the phenomenological researcher disappears from the explanation: the meaning of the relationship with the sacred.

## The Reductive Position

- **Émile Durkheim** opened the door when he explained the sacred as a symbolic representation of society itself.
- **The cognitive science of religion** (Pascal Boyer, Justin Barrett) goes further: religion is a by-product of general evolutionary cognitive mechanisms (agency detection, minimally counter-intuitive concepts) that have no religious specificity in themselves.
- **The counter-methodological argument**: the claim that religion is a "sui generis essence that cannot be reduced" is not a falsifiable hypothesis, but a methodological immunity granted in advance. It removes the religious phenomenon from the scope of the naturalistic scientific explanation to which everything else is subject.

## Attempts at Mediation

Some researchers (such as Ann Taves in *Religious Experience Reconsidered*, 2009) propose dismantling "religion" into components (experience, ritual, belief), each subject to explanation at a different level. This transcends the reduction/non-reduction dichotomy itself, rather than settling it in favour of one of its poles.

Wayne Proudfoot, in *Religious Experience* (1985), distinguished *descriptive* reduction (describing an experience in terms the subject would not use, which he argued misrepresents it) from *explanatory* reduction (explaining it by causes the subject does not recognize), and defended the legitimacy of the latter. Robert Segal, in *Explaining and Interpreting Religion* (1992), argued for the legitimacy of social-scientific explanation of religion as distinct from interpreting its meaning.

## The Debate's Effect on the Field's Method

This disagreement intersects directly with the emic/etic debate. The non-reductive position tends towards a method that approaches the believer's understanding from within (the *epoché* of phenomenology). The reductive position adopts the stance of the external observer, who does not assume either the truth or the particularity of the belief.
