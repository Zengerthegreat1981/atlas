# The Debate in Symbolic Interactionism: Blumer's Interpretive Fluidity versus Stryker's Measurable Structure

A methodological split within the symbolic interactionist tradition itself, between a version that stresses the open-ended, interpretive character of social meaning and another that seeks to reintroduce a testable, measurable structure without abandoning the core of the perspective. The two positions were not a single exchange of replies but a divergence in programme, visible in Herbert Blumer's *Symbolic Interactionism: Perspective and Method* (1969) and Sheldon Stryker's *Symbolic Interactionism: A Social Structural Version* (1980).

## Blumer's Position: Continuous Interpretation Resists Prior Structuring

Blumer, who coined the term "symbolic interactionism" in 1937, built the perspective on three premises: people act toward things on the basis of the meanings those things have for them; these meanings arise out of social interaction; and meanings are handled and modified through an interpretive process used by the person in dealing with the things encountered. Because meaning is continually built and rebuilt in each situation, he held that attempts to fix it in predefined structural categories, such as roles with a settled content, distort the phenomenon they are meant to explain. In "Sociological Analysis and the 'Variable'" (1956) he criticised variable-centred analysis for treating complex interpretive processes as the effects of discrete factors, and he defended exploration and inspection through close, qualitative study as the method suited to the empirical world.

## Stryker's Position: Structure Constrains and Makes Prediction Possible

Stryker argued that society imposes relatively stable structures (roles, statuses, networks of relationships) that limit the openness of interaction and make systematic, empirically testable propositions possible. He developed identity theory, in which the self is composed of a hierarchy of role-based identities, and the "salience hierarchy": the probability that a given identity will be invoked across situations depends on the person's commitment to it, understood as the number and importance of the social relationships that depend on being that kind of person. He treated this as a development of symbolic interactionism rather than a betrayal of it, because it kept the interactionist premises about self and society while specifying how social structure enters the self.

## Impact of the Debate

The divergence is usually described as one of the main internal methodological divisions in symbolic interactionism, producing two currents that continued in parallel: a processual, qualitative current that followed Blumer, and a structural current that followed Stryker and later Peter Burke and Jan Stets. The distinction still marks a line within contemporary social psychology between those who prefer sensitivity to meaning formed in interaction and those who prefer measurement and statistical testing. Critics of the structural version, however, argue that it treats roles and identities as more fixed than a processual view would allow, while critics of the processual version argue that it leaves little room for systematic testing and cumulative theory.

## Measurement Legacy: The Iowa School

Manford Kuhn and his students at the University of Iowa had earlier tried to make interactionist ideas measurable, most visibly through the Twenty Statements Test ("Who Am I?"), developed by Kuhn and Thomas McPartland (1954), in which respondents describe themselves in twenty statements that can then be coded. This approach is usually contrasted with Blumer's Chicago version of the perspective. Stryker's own work, developed over the 1960s and 1970s at Indiana University, shares the Iowa concern with testable propositions, while giving more weight to the interactional and structural determinants of identity than the Iowa approach did.

## Identity Control Theory

Peter Burke later developed identity control theory, in which a person's identity is a set of meanings held as a standard; the person perceives meanings in the situation, compares them with the standard, and acts to reduce any discrepancy, in a feedback loop modelled on control systems theory. Burke and Stets presented identity theory at length in *Identity Theory* (2009), and Stryker and Burke discussed their relationship in "The Past, Present, and Future of an Identity Theory" (*Social Psychology Quarterly*, 2000).
