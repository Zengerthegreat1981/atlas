# Foreign Aid Effectiveness Debate (Sachs vs. Easterly)

The **debate over the effectiveness of foreign aid** is an extended academic and policy controversy within development economics, which crystallized in a particularly clear form after 2005 between two opposing positions represented by the economist Jeffrey Sachs (who advocates a large and coordinated increase in international aid to break the poverty trap) and the economist William Easterly (a sharp critic of the effectiveness of large-scale aid, who warns against repeating its historical failure).

## Sachs's position: breaking the poverty trap with a big push

In his book *The End of Poverty* (2005), Jeffrey Sachs argued that the world's poorest countries, especially in sub-Saharan Africa, are trapped in a structural "poverty trap": income is so low that it does not allow enough saving for the investment in infrastructure, health and education needed to escape this trap on their own, whereas a large, coordinated package of international aid (simultaneous investments in malaria control, agriculture, water and education) could break this vicious circle in one push. He partly draws on the "big push" idea of early development economics (Rosenstein-Rodan, 1943).

## Easterly's position: a repeated historical failure

William Easterly argues that large, centrally designed aid programmes have a weak record in low-income countries, and that the Marshall Plan is a poor analogy: it supported the reconstruction of already industrial economies in Western Europe, not a blueprint for initiating development. He warns that large, poorly targeted aid may reinforce corruption and weaken the accountability of governments to their own taxpayers if finance depends more on donors than on domestic revenue. He criticises confidence that one comprehensive plan, imposed from above, can dismantle a problem as multidimensional as poverty.

## Planners versus searchers

Easterly formulated a central conceptual distinction to characterize this disagreement: "Planners", who assume sufficient prior knowledge of the correct comprehensive solution and seek to impose it from above (the position in which he places Sachs's thesis), and "Searchers", who try small partial solutions and learn gradually from failure and success. The latter is the approach Easterly himself prefers, and he argues that many historical aid failures stem from the dominance of an overconfident "planner" mindset that believes it can predict the course of complex development in advance.

## A third position: the empiricists between the two sides

In their book *Poor Economics* (2011), Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo offered a third position that adopts neither of the opposing overall positions. Instead of debating the overall "size" of aid required, they propose focusing on testing the effectiveness of each specific intervention separately through controlled randomized trials, regardless of one's overall ideological stance on increasing or cutting aid. This methodological position partly intersects with Easterly's scepticism toward ready-made comprehensive solutions, without necessarily adopting his fundamental opposition to increasing the overall volume of aid.

## Questions not yet resolved

The debate remains open with no final academic consensus. Assessments of the effectiveness of specific aid programmes (such as distributing anti-malaria mosquito nets or school feeding programmes) differ depending on the evaluation methodology and the local context studied. The deeper disagreement over the overall volume of aid needed globally and the design of its institutional architecture remains as much a normative and political question as a purely empirical one, reflecting differing positions on the role of the state and international institutions in the development process.

## Applications and debates in the Arab region

Questions of aid effectiveness also feature in development debates in the Arab region, for example in Egypt and Jordan, which have been large recipients of foreign aid. They raise similar debates about the impact of reliance on foreign aid on the independence of national economic decision-making and on the accountability of local governments to their citizens rather than to external donors.

## A direct public debate between the two sides

The disagreement has not been confined to separate publications; it developed into direct public debates and mutual critical articles between Sachs and Easterly in journals and media outlets, in which each criticised the other for oversimplifying a complex empirical reality.

## Institutional dimensions of the debate

The disagreement is part of a wider shift in the aid community toward evaluating the results of individual programmes rather than merely measuring the volume of funds disbursed. Sachs, for his part, directed the UN Millennium Project (2002-2006), which set out how the Millennium Development Goals adopted by the United Nations in 2000 could be achieved.
