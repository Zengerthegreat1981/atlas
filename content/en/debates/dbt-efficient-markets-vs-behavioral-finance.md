# Efficient Market Hypothesis vs. Behavioral Finance

A central debate in financial economics on a question that sounds technical but carries vast consequences for the regulation of financial markets and for investment management: do the prices of financial assets (stocks and bonds) always reflect all available information rationally and instantly (the efficient market hypothesis), or do prices deviate systematically and repeatedly from their "true" value because of collective psychological biases among investors (behavioural finance)?

## The Efficient Market Hypothesis: No Room for Systematic Profit

Eugene Fama gave the efficient market hypothesis its canonical statement in his review article of 1970 (building on earlier work by Paul Samuelson, Harry Roberts and others). It holds that the prices of financial assets in a deep competitive market fully and instantly reflect all available information (in three degrees, weak, semi-strong and strong, depending on the type of information). It is therefore impossible for any investor to achieve returns that systematically and consistently exceed the market without bearing additional risk, because any new piece of information is reflected immediately in the price as soon as it appears, through the competition of thousands of informed investors. This hypothesis rests on the assumption that investors, taken together, are rational enough to correct any price deviation from the fundamental value of an asset quickly, through arbitrage operations.

## The Behavioural Finance Critique: Systematic Collective Biases

Robert Shiller, Richard Thaler and others, drawing on the heuristics-and-biases research and prospect theory (1979) of Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky in cognitive psychology, developed a methodological critique of the efficient market hypothesis that founded the field of "behavioural finance." They argued that real investors are not fully rational. Instead, they are subject to systematic and recurrent psychological biases (overconfidence, confirmation bias, herd behaviour, loss aversion). They further argued that these biases are not necessarily eliminated by statistically aggregating large numbers of investors, because they may be correlated and shared among them (such as waves of collective optimism or panic). As a result, they are reflected in market prices themselves rather than being corrected automatically by arbitrage. Robert Shiller argued in 1981 that stock-price volatility is much greater than subsequent changes in dividends can justify under a simple present-value model with a constant discount rate. He read the excess volatility as evidence of a speculative component in prices. The result depends on that model of discount rates; defenders of efficiency replied that changing risk premia can rationalise large price movements.

## Financial Bubbles as a Testing Ground

Recurrent financial bubbles throughout history (the railway bubble of the nineteenth century, the dot-com bubble of the late 1990s, and the American mortgage crisis that led to the global financial crisis of 2008) have been regarded as a central testing ground for the debate. Proponents of behavioural finance see in these bubbles clear evidence of extended collective price deviations that are difficult to explain with a purely rational model. Some supporters of market efficiency (Eugene Fama himself among them) argue that what looks like a "bubble" in hindsight may at the time have been a rational response to available information and reasonable expectations that later proved mistaken. They also hold that identifying a "bubble" in advance, with sufficient confidence, is much harder in practice than identifying it retrospectively after it has collapsed.

## The Controversial Shared Nobel Prize of 2013

The 2013 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences was shared by Eugene Fama, a leading exponent of the efficient-market hypothesis, Robert Shiller, a leading critic of it, and Lars Peter Hansen. The citation was for empirical analysis of asset prices, not an official verdict that the two theories are equally true. Commentary often read the shared prize that way. A common working compromise, which neither author fully accepts as a settlement, is that near-term price changes are hard to forecast from public information, while valuation ratios have some association with subsequent multi-year returns.

## Regulatory and Practical Implications

The debate carries direct regulatory consequences. If markets are fully efficient, excessive government regulation of financial markets is unnecessary and may even be harmful, since it impedes the flow of information; transparency and the prevention of fraud would then suffice. If markets are subject to systematic collective behavioural deviations, by contrast, this justifies additional precautionary regulatory instruments (such as counter-cyclical bank capital requirements, or limits on excessive financial leverage) to curb the build-up of systemic risks arising from collective speculative bubbles that market mechanisms alone may struggle to correct in time.

## Its Methodological Significance: The Limits of Collective Rationality

This debate represents one of the most important contemporary tests of the assumption of "rationality" on which most neoclassical economics was built throughout the twentieth century. It poses a question deeper than asset pricing alone: do the individual rationality assumed in standard economic models hold up when many individuals gather in a single market governed by dynamics of mutual expectations and collective behavioural contagion? Or does the gathering itself produce collective behaviour that is less rational than the behaviour of the individuals assumed to act in isolation?
