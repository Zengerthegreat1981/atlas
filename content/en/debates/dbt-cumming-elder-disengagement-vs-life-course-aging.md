# The Aging Theory Debate: Cumming's Functionalist Disengagement Theory vs. Elder's Life Course Perspective

The shift from the functionalist "disengagement theory" to the "life course perspective" is a significant theoretical transformation in the sociology of aging, turning the question from "what is the social function of aging?" to "how is the life course shaped by the interaction of historical context, social ties and individual agency?" The two works are not a direct exchange of replies; the debate is a reconstruction of a broader movement in the field.

## Cumming's Disengagement Theory

Elaine Cumming and William E. Henry proposed in *Growing Old: The Process of Disengagement* (1961), drawing on the Kansas City Study of Adult Life, that a gradual, mutual withdrawal between the aging person and society is a natural and functional process: it relieves the individual of the pressure of role expectations and lets society transfer roles smoothly to younger people as death approaches. The theory was functionalist in orientation and presented disengagement as a universal process, though it allowed for variation in its timing and form, and it suggested that satisfaction in old age could accompany reduced social involvement.

## Criticism and the Activity Alternative

The theory drew wide and sharp criticism. Critics held that it risked justifying the social marginalisation of older people as natural and desirable, that it generalised from one sample in one American city, and that it ignored variation by class, gender, health and cohort. Robert Havighurst and colleagues had already advanced a different view, usually called activity theory, in which life satisfaction in later life is associated with remaining active and replacing lost roles, and empirical tests in the 1960s and 1970s gave mixed support to both positions. Arlie Hochschild's "Disengagement Theory: A Critique and Proposal" (*American Sociological Review*, 1975) argued that the theory was not adequately testable and neglected the social conditions that produce withdrawal. Both theories shared the assumption of a single model of "successful" aging, which the life course perspective later questioned.

## Elder's Life Course Perspective

Glen H. Elder Jr.'s *Children of the Great Depression* (1974) analysed members of the Oakland Growth Study, born in 1920–21, following them from childhood through the economic hardship of the 1930s into adulthood. It showed that the effects of economic deprivation depended on the age at which children experienced it, on their families' situation, and on how families adapted. From this and later work Elder articulated the principles of the life course perspective: the importance of historical time and place, the timing of lives (when events occur in a person's life), linked lives (interdependence within family and social networks), and human agency within constraints. The perspective rejects a single functional timetable of aging and treats later life as the outcome of earlier trajectories and historical context.

## Impact

The life course perspective became a leading framework in the sociology of age and generations, with greater attention to inequality, cohort differences and the interdependence of lives, and disengagement theory is now treated mainly as a historical landmark whose claims have been largely abandoned in their strong form.
