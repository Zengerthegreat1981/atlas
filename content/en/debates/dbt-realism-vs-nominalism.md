# Realism versus Nominalism: Universals — Real or Just Names?

A pivotal debate that occupied Latin scholasticism for centuries, with roots in the Greeks (Plato vs. Aristotle). The medieval discussion was sparked by Boethius's Latin translation of and commentaries on Porphyry's *Isagoge*, which raised the question of whether genera and species exist in themselves or only in thought. The question: does "humanity" (for example) have a real existence independent of individuals?

## The First Position: Realism — Universals Are Real

- **Extreme realism (Platonism)**: universals exist "before" things, in the world of Forms. "Humanity" is an ideal model in which every individual participates.
- **Moderate realism (Aristotelianism)**: universals are "in" things (Universalia in re), but have a mental existence (abstraction).
- **Representatives**: William of Champeaux (an early extreme realist), Anselm of Canterbury, Thomas Aquinas (moderate realism), Duns Scotus.
- **The theological stake**: Anselm of Canterbury argued against Roscelin that denying real universals threatens the unity of the divine essence in the Trinity. Roscelin's view was condemned at the Council of Soissons (1092) as implying three gods.

## The Second Position: Nominalism — Universals Are Only Names

- **Strict nominalism**: universals are "sounds" (Flatus vocis) with no reality at all.
- **Conceptual nominalism (Conceptualism)**: universals are "concepts" in the mind, not entities in the world.
- **Representatives**: Roscelin of Compiègne, Peter Abelard (often described as a moderate nominalist or conceptualist, who held that universal words, not things, are predicated of many), William of Ockham.
- **The argument**: the great plurality (individual substances) does not require a "substance" higher than them.

## The Early Twelfth-Century Dispute

Peter Abelard, in his *Historia calamitatum*, recounts that he challenged his teacher William of Champeaux in Paris in the early twelfth century over his realism, and that Champeaux modified his doctrine. Champeaux died c. 1121–1122.

## Major Points of Difference

- **The concept of substance**: the realists said "substance = a common essence," the nominalists said "substance = an individual."
- **The concept of property**: the realists said "property = a subsidiary entity," the nominalists held that whatever properties exist are themselves individual (for Ockham, particular qualities such as this whiteness), not entities shared by many things.
- **In theology**: the Trinity was the classic test case. If a shared divine nature is only a name, the three persons risk becoming three individuals; the realists therefore insisted on a real common nature.

## Why Does the Debate Matter?

1. **Early modern philosophy**: Hobbes held a nominalist view of general terms, and Locke, Berkeley, and Hume, in different ways, rejected real universals, shaping British empiricism; Locke explained general terms through abstract general ideas, which Berkeley and Hume rejected.
2. **Ockham's legacy**: Ockham's parsimony ("Ockham's razor") and logic of terms influenced late medieval and early modern thought.
3. **Analytic philosophy**: in the 20th century, Russell (*The Problems of Philosophy*, 1912) defended universals, while Goodman and Quine argued for nominalism in a joint paper of 1947 (Quine later accepted abstract classes), and D. M. Armstrong later defended realism about universals.
4. **A comparison with Islamic theology**: the debate over divine attributes is sometimes compared loosely to it, since the Mu'tazila denied attributes distinct from God's essence while the Ash'aris affirmed real attributes that are "neither identical with nor other than" the essence. The analogy is imperfect, as the Islamic discussion has its own terms.
