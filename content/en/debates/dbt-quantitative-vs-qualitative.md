# Quantitative versus Qualitative

The quantitative–qualitative debate is the oldest and most persistent methodological argument in sociology: should the discipline count social life, or understand it from the inside? Quantitative sociology treats social phenomena as measurable variables whose relationships can be tested across large populations; qualitative sociology treats them as meaningful action that must be interpreted in context. The disagreement is not only about technique — surveys versus interviews — but about what social reality is and what kind of knowledge about it counts as scientific. It has shaped departments, journals, funding, and the training of generations of sociologists, and although the fiercest version of the argument has faded, its terms still organise how sociologists justify their methods.

## The Quantitative Position

The quantitative case begins with Émile Durkheim, who in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) insisted that social facts be treated as things and studied by their external regularities. His study of suicide (1897) showed that even the most private act varies systematically with religion, marital status, and economic crisis — a pattern no amount of individual biography could reveal. The argument was taken up by Paul Lazarsfeld and the survey tradition: standardised questions, representative samples, and statistical analysis let sociologists estimate how widespread a pattern is, how strongly variables are associated, and whether a finding is likely to hold beyond the cases observed. Measurement, replication, and generalisation are the virtues claimed here. Its critics reply that counting presupposes that the categories counted are already understood, and that a correlation between two variables explains nothing until someone says what the people involved meant by their actions.

## The Qualitative Position

The qualitative case begins from the opposite premise, articulated by Wilhelm Dilthey in his *Introduction to the Human Sciences* (1883): the human sciences study meaning, and meaning is grasped by understanding, not by measurement. Max Weber made this the core of sociology, defining it as the interpretive understanding of social action. Herbert Blumer's *Symbolic Interactionism* (1969) argued that people act toward things on the basis of the meanings those things have for them, and that such meanings are negotiated in interaction — so the sociologist must get close enough to see them being made. Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss's *The Discovery of Grounded Theory* (1967) gave this stance a systematic method: theory built upward from field notes and interviews rather than imposed from above. Ethnography, participant observation, and the in-depth interview became its instruments. The claim is depth over breadth: a single setting studied well can reveal mechanisms that a survey can only register as a statistical residue.

## The Paradigm Wars

By the 1980s the disagreement had hardened into what later writers called the paradigm wars; Alan Bryman's *Quantity and Quality in Social Research* (1988) analysed the claim, common at the time, that the two approaches rested on incompatible philosophies, so that mixing them was incoherent. Quantitative work was accused of positivism, of treating people as objects; qualitative work was accused of subjectivism, of producing vivid stories that could not be checked. The strongest form of the incompatibility thesis held that each method carried its own epistemology and that a researcher had to choose. Pragmatists replied that methods are tools, not worldviews, and that the choice should follow the question. Norman Denzin argued for triangulation — using several methods so that the weaknesses of one are covered by the strengths of another — while the mixed-methods movement, given textbook form in the early 2000s by works such as John Creswell's *Research Design* (2003), made combination itself a respectable design rather than a compromise.

## Contemporary Status

Today the war is largely over and the division of labour has settled into something more pragmatic. Mixed-methods research is mainstream in sociology, public health, and evaluation studies, and most large projects combine a survey with interviews or fieldwork. Yet the underlying philosophical disagreement has not disappeared; it has moved. The rise of computational sociology and "big data" has revived the quantitative ambition on a new scale, with critics warning that enormous datasets reproduce the old positivist error of mistaking pattern for explanation. Meanwhile qualitative research has professionalised its own standards of rigour — systematic coding, software-assisted analysis, and explicit criteria for trustworthiness — partly in answer to the charge of anecdotalism. The debate now turns less on which method is superior than on how each kind of evidence warrants its claims, and on whether a single study can honestly hold both logics at once. The positions remain distinct, but the most productive sociology is usually bilingual.

The choice between the two approaches is also a choice about what a study can claim. A survey can show that unemployment predicts poor health across a population, but it cannot show how unemployment is lived, what it means to lose a job in a particular community, or why some people cope and others do not. An ethnography can show all of that, but it cannot show how common the pattern is. Each method therefore answers a different question well and the other badly, and much of the debate's heat came from treating a division of labour as a contest for the soul of the discipline.
