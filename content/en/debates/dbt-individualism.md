# The Individualism Critique

Existential therapy speaks of freedom, choice, and responsibility — and addresses an individual with no class, no gender, no colour, and no legal status. The individualism critique, the strongest contemporary criticism of the school, charges that this language turns from liberation into blame the moment the client is under the weight of poverty, discrimination, or violence. The criticism comes from inside the tradition as much as from outside it, and it asks whether a therapy built on the free individual can see power at all. It is the debate that connects existential therapy to feminism, liberation psychology, and critical theory — and the one the school has answered least completely.

## The Charge

The charge is simple and concrete. "You still have a choice" said to an undocumented migrant worker, or "you are responsible for your attitude" said to a woman in a violent relationship: both sentences are theoretically correct and practically harmful in those positions. The language of freedom presupposes a subject who can act, and when the subject cannot — because the state, the labour market, or a partner has closed the options — the language does not describe the situation but misdescribes it, and the misdescription lands on the client as guilt. The critique is not that freedom is unreal, but that freedom is always exercised inside conditions, and that a therapy which talks about the first while ignoring the second becomes an instrument of the status quo. The client is told to take responsibility for circumstances she did not make, and the structures that made them escape examination.

## The Reply from Inside the Tradition

The reply from inside the tradition points out that Simone de Beauvoir said this in the philosophical origin itself. In *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947), Beauvoir argued that freedom is entangled with the freedom of others, and that the lived situation constrains freedom in fact, not only in theory: oppression is precisely the condition in which a person's freedom is systematically denied by others. The problem, on this reading, is not in the philosophy but in what was dropped when it was carried into the American clinic, where the language of choice was separated from Beauvoir's analysis of situation and oppression. A second, institutional reply comes from the British current: van Deurzen's four dimensions of existence make the social dimension one of four mandatory dimensions of every case formulation, so that class, gender, and culture are built into the framework rather than added as an afterthought.

## A Parallel Critique from Outside

A parallel theoretical critique comes from outside the school. Herbert Marcuse argued in *One-Dimensional Man* (1964) that the liberal individualism which addresses a "free individual who chooses" is itself an ideological product of an advanced industrial society that absorbs criticism and converts it into individual consumption — so that inviting the client to "take responsibility" complements, rather than replaces, the social structure of domination that produced the suffering in the first place. Liberation psychology, developed by Ignacio Martín-Baró in El Salvador in the 1980s, made the same point from the side of practice: a psychology that treats the individual as the unit of change, in a context of political violence and structural poverty, mislocates both the problem and the remedy. These critiques do not belong to existential therapy, but they set the standard against which its own response is judged.

## What Remains Unresolved

No developed existential tradition yet engages social structure with the seriousness that liberation psychology or critical currents do, and the dialogue between them is still at its beginning. The British current's social dimension is the clearest structural reform inside the school, but it is a dimension of assessment, not a theory of power, and it does not by itself tell the therapist how to work with a client whose unfreedom is produced by institutions rather than by her own avoidance. The practical stakes are immediate: whether the therapist asks "what do you choose to do about your situation?" without questioning the situation itself, or asks "who made this situation, and who benefits from your remaining powerless inside it?" as a first step. That is a fundamental difference in every session with clients from marginalised groups, and it is the question on which the future credibility of existential therapy with those clients depends.

The critique also has a practical corollary for training. A therapist trained only in the language of choice and responsibility will hear a client's account of oppression as a description of attitude — as something to be reframed — rather than as a report about the world. Training that takes the critique seriously therefore has to teach something existential programmes have rarely taught: how to recognise structural constraint, how to distinguish a client's avoidance from a real absence of options, and when the appropriate response is not interpretation but advocacy, referral, or solidarity. Until that teaching exists, the individualism critique will keep returning, because each new generation of therapists will reproduce the same blind spot in the consulting room.
