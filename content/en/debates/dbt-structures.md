# Structures or None?

Are there fixed existential structures? The dispute takes place inside the British current itself, and though it looks technical, its consequences run deep. The question is whether human existence has general structures — dimensions or concerns that every person lives in — whose knowledge makes training, guidance, and organisation possible, or whether any general structure imposed on experience betrays the phenomenological method at its root. The argument explains why two British texts from the same school can read as if they came from two different schools, and why it is so difficult to write a single introduction to the British current.

## The First Position — Structures Exist

The first position holds that there are general structures of human existence, and that knowing them is what makes therapy teachable. Emmy van Deurzen formulated the four dimensions of existence — physical, social, personal, and spiritual — in *Existential Counselling in Practice* (1988; 3rd ed. as *Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy in Practice*, 2012) as a systematic training tool, giving new therapists a map of the territories in which a client's difficulties can appear. Irvin Yalom, in *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980), offered a parallel structure: four ultimate concerns — death, freedom, isolation, and meaninglessness — that every person must confront. Karl Jaspers, earlier, had described four limit situations (*Grenzsituationen*) — death, suffering, struggle, and guilt — in *Philosophy* (1932) as the boundaries where ordinary life breaks down. In each version, the claim is the same: beneath cultural and personal variation, human beings face the same givens, and a therapist who knows them can orient herself, teach others, and compare cases.

## The Second Position — No Structures

The second position holds that any general structure imposed on experience betrays the phenomenological method itself. Ernesto Spinelli, in *Practising Existential Psychotherapy: The Relational World* (2007), argues that therapy is entirely relational: what matters is what appears in this relationship, now, without a prior map. A four-dimensional scheme, however useful, tells the therapist what to look for before the client has spoken, and thereby replaces description with classification. The strict phenomenologists in the British current insist that the therapist must stay with what shows itself in this particular encounter, and that the work is relational through and through. The gain is fidelity to experience in its singularity; the cost is that teaching becomes difficult, because there is no stable content to transmit, and comparison becomes nearly impossible, because there are no fixed categories to compare.

## The Real Question Beneath It

The underlying question is whether systematic teaching is possible without classification. If the answer is no, then the school is condemned to be an individual art that cannot be passed on — each therapist must reinvent it alone. If the answer is yes, then the school has accepted some measure of the very "imposition" it criticises in other approaches. The dispute also has a cross-cultural edge: those who affirm fixed structures assume that a framework such as Yalom's four concerns can travel across cultures and be measured comparatively, while those who reject structures regard any such comparison as a distortion of local experience. Research on existential anxiety or meaning across cultures is therefore possible on one side of the debate and incoherent on the other.

## Contemporary Status

The dispute remains unresolved, and it structures the British current from within. Van Deurzen's dimensional model dominates training, because training needs a curriculum; Spinelli's relational model dominates the theoretical conscience of the school, because it states most purely what the phenomenology was meant to be. Practitioners typically move between the two, using dimensions as a background orientation while insisting that no dimension be imposed on a particular client. The tension is productive rather than paralysing: it keeps the school honest about the gap between its maps and the territory, and it explains why the British current, more than any other, has produced both the most systematic textbooks and the most radical critiques of systematisation in existential therapy.

The structures debate also decides what a textbook of existential therapy can be. A book organised around fixed dimensions can be written once and taught everywhere, with exercises, case examples, and assessment criteria attached to each dimension — which is why van Deurzen's textbook has run through three editions and become a widely used training text. A book faithful to the no-structures position cannot offer that: Spinelli's work is instead a sustained reflection on the therapeutic relationship. The difference is visible on the shelf, and it is one reason the two authors, though colleagues within the same British current, are cited for opposite lessons about what existential therapy is.
