# Colonial-Site Ethnography vs. Domestic Lived Religion

This entry sets out a methodological and political contrast between two generations of religious ethnography. It is an analytical contrast, informed by Meredith McGuire's survey *Lived Religion* (2008) and the lived-religion literature, rather than a documented dispute between individuals; the two generations are separated in time and never debated one another directly.

## First Generation: The Distant Colonial Site

Bronisław Malinowski and E. E. Evans-Pritchard (see `thk-malinowski` and `thk-evans-pritchard`) applied the method of participant observation to societies classified from the position of the European researcher as "other" geographically, culturally and politically: the Trobriand Islands under Australian-administered colonial rule in Papua, and the Azande and Nuer in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan. The researcher there was an outsider coming from the center of the empire to its periphery, and his relationship to the subject of study, however deep his fieldwork immersion, was conditioned by the colonial power structure that made his access possible in the first place.

## Second Generation: The Domestic "Lived Religion"

Robert Orsi (see `thk-robert-orsi`) transferred the same ethnographic method (long immersion, participant observation) to within the society to which the researcher himself belongs or directly adjoins: Italian Catholic immigrants in East Harlem, New York. The subject of study was no longer a distant "other" to be discovered, but part of the contemporary Western urban fabric itself, from which the academic field addresses its audience. David D. Hall (see `thk-david-d-hall`) paralleled this shift from a historical angle, applying a similar attention to ordinary practice to colonial New England religion (as in *Worlds of Wonder, Days of Judgment*, 1989) through archival sources instead of the living field.

## What Actually Changes?

The difference is not limited to geography. It touches three interrelated methodological dimensions, which the lived-religion literature (including McGuire) discusses:

- **Balances of field power**: In the first generation, the epistemic and political authority was structurally tilted toward the researcher coming from the colonial center; in the second generation, the researcher often shares with the subject of study a partial class, religious or ethnic affiliation (Orsi himself is of Italian Catholic origin).
- **The object of observation**: The first generation focused on complete belief systems and institutions (magic, revelation, the structure of ritual kinship); the second generation focuses on the everyday informal bodily practice of ordinary believers (household statues, vows, popular processions), apart from the official written doctrine. McGuire frames this as "lived religion," as distinct from religion as prescribed by institutions.
- **Default neutrality**: The first generation largely assumed that a stable ethnographic distance could be achieved through systematic immersion. The second generation explicitly acknowledges that the researcher's position in relation to his subject, not merely the length of his field stay, is a methodological variable that cannot be neutralized. This keeps the question of insider/outsider (see `con-emic-etic-study-of-religion`) open at a deeper level in the second generation, not less so.

## Beyond Europe and America: South Asian Cases

Ethnography of religion in South Asia shows that the two-generation contrast cannot simply be transferred. Here the region once studied under colonial rule is also home to many of its ethnographers, and the boundary between distant and domestic is blurred. Kirin Narayan, whose *Storytellers, Saints, and Scoundrels* (1989) examines a Hindu holy man's teaching through stories in western India, later questioned the opposition between "native" and outside anthropologists (1993), arguing that every researcher holds shifting identities shaped by class, education, gender and family ties. Joyce Flueckiger's *In Amma's Healing Room* (2006), a study of a Muslim woman healer in Hyderabad, attends to "vernacular" Islam as practised locally, across lines of religious affiliation, while the author writes as an outsider to that community. The collection *Popular Christianity in India* (2002), edited by Selva Raj and Corinne Dempsey, likewise examines practice that does not always follow institutional prescription. The shared interest in everyday religion travels well; the map of centre and periphery does not. Power relations must be identified case by case, including those of caste, which the colonial/domestic pairing does not capture.

## Impact on the Field

The second generation did not abolish the method of the first generation but absorbed it and broadened its scope. The field of religious ethnography today extends from the classical studies of non-Western societies (continued through later schools such as the study of traditional African religions) to the ethnography of contemporary Western urban societies themselves, with a growing critical awareness of the power structure inherent in the act of observation. "Lived religion" has since become a recognized approach within the study of religion.
