# The Debate on Human Nature in Early Confucianism: Mencius's Goodness against Xunzi's Badness

A classic dispute in early Confucianism (fourth to third century BCE): is human nature good in its origin or inclined toward evil? The debate remains open after 23 centuries, and formed a dividing point between the "Confucianism of cultivation" (Mencius) and the "Confucianism of external training" (Xunzi). The common root of both sides is the deliberately ambiguous saying of **Confucius** in the *Analects* (論語, 17:2): "By nature people are close to one another; by practice they grow far apart" (性相近也、習相遠也), a statement that did not settle the direction, so later Confucian thinkers differed sharply in interpreting it into opposing directions.

## The First Position: Good Nature (Mencius)

Mencius (Mengzi) holds that the "heart of compassion" (惻隱之心 cèyǐn zhī xīn), that is, the spontaneous response to the sight of others' pain, is conclusive evidence that the "four sprouts" (sìduān 四端) of goodness—compassion (sprout of benevolence, 仁 *rén*), shame and dislike (sprout of righteousness, 義 *yì*), deference and modesty (sprout of ritual propriety, 禮 *lǐ*), and the sense of right and wrong (sprout of wisdom, 智 *zhì*)—are **innate in every human being**. Mencius illustrates this with the case of a person who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well and feels alarm and compassion without calculation (2A:6). These sprouts are not imposed "from outside" but belong to the makeup of the human being; they can be damaged or stunted by a harsh environment and neglect, as the trees of Ox Mountain are stripped by axes and grazing (6A:8). Virtue is therefore the **cultivation** of an existing disposition rather than the acquisition of something foreign. Morality begins from within, and society supports it.

### An intermediate position: Gaozi

Mencius's own opponent in Book 6A, Gaozi, held that human nature is morally neutral, like a willow that can be shaped into cups and bowls, or like water that flows east or west as it is channeled. He also argued that benevolence is internal while righteousness is external. Mencius replied that water has an inherent tendency to flow downward, and that moral direction is likewise native to human nature. This exchange shows that the debate predates Xunzi and was not a simple two-way split.

## The Second Position: Nature Inclined to Evil (Xunzi)

Xunzi holds that a human being is born with "sensory desires" (欲 yù) which, if left to themselves, collide with the interests of others. Left unchecked, these desires lead to strife and disorder, so for Xunzi human nature is "bad" (惡 è) in the sense of being disorderly when unregulated. For him, goodness is the product of conscious effort (偽 wěi, often translated "artifice"): nature supplies the raw material, and ritual (禮), teachers, law and custom shape it. The heart-mind (心 xīn) can recognize and affirm the Way, but this capacity must be trained through sustained learning and discipline.

## Why This Debate Matters

- **Educationally**: Mencius supports "education based on encouraging natural inclination" (growth). Xunzi supports "education based on discipline" (shaping).
- **Politically**: Mencius supports "moral legitimacy" (a ruler who loses the hearts of the people loses the mandate of Heaven). Xunzi supports "rule by law and custom" (closer to Legalism).
- **Theologically**: in the twelfth century Zhu Xi made Mencius the orthodox line of the Confucian tradition (the *Mencius* is one of his "Four Books"), while Xunzi was marginalized, a verdict that modern scholarship has partly reconsidered.
- **Intersections**: Both thinkers hold that people can become sages through learning and cultivation (Xunzi explicitly says that "the person in the street can become a Yu"); the difference lies in whether the moral direction is rooted in native dispositions or must be built from outside.
