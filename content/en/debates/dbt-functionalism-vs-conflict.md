# Functionalism versus Conflict

Functionalism and conflict theory are the two great rival answers to sociology's most basic question: what holds society together, and what tears it apart? Functionalists see society as a system of interdependent parts, held together by shared values and mutual need. Conflict theorists see society as an arena of competing groups, held together — when it is held together at all — by power, coercion, and temporary compromise. This entry treats the dispute as a debate between theoretical positions, not as a survey of either school: the Durkheimian and Parsonian tradition on one side, and the Marxian and conflict tradition on the other.

## The Functionalist Position: Integration through Shared Values

Functionalism descends from Émile Durkheim, who argued in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) that modern societies are integrated not by likeness but by interdependence: the division of labour makes people need one another, and shared moral sentiments — the collective conscience — give that interdependence a binding force. Talcott Parsons gave this vision its most systematic form in *The Social System* (1951). For Parsons, a social system must solve four functional problems — adaptation, goal attainment, integration, and pattern maintenance (the AGIL scheme he elaborated in the early 1950s) — and institutions persist because they contribute to these functions. Socialisation and social control transmit shared values from one generation to the next, so order is ultimately a moral achievement: people obey norms because they have internalised them, not because they are forced to. Robert Merton, in *Social Theory and Social Structure* (1949), made functionalism more flexible and empirical. He distinguished manifest functions (intended consequences) from latent functions (unintended ones), introduced the concept of dysfunction, and insisted that not everything that exists is functional for everyone — a qualification that kept functionalism honest without abandoning its core claim that societies are integrated wholes.

## The Conflict Position: Order through Power

Conflict theory begins where functionalism ends: with the observation that societies are divided by unequal interests, and that what looks like consensus is often domination. Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, in *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) and in *Capital* (1867), argued that the history of class society is the history of class struggle, and that the state, law, and dominant ideas serve the interests of the ruling class. Mid-twentieth-century conflict theorists developed this insight for societies that did not fit Marx's revolutionary script. Ralf Dahrendorf, in *Class and Class Conflict in Industrial Society* (1959), argued that conflict arises not only from property but from authority: in every organisation, some command and others obey, so conflict between imperatively coordinated groups is built into social structure. Lewis Coser, in *The Functions of Social Conflict* (1956), showed that conflict is not simply destructive — it clarifies group boundaries, tests the strength of norms, and can force change — while still treating conflict as one process among others within a functioning social system. C. Wright Mills, in *The Power Elite* (1956), applied the conflict lens to American institutions, arguing that political, military, and corporate elites form an interlocking power elite whose shared interests shape policy. For conflict theorists, order is a temporary settlement imposed by the powerful, and the sociologist's task is to show whose interests it serves.

## Arguments and Counter-Arguments

Each side charges the other with a fatal blind spot. Functionalists argue that conflict theory cannot explain stability: if society is nothing but competing interests, why do institutions endure, why do people follow rules that disadvantage them, and why does everyday life proceed in an orderly fashion? They also argue that conflict theory reduces everything to power, ignoring the genuine moral consensus that binds families, professions, and nations. Conflict theorists reply that functionalism is inherently conservative: by treating existing institutions as functional, it legitimises them, and by labelling protest as dysfunction, it turns the sociologist into an apologist for the status quo. They point out that "shared values" are often the values of the dominant group, imposed through schools, media, and law, and that functionalism has no convincing account of how such values came to be shared in the first place. Randall Collins, in *Conflict Sociology* (1975), sharpened the conflict case by grounding it in everyday interaction: status, resources, and emotional energy are contested in every encounter, so conflict is not an occasional breakdown of order but the routine texture of social life.

## Contemporary Status

Neither pure functionalism nor pure conflict theory survives as a dominant paradigm today, but the debate they staged still structures the field. Functionalism's lasting contribution is the question of integration: how do complex, differentiated societies hold together? Its vocabulary — functions, dysfunctions, latent consequences — remains part of everyday sociological language, and neo-functionalists have revived Parsons's concern with values while dropping his assumption of smooth equilibrium. Conflict theory's lasting contribution is the question of power: who benefits from a given arrangement, and what would have to change for the losers to gain? That question now runs through feminist theory, race theory, and the study of organisations and states, even where Marx is not cited. Contemporary theory typically combines the two lenses: a welfare state, for example, can be analysed both as a functional mechanism that integrates society and as a product of class struggle that serves some interests better than others. The debate endures because both insights are true at once — societies are integrated and divided, consensual and coercive — and the task of sociology is to show how, in each case, the two are connected.
