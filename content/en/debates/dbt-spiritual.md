# The Spiritual and the Secular

Does existential therapy have a spiritual dimension, or does admitting one betray the project? The dispute weighs heavier in the Arab context than in the context where it arose — because the school grew up in a secular setting where the question was optional, and is carried into a setting where a living religious framework is the norm rather than the exception.

## The case for opening

Viktor Frankl gives the human being a spiritual dimension of its own, one that does not fall ill and cannot be reduced to the psychological and the bodily. This is the core of his break with Freud, and the foundation of the meaning-centred school he built after his experience in the Nazi concentration camps, set out in *Man's Search for Meaning* (German original 1946). For Frankl, the spiritual dimension is where freedom, responsibility, and the search for meaning live; to ignore it is to amputate the person.

Emmy van Deurzen reaches a similar place from a different direction: her four dimensions of existence include a fourth, spiritual dimension — values, belief, and larger meaning — alongside the physical, social, and personal. Both hold that ignoring this dimension cuts something essential out of the human being, and that a therapy which cannot speak of values and ultimate meaning cannot speak of the things that actually bring people to therapy.

Paul Tillich belongs on this side too: in *The Courage to Be* (1952) he treated faith as a matter of ultimate concern, and described anxiety in three forms — of death, of meaninglessness, and of guilt.

## The case for refusal

Existential philosophy in its origins arose against the collapse of metaphysical guarantees. Sartre's *Being and Nothingness* (1943) makes the point in its starkest form: existence precedes essence, there is no given human nature and no transcendent guarantor, and the human being is condemned to freedom. From this side, reintroducing transcendence through the back door of a "spiritual dimension" empties the project — it restores, in therapeutic dress, exactly the guarantees the philosophy set out to live without.

The refusal is not hostility to religion as a human fact. It is a claim about method: the therapist's task is to stay with the client's freedom and finitude, not to supply a framework that resolves them. A spiritual dimension, on this view, risks turning therapy into pastoral care, and the therapist into a guide towards meanings the client has not chosen.

## What it changes in the room

Three very different practices branch out of this dispute. May the therapist work on the client's religious belief as a resource — drawing on prayer, scripture, or communal belonging as genuine therapeutic material? Or treat it as any other content, neither privileged nor excluded, describing it phenomenologically like everything else? Or avoid it, steering the work back to secular ground whenever belief enters the room? A therapist's answer to the philosophical question decides which of these three they actually do, often without noticing the decision has been made.

## Why it concerns us more

The school arose in a secular context where the question was optional, and it is carried into a context where the religious frame is the original setting, not the exception. Importing the dispute unchanged, without rethinking it, is exactly what this Atlas warns against. A tradition of this question already exists here: al-Ghazali, in *Ihya Ulum al-Din* (late eleventh century), treated the examination of the soul and its diseases of the heart as an inseparable part of the religious system, not an optional addition — the reverse of the order Frankl started from.

## What has not yet been written

There is no sufficient systematic work on how existential therapy is practised inside a living religious framework rather than as a cultural background. That is a gap an Arab researcher could fill — by describing what actually happens when therapist and client share a religious world and work existentially within it, and what changes when they do not share it.

## What follows in practice

If you favour spiritual openness (Frankl, Tillich, Marcel), you allow the religious and spiritual dimension to be a legitimate source of meaning in the session, and you let a devout client use their framework of faith as an authentic therapeutic resource rather than a topic to be set aside or handled with cold clinical neutrality. This suits many clients with strong religious backgrounds, but it risks the therapist sliding — deliberately or not — into a preaching or missionary stance that imposes or favours their own religious outlook.

If you favour the strict secular position (Sartre and most of the French current), you keep complete neutrality towards religious belief and treat meaning as a purely human construction, independent of any transcendent reference. This protects against imposing any particular religious frame in a religiously diverse therapeutic setting. The risk is that a devout client feels their most important frame of knowledge is excluded or treated with implicit doubt, which weakens the therapeutic alliance.

In the Arab context in particular, this dispute is the most practically urgent one for an Arab therapist, since the great majority of clients carry an active religious frame in their daily lives — and adopting the strict secular position without awareness of this context may distance the therapist from their clients more than it brings them closer.
