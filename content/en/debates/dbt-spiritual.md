# The Spiritual and the Secular

Does existential therapy possess an inherent spiritual dimension, or does admitting one compromise the existential project? While existential psychotherapy developed largely within European secular and post-Christian contexts, its cross-cultural application brings it into dialogue with cultures and communities where active religious faith and metaphysical commitments form the foundational framework of daily life and psychological distress.

## The Case for Spiritual Openness

Viktor Frankl attributed to the human person an autonomous "noological" or spiritual dimension (*noetische Dimension*) that remains inviolable and cannot fall ill, distinguishing human existence from somatic and purely psychological drives. Setting logotherapy apart from Freudian reductionism, Frankl argued in *The Doctor and the Soul* (1946/1955) and *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946) that freedom, personal responsibility, and conscience reside in this spiritual dimension, and that psychotherapy amputates the whole person if it ignores spiritual striving.

Emmy van Deurzen similarly integrated a spiritual dimension (*Überwelt*) as the fourth mandatory axis in her structural model of existence, alongside the physical (*Umwelt*), social (*Mitwelt*), and personal (*Eigenwelt*). In van Deurzen's framework, the *Überwelt* encompasses the client's values, moral ideals, philosophies of life, and ultimate convictions.

Christian existentialists such as Paul Tillich and Gabriel Marcel also influenced this branch. In *Dynamics of Faith* (1957), Tillich defined faith as the state of being ultimately concerned, and in *The Courage to Be* (1952) he analyzed how spiritual courage confronts existential anxieties of fate and death, emptiness and meaninglessness, and guilt and condemnation.

## The Secular Phenomenological Critique

Conversely, the secular existential tradition, derived from Jean-Paul Sartre, Albert Camus, and Simone de Beauvoir, insists that existential analysis must operate within radical ontological finitude. In *Being and Nothingness* (1943) and the lecture *Existentialism Is a Humanism* (1946), Sartre maintained that existence precedes essence: there is no pre-given human nature, no cosmic blueprint, and no transcendent guarantor. The human being is "condemned to be free" and must bear the agony of self-definition in an indifferent universe.

From this perspective, introducing a spiritual dimension risks re-importing metaphysical consolations through the back door, offering therapeutic substitutes for the very illusions philosophy sought to dismantle. Methodologically, secular existentialists argue that the clinician's responsibility is to help the client confront groundlessness and responsibility directly, cautioning that spiritualized frameworks can degenerate into pastoral guidance or clinician-imposed dogmas.

## Clinical Practice and Cross-Cultural Encounters

This divergence generates contrasting clinical postures:
1. **Spiritual openness**: The clinician validates religious and spiritual commitments as authentic sources of meaning, psychological resilience, and existential values. Devout clients are encouraged to explore prayer, communal solidarity, and religious narratives as resources. Clinicians, however, must guard against inadvertently adopting an evangelical or didactic stance that imposes moral values.
2. **Methodological secular neutrality**: The clinician treats religious content phenomenologically—neither privileging nor invalidating it—viewing belief as one among many human expressions of meaning-making. While protective of client autonomy in pluralistic settings, an unreflective secular stance risks marginalizing clients for whom faith is their core interpretive horizon.

In traditional and non-Western settings, including the Arab and Islamic world, this tension is particularly acute. Classical Islamic thought offers rich precedents for examining the self, spiritual disorders, and moral purification, such as Abu Hamid al-Ghazali's psychological and spiritual taxonomy in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din* (The Revival of the Religious Sciences). Contemporary researchers note that effective cross-cultural existential practice requires navigating the client's lived religious universe without imposing alien secular reductionism on the one hand, or abandoning phenomenological rigor on the other.
