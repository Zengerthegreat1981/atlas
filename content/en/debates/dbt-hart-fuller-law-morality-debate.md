# The Hart–Fuller Debate (Law and Morality)

One of the best-known debates in twentieth-century Anglo-American philosophy of law, conducted between **H.L.A. Hart** and **Lon L. Fuller** through two successive articles in the *Harvard Law Review* in 1958, and continued in their later books (Hart's *The Concept of Law*, 1961, and Fuller's *The Morality of Law*, 1964).

## The Flashpoint: Laws of the Nazi Regime

The debate was practically sparked by a question posed after the war: **were Nazi statutes genuinely laws, even where they were grossly unjust, or did they fail to be law at all because of their monstrousness?** The central example was the post-war "grudge informer" case, decided by the Oberlandesgericht Bamberg in 1949: a woman had denounced her husband to the authorities in 1944 for remarks critical of Hitler, under Nazi statutes, and he was sentenced to death (the sentence was not carried out). She was prosecuted after the war for unlawful deprivation of liberty, and the court convicted her, holding that the statutes could not shield her because they offended the conscience of decent people.

## Hart's Position: Separation, then Retrospective Legislation rather than Denying the Past of the Law

Hart holds that the Nazi statutes were laws in the correct **descriptive sense** (they were enacted according to the procedures then recognized), but that they were **so grossly unjust** that they ought to be morally condemned and disobeyed. The remedy, in his view, is an explicit **retrospective enactment** that declares these laws will not be recognized, rather than the claim that they were never law at all. Maintaining the positivist separation between "is it law?" and "is it just?" preserves, in Hart's opinion, the **explicitness of the moral choice** that a society confronts after tyranny, rather than concealing it behind a manipulation of the definition of "law" itself.

## Fuller's Position: Failure to Achieve Inner Morality Removes Legal Character

Fuller holds that the Nazi legal system, with its secrecy, retroactivity and disregard for the principles of legality, failed grossly to achieve the **inner morality of law**, so that its enactments cannot properly be counted as "law" in the full sense at all. Thus there is no need for a retrospective enactment to annul them; they were never law to begin with, and so there was nothing to annul.

## The Importance of the Debate

The debate goes beyond the particular episode of Nazism to reveal two competing assumptions about the nature of law itself. Is "legality" a purely formal attribute (procedural in its source only), or does it include a minimum of procedural-moral conditions, such that the absence of these conditions prevents the production of law at all? This distinction remained a central axis in every subsequent discussion of the relationship between law and morality (including Ronald Dworkin's later critique of both).
