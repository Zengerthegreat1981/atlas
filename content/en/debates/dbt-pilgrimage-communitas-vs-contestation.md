# Pilgrimage Debate: Communitas vs. Contestation

A central debate in the comparative anthropology of religious pilgrimage. It arises from the conflict between two theoretical readings of what happens socially when pilgrims gather at a sacred site.

## The First Position: The Communitas Thesis (Turner)

Victor Turner proposed, in *The Ritual Process* (1969) and in the later study *Image and Pilgrimage in Christian Culture* (1978, co-authored with Edith Turner), that pilgrimage is a "liminoid" or liminal-like journey. In *The Ritual Process* itself pilgrimage is treated only briefly; the fuller pilgrimage argument appears in the 1978 work. It temporarily removes the pilgrim from their usual social structure (class, profession, lineage) and dissolves the differences among pilgrims in a state of spontaneous collective fraternity that he called "communitas." In this reading, the pilgrimage site is by nature a unifying space.

## The Second Position: The Contestation Thesis (Eade and Sallnow)

John Eade and Michael Sallnow argued in *Contesting the Sacred* (1991) that this picture simplifies the field reality. In their multiple field studies, pilgrimage sites appeared as "polyphonic" spaces in which different groups (church authorities, pilgrims from diverse backgrounds, and local and commercial authorities) contest the interpretation of the site's meaning and control over it. Contestation, not unity, is therefore the dominant structural feature.

## The Position of Erik Cohen in the Debate

Erik Cohen's classificatory framework stands at a distance from both sides. He did not adopt Turner's assumption of symbolic unity, but he also did not focus on the dynamics of political conflict as Eade and Sallnow did. Instead he proposed a classificatory-sociological framework for types of motivation (pilgrim/tourist), which can be read as complementing both positions rather than as a decisive alternative to either.

## Relation to the Islamic Hajj

The Muslim hajj to Mecca is sometimes cited as a comparative test of both models. The rites of the hajj, namely the unified state of ihram, which dissolves class and ethnic distinctions among pilgrims, and the collective circumambulation around the Ka'ba, appear to embody Turner's communitas thesis strongly. However, later anthropological studies of the management of the modern hajj season (the allocation of national quotas, differences in the conditions of pilgrims from different countries, and political and religious authority over the administration of the two holy sanctuaries) also highlight a dimension of "contestation." This parallels what Eade and Sallnow described in the Christian context. The hajj has been less studied in this specific framework than Christian pilgrimage.

## Later Synthesis in the Literature

Later scholars, among them Simon Coleman and John Eade in the edited volume *Reframing Pilgrimage* (2004), suggested that the two positions are not necessarily contradictory, but describe two coexisting aspects of the same phenomenon. On this view, moments of communitas can arise within the ritual itself (for example, collective moments within the pilgrimage rites), while a broader institutional structure of contestation over the management of the site and the interpretation of its meaning continues in parallel among the different actors. This synthesis does not cancel the original disagreement between Turner and Eade and Sallnow. It suggests, however, that choosing one of the two models as an exclusive explanation may be an excessive simplification of a phenomenon that is multi-layered in time and society.
