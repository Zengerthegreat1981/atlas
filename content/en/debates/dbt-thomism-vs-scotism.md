# Thomism versus Scotism: Intellect or Will?

One of the great debates of Latin scholasticism, associated with the **Dominican** (Thomist) and **Franciscan** (Scotist) traditions. The core question: in the human being and in God, what has priority, the intellect (*intellectus*) or the will (*voluntas*)? The positions are often labelled "intellectualism" (Aquinas) and "voluntarism" (Scotus), though both labels simplify nuanced views, and both thinkers held that intellect and will cooperate in every free act.

## The first position: Thomism (intellect first)

- **Thomas Aquinas** (c. 1225-1274), in the *Summa Theologiae* (especially I, Question 82, and I-II, Questions 9-17, written c. 1265-1273): the intellect is the higher power in an absolute sense, because its object, being and truth, is more universal than the will's object, the good as apprehended. The will is a "rational appetite": it follows what the intellect presents as good (*sub ratione boni*), and free choice (*liberum arbitrium*) arises from rational deliberation.
- **Ethics**: the will is moved by the good as understood, so moral action is guided by practical reason and the virtue of prudence, while the will is perfected by the virtues of justice and charity.
- **Theology**: in the beatific vision, blessedness consists essentially in an act of the intellect, seeing God, with the will's delight following upon it. Divine action also follows God's wisdom and goodness, so God's commands are not arbitrary.
- **Natural law**: Aquinas holds that natural law is the participation of rational creatures in the eternal law, grasped by reason.

## The second position: Scotism (the will's self-determination)

- **John Duns Scotus** (c. 1265/66-1308), in the *Ordinatio* (his Oxford lectures, revised c. 1300-1304; see for example Book II, Distinction 25 on freedom, and Book IV, Distinction 49 on beatitude): the will is a distinct rational power that is not determined by the intellect. It is "self-determining": even when the intellect presents an object, the will can will it, refuse it, or will something else. Scotus connects this with his view of freedom as the capacity for opposite acts (contingency in the will itself).
- **Ethics and beatitude**: Scotus holds that beatitude consists chiefly in an act of the will, loving God, rather than in the intellect's vision. Charity is the highest of the virtues.
- **Theology**: God's will is free in relation to creation: the created order is contingent because God freely wills it. On Scotus's view only the first table of the Decalogue (duties toward God) is strictly necessary, while the second table depends on God's free choice.
- **Distinctive doctrines**: Scotus also taught the univocity of being (that "being" has one sense when said of God and creatures) and individuation by "thisness" (*haecceitas*).

## The main points of difference

- **Human freedom**: for Aquinas, freedom is rooted in reason, in the ability to deliberate about means to the good. For Scotus, freedom lies in the will's active power to determine itself.
- **Beatitude**: Thomists locate happiness primarily in the intellect's vision of God; Scotists locate it primarily in the will's love of God.
- **Divine freedom and necessity**: Thomists emphasize that God's will is guided by his wisdom and goodness; Scotus emphasizes that the contingent order of creation depends on God's free will, and that many moral truths could have been otherwise.

## Why does the debate matter?

1. **Voluntarism in late medieval thought**: Scotus's emphasis on divine and human will influenced later Franciscan thinkers, among them William of Ockham, who radicalized the stress on divine freedom, though Ockham's positions differ from Scotus's.
2. **Context of the 1277 condemnations**: the debate unfolded after the 1277 Paris condemnations, which censured propositions seen as limiting divine freedom and fed discussion about necessity and contingency.
3. **Religious orders and schools**: the two traditions became associated with the Dominican and Franciscan orders, shaping curricula and disputations in the universities for centuries.
4. **Modern debates**: questions of freedom, the priority of reason or will in ethics, and the status of natural law continue in modern philosophy and theology, in versions that draw on both traditions.
