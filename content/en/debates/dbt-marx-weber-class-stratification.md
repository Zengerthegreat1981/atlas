# Marx's Unidimensional versus Weber's Multidimensional Stratification

A fundamental disagreement, usually reconstructed from the writings of Karl Marx and Max Weber rather than from any direct exchange between them, over how many independent dimensions social stratification has and over whether the ownership of economic resources suffices to explain it. Weber's later work is widely read as a partial reply to a simplified version of the Marxian position, which he engaged without wholly rejecting.

## Marx's Position: Class as the Fundamental Economic Dimension

In the Marxian account, classes are defined by their relation to the means of production. Capitalist societies tend toward polarisation between the bourgeoisie, which owns the means of production, and the proletariat, which owns only its labour power and must sell it. Other forms of inequality, such as political power and social honour, are treated as ultimately dependent on this economic structure, the "base" on which a legal and political "superstructure" rises. Marx's own writings were less schematic than this summary suggests: in *The Eighteenth Brumaire* (1852) and elsewhere he described several classes and class fractions (landowners, the petty bourgeoisie, peasants), and the unfinished chapter on classes at the end of *Capital*, volume 3 names three great classes. The "one-dimensional" label therefore describes the explanatory priority he gave to economic relations, not a belief that society contains only two groups.

## Weber's Reply: Class, Status and Party

Weber, in the part of *Economy and Society* (published posthumously in 1922) known as "Class, Status, Party," treated the distribution of power in a community as having three analytically distinct, though interacting, aspects. **Class** refers to a shared market situation, the typical chance of supplying goods and obtaining income determined by property and marketable skills. **Status** (*Stand*) refers to social honour, claimed and granted, that is expressed in a shared style of life and often in restrictions on social intercourse; it may or may not follow economic position, and a status group may look down on the newly rich. **Party** refers to groups oriented to acquiring social power, which in modern societies operate through organisations such as political parties, but also through associations and interest groups. Weber argued that these orders interact and may reinforce one another, but that none can simply be deduced from another. He also insisted that class situation is, at root, a market situation, and that classes are not communities in the way status groups are.

## The Effect of the Debate

The contrast was a founding reference point for the sociology of stratification. Much contemporary class analysis uses hybrid frameworks. Erik Olin Wright, for example, introduced "contradictory class locations" in *Class, Crisis and the State* (1978) and later, in *Classes* (1985), built a framework centred on exploitation that combined Marxian ideas about ownership with Weberian considerations of skills and organisational authority. Whether economic class determines the other dimensions "in the last instance" or is only one dimension among several remains contested.
