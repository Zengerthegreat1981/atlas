# Conversation Analysis vs. Critical Discourse Analysis

A long methodological dispute between conversation analysis (CA), which holds that external categories such as class, gender or power should enter an analysis only where the participants themselves orient to them in their talk, and critical discourse analysis (CDA), which holds that domination often operates through assumptions that are never spoken.

## The Parties to the Debate

Conversation analysis was founded by Harvey Sacks together with Emanuel Schegloff and Gail Jefferson in the 1960s and 1970s. It rests on a strict methodological position: claims about the structure of talk must be grounded in what participants themselves display as relevant in the sequence of the interaction, and not in the analyst's prior theoretical assumptions about external structures that the participants did not make relevant. Critical discourse analysis, associated with Norman Fairclough, Teun van Dijk and Ruth Wodak, begins from the opposite concern. Its practitioners argue that power and ideology often work through shared, implicit assumptions that need no explicit utterance to be socially effective, so that confining analysis to what is visibly oriented to by speakers leaves the most effective mechanisms of domination unexamined.

## The Exchange

In "Whose Text? Whose Context?" (*Discourse & Society*, 1997), Schegloff argued that importing a critical interpretive frame into the analysis of talk risks projecting the analyst's own political commitments onto the data in place of showing what the participants themselves produce and orient to. Margaret Wetherell, in "Positioning and Interpretative Repertoires" (1998), proposed combining a CA-style attention to sequence with a Foucauldian interest in wider discursive resources, and Michael Billig, in "Whose Terms? Whose Ordinariness? Rhetoric and Ideology in Conversation Analysis" (*Discourse & Society*, 1999), replied that CA's own vocabulary and its claim to neutrality embody ideological assumptions. Schegloff answered in turn (1998, 1999). Critics of CA have also argued that its restraint amounts to an implicit conservatism, because it ignores the structural context of power in which talk occurs; CA practitioners reply that the restraint is a discipline of evidence, not a denial that power exists.

## Applied Example

The disagreement can be illustrated with a medical consultation. A conversation analyst examines how the interaction is organised sequentially: who initiates questions, how overlap and silence are handled, how a recommendation is formulated and received. The analyst does not treat the doctor's institutional authority as an explanatory variable unless the participants' conduct shows an orientation to it. A critical discourse analyst typically assumes at the outset that the institutional asymmetry between doctor and patient shapes the interaction even when it is not made explicit, and looks for its traces in wording, terminology and control of the topic and the turn order. Conversation-analytic research on medical interaction (for example by John Heritage and Douglas Maynard) has itself documented institutional asymmetries from within the CA framework, which some regard as a partial rapprochement.

## Impact

The debate remains alive in sociolinguistics and discourse studies, and it reflects a wider tension in the social sciences between close empirical description and explicitly critical political commitment.
