# The Social Movement Theory Debate: European New Social Movement Theory vs. American Resource Mobilization Theory

From the late 1970s a contrast emerged between two traditions, distinct in geography and emphasis, about how to explain the movements that appeared after the 1960s (student, environmental, feminist, peace, gay rights): a European tradition that stressed identity, culture and the changing character of society, and an American tradition that stressed organisation, resources and strategic action. The contrast is partly a retrospective construction, since the two literatures developed largely in parallel before being brought into dialogue, notably in Jean Cohen's "Strategy or Identity: New Theoretical Paradigms and Contemporary Social Movements" (*Social Research*, 1985).

## New Social Movement Theory (Europe)

The French sociologist Alain Touraine, who had developed the idea of a "post-industrial society" (1969) and, in *La Voix et le regard* (1978), a programme of the sociological study of social movements, and the Italian sociologist Alberto Melucci argued that the movements of the 1960s and after differ from the classical labour movement. In their view, advanced societies had shifted the central arena of conflict from industrial production toward the control of cultural orientations, personal life, and the production of meaning and identity. Movements are accordingly concerned less with redistributing material goods than with autonomy, identity and the definition of everyday life. Melucci's *Nomads of the Present* (1989), drawing on work from the early 1980s, developed this emphasis on collective identity as something constructed in the process of action.

## Resource Mobilization Theory (United States)

John D. McCarthy and Mayer N. Zald, in "Resource Mobilization and Social Movements: A Partial Theory" (*American Journal of Sociology*, 1977), argued that grievances are relatively constant and abundant, so that the emergence and success of movements depend on variations in resources (money, labour, legitimacy, organisational capacity), on movement organisations, and on the wider environment. They built on the rational-actor tradition of Mancur Olson's *The Logic of Collective Action* (1965) and on a critique of earlier collective behaviour theory, which had tended to treat movements as products of strain or irrational crowd psychology. On this view the same organisational logic can be applied to all movements, whatever their ideological or identity content.

## The Core of the Disagreement

The contrast set a European stress on historical rupture, with a new kind of society and new kinds of conflict, against an American stress on analytical continuity, with a single set of organisational and strategic processes. It raised the question whether the "newness" of the new movements was real or an artefact of European theoretical framing; empirical historians pointed out that many features of the supposedly new movements, such as concern with identity and culture, had earlier precedents.

## Impact

Later work drew elements from both sides and from other sources. The political process approach of Doug McAdam (1982), Charles Tilly and Sidney Tarrow gave attention to political opportunities and mobilising structures, while collective identity and cultural meaning came to the fore through the framing perspective of David Snow and colleagues (1986) and through Melucci's work. The programmatic volume *Comparative Perspectives on Social Movements*, edited by McAdam, McCarthy and Zald (1996), combined political opportunities, mobilising structures and framing processes as the three main approaches to explaining movements.
