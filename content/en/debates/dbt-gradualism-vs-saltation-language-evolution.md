# Gradualism vs. Saltation in Language Evolution

A theoretical debate in the science of language evolution about the time-scale on which human linguistic capacity emerged. Did language evolve gradually through multiple transitional stages spanning thousands or hundreds of thousands of years, or did it appear as a relatively sudden "leap" in evolution, linked to a specific genetic or cognitive mutation that granted full grammatical capacity in one relatively rapid step?

## The Gradualist Position

Proponents of this position argue that human language evolved through a series of accumulating transitional stages, beginning with limited primitive gestural or vocal communication systems (partially resembling the communication systems of some higher primates), passing through lexical systems without complex grammar (resembling what is known as "proto-language"), and gradually arriving at the recursive grammatical systems found in languages documented today. Whether recursion is a property of every language is itself disputed. This position rests on the general principle of evolutionary continuity in biology, and on studies of partial symbolic abilities in some trained higher primates, as a possible indicator of a transitional stage similar to what human ancestors went through.

## The Saltationist Position

Derek Bickerton (*Language and Species*, 1990) and, in a different form, Noam Chomsky and Robert Berwick (*Why Only Us*, 2016) are prominent representatives of positions of this kind. Bickerton argues that the transition from pre-linguistic communication to full language with its recursive structural properties was closer to a sharp qualitative transformation than to a slow gradual process. He draws partly on his study of newly emerged creole languages (such as Hawaiian Creole), which he argues arise relatively completely within a single generation of children exposed to a simplified and irregular "pidgin" input. He explained this through the "language bioprogram hypothesis": children impose a ready-made innate grammatical structure on any unstructured linguistic input they receive, rather than learning complex grammar gradually from scratch. This position also intersects with some readings of generative grammar that link the emergence of recursive grammatical capacity to a single relatively sharp cognitive mutation in human evolution.

## The Difficulty of Resolution

This debate faces a fundamental methodological obstacle: the absence of any direct fossil trace of spoken language itself (unlike stone tools or skeletons). All the evidence is therefore inferential and indirect (from the anatomy of the larynx and jaw in human ancestors, and from comparisons with sign language, creoles and the great apes), which makes a decisive separation between the two hypotheses remote so far.

## Evidence from Creole Studies and Emerging Sign Language

Bickerton drew a large part of his argument from Hawaiian Creole. He claimed that children of immigrant labourers, exposed mainly to pidgin, produced within a generation a creole with regular grammar absent from that input, and he treated this as evidence for an innate bioprogram. Later historical work on Hawaiian has disputed how sudden and how independent of input that expansion was. A separate and better documented case is Nicaraguan Sign Language. Deaf children who entered schools without a shared conventional sign language developed, across successive cohorts, a sign system of increasing structure. Ann Senghas and colleagues reported that later cohorts segmented and sequenced components of motion, such as manner and path, more than the first cohort. The case shows structure emerging in a new language; it does not by itself decide between a single mutation and gradual cultural evolution.

## The Use of Computational Simulation Models

Since the late 1990s researchers, notably Simon Kirby and colleagues, have developed computational models to simulate how a complex grammatical structure can emerge over successive generations of "iterated learning," in which each generation learns from the limited linguistic sample produced by the previous generation and then transmits it, with slight modifications, to the next. These simulations have shown that organized grammatical structures may arise gradually from this cumulative process without assuming a single decisive innate mutation. This is used as a supporting argument for the gradualist position, although critics continue to question how realistic these simplified models are compared with the actual complexity of real historical human linguistic evolution.

## Why the Debate Matters for Understanding Linguistic Innateness

This debate intersects closely with the broader debate about the innateness of human language. The saltationist position tends to align with the hypothesis of a specialized innate mechanism that arose relatively suddenly (such as a single genetic mutation that enabled recursive grammar), while the gradualist position tends to align with cumulative explanations that rely on general cognitive mechanisms that evolved gradually rather than a separate specialized linguistic faculty. This makes the distinction between the two positions consequential beyond the question of tempo alone.

## The Role of Evolutionary Anatomy

Both sides also draw on evidence from the fossil anatomy of human ancestors. Debates about the evolution of vocal anatomy (such as the lowered larynx in humans, which widens the range of speech sounds but increases the risk of choking) have been used by both sides, but they are inconclusive. The larynx itself does not fossilize, only the hyoid bone and skull features survive, and a descended larynx has also been found in some other mammals (Fitch and Reby, 2001), so its significance for language is disputed. The fossil record alone does not decide between the hypotheses, since it can be read in ways compatible with each.
