# Is Psychosis a Meaningful Journey?

The costliest dispute in the school's history, and the most instructive as a lesson in the limits of interpretation. R. D. Laing proposed that what is called psychosis might be an intelligible response to an unbearable situation — familial or social — and that the patient's "incomprehensible" speech might carry meaning if read in its context. The institution of psychiatry itself, he argued, might be part of the problem.

## The proposal

Laing's proposal, developed in *The Divided Self* (1960) and *Self and Others* (1961), was that psychotic experience has an intelligible structure: a person whose world has become unliveable retreats into strategies — withdrawal, compliance, the construction of a "false self" — that look like symptoms from outside but make sense as defences from inside. In *Sanity, Madness and the Family* (1964), written with Aaron Esterson, he argued that the families of people diagnosed with schizophrenia showed patterns of communication — contradictory demands, the denial of the contradiction — that made the "mad" response understandable.

The practical conclusion followed: if breakdown is a response to a situation, then the response should be a different situation, not a drug and a locked ward. With colleagues he founded the Philadelphia Association in 1965 and opened Kingsley Hall in East London the same year — a household where residents lived through disturbed states with minimal use of medication, electroconvulsive therapy, or physical restraint, supported by fellow residents, on the wager that madness might be a self-healing process, a breakthrough rather than a breakdown. The Hall ran until 1970. In *The Politics of Experience* (1967) he popularised the view of a psychotic episode as a possible breakthrough, a potentially transformative journey, rather than only a breakdown.

## What was right in it

Three things in the proposal have survived and become part of good practice today. First, that the treatment of patients was in many places humiliating and inhuman — the locked ward, the forced treatment, the refusal to listen — and that this was a moral scandal, not a clinical necessity. Second, that the patient's speech deserves listening rather than reduction: even speech that sounds incoherent may have an internal logic worth following. Third, that context is systematically neglected — that a person's history, family, and social world are treated as background when they may be the heart of the matter.

These three have become part of good practice today.

## What was costly

The costs were real, and they are the reason the dispute still matters. Effective treatments were delayed for people who needed them: a person in acute psychosis who is offered a voyage instead of an antipsychotic may suffer longer, and may endanger themselves and others, while the voyage is awaited. Families — and mothers in particular — were loaded with a guilt for which there was no evidence: the claim that family communication caused schizophrenia was never established, and the older notion of the "schizophrenogenic mother" (coined by Frieda Fromm-Reichmann in the 1940s, and not by Laing) fed the same blame and became a source of suffering in its own right.

And the whole school paid a reputational price. In the public mind, existential therapy became tied to a position of rejecting psychiatry, and it still pays that price today — every existential therapist working with psychosis must first demonstrate that they are not repeating Laing's errors.

## The position today

Almost no one inside the school today holds the strong form of the proposal — that psychosis is a meaningful journey that should be allowed to run its course without medical intervention. But the weak form — that psychotic experience has a structure that can be described from the inside — is very much alive in contemporary psychopathological phenomenology, and with far more precise tools than Laing had. Researchers in the phenomenological tradition now describe the disturbances of self-experience in schizophrenia — the loss of the sense of being the subject of one's own thoughts, the disturbance of time and of the boundary between self and world — in careful descriptive terms that owe something to Laing's insistence on listening, without owing anything to his refusal of medicine. Louis Sass's *Madness and Modernism* (1992) is the best-known example: a phenomenological account of psychosis as a form of consciousness, not a journey to be celebrated.

## The generalisable lesson

Interpretation without limits turns against those it was made to serve. Being partly right in insight is not enough when the price of error is a human being. Laing's case is taught in the school not as a heresy to be denounced or a martyrdom to be celebrated, but as a caution: a humane insight, pursued without restraint and without evidence, can do the very harm it set out to repair.

## What follows in practice

If you side with Laing, you listen to the content of psychotic experience as potentially meaningful, tied to the context of the patient's family and social life, and you postpone the quick pharmacological decision in favour of deeper understanding. This may open a real therapeutic space for some mild or borderline cases — but it is a genuine risk in acute psychosis that needs urgent medication, where delay is not neutral: it may prolong real suffering or put the patient and their family at risk.

If you reject Laing — the dominant position today — you treat psychosis as a medical condition calling for pharmacological intervention first, keeping a space for phenomenological, descriptive listening to the patient's experience as a complement, not a substitute. This is safer clinically and more consistent with the evidence, but it risks losing what Laing was right about: that the patient's "incomprehensible" speech may carry an internal logic worth listening to rather than dismissing as mere "symptoms".
