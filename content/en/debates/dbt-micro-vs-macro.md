# Micro versus Macro

The micro–macro debate asks at which level social life should be explained: in the face-to-face interactions of individuals, or in the large-scale patterns of institutions, classes, and whole societies. It is not a quarrel about subject matter — everyone agrees both levels exist — but about explanation. Does the macro level have a reality of its own, or is it only a shorthand for what individuals do? And can micro-level research ever explain macro-level outcomes such as inequality, revolutions, or demographic change? The disagreement has structured sociological theory and method since the discipline's founding, and it still decides what counts as a good explanation.

## The Macro Position

Émile Durkheim founded the macro side by insisting that social facts are things, external to the individual and constraining them. In *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) he argued that a social fact must be explained by another social fact, not by psychology; in *Suicide* (1897) he showed that even the most personal act varies regularly with social conditions such as religious integration. Later macro sociology took many forms: Talcott Parsons's functionalism, which read institutions as parts of a social system, and structural traditions that treat class, the state, and the economy as realities that shape action from above. The macro argument is that patterns persist across generations of individuals, that people are born into structures they did not choose, and that explaining those patterns by adding up individual choices misses precisely what makes them social.

## The Micro Position

The micro side answers that only individuals act, and that any macro pattern must be shown to arise from situated action. Max Weber, in *Economy and Society* (1922), insisted that sociology explains by interpretive understanding (*Verstehen*) of meaningful individual action, and that collectives such as the state are legitimate objects only insofar as they refer to the actions of individuals. Erving Goffman, in *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (1959), showed how social order is produced moment by moment in interaction, through performances, rituals, and the management of impressions. Randall Collins radicalised the position in *Interaction Ritual Chains* (2004), arguing that the situation, not the individual, is the starting point — but still that everything macro is built from chains of encounters and the emotional energy they generate. The micro argument is that macro concepts too often name what needs explaining and then pretend to have explained it.

## Bridges Between the Levels

The most influential work of the last generation has tried to connect the levels rather than choose between them. James Coleman's *Foundations of Social Theory* (1990) offered the famous "boat" diagram: macro conditions shape individual action, individuals interact, and their interaction produces new macro outcomes — explanation must trace the whole loop. Anthony Giddens's structuration theory, set out in *The Constitution of Society* (1984), argued that structure is both the medium and the outcome of action: people draw on rules and resources and in doing so reproduce or change them. Pierre Bourdieu's theory of practice, published in English as *Outline of a Theory of Practice* (1977), gave the bridge a mechanism — habitus, the embodied dispositions through which structures live inside individuals and generate action without conscious calculation.

## Contemporary Status

The debate has not been resolved; it has been reformulated. Few theorists now defend a pure micro or pure macro position. Analytical sociology studies social mechanisms that link levels, multilevel modelling lets researchers estimate individual and contextual effects together, and computational sociology simulates how macro patterns emerge from micro rules. The live questions are methodological: when is a macro explanation legitimate without micro foundations, and when does micro research fail to scale up? What survives from the original debate is its discipline: every sociological explanation must now say which level it operates on and how it relates to the others.

## Why It Matters for Research Design

The debate also decides how research is done, not only how it is theorised. Micro research leans on ethnography, conversation analysis, and interviews, which capture meaning and process but struggle to generalise; macro research leans on surveys, census data, and comparative history, which capture pattern but flatten the action that produces it. A study of unemployment, for example, can show either how individuals experience job loss or how labour markets distribute it — and each design answers a different question. The most persuasive recent work combines them: ethnographies nested inside surveys, network data linked to life histories, and field experiments that vary macro conditions to observe micro responses. This debate is about levels of explanation, which distinguishes it from the question of what society is as an object — it is a dispute over how explanations should be built, not over whether society exists.
