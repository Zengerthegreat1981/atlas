# The Family Theory Debate: Parsons-Bales's Fixed Gendered Functional Roles vs. Giddens's Negotiated "Pure Relationship"

The contrast between the mid-century American functionalist conception of the nuclear family and Anthony Giddens's analysis of intimate relationships in late modernity illustrates a large change in sociological family theory across four decades. The two works are separated by a generation and were not written in reply to one another; the debate is a reconstruction.

## The Position of Parsons and Bales: Complementary Roles

Talcott Parsons and Robert F. Bales, in *Family, Socialization and Interaction Process* (1955), analysed the American urban middle-class nuclear family as a small system with two basic functions: the primary socialisation of children and the stabilisation of adult personalities. They linked these to a differentiation between an "instrumental" role, mainly taken by the husband and father (relating the family to the occupational world and providing income), and an "expressive" role, mainly taken by the wife and mother (emotional support and integration of the family). They treated this division as functional for the family, and as related to the biological facts of childbearing, in the context of an isolated nuclear family in an industrial society.

## The Critique of the Gendered Division and Giddens's Alternative

This account was later criticised, notably by feminist scholars, for presenting a particular historical and class-specific arrangement as a functional necessity and for legitimating an unequal division of labour. In *The Transformation of Intimacy: Sexuality, Love and Eroticism in Modern Societies* (1992), Giddens argued that, with the decline of tradition, the growth of women's economic and social autonomy and the separation of sexuality from reproduction ("plastic sexuality"), relationships increasingly approximate what he called the "pure relationship": one entered into for its own sake, for what each person can derive from it, and continuing only as long as it delivers enough satisfaction to both partners. Its characteristic ideal is "confluent love," which is contingent and based on equality in emotional give and take, in contrast to romantic love's promise of permanence.

## The Core of the Disagreement

The contrast concerns the nature of intimate bonds: on the one hand a stable institution with complementary roles defined by function, and on the other a negotiated and reflexively monitored relationship whose continuation depends on mutual satisfaction. Giddens treated the change as potentially emancipatory in its tendency toward equality, but he also noted that it makes relationships more fragile and more liable to end.

## Criticism and Impact

Giddens's account is a theoretical interpretation, not an empirical study, and it has been questioned. Lynn Jamieson, in "Intimacy Transformed? A Critical Look at the 'Pure Relationship'" (*Sociology*, 1999), argued that empirical research shows continuing gender inequality, material interdependence and commitments to family obligations that sit uneasily with the model. Ulrich Beck and Elisabeth Beck-Gernsheim's *The Normal Chaos of Love* (1990) offered a related but different account of individualisation in love. Arlie Hochschild's *The Second Shift* (1989) documented persistent inequalities in domestic labour. Together these works helped shift family sociology toward the diversity of family forms and the negotiated, unequal character of everyday intimacy.
