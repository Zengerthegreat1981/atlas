# The 'Sacred Books of the East' Project as a Colonial Production of Knowledge: Müller and the Orientalism Critique

A critical debate in which later scholars apply the line of critique opened by Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), which concerns chiefly Near Eastern and Islamic studies, to the founding project of the comparative science of religion itself. The series *The Sacred Books of the East*, overseen by Friedrich Max Müller between 1879 and 1910, published some fifty volumes (including an index volume) of Asian religious texts (including Vedic and Upanishadic texts, the Avesta, the Qur'an in E. H. Palmer's translation, and Buddhist, Jain, Daoist and Confucian texts) in English under the auspices of the Clarendon Press, Oxford.

## Said's Thesis Applied to the Project

The critique of Orientalism (see `con-orientalism`) holds that European knowledge of "the East" in the nineteenth century was not academically neutral in isolation from colonial power relations, but formed part of the structure of domination itself: whoever holds the authority of translation, editing and classification holds the authority to define "the East" in the interest of the dominant West, even when the apparent motive is sincere academic admiration for the texts studied. Applying this thesis to Müller's project asks: was framing religious texts that were radically different from one another within a single European category ("sacred books", on the model of the Christian Bible), and arranging and editing them under the full supervision of a European colonial institution (Oxford, at the height of the British colonial era in India), a genuinely neutral epistemic act, or a form of reproducing colonial hierarchy in knowledge?

## The Counter-Position: Good Faith That Does Not Deny Structural Effect

Historians of the field (Sharpe, in *Comparative Religion: A History*) note that Müller himself was an explicit defender of the dignity of Eastern texts against the prevailing Christian missionary arrogance of his time, and rejected treating them as inferior "heathenism" — which makes the attribution of a direct colonial intention to him personally a disputed matter. However, from a structural critical perspective in the manner of Said, this does not negate that the project, regardless of its author's subjective intention, objectively produced knowledge "about" the East that was framed, classified and governed by European standards, within a colonial institution, at a colonial historical moment.

## Impact on the Contemporary Field

This debate puts the comparative science of religion before a self-examination of its origins: can the field transcend the colonial conditions of its emergence through continuous methodological review (as critics such as Talal Asad and Richard King have attempted), or do its very classificatory tools ("religion" as a cross-cultural mass, the "Holy Book" as a general model) carry a structural colonial imprint that cannot be removed merely through good intentions?
