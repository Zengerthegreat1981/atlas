# The Hart–Devlin Debate (Enforcing Morals as Law)

A central debate in the philosophy of criminal law, between **H.L.A. Hart** and **Patrick Devlin** in the late 1950s and early 1960s, around one direct practical question: **may the state criminalize a private behavior merely because it violates public morality, even if it harms no one but its own agent?**

## Context: The Wolfenden Report and the Criminalization of Homosexuality

The debate was practically provoked by the **Wolfenden Report** (1957), which recommended repealing the criminalization of homosexual relations between consenting adults in private in Britain. It relied on the principle that the function of criminal law is not to enforce a particular moral pattern, but to **protect individuals from harm** and to prevent only public indecency and exploitation.

## Devlin's Position: Society as a Moral Community

Devlin opposed this logic, arguing that every society has a **shared moral fabric** indispensable to its cohesion. In his view the state may protect this fabric through criminal law just as it protects its political security, even where there is no direct harm to a third party. Cumulative moral disintegration, he held, is a slow structural threat no less dangerous than a direct one.

## Hart's Position: The Harm Principle (Based on Mill)

Hart replied by reviving the **harm principle** formulated by John Stuart Mill in *On Liberty* (1859): an individual's liberty may be restricted legally only to prevent harm to others, not merely because the individual's conduct violates the morals of the majority. Hart accused Devlin of conflating **actual moral disintegration threatening the survival of society** (an empirical claim that Devlin did not prove) with **mere change in prevailing moral standards** (which is natural in every living society and does not necessarily call for criminalization).

## Impact

This debate directly influenced later reforms of British criminal law. Homosexual acts between consenting adults in private were decriminalized in England and Wales by the Sexual Offences Act 1967. The debate remains a basic reference in discussions between **legal moralism** and **legal liberalism** to this day, especially on issues such as the regulation of drug use, assisted dying and same-sex marriage.
