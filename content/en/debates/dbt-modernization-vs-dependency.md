# Modernization versus Dependency

Why are some societies rich and industrialised while others remain poor? From the 1950s to the 1970s two families of answers confronted one another. Modernization theory located the main conditions of development inside each society, in its economy, institutions and values. Dependency theory argued that poverty in much of Latin America, Africa and Asia was bound up with the way those regions had been drawn into a world economy dominated by others. The quarrel concerns both the causes of global inequality and the unit of analysis: the national society or the international system.

## The Modernization Position

Modernization theory took shape mainly in the United States after the Second World War, drawing on classical contrasts between traditional and modern societies and on Talcott Parsons's structural functionalism. Daniel Lerner's *The Passing of Traditional Society* (1958), a study of the Middle East, linked urbanisation, literacy, mass media and political participation, and stressed "empathy", the capacity to imagine oneself in another's situation. W. W. Rostow's *The Stages of Economic Growth* (1960), subtitled "A Non-Communist Manifesto", proposed five stages: traditional society, the preconditions for take-off, take-off, the drive to maturity, and the age of high mass consumption. Alex Inkeles and David H. Smith, in *Becoming Modern* (1974), used surveys in six developing countries to argue that schooling and factory work foster modern attitudes. On this view poorer societies can follow a broadly similar path, helped by investment, education, technology and institutional change.

## The Dependency Position

One source of the reply was the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch and the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America, whose work around 1950, alongside Hans Singer's, argued that exporters of primary goods faced a long-run tendency toward worsening terms of trade with industrial "centres". Paul Baran's *The Political Economy of Growth* (1957) gave a Marxist account of how surplus was drained or wasted in poorer economies. Andre Gunder Frank, in "The Development of Underdevelopment" (1966) and *Capitalism and Underdevelopment in Latin America* (1967), argued that underdevelopment is not an original condition but a product of the same capitalist process that developed the metropoles. Fernando Henrique Cardoso and Enzo Faletto, in *Dependencia y desarrollo en América Latina* (1969), offered a more historical and political version: dependence takes different forms according to class alliances and the state, and some growth is possible within it. Theotonio dos Santos and Samir Amin developed further variants.

## Arguments and Counter-Arguments

Dependency writers charged that modernization theory treated Western history as a universal template and ignored colonialism, trade and foreign capital. Critics within sociology, such as Joseph Gusfield (1967), also questioned the sharp opposition between tradition and modernity. Defenders of modernization, and some Marxists, replied that dependency theory underrated domestic institutions and class relations, was difficult to test, and could not easily explain rapid industrialisation in East Asian economies that traded heavily with rich countries. Bill Warren's *Imperialism: Pioneer of Capitalism* (1980) argued from a Marxist standpoint that capitalist industrialisation was in fact advancing in the Third World. Peter Evans's *Dependent Development* (1979), on Brazil, showed how the state, multinational firms and local capital could combine to produce industrial growth that remained dependent and unequal.

## Contemporary Status

Neither position survives unchanged. Immanuel Wallerstein's world-systems analysis, beginning with the first volume of *The Modern World-System* (1974), took up the critique at the level of a single system divided into core, semi-periphery and periphery. Research on the developmental state shifted attention to the capacities of governments, while Ronald Inglehart and Christian Welzel (2005) revived a revised, non-linear modernization argument about economic development, changing values and democracy. Current work usually combines domestic institutions with a country's position in global production and finance.
