# The Substantivist–Formalist Debate in Economic Anthropology

A central controversy that occupied economic anthropology from the late 1950s to the 1970s, on a fundamental question: can the tools of neoclassical economics (scarcity, rational utility maximisation, supply and demand) be applied to understand economic activity in "primitive" or "traditional" non-capitalist societies?

## The Substantivist Position

Karl Polanyi, whose *The Great Transformation* (1944) argued that the self-regulating market was a modern invention, set out the key distinction in "The Economy as Instituted Process" (1957), between two meanings of the word "economy": the "formal" meaning (the rational allocation of scarce resources among alternative uses, a meaning specific historically to modern market societies), and the "substantive" meaning (the institutional process by which a society obtains its material livelihood, whatever its mechanisms). Polanyi argued that most historical societies organised their economies through mechanisms of **reciprocity** and **redistribution**, rather than through **market exchange** alone, and that imposing the logic of the modern market analytically on these societies is a grave anthropological distortion. The economist George Dalton and the anthropologist Paul Bohannan were prominent advocates of this position in anthropology, and Marshall Sahlins developed it further in *Stone Age Economics* (1972), arguing that "needs" and "scarcity" are themselves shaped culturally rather than given by nature.

## The Formalist Position

Formalist anthropologists (chiefly Raymond Firth and Harold Schneider) responded that the formal principles of rational choice under the constraint of scarcity are indeed universal. Every human being, in any society, faces choices between alternative uses of limited resources (time, land, labour), and the formal tools of economic analysis (cost-benefit analysis, utility maximisation) can be applied with methodological caution to any economic system regardless of its cultural context.

## Non-Resolution and Legacy

The debate was never definitively resolved. It was later considered that the two positions address different levels (the large institutional structures in the substantivist view, and marginal individual behaviour in the formalist view), which may not necessarily stand in complete contradiction. But the substantivist legacy remained the more influential in later economic anthropology, and it shaped later work on exchange, value, and debt (for example, that of David Graeber) and the continuing anthropological critique of the universality of "Homo economicus", the rational economic man.
