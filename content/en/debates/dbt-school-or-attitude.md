# School or Attitude?

The oldest dispute in existential therapy is also the one that decides its institutional fate, not only its theory: is existential therapy a school, or an attitude? The question is whether the existential approach is a distinct professional identity — with its own ontology, method, concepts, models of suffering and change, and training programmes — or a philosophical way of seeing the human being that any therapist, cognitive, analytic, or systemic, can adopt without changing schools. The answer determines who may practise, who pays, and what counts as competence. It is a debate about identity, and it is fought between the founders of the British current themselves, which is why it has never been settled by appeal to an outside authority.

## The First Position — An Attitude

Ernesto Spinelli, himself one of the founders of the British current, argues from inside against his own colleagues. In *Practising Existential Psychotherapy: The Relational World* (2007), he presents existential therapy as entirely relational: what happens between therapist and client, here and now, is the only material, and there is no general theoretical structure to be applied in advance. Even the "four dimensions of existence" formulated by Emmy van Deurzen are, for Spinelli, a structure imposed on experience that betrays the phenomenological method, which requires describing what appears rather than arranging it beforehand. On this view, existential therapy needs no separate training and no separate professional identity: a cognitive or analytic therapist who adopts this philosophical stance is already practising existentially. The position preserves philosophical fidelity at the price of institutional invisibility — an attitude cannot be accredited, examined, or defended as a profession.

## The Second Position — A School

Emmy van Deurzen is the figure who organised the British current and turned it from scattered individuals into an institution. She founded the Society for Existential Analysis in 1988, together with its journal *Existential Analysis*, and in 1996 she founded the New School of Psychotherapy and Counselling in London to provide independent, accredited training. Her textbook, *Existential Counselling and Psychotherapy in Practice*, first published in 1988 as *Existential Counselling in Practice* and reissued in a third edition in 2012, gave existential therapy its own ontology, method, and practical concepts — above all the four dimensions of existence (physical, social, personal, and spiritual) as an alternative to Irvin Yalom's four ultimate concerns. For van Deurzen, treating existential therapy as "merely an attitude" means dissolving it into other schools and losing the right to independent professional recognition, to a place in health systems, and to the training of new practitioners under its own name.

## What Follows from the Decision

The stakes are material, not abstract. Professional recognition, entry into health and insurance systems, research funding, university teaching, and the right of certificate holders to practise independently all depend on whether existential therapy counts as a school. A school can set standards, examine candidates, and negotiate with regulators; an attitude cannot. The debate therefore connects directly to the evidence dispute and to the question of structures: a school needs shared concepts and assessable competences, while an attitude resists exactly that standardisation. Spinelli's objection is that standardisation betrays the phenomenological spirit that opposed ready-made protocols in the first place; van Deurzen's reply is that without it, existential therapy cannot survive as a practice that new generations can learn and clients can find.

## Contemporary Status

Institutionally, the second position has prevailed: there are now associations, journals, training programmes, and world congresses for existential therapy, including the first World Congress for Existential Therapy in London in 2015. Theoretically, the first position is still alive inside the school itself, especially among those who see codification as a betrayal. The practical consequence is visible in every contemporary training programme: whether "existential competence" is measured by examination against defined standards, as van Deurzen's New School does, or left to a more subjective assessment based on long-term personal supervision, as Spinelli's relational model implies. The dispute defines the shape of existential training today, and it remains open because both sides can claim the tradition: the philosophers resisted systems, and the therapists needed institutions.

The dispute also reaches beyond Britain. In the United States, the existential current developed through Rollo May's *Existence* (1958) and Yalom's textbooks, where the school-versus-attitude question took a different form: whether existential therapy was a distinct modality or a philosophical depth dimension running through all modalities. Yalom himself treated it as the latter — a way of deepening any therapy by attending to ultimate concerns — while training institutes treated it as the former. The same tension appears in continental Europe, where Daseinsanalysis claims school status while the phenomenological tradition it draws on resists institutional form. The British debate is thus the clearest local expression of a dilemma the whole tradition faces wherever it tries to organise itself.
