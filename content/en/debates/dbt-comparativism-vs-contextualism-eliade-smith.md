# Comparativism vs. Contextualism: Eliade, J. Z. Smith, and the Method of Religious Comparison

A central methodological debate in comparative religion, centered on the question: **How can two religious phenomena from cultures distant in time and place be legitimately compared?** Its two main parties are historians of religion who were colleagues at the University of Chicago: Mircea Eliade, who practiced the method, and Jonathan Z. Smith, who later critiqued it from within the same institution.

## The "Comparativist" Position: Eliade

Eliade sought to build a general "morphology" of religious patterns across cultures. He compared Siberian shamanic rituals with Indian yoga and Australian myths, searching for a hidden common structure of the human religious experience (the sacred and the profane, the axis mundi; see `con-axis-mundi-sacred-space-eliade`). The implicit assumption is that there exist religious patterns that transcend history and culture, which can be uncovered by gathering examples from multiple sources, without requiring a direct historical connection between them.

## The "Contextualist" Critique: Smith

In his essay "In Comparison a Magic Dwells" (within `wrk-imagining-religion-smith`), Smith attacked this method as a methodologically unjustified selection. The comparativist chooses two phenomena that look alike on the surface from among thousands of possibilities, without explaining why these two were chosen rather than others. Smith's title plays on the idea of comparison as a kind of "magic": it produces an apparently objective similarity that is in fact the scholar's own act of selection. Smith's alternative is a comparison governed by an explicit and limited research question (not "religion" in general), with a stated methodological explanation for why each side of the comparison was chosen, and careful attention to differences rather than to shared patterns alone.

Smith applied this critique to a specific historical case in his book `wrk-drudgery-divine-smith` (1990): the history of comparing early Christianity with Hellenistic mystery religions. He showed that many of these comparisons were driven by internal sectarian polemics rather than neutral research.

## Further Positions: Lincoln and Doniger

Bruce Lincoln (`thk-bruce-lincoln`), in works such as *Theses on Method* (1996), treats religion as a kind of discourse and comparison as a tool of critical inquiry, keeping comparison but subordinating it to explicit analysis of the authority and interests behind religious claims.

Wendy Doniger (`thk-wendy-doniger`) represents another position. She does not abandon the broad comparative ambition of the field. She compares Indian and Greek myths in search of shared narrative patterns, as in *Splitting the Difference* (1999). But she grounds this comparison in a rigorous philological reading of the original text in its language, rather than in Eliade's phenomenological abstraction about "the sacred." She thus offers a partial textual justification (why this particular text) that responds to some of Smith's critique, without accepting his skeptical conclusion about comprehensive comparison as such.

## The Impact of the Debate on the Field

This debate, though ostensibly limited to the "method" of comparison, reshaped the entire field after the 1980s. Historians became more cautious about comprehensive generalizations in the manner of Eliade, and more committed to explicitly delimiting and justifying the scope of comparison in the manner of Smith. The debate intersects with the reductionism debate (`dbt-reductionism-vs-non-reductionism-religious-studies`) and the debate over defining religion (`dbt-defining-religion-as-analytic-category`) without coinciding with either. Eliade defended the irreducibility of the sacred, whereas Smith's critique concerns the scholar's categories and the method of comparing religious phenomena *with one another*; the debate is therefore distinct from the reductionism debate.
