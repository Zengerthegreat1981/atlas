# Positivism versus Interpretivism

This debate is about whether sociology is a science in the same sense as physics, or a discipline of a different kind altogether. Positivism holds that social life can be explained by discovering law-like regularities through observation, just as natural science explains nature. Interpretivism holds that social life is made of meaningful action, and that explaining it requires understanding what people mean by what they do — something no measurement can deliver on its own. The dispute is not about a single school or a single concept: it is the running argument over the very aim of sociology, fought out between its founders and still alive in the philosophy of social science. Unlike a history of Comte's positivism or an account of Weber's concept of understanding, this entry is about the confrontation between the two positions and what each side claims the other cannot do.

## The Positivist Position

Auguste Comte, who coined the term sociology, set out the positivist programme in his *Course of Positive Philosophy* (1830–1842): society passes through stages, and the study of society must pass from theology and metaphysics to positive science, discovering invariant laws. Émile Durkheim gave the programme its classical sociological form in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895), treating social facts as things and explaining them by their causes and functions rather than by the intentions of actors. Karl Popper, a critic of the logical positivists but a defender of a single scientific method, proposed in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934; English edition 1959) to replace the search for verified laws with falsification: a claim is scientific only if it can be tested and possibly refuted. In sociology, the positivist impulse survives in the search for robust, replicable regularities — the survey, the experiment, and the statistical model — and in the insistence that a sociological claim must be checkable by evidence independent of the researcher's sympathies.

## The Interpretivist Position

The interpretivist reply begins with Wilhelm Dilthey's distinction, in his *Introduction to the Human Sciences* (1883), between the natural sciences, which explain (*erklären*), and the human sciences, which understand (*verstehen*). Max Weber made understanding the defining task of sociology, arguing that even a statistical regularity is not yet an explanation until the meaning of the action is grasped. Alfred Schutz, in *The Phenomenology of the Social World* (1932), showed that the social world is already interpreted by its members before the sociologist arrives, so that sociological concepts are interpretations of interpretations. Peter Winch, in *The Idea of a Social Science* (1958), pushed the point further: the rules of a social practice can only be understood from inside it, and a sociologist who imposes external categories misunderstands what she studies. Hans-Georg Gadamer's *Truth and Method* (1960) and Charles Taylor's essay "Interpretation and the Sciences of Man" (1971) added that interpretation is not a poor substitute for explanation but the condition of any human knowledge, including self-knowledge.

## The Flashpoints

Three questions keep the debate alive. First, explanation versus understanding: can a cause be stated without a meaning, and can a meaning be stated without a cause? Donald Davidson's "Actions, Reasons, and Causes" (1963) argued that reasons can be causes, blurring the boundary the debate assumes. Second, value-freedom: positivists demand that values be kept out of research, while interpretivists reply that the choice of what to study, and the language used to describe it, is already value-laden. Third, the unity of science: positivists insist on one scientific method for nature and society, while interpretivists insist that human beings, unlike atoms, can answer back, reinterpret the researcher's categories, and change their behaviour in light of sociological findings — the phenomenon Anthony Giddens called the double hermeneutic.

## Contemporary Status

Neither side has won, and the most influential recent positions are attempts to move beyond the standoff. Roy Bhaskar's critical realism, set out in *A Realist Theory of Science* (1975), accepts that social structures are real and have causal powers, while insisting that they are concept-dependent and can only be known through interpretation. Pragmatists argue that the test of a method is what it lets us do, not its philosophical pedigree. Analytical sociology, associated with Peter Hedström's *Dissecting the Social* (2005), seeks precise mechanisms while accepting that mechanisms in social life run through beliefs and desires. In practice, most sociologists now work with both logics, but the debate still matters: it decides what counts as evidence, what a good explanation looks like, and whether sociology should aspire to prediction, to understanding, or to both at once.

The debate also shapes what sociologists do with their findings. A positivist result invites intervention: if a variable causes an outcome, changing the variable should change the outcome, which is why positivist sociology has been close to policy and to the state. An interpretive result invites something else — understanding, and sometimes critique: to show how a practice makes sense to its participants is also to show that it could be otherwise, since meanings can be remade. The two ambitions are not easily combined, and the tension between prediction and understanding is one reason the debate has outlived every attempt to declare it finished.
