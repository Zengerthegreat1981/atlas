# Critique of Development Anthropology

## The Debate

The debate centers on whether international "development" projects (carried out by organizations such as the World Bank, United Nations agencies and non-governmental organizations) actually serve the interests of the communities they target, or whether in essence they are a mechanism for reproducing economic and political relations of domination similar to colonialism, through an apparently "neutral" discourse that conceals deep political dimensions.

## James Ferguson's Thesis: "The Anti-Politics Machine"

The anthropologist **James Ferguson** offered the most influential critique of this field in his book **"The Anti-Politics Machine: 'Development,' Depoliticization, and Bureaucratic Power in Lesotho"** (*The Anti-Politics Machine*, 1990), based on a case study of a failed development project in Lesotho (1975–1984). Ferguson showed that the discourse of "development" transforms problems of poverty, which are at root **political** problems linked to the distribution of power and resources, into purely **technical** problems that neutral experts can solve with technical solutions (improved seeds, infrastructure, administrative training), ignoring the actual political structures that produce poverty in the first place. The paradox, as Ferguson sees it, is that these projects often fail to achieve their stated goals, yet they "succeed" in expanding the bureaucratic power of the state and its penetration into the lives of the population, an unintended but structural side effect.

## Arturo Escobar's Thesis: "Encountering Development"

The Colombian anthropologist **Arturo Escobar** developed a complementary critique in his book **"Encountering Development: The Making and Unmaking of the Third World"** (*Encountering Development*, 1995). He analyzed "development" as a **discourse** (in the Foucauldian sense) that produced the "Third World" itself as a category requiring intervention and reform from outside, through a network of institutions, experts and technical knowledge that subjected whole societies to imported classifications and solutions, in what amounted to a new form of power exercised through expertise.

## Impact

This critique deeply influenced the reassessment of the work of anthropologists within development institutions themselves. It prompted some to call for "post-development" approaches that question the assumption that all societies must "develop" along a single Western development model, while others pushed for working from within to reform development practices so that they become more politically and locally sensitive.

## Limitations

This theoretical line is sometimes criticized for not offering, despite its analytical precision, a clear practical alternative for addressing actual, tangible poverty. Rejecting "development" as a whole discourse may in practice translate into neglecting the urgent material needs of communities that are genuinely poor, while awaiting solutions that transcend the development discourse entirely.
