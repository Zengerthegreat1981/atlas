# Austerity vs. Stimulus after 2008

Following the global financial crisis of 2008, and the severe recession that followed, and the intensification of the European sovereign debt crisis (particularly in Greece, Ireland, Portugal and Spain), economists and policymakers divided over a central question. Does the crisis call for broad fiscal stimulus (rising public spending and a temporary budget deficit) to raise aggregate demand and speed recovery? Or does it call for strict fiscal discipline ("austerity") to reduce public debt and restore financial market confidence, which is considered a precondition for any sustainable recovery?

## The stimulus argument: a deficiency of effective demand

Advocates of stimulus, drawing on direct Keynesian analysis, argued that the crisis resulted from a collapse of private demand (consumption and investment) after the shock to the financial sector. The debt-laden private sector tended to cut its spending to reduce its indebtedness ("deleveraging"). If the government also cut its public spending at the same time, the aggregate contraction would deepen rather than be cured. This was especially so in an environment in which the interest rate is close to zero (the "liquidity trap"), so that monetary policy alone cannot effectively stimulate the economy. Advocates of this position, among them **Paul Krugman** and **Joseph Stiglitz**, called for temporary broad government spending to offset the collapse in private demand; the US stimulus programme (ARRA) of 2009 reflected part of this view. They argued that the "fiscal spending multiplier" is highest precisely in times of deep recession and when interest rates are near zero.

## The austerity argument: public debt risks and market confidence

Advocates of austerity, including European Union policymakers and the UK government after 2010, argued that the accumulation of public debt to high levels threatens long-term financial stability and raises market fears that governments may be unable to repay their debts. This could suddenly raise the cost of government borrowing, as actually happened in Greece, and lead to a deeper crisis. Some also advanced the idea of **"expansionary austerity"**: cutting public spending could raise the confidence of investors and consumers about the sustainability of public finances, thereby stimulating private investment and consumption that would offset the fall in public spending. This hypothesis drew on earlier work such as Giavazzi and Pagano (1990) and Alesina and Ardagna (2010). Separately, the study by **Reinhart and Rogoff** (2010) reported that median growth was markedly lower in countries whose public debt exceeded about 90 percent of GDP, and it was widely cited in support of fiscal consolidation.

## The Herndon–Ash–Pollin study and the undermining of the statistical basis of austerity

One of the most prominent statistical references of the austerity argument was seriously shaken in 2013. While attempting to replicate the results of the Reinhart and Rogoff study for a graduate course, **Thomas Herndon** (with **Michael Ash** and **Robert Pollin**) found errors in the original Excel spreadsheet, including the unintended exclusion of data for several countries from an averaging formula, together with questionable data selection and weighting. Reinhart and Rogoff acknowledged the coding error but defended the broader finding that high debt is associated with lower growth. They showed that correcting these errors reveals a much weaker and less sharply discontinuous relationship between debt level and growth than the original study claimed. This provoked wide media and academic controversy about how far influential policy arguments relied on a result that did not withstand re-examination, although the causal direction between debt and growth was disputed in any case.

## The Arab dimension: Egypt, Tunisia and IMF programmes

A number of Arab economies, particularly Egypt and Tunisia after the revolutions of 2011, experienced a local version of the same debate. Their governments negotiated loan programmes with the International Monetary Fund (Tunisia in 2013, Egypt in 2016 after earlier talks stalled) that included reductions in energy and food subsidies and control of the budget deficit. This came at a time when both economies faced severe social and political pressures arising from the political transition and the disruption of economic activity after the revolution. This coincidence prompted a local debate about the same tension that governs the global debate: should the decline in economic activity be addressed through fiscal discipline that satisfies international creditors and reassures markets, or by preserving social spending and subsidies at a politically and socially highly sensitive moment?

## A question not resolved in theory

The debate has not been settled academically by full consensus. Later reviews, including research by IMF economists (Blanchard and Leigh, 2013), concluded that the fiscal multiplier had been underestimated and is larger in times of deep recession and near the zero lower bound on interest rates than in normal times. This was regarded as a partial retreat from the earlier optimistic estimates regarding expansionary austerity. It did not, however, mean a complete abandonment of concerns about the sustainability of public debt among some policymakers.

## Its significance as a reproduction of the 1930s debate

This debate is a direct contemporary restatement of the disagreement between Keynes and Hayek in the 1930s. The same tension appears between active government stimulus to fill a demand gap, and fiscal discipline that fears the aggravation of imbalances if government intervention continues. It has been re-expressed in the language of contemporary fiscal policy and its statistical tools, rather than in the language of the original Austrian business cycle theory.
