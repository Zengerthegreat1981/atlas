# Historical versus Ahistorical

Does sociology need history? The historical–ahistorical debate asks whether social life can be understood through timeless patterns, or only through the particular past that produced the present. The ahistorical position holds that sociology should seek general laws and mechanisms that hold across times and places, treating history as background noise. The historical position holds that social structures are the sediment of past struggles, that the present is one outcome among many that might have happened, and that a sociology without history mistakes a moment for a law. The argument runs through the discipline's founding texts and its most famous works, and it decides, in practice, whether a sociologist reaches for a dataset or an archive.

## The Ahistorical Position

The ahistorical case was stated most sharply by Émile Durkheim in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895): social facts should be explained by other social facts, not by individual psychology, and the cause that produces a practice should be distinguished from the function it serves; Durkheim himself nonetheless made extensive use of comparative and historical material. Talcott Parsons's *The Social System* (1951) carried this into structural functionalism, modelling society as a system of interlocking parts in equilibrium, with change treated as a disturbance rather than the normal state. Robert Merton's middle-range theory likewise sought propositions of limited scope that could be tested across different settings. The attraction is clear: a sociology of timeless mechanisms can claim the status of a science, producing findings that travel. The cost, critics say, is presentism — taking the institutions of one's own time, such as the nation-state or the nuclear family, as the natural furniture of social life, and then discovering them everywhere.

## The Historical Position

The historical case is as old as the discipline. Marx's account of capitalism is a history of how one mode of production gave way to another, and Weber's studies of the world religions, bureaucracy, and rationalisation are comparative and historical in method. Norbert Elias's *The Civilizing Process* (1939) showed how something as intimate as table manners was the product of centuries of state formation. Fernand Braudel, in *The Mediterranean* (1949) and his essay on the *longue durée* (1958), argued that the deepest social structures change only over centuries, and that event-driven history misses them entirely. C. Wright Mills, in *The Sociological Imagination* (1959), made the point programmatic: biography, history, and social structure are the three coordinates of any adequate sociological question, and a sociology that drops history becomes an inventory of the present. Philip Abrams's *Historical Sociology* (1982) gave the position its manifesto, insisting that history and sociology are one enterprise, not two disciplines that occasionally borrow from each other.

## The Revival of Historical Sociology

The debate sharpened in the 1960s and 1970s, when a generation of sociologists turned to comparative history to answer questions that surveys could not. Barrington Moore's *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy* (1966) compared routes to the modern world in England, France, the United States, China, Japan, and India, with Germany and Russia discussed only in passing. Theda Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) explained revolutions through state structures and international pressures rather than through the intentions of revolutionaries. Charles Tilly, in *Big Structures, Large Processes, Huge Comparisons* (1984), argued that sociologists must think in centuries and continents, not in survey waves. These works demonstrated that historical explanation could be systematic and comparative without becoming mere storytelling, and they established historical sociology as an organised field, with its own section in the American Sociological Association from the 1980s.

## Contemporary Status

The historical position has largely won the institutional argument: few sociologists today would defend a wholly ahistorical science, and concepts such as path dependence, critical junctures, and institutional legacies — drawn from historical institutionalism — are now common currency across the discipline. Yet the tension persists in a new form. Variable-centred research still dominates much of the field, and its critics argue that it smuggles ahistorical assumptions into its models by treating the present as a stable baseline. Meanwhile historians of the global turn warn that comparative-historical sociology still takes Europe as its template. The live question is no longer whether sociology needs history, but how much of it, and of what kind: whether the discipline should explain the present as the outcome of a long past, or treat the past as one source of evidence among others for general mechanisms that operate in every period.

The debate also turns on what counts as a cause. In ahistorical sociology, a cause is a variable whose value changes the outcome; in historical sociology, a cause is a sequence — a revolution, a war, a reform — whose timing and order matter as much as its presence. The historian's complaint is that variable language flattens sequences into correlations and loses the story; the variable sociologist's complaint is that narrative explanation cannot say how much each factor contributed. Comparative-historical work tries to hold both, using comparison to test which sequences matter while keeping the sequence itself in view.
