# Lexical Modernization: Coinage/Borrowing vs. Linguistic Purism in Arabic Academies

An ongoing linguistic debate since the founding of the modern Arab language academies (Damascus, 1919, and Cairo, 1932) over the best way to supply Arabic with new terms that keep pace with modern sciences and technologies. The question is whether it is preferable to coin terms from the classical Arabic roots themselves, by analogy with their traditional morphological patterns (such as forming the word for "telephone" from an old Arabic root rather than borrowing a foreign word), or whether direct borrowing from foreign languages should be accepted when suitable Arabic coinage is unavailable or when borrowing is simpler in practice.

## The Position of Coinage and Standard Derivation

Proponents of this position, represented by many founders of the Arabic language academies, argue that Arabic possesses an immense derivational capacity latent in its root-and-pattern morphological system, and that precise scientific terms can be generated from authentic Arabic roots by analogy with inherited morphological patterns (such as the pattern *faʿʿāla* used for appliances, as in *thallāja* "refrigerator," or *mifʿāl* for tools, as in *miftāḥ* "key"). Derivation by analogy (*qiyās*), blending (*naḥt*) and the Arabization of loanwords (*taʿrīb*) were the main methods debated. In this way they preserve the coherence of the Arabic morphological system and the unity of its lexicon across new fields of knowledge, and avoid the accumulation of foreign words that are difficult to inflect according to Arabic morphological rules.

## The Position of Accepting Practical Borrowing

Proponents of this position, including linguists such as Ibrahim Anis, an Egyptian academy member who combined modern linguistic training with knowledge of the grammatical heritage and stressed the role of actual usage, argue that insisting on exclusively coining every new term sometimes produces artificial, cumbersome words that do not survive actual usage (since both ordinary speakers and specialists prefer the foreign word that is in practical common use over the officially proposed coinage). They further argue that all living languages, including Arabic throughout its long history (such as its borrowing of Persian, Greek, and Turkish words across its various periods), continue to thrive through borrowing rather than through an artificial absolute purity. The true measure of success, in their view, is what actual usage among speakers settles on, not what is decided administratively in an academy hall.

## The Reality of Practical Tension

The history of the Arabic language academies reveals this tension in practice. Many officially coined terms never came into actual use outside official documents, with popular and professional usage preferring the foreign word or its phonetically Arabized form. Meanwhile, other coined terms succeeded widely and became established in both everyday and official use. It is therefore difficult to predict in advance which proposal will withstand the test of actual usage, which makes the academy's decision alone insufficient on its own to ensure that a proposed term gains currency.

## The Continuing Debate

The debate remains present in the meetings of contemporary Arabic language academies, especially as new technical terms emerge rapidly (in computing and artificial intelligence, for example) at a pace that exceeds any language academy's ability to keep up officially with approved coined terms before popular usage settles on an Arabized or directly borrowed alternative.

## Concrete Examples of Success and Failure

Concrete examples illustrate the varied outcomes of this debate in practice. Coinages such as *hātif* ("telephone"), *midhāʿ* ("radio"), *sayyāra* ("car") and *ḥāsūb* ("computer") circulate widely in written and formal Standard Arabic, although the spoken dialects often keep borrowed forms such as *tilifōn* or *kumbyūtar*. Television shows a similar pattern: the borrowed and Arabized forms *tilifizyūn* and *tilfāz* are in everyday use, and alternative coinages did not displace them. The outcome is difficult to predict on purely linguistic criteria, since factors such as ease of pronunciation and early dissemination in the media play as important a role as the morphological correctness of a proposal.

## Divergent Approaches Among Arabic Academies

The various Arabic language academies (Cairo, Damascus, Baghdad, Rabat, and Amman) did not adopt a uniform, strict position on this debate. Their degree of strictness regarding exclusive coinage versus acceptance of borrowing varied over their history and according to the nature of the terms under consideration (precise technical scientific terms versus general everyday vocabulary). At times, the decisions of different academies differed concerning the same term for the same new concept, which sometimes produced regional terminological duality within Arabic itself (for example, different terms for the same technical concept between the Mashriq and the Maghrib), adding to the complexity of the general linguistic landscape.

## Contemporary Challenges in an Age of Technological Acceleration

The debate has become more pressing in the contemporary digital age, where thousands of new technical terms appear every year in fields such as computing, artificial intelligence, and digital communication, at a rate far exceeding the ability of any official language academy to keep pace through periodic, relatively slow meetings. This has prompted some researchers to call for faster and more decentralized mechanisms for generating terms (such as open collaborative platforms in which specialists and users participate together), rather than relying exclusively on slow official academy decisions. This development is still in its early stages, and it has not yet settled the future relationship between the official academies and these new initiatives.
