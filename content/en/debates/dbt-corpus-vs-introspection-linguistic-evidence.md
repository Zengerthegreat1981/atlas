# Corpus Data vs. Native-Speaker Introspection as Linguistic Evidence

A long-standing methodological debate in linguistics about which type of evidence is most reliable for building a linguistic theory. One side relies on the intuitive judgments (introspection) of native speakers on the acceptability of artificial sentences, which may never actually have been uttered. The other relies on data extracted from large corpora of recorded actual language use.

## The Generative Intuition Position

Generative grammar methodologically relies on the native speaker's intuitive judgment about the acceptability of a particular sentence. A speaker might be asked whether a sentence is grammatically correct in their language, even if that sentence was never actually uttered before. Proponents of this method justify it by arguing that the theoretical goal is not merely to describe what has been said. The goal is to uncover the underlying linguistic "competence" that enables a speaker to judge the acceptability of an infinite number of sentences they have never heard. They also argue that certain sentences, though rarely found in any corpus, remain decisive evidence for the validity or invalidity of a particular grammatical rule. Examples are sentences that test fine grammatical constraints that rarely occur naturally in spontaneous speech.

## The Corpus Linguistics Position

Proponents of corpus linguistics extend a structuralist tradition that goes back in part to the distributional methods of Zellig Harris (*Methods in Structural Linguistics*, 1951). Charles Fillmore (1992) coined the contrast between "armchair linguistics" and corpus work. They argue that a speaker's intuitive judgment is vulnerable to serious methodological biases. The speaker, and often the linguist judging his own sentence, may be influenced by prescriptive standards learned at school that do not reflect actual spontaneous usage. The speaker may also display contradictions between conscious judgment and unconscious linguistic behavior. Large corpora, by contrast, provide objective evidence that can be replicated and checked independently by other researchers. Statistical analysis of the frequency of patterns in a corpus reveals fine usage tendencies, such as the preference for one grammatical construction over another in particular contexts, which intuition alone finds hard to observe with comparable quantitative precision.

## The Core Point of Disagreement: Negative Data

A significant part of this debate centers on "negative data," that is, unacceptable grammatical sentences. Intuition can directly judge that a particular sentence is "unacceptable," which is a crucial piece of evidence for building constraining grammatical rules. A corpus, however large, cannot prove that a sentence is impossible merely by its absence. A sentence may be absent purely by statistical chance rather than because it is grammatically forbidden. This is considered a strong argument for the necessity of intuition in some aspects of grammatical research, however large the corpus becomes.

## Contemporary Practical Convergence

Most contemporary linguists in practice combine the two sources. They use corpora to discover broad usage patterns that are statistically reliable and recurrent. They retain intuition as a necessary tool for testing the precise limits of grammatical acceptability in borderline cases that rarely occur naturally. This practice does not settle the deeper theoretical dispute over which of the two sources is, in principle, the fundamental "linguistic evidence" on which a theory should be built.

## Experimental Studies Testing the Reliability of Intuition

Experimental syntax has tested the reliability of linguistic intuition directly, by collecting acceptability judgments from large groups of ordinary speakers and comparing them with the judgments published in the linguistic literature. Critics (for example Gibson and Fedorenko) argued that informal judgments by linguists risk bias, since the linguist may be influenced by the theory under test. Systematic replication work by Jon Sprouse, Diogo Almeida and colleagues, however, found that the large majority of judgments published in the leading generative literature (about 95 to 98 percent in the samples tested) were replicated in formal experiments with naive participants, though it also found that results could differ for subtle contrasts. Studies comparing judgments with corpus frequencies find that the two sources generally correlate but can diverge, especially for rare or complex constructions.

## The Replication Crisis and the Methodology of Generative Linguistics

The debate was sharpened by the wider "replication crisis" in psychology, which prompted questions about whether published grammatical judgments would hold up when retested on larger samples. As noted above, the replication studies largely supported the published data, but the discussion has led some journals and researchers to encourage formal experimental evidence alongside informal judgments, particularly for contested or subtle contrasts.

## Computational Linguistics as a Third Party

Modern computational linguistics has added a new dimension to this debate. Large language models, trained statistically on vast text corpora with no explicit grammar programmed in, can perform well on tests of grammatical acceptability, such as minimal-pair benchmarks like BLiMP (Warstadt and colleagues, 2020), although their performance varies by phenomenon. This raises new questions. Does such statistical learning suffice to capture the abstract linguistic competence that generative grammar attributes to specialized innate knowledge? Or does good performance on such tests reflect something different from human grammatical knowledge? The question is currently open.
