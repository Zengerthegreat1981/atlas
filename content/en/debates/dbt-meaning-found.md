# Discovered or Created?

Is meaning discovered or created? This is the deepest philosophical disagreement inside existential therapy, and the one with the clearest effect on what the therapist does. The question is whether meaning is something already present in a situation, waiting to be found, or something human beings make for themselves in a silent universe. One side gives the therapist permission to guide; the other forbids it. The dispute is about the nature of meaning itself, not about the logotherapy branch as an institution — though the branch exists largely because its founder took one side of this question and built a method on it.

## Frankl's Position — Meaning Is Discovered

Viktor Frankl argued that every situation holds a latent meaning waiting to be discovered, even suffering that cannot be changed. In *Man's Search for Meaning* (1959; German original, 1946), he described his experience in the Nazi concentration camps of 1942–1945 as the empirical ground of this claim: those who survived with their humanity intact were those who found a meaning in their suffering — a task, a love, or an attitude toward the unavoidable. Life, in Frankl's famous formulation, asks the questions, and the human being answers with his life. The position presupposes an order of meaning wider than the individual: meaning is not invented but recognised, and the therapist may therefore direct, suggest, and encourage the client toward what the situation demands. Frankl's three sources of meaning — creative work, love, and the attitude taken toward unavoidable suffering — are presented as discoveries about the world, not preferences of the therapist.

## The Secular Position — Meaning Is Created

Irvin Yalom stated the opposing view in *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980), in the chapter on meaninglessness. The cosmos is silent and gives no meaning ready-made; existential emptiness is not an illness but an accurate description of the position of a being thrown into a universe without a prior design. Meaning is made — by commitment and engagement in a project that reaches beyond the self — and it can be made, collapse, and be made again. Yalom criticised Frankl's directive tendency explicitly, arguing that imposing a "latent meaning" on the client risks denying the client's freedom to build her own meaning. On this view, the therapist's role is to accompany the client in the making, not to point at what is already there. The position descends from Sartre and from the secular wing of existential philosophy, for which the absence of given meaning is the starting point of freedom rather than a problem to be solved.

## The Effect in the Session

The difference is visible in the room. A therapist who believes meaning is discovered may guide, propose, and stimulate — which is what the meaning branch does in practice, using Socratic dialogue to lead the client toward the latent meaning of the situation. A therapist who believes meaning is created regards guidance as tutelage and confines herself to accompanying the client in the making. The dispute also explains an asymmetry that is often noticed but rarely explained: it is the meaning branch alone, among existential currents, that possesses standardised interventions and empirical evidence. A directive intervention can be manualised and measured; non-directive accompaniment cannot, which is why the evidence debate and the meaning debate are the same argument seen from two sides.

## Is There a Middle Way?

Some contemporaries propose a middle position: meaning is neither invented from nothing nor awaited ready-made, but discovered inside what is actually available to this person in this situation. The proposal is practically useful — it lets the therapist attend to real possibilities without claiming a cosmic order — but it does not settle the philosophical question, because it leaves open whether those possibilities were "there" before the therapist named them. The contemporary status is therefore a working pluralism: logotherapists continue to practise discovery, secular existentialists continue to practise creation, and many therapists move between the two without resolving the metaphysics. The choice still matters, because it decides whether the therapist may use Frankl's directive tools, such as Socratic dialogue aimed at a latent meaning, or must hold to a more reticent phenomenological silence that refuses any intervention in a predetermined direction.

The debate also touches the question of who the therapist serves. If meaning is discovered, the therapist serves a truth larger than the client: the meaning that the situation demands, which the therapist is trained to discern. If meaning is created, the therapist serves the client's freedom: nothing is owed to any order beyond the person herself. The first view can justify the therapist's authority — she knows what the situation asks — and it can also justify its abuse, when the therapist's own values are presented as the situation's demands. The second view protects the client from that imposition, but it can leave the therapist with nothing to offer except accompaniment, which some clients experience as abandonment at the moment they most need direction.
