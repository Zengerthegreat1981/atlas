# Universalism versus Particularism

Should the same rule apply to everyone, or should judgement depend on who is involved? This is the debate between universalism and particularism — not to be confused with the debate between universalism and relativism, which concerns whether moral truths and cultural values hold across cultures. Universalism versus particularism is a narrower, sociological question, framed by Talcott Parsons as one of his pattern variables: when an actor must decide how to treat another person, should they apply a general standard that ignores the particular relationship, or should the specific relationship — kinship, friendship, loyalty — shape the judgement? The two positions describe different ways of organising social life, and the tension between them runs through law, bureaucracy, family, and politics.

## The Universalist Position: One Rule for All

The universalist position holds that legitimate social order requires general standards applied impartially. A judge should decide a case on the law, not on whether the defendant is a relative; an examiner should mark a paper on its merits, not on who wrote it; a civil servant should allocate benefits by rule, not by favour. Max Weber, in *Economy and Society* (1922), made the institutional case for universalism: modern bureaucracy works precisely because it is impersonal, applying written rules to all cases alike, and this impersonality is what makes it fair, predictable, and efficient. Talcott Parsons, in *The Social System* (1951), formalised the idea as a pattern variable: the universalism–particularism dilemma is one of five binary choices every actor must resolve before acting, and modern societies are characterised by a systematic shift toward the universalist pole — universalistic standards, achievement-based evaluation, specific roles, and affective neutrality. Robert Merton, in his essay on the normative structure of science (1942, reprinted in *Social Theory and Social Structure*), treated universalism as one of the norms of science itself: a claim is judged by impersonal criteria, not by the nationality, race, or status of the person who makes it. For universalists, particularism is not warmth or loyalty but corruption by another name: nepotism, favouritism, and the rule of connections rather than rules.

## The Particularist Position: Relationships Matter

The particularist reply is that no human society can run on universal rules alone, and that pretending otherwise is both unrealistic and inhumane. Ferdinand Tönnies, in *Community and Society* (1887), drew the classic contrast between *Gemeinschaft* — community bound by kinship, custom, and feeling, where people are valued for who they are — and *Gesellschaft* — society bound by contract and interest, where people are valued for what they do. Particularism is the logic of *Gemeinschaft*: a parent shows leniency to their own child, a friend helps a friend before a stranger, and a community cares for its own members first. Particularists argue that such partiality is not a failure of morality but a different morality — an ethics of loyalty, care, and responsibility to concrete others, as opposed to an ethics of abstract principle. Michael Walzer, in *Spheres of Justice* (1983), argued that different spheres of social life properly follow different distributive principles: the market may run on universal rules, but family, friendship, and membership in a political community involve particular obligations that cannot be reduced to impartiality. Carol Gilligan, in *In a Different Voice* (1982), contrasted an ethics of abstract justice with an ethics of care attentive to concrete relationships, a contrast that later writers used to question the primacy of impartial rules.

## Arguments and Counter-Arguments

Each side accuses the other of a characteristic blindness. Universalists argue that particularism, however warm it feels, is the root of inequality: when jobs, licences, and public goods are distributed by connection rather than by rule, those without connections lose, and the result is a society of insiders and outsiders. They point to patronage systems and family firms as evidence that particularism entrenches privilege. Particularists reply that universalism, pushed to its limit, produces a cold and alienating world: a welfare state that treats everyone by the same formula ignores the real differences between their situations, and a bureaucracy that refuses to consider who a person is can be cruelly unjust in its impartiality. They also argue that universal rules are never as neutral as they claim — they are written by particular people, in particular cultures, and they tend to favour those whose lives already fit the rule. Parsons himself recognised that no society is purely one or the other: the pattern variables describe dilemmas that actors resolve differently in different roles, and even the most universalistic modern society reserves particularistic treatment for the family, while even the most traditional society uses universalistic standards in markets and courts.

## Contemporary Status

The debate remains live because modern societies keep rediscovering that they need both principles. Affirmative action policies, for example, are universalistic in their aim — equal opportunity for all — but particularistic in their means, taking group membership into account. Debates about immigration turn on the same tension: should states treat all human beings by the same standard, or do citizens have particular obligations to their compatriots that justify favouring them? S. N. Eisenstadt's argument for "multiple modernities" (2000) suggests that different societies resolve the tension in different ways: some modernities lean toward universalistic institutions, others preserve strong particularistic loyalties within a modern framework, and neither is a failure to modernise. In sociology, the lasting contribution of the debate is Parsons's framing itself: universalism and particularism are not stages of history, with one replacing the other, but permanent poles of social action, and every institution can be described by where it sits between them. The question is no longer which side wins, but how a society balances the two — and what happens when the balance breaks down.
