# Positionality and Reflexivity in Ethnographic Fieldwork

A recent methodological debate about **the anthropologist's own position** and its effect on the research process: how does **being** a woman or a man, a Westerner or someone from the Global South, shape what you see and hear in the field?

## The Traditional Position (Positivist Anthropology)

**Classical anthropology** assumed that the researcher could be **neutral**:
- The data are objective
- Participant observation makes you a **neutral** observer
- Your personality and beliefs do not affect the results
- The result: an ethnographic text **without a voice** for the researcher (the invisible narrator)

**Example:** Malinowski's published Trobriand monographs, such as *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922), present the ethnographer largely as a detached observer, while his private field diary, published posthumously in 1967, revealed the personal struggles and attitudes that the monographs left out.

## The Reflexive Position (Reflexive Anthropology)

**Feminists and contemporary anthropologists** said: this is **a falsehood**.

**Positionality:**
- You are **not** neutral: you are a woman or a man
- You are a Westerner entering a non-Western society (an implicit colonial power)
- You belong to a particular class, race and age group
- **All of this affects** who speaks with you, what they say, and what you hear

**A realistic example:**
- If you are a **foreign man**, women in the society **may not trust you** (fear of a foreign male authority)
- If you are a **foreign woman**, men may not admit you into **exclusive spaces** (cultural prohibition)
- If you share the **same nationality and skin color**, you may have a privilege you do not see

**Reflexivity:**
- The solution is not to **hide** your position, but to **confront it directly**
- Study how you are viewed
- Acknowledge the limits of your vision
- **Write about yourself** as part of the ethnography

## The Heated Debate

### First wave: traditional resistance (1980–1990)

**Traditional researchers** said:
- Including the self would make the work **self-indulgent** and **unreliable**
- Ethnography is about "the other," not about the researcher
- Excessive reflexivity **distracts the reader**

**The reflexive reply:**
- Ethnography is **always** about the researcher, whether or not they conceal it
- Concealment is **deception** (claiming to have no stake in the findings)
- Transparency is more honest than the claimed neutrality

### Second wave: the feminist division (1990–2000)

**Feminists themselves were divided:**
- Some said: reflexivity is dangerous, since it may turn anthropology into **personal memoir**
- Others said: no, this is **necessary**; ignoring your position means ignoring your power (especially as a woman or someone from the Global South)

**Example:** a researcher studying her own community, as in debates over the "native" anthropologist, occupies a distinctive position: she has knowledge from the inside, but also a personal stake. **Suppressing this would deprive anthropology of strength.**

### Third wave: the current balance (2000–present)

Today, the trend is to:
- **Place your position** at the center of the text (not at the margins)
- **But** not devote every page to yourself: the "I" must **serve** the larger understanding
- Use your position as a **tool** for deeper understanding, not as a center for self-absorption

## Practical Applications

### Example 1: studying a village wedding
- If you are a **woman**, you may be accepted into **intimate spaces** (the women's tent) from which men are excluded
- Do not ignore this advantage: **mention it**, because it explains how you obtained your information

### Example 2: translation and dialogue
- When you translate a sensitive word, your position **shapes** the translation
- Acknowledge this: "As a researcher from a large city, I chose this word to reflect..."
