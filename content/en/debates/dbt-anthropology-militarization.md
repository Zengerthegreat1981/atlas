# Anthropology & Militarization

An ongoing ethical and professional debate within anthropology over the legitimacy of anthropologists' collaboration with military and intelligence institutions. This includes providing direct cultural knowledge for operational purposes, or taking part in military-funded programs. It also concerns the conflict between the researcher's ethical commitment to the people they study and their engagement, or use, by a party seeking to control or subdue them.

## Historical Roots

The American anthropologist David Price, one of the leading researchers of this history, argues that anthropology's relationship with military projects is as old as the discipline itself. It runs from the colonial use of ethnographic knowledge to administer colonies, through the wartime intelligence work of American anthropologists in the Second World War, to the controversial "Project Camelot" of the mid-1960s. Through that project, the US Army sought to fund social research on the causes of insurgency and revolution, with Chile as a planned site, and it was cancelled after public exposure. Price documented this history in detail in *Weaponizing Anthropology: Social Science in Service of the Militarized State*.

## Contemporary Debate: The Human Terrain System

The debate reached its contemporary peak with the Human Terrain System (HTS) program, launched by the US Army in the first decade of the 2000s. The program recruited social scientists, including anthropologists, to accompany military units in Afghanistan and Iraq and to provide local social and cultural knowledge in support of counterinsurgency operations. A large number of anthropologists objected to the program. Price, together with colleagues including Roberto González, helped found the "Network of Concerned Anthropologists" in 2007, whose members publicly pledged not to take part in counterinsurgency work. The AAA's executive board issued a statement in 2007 criticizing the program as an unacceptable application of anthropological expertise. The concerns it raised centred on research ethics principles: the voluntary informed consent of those studied, transparency about how research results are used, and avoiding harm to research subjects, all of which are difficult to secure when the researcher is embedded within a military force engaged in combat operations.

## The Two Sides of the Debate

- **Position of principled refusal**: Any direct collaboration with military or intelligence institutions corrupts the essence of ethnographic work, which rests on mutual trust with those studied. It turns cultural knowledge into a tool of control and subjugation rather than of mutual understanding, regardless of the stated intentions of the individual researcher.
- **Position of "critical engagement"**: Complete withdrawal from any interaction with military institutions leaves the formulation of security policy without any precise cultural and humanitarian input. The presence of informed anthropologists within these systems may reduce the cultural harm caused by military decisions made in complete cultural ignorance, even though such engagement remains fraught with serious ethical risks.

## Significance

This debate reflects a deeper tension over the independence of the social sciences from sources of funding and power that may seek to use their findings for coercive purposes. The tension has not been resolved, even within the professional associations themselves, despite their publicly stated positions.

## Limitations

The dividing line between "rejected collaboration" and "ethically acceptable advice" is not always clear in practice. The range of possible interactions between anthropology and security institutions extends from general cultural teaching to direct operational accompaniment, with no normative dividing line agreed upon across these different degrees.
