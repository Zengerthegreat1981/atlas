# Is 'Religion' a Definable Analytic Category?

Is there actually a single "phenomenon" called "religion" that can be defined by an all-inclusive, exclusive boundary, in which Christianity, Buddhism, Siberian shamanism and Australian Aboriginal rituals all share? Or is the very term "religion" a modern Western academic invention, which imposes an artificial unity on very different practices and conceptions that did not necessarily classify themselves within a single category before the scholars gave it this name?

## Jonathan Z. Smith's Thesis: "Religion" as the Scholar's Invention

The most incisive and widely cited formulation of this question came from Jonathan Z. Smith in *Imagining Religion* (1982): "There is no data for religion." That is, any ethnographic or textual observation arrives unclassified in advance as "religious." The classification itself is an analytical act that the researcher performs when he decides that this practice or that text belongs to a category called "religion," rather than discovering an objective essential boundary existing in reality, awaiting discovery.

## Methodological Consequences

If Smith's thesis is sound, the researcher's task changes fundamentally. Instead of asking "what is the essence of religion?" (as the phenomenologists do: Otto searching for the "numinous," Eliade searching for the "sacred"), the correct question becomes "why, and in whose service, does the classification of this particular practice under the category of religion serve, in this particular research context?" This question approaches the position of Talal Asad from a different angle (anthropological-historical in his case, methodological-analytical in Smith's), but both agree that "religion" is a historically produced category, not a naturally given one.

## Position on the Debate over Reduction

This debate intersects with the debate over reductionism and non-reductionism (`dbt-reductionism-vs-non-reductionism-religious-studies`), but it is not identical to it. That debate asks "can religion be explained by non-religious factors?", presupposing a specific "religion" already defined and in need of explanation. Smith's question is logically prior: "does a coherent category called religion actually exist at all, one that is worth asking the question of explanation about?"

## An Overlooked Precedent: Wilfred Cantwell Smith

About two decades before Jonathan Z. Smith, Wilfred Cantwell Smith (`thk-wilfred-cantwell-smith`) argued in *The Meaning and End of Religion* (1962, `wrk-meaning-and-end-of-religion-cantwell-smith`) that the singular abstract term "religion" is a modern European invention that does not reflect how believers actually lived their faith. He proposed abandoning it in favor of a distinction between personal living "faith" and the collective "cumulative tradition," which can be studied objectively. His argument is linguistic, historical and humanistic in character, unlike the later epistemological-analytical argument of Jonathan Z. Smith. Yet both arrive at the same caution about treating "religion" as a unified, essential, ready-made entity.

## The Constraint

Critics of the category raise a practical question: if the field abandons the term "religion" entirely, with what analytical tool does it continue its comparative work? Jonathan Z. Smith himself did not call for abandoning the term. In "Religion, Religions, Religious" (1998) he argued that "religion" should be retained as a second-order, self-consciously defined term of scholarly discourse, for which the scholar must give an explicit account. Wilfred Cantwell Smith's proposal to drop it, by contrast, left the comparative question open.

## The Middle Response: Saler and "Family Resemblance"

The anthropologist Benson Saler (`thk-benson-saler`) offered the most famous methodological response rejecting both sides of the debate in *Conceptualizing Religion* (`wrk-conceptualizing-religion-saler`, 1993). He positions himself between the complete abandonment of the term "religion" (as proposed by Wilfred Cantwell Smith) and the view that it is an arbitrary construction of the scholar. He proposes using it as a flexible analytical tool in the Wittgensteinian sense of "family resemblance": a group of phenomena sharing an interlocking network of recurring features, without a strict all-inclusive essential boundary. Saler also draws on prototype theory from cognitive psychology. According to Saler, this suffices to permit comparative work to continue without falling into the trap of assuming a fixed natural essence.
