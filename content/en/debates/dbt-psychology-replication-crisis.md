# The Replication Crisis in Psychological Science

A methodological and epistemological earthquake that shook the field of experimental and social psychology following the failure to replicate more than half of the studies published in prestigious journals, igniting a sharp dispute over the credibility of accumulated psychological knowledge and the reform of research practices.

## First position: the methodological reform and open science movement (doubting the robustness of classic behavioral effects)

This position took off after the publication of the Reproducibility Project: Psychology (2015) led by Brian Nosek, which attempted to replicate 100 studies from three leading journals and found that about 36% of the replications produced statistically significant results (compared with 97% of the originals), while about 39% were subjectively judged to have replicated the original effect. Reformers (such as Uri Simonsohn and Daniël Lakens) hold that the field was contaminated with questionable research practices (QRPs) such as statistical fishing for significance (p-hacking), formulating hypotheses after the results are known (HARKing), and publication bias that hides null results. This camp demands mandatory preregistration, open data sharing, higher standards of statistical power, and a reassessment of the grand theories.

## Second position: defenders of context sensitivity and conceptual replication (situational specificity and the limits of abstract statistical generalization)

Senior researchers (such as Susan Fiske, Timothy Wilson, and Daniel Gilbert) argue that failure to replicate does not necessarily mean the original phenomenon is false; psychological and social phenomena are highly sensitive to the historical, cultural and demographic context of the sample and to the experimenter's clinical skill. They hold that blind literal replication may fail because of changing cultural norms or subtle differences in the laboratory environment, warning against what Fiske called "methodological terrorism" and an inspectorial tendency that may stifle innovation and bold theorizing in the behavioral sciences in favor of dry technical statistical audits.

## Reforms and Their Effects

Responses have included preregistration, open data and materials (for example on the Open Science Framework) and Registered Reports, in which journals accept a study before results are known. A comparison by Scheel, Schijen and Lakens (2021) found that a much smaller share of Registered Reports reported positive results (about 44%) than standard articles (about 96%), which is consistent with a reduction in publication bias.

## Why this dispute matters clinically and scientifically

This debate rebuilt the scientific foundations of psychology as a whole; it led to a rigorous sifting of textbooks and cast serious doubt on several well-known phenomena (such as ego depletion, some social-priming effects, and power posing). Clinically, the debate obliges the therapist not to adopt any interventional technique without examining the strength of its empirical evidence and its independent open replications.
