# Universalism versus Relativism

Are there moral principles, human rights, and social-scientific laws that hold for all people in all places — or is every judgement bound to its own cultural context? This debate, which spans anthropology, sociology, and moral philosophy, asks whether universal standards are a genuine discovery or a disguised imposition. Universalists argue that some things are true of human beings as such: that torture is wrong everywhere, that all societies face the same basic problems of order and reproduction, and that sociology can therefore formulate general laws. Relativists argue that values, norms, and even categories of thought are products of particular cultures, so judging one culture by another's standards is a category mistake — and historically, a tool of domination. The debate is not an abstract quarrel: it decides how sociologists study other societies and whether international institutions may intervene in the name of human rights.

## The Relativist Position: Boas and Cultural Relativism

The relativist position was formulated as a deliberate attack on the racist evolutionary theories of the nineteenth century, which ranked cultures on a ladder from "primitive" to "civilised." Franz Boas, in *The Mind of Primitive Man* (1911), argued that cultures cannot be ranked on any objective scale of higher and lower, because each culture is a historical product with its own internal logic, and every person perceives the world through the lens of the culture they were raised in. Ruth Benedict extended the argument in *Patterns of Culture* (1934), showing that traits which look bizarre or immoral from outside — such as the Kwakiutl potlatch or the Zuni avoidance of competition — make sense as expressions of a coherent cultural configuration. Melville Herskovits applied the principle directly to politics in 1947, when he drafted the American Anthropological Association's "Statement on Human Rights," which criticised the United Nations' draft Universal Declaration as an imposition of Western values on other cultures — a form of cultural imperialism dressed up as universality. For relativists, the method follows from the ethics: ethnographers must suspend their own judgements and understand a practice from within, and the discipline must refuse to rank cultures.

## The Universalist Position: Human Rights and General Laws

Universalists reply that relativism, taken strictly, leaves us unable to condemn anything; the ethnographic case is complicated by the distinction between understanding and judging. If every practice must be judged only by its own culture's standards, then practices that harm women, minorities, or dissenters within a culture become immune to criticism, and relativism ends up complicit with local oppression. Renato Rosaldo's essay "Of Headhunters and Soldiers" (1984) and his *Culture and Truth* (1989) distinguish cultural from ethical relativism: understanding Ilongot headhunting from within, as an ethnographer must, is a different matter from endorsing or condemning it. Universalists in sociology make a parallel scientific argument. Émile Durkheim's comparative method assumed that social facts — such as the relationship between integration and suicide — operate across societies, and Robert Merton's sociology of science treated universalism as a norm of science itself: truth claims are judged by impersonal criteria, not by who makes them. Jack Donnelly, in *Universal Human Rights in Theory and Practice* (1989), defended a middle universalism: human rights are universal because they protect the human dignity that all people share, but their implementation can and should vary with cultural context.

## Middle Positions and the Relativism Distinction

Much of the most productive work in the debate has come from refusing the stark choice. Clifford Geertz, in "Anti Anti-Relativism" (1984), opposed both the relativist who denies any cross-cultural standard and the anti-relativist who fears all cultural difference: we must interpret other cultures in their own terms, but interpretation is not the same as endorsement. A second distinction separates methodological from ethical relativism: adopting a neutral stance while doing fieldwork — learning the internal logic of a practice — does not oblige the researcher to suspend all moral judgement as a citizen or as a political actor. A third position, sometimes called dialogical or critical universalism, proposes that universal principles can be reached through cross-cultural dialogue rather than imposed from one centre: norms such as the prohibition of torture gain their authority precisely because they can be defended in many cultural idioms at once. Michael Walzer, in *Thick and Thin* (1994), made a similar point: every culture has a "thick," local morality, but beneath it lies a "thin," minimal morality — against murder, oppression, and cruelty — that people recognise across cultures when they encounter one another's struggles.

## Contemporary Status

The debate remains unresolved, but its centre of gravity has shifted. Few anthropologists or sociologists now defend absolute relativism, and few defend a universalism that ignores cultural difference. The live questions are practical and institutional: when may international courts, aid agencies, or researchers intervene in the name of universal standards, and who gets to define those standards? Kwame Anthony Appiah, in *Cosmopolitanism* (2006), argued that universal values and respect for local difference are compatible: we can share obligations to strangers while disagreeing profoundly about the good life. In sociology, the debate has also become methodological: cross-national survey research assumes that concepts such as trust, religiosity, and family can be measured comparably across cultures, while ethnographers warn that such comparisons smuggle in Western categories. The lasting contribution of the debate is therefore a discipline: any universal claim in sociology must now survive the relativist's challenge — to show that it is not merely one culture's values projected onto the rest of humanity.
