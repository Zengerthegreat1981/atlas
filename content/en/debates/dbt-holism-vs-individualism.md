# Holism versus Individualism

The holism-versus-individualism debate asks what social reality is made of: wholes that are more than their parts, or individuals whose actions and relations make up everything there is. It is the ontological cousin of the micro–macro debate, but it is not the same dispute. Micro–macro is about levels of explanation; holism–individualism is about what exists. The holist says that groups, institutions, and cultures have properties that no individual has and that cannot be reduced to individual properties. The individualist says that only individuals and their relations are real, and that talk of wholes is a convenient shorthand. Everything in social theory — how we explain, what we can predict, what we hold responsible — depends on which answer we give.

## The Holist Position

Émile Durkheim is the founding holist. In *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) he argued that social facts are external to the individual, constraining, and *sui generis* — of their own kind — and that a society is not the sum of its members but a reality that emerges from their association. A crowd, a religion, a legal system: each has properties — a suicide rate, a sacred quality, a binding force — that belong to no single member. Later holisms took different forms: structural functionalism, which treated societies as systems with needs and functions; structuralism, which located the real in relations between positions rather than in persons; and the claim, made by many sociologists of culture, that meanings and norms exist as shared, public realities that individuals inherit rather than invent. The holist argument is that reduction fails: explain a marriage rate or a market crash by individual psychology alone, and you lose the phenomenon you set out to explain.

## The Individualist Position

Methodological individualism, the doctrine most associated with Max Weber, holds that sociology must build its explanations from the meaningful action of individuals. In *Economy and Society* (1922) Weber treated collectives as legitimate objects only insofar as they could be traced back to the actions and orientations of individuals. Karl Popper sharpened the position in *The Open Society and Its Enemies* (1945), attacking holism and historicism as doctrines that licence prophecy about the fate of whole societies and excuse the individual from responsibility. Jon Elster, in *Nuts and Bolts for the Social Sciences* (1989), restated the individualist programme for a later generation: explanations in the social sciences must specify mechanisms — the beliefs, desires, and opportunities of individuals — through which outcomes are produced. The individualist argument is that wholes explain nothing on their own: a "society" that acts, wants, or needs is a metaphor smuggled in as an agent.

## Emergence: The Middle Ground

The most influential recent attempts to settle the debate refuse both extremes and argue for emergence. Roy Bhaskar's critical realism, set out in *The Possibility of Naturalism* (1979), holds that social structures are real and causally effective — they enable and constrain action — but that they exist only through human activity and are transformed by it. Margaret Archer developed this into the morphogenetic approach in *Realist Social Theory* (1995): structure and agency are distinct strata of reality, each with its own emergent properties, operating over different stretches of time, so that structures condition action, action interacts, and the outcome elaborates or reproduces structure. On this view the holist is right that wholes have causal powers no individual has, and the individualist is right that those powers exist only in and through what people do. Emergence is not a compromise for politeness; it is a different ontology, in which levels are real and related.

## Contemporary Status

The debate continues, but its centre has moved. Analytical sociology demands mechanisms at the level of actors while accepting that mechanisms can operate on emergent entities such as organisations and networks. Network analysis treats relations as real structures without treating "society" as a single whole. And the question of responsibility keeps the dispute practical: can a corporation be blamed, can a culture be held responsible, can a nation apologise — or must blame always land on individuals? Contemporary sociology mostly answers "both": collectives have emergent properties and causal powers, but those powers work through the actions of individuals, and explanations that skip either level are incomplete. The debate survives because every explanation, and every assignment of blame, quietly takes a side in it.

The dispute has also gained a new testing ground in the study of organisations and digital platforms. A social media feed, a credit-scoring algorithm, or a corporate policy is plainly not an individual, yet it shapes what millions of individuals do; at the same time it exists only because engineers built it, users feed it, and managers maintain it. To call the algorithm a cause is holism; to dissolve it into the actions of its designers and users is individualism. Neither description is wrong, and neither is sufficient alone — which is why the old philosophical debate now reappears in practical arguments about who is accountable when a system harms people, and what kind of intervention could change it.
