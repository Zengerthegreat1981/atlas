# Care Work Value in Capitalism: Recognition vs. Devaluation

## The Debate

Why is "care work" (child-rearing, caring for the elderly and the sick, nursing and teaching) valued with low pay and low social status, despite being indispensable to the continuation of any society? And how can political economy and feminist anthropology restore the recognition of its real value within a capitalist system that measures value primarily by the market and by profit?

## First Position: Critique of Capitalism's Devaluation of Care Work

The feminist economist Nancy Folbre (University of Massachusetts Amherst), across a series of works from *The Invisible Heart: Economics and Family Values* (2001) to the edited volume *For Love and Money: Care Provision in the United States* (2012), developed a systematic critique of traditional economics' blindness to care work. Most care work is "non-market" in nature (performed for free within the family), or paid very poorly when it is marketed (as in the work of nannies and nurses), and it is not counted at all in the gross domestic product, although, according to Folbre, it is what actually produces, develops and maintains the "human capabilities" on which the entire economy rests. Folbre argued that this "devaluation" is not incidental but structural: the capitalist economy benefits from the availability of cheap or free care work (performed mostly by women) without recognizing its real value in official accounts.

## Second Position: Alternative Explanations for Low Pay

Some economists link the low wages of care professions to "neutral" market factors (such as an oversupply of qualified labor for these jobs, or the difficulty of measuring productivity in them), not necessarily to a structural gender dynamic. They propose market solutions (such as private care insurance) rather than a radical restructuring of the economy.

## Earlier Roots

The question has a longer history in the "domestic labour debate" within Marxist and feminist political economy. Margaret Benston's "The Political Economy of Women's Liberation" (1969), Mariarosa Dalla Costa and Selma James's *The Power of Women and the Subversion of the Community* (1972), and Wally Seccombe's "The Housewife and her Labour under Capitalism" (1974) asked how unpaid housework contributes to the reproduction of labour power, while Silvia Federici's *Wages Against Housework* (1975) turned the question into a political demand. Marilyn Waring's *If Women Counted* (1988) later criticised the exclusion of unpaid work from national accounting systems. For paid care, Paula England, Michelle Budig and Folbre examined the relative pay of care occupations in "Wages of Virtue" (2002), finding evidence of a pay penalty for care work, which differs across occupations.

## Contemporary Development

These debates helped crystallize the concept of the "care economy" as a distinct field of public policy, with growing calls to incorporate the value of unpaid work (such as domestic child care) into alternative national income accounts, and to expand paid parental leave and government support for care providers. Feminist anthropology linked this debate to a broader analysis of how the burden of care is distributed globally through "global care chains," which transfer care workers from the Global South to perform this work in the families of the wealthy North.
