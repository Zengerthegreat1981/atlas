# The Debate over Explaining Revolutions: Skocpol's Structural Collapse vs. Sewell's Cultural-Ideological Critique

An influential discussion raised by the historian and sociologist William H. Sewell Jr. in response to Theda Skocpol's structural explanation of the great social revolutions, set out in her *States and Social Revolutions* (1979). Sewell's critique appeared in 1985 in the *Journal of Modern History*, together with a reply by Skocpol.

## Skocpol's Structural Position

Skocpol compared the French, Russian and Chinese revolutions and argued that social revolutions, meaning rapid transformations of state and class structures accompanied by class-based revolts, are explained by structural conditions rather than by the aims or ideologies of revolutionaries. In her account, old-regime states in agrarian societies, under pressure from more advanced international competitors, suffered administrative and military breakdown when they could not meet external challenges; the breakdown opened the way for peasant rebellions that the structure of agrarian class relations and village solidarity made possible. She argued that revolutionary ideologies and leaderships shaped the later outcomes, particularly the form of the new state, but that they did not explain why the revolutions occurred; she criticised "voluntarist" accounts that treated revolutions as made by determined vanguards.

## Sewell's Cultural-Ideological Critique

In "Ideologies and Social Revolutions: Reflections on the French Case" (1985), Sewell argued that the structural approach could not explain the French Revolution without attention to ideology and culture. He held that the collapse of royal authority created a political opening, but that the course of events was shaped by the way actors understood what was possible, using concepts such as popular sovereignty, the nation and citizenship that had developed in Enlightenment political thought and in the political culture of the late old regime. Ideology was therefore not simply a reflection of structural position but a causal element in how structural crisis was translated into revolutionary action. He proposed that structure and culture be treated as interacting, rather than treating the first as the cause and the second as dependent.

## Skocpol's Rejoinder

Skocpol replied in the same issue, "Cultural Idioms and Political Ideologies in the Revolutionary Reconstruction of State Power: A Rejoinder to Sewell," acknowledging a role for cultural idioms and ideologies in shaping the particular forms of post-revolutionary regimes, while maintaining that they are secondary to the structural conditions that make revolution possible. The Iranian Revolution of 1979, which she discussed in an essay of 1982 on the role of Shi'a Islam, was often cited in this connection as a case in which religious ideology and leadership were prominent.

## Impact

The exchange belongs to the broader "cultural turn" in comparative and historical sociology, in which attention to meaning, discourse and ideology was combined with structural analysis. Sewell developed his own position further in later work on how events can transform structures, collected in *Logics of History* (2005), and Skocpol's later *Social Revolutions in the Modern World* (1994) paid more attention to ideology and leadership.
