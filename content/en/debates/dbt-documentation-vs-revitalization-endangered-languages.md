# Documentation vs. Revitalization Priorities for Endangered Languages

A sharp practical debate within the field of endangered languages, arising from a difficult reality: the human and financial resources available to address thousands of endangered languages around the world are very limited relative to the scale of the crisis, which forces difficult choices about where effort should be directed first when it is impossible to do everything at once.

## The Documentation Priority: Saving What Can Be Saved Intellectually

Language documentation emerged as a distinct subfield in the 1990s, defined by Nikolaus Himmelmann (1998) as the compilation of a lasting, multipurpose record of a language, in contrast with grammatical description alone.

Proponents of giving priority to documentation argue that many languages endangered today have reached a very advanced stage of decline (only a few elderly speakers remain, or their actual transmission to new generations has already ceased). Full "revitalization" of their natural transmission across generations becomes practically impossible regardless of how much effort is expended, whereas their detailed scientific documentation (sound recordings, grammar, and dictionary) remains possible and urgent. This at least preserves the linguistic and cultural knowledge for future generations and researchers, even if the language itself is not destined to continue as a living spoken language.

## The Revitalization Priority: A Language as a Living Right, Not an Archival Trace

Proponents of giving priority to revitalization, most of whom are activists within the speaking communities themselves rather than outside researchers alone, argue that language is not merely archival material for scientific preservation but a living cultural and identity right of the community that speaks it. They argue that an exclusive focus on "documentation for archiving" can in practice become an implicit abandonment of the possibility of actually reviving the language. They cite the revival of Hebrew, from a language used mainly in writing and liturgy to a daily spoken language, and of Hawaiian, through immersion "language nest" schools, the Pūnana Leo, begun in 1983, as cases in which transmission was rebuilt. Both cases had unusual institutional support. Hebrew's shift was tied to immigration and state-building, and Hawaiian revitalization has produced many second-language users without restoring the language as the home language of the whole community. They are therefore weak guides to what a small field team can expect elsewhere.

## A Practical Rather Than Purely Theoretical Tension

This debate does not represent a theoretical disagreement about what language is or its value so much as a practical tension over allocating limited resources. A small fieldwork team visiting a single endangered linguistic community may face a real choice between spending the available time recording as much natural speech as possible from the last elderly speakers (documentation), or training young members of the community directly, accompanied by older people (revitalization through the master-apprentice method), with the sacrifice that each choice entails of time that could have been devoted to the other option.

## Reconciling Positions

Many contemporary researchers see documentation and revitalization as not necessarily mutually exclusive but practically complementary. Well-documented materials (analyzed texts, dictionaries, and sound recordings) become the primary teaching resource for any subsequent revitalization program, so that good documentation serves the goals of revitalization rather than competing with them. Nonetheless, the practical tension over the priority of allocating limited time and resources persists in concrete, real cases.

## An Ethical Dimension: Who Decides the Priority?

Many contemporary researchers add a central ethical dimension to this debate. They argue that the decision about the priority between documentation and revitalization should not be left to the external academic researcher alone, as the party with the most funding and technical training. Rather, it should fundamentally return to the speaking community itself. That community may sometimes prefer to invest its limited energy in symbolic and identity-based revitalization efforts, even if their chances of full success are statistically small. Another community may prefer to focus on pure scientific documentation if it considers that actual revitalization has become unrealistic in its particular circumstances. This shift toward the centrality of the community's own decision, rather than the external researcher's alone, is a widely noted methodological and ethical change in the field in recent decades.

## The Role of Digital Technology in Easing the Tension

Modern digital technologies (language-learning applications, educational video platforms, and artificial intelligence tools for processing low-resource languages) have helped ease some of the intensity of this practical tension. They have enabled faster and relatively cheaper digital documentation (recording audio and video with a mobile phone instead of previously expensive specialized equipment), which frees comparatively more time and resources for direct revitalization activities with the community. Nonetheless, disparities in the available resources between different languages (especially between languages with organized supportive communities and others with no institutional support at all) remain a decisive factor in determining which of the two paths becomes practically feasible in each specific case.

## Mixed Examples of Success

Beyond the frequently cited successes of Hebrew and Hawaiian, there are other, less complete cases that illustrate the complexity of balancing the two options. Some revitalization programs for Native American and Australian Aboriginal languages have achieved limited partial success (an increase in the number of learners of the language as a second language, without the language returning to being the full mother tongue of a broad community). Some researchers regard this result as a genuine partial success deserving continuation, while others see it as evidence that resources could have been directed more efficiently toward comprehensive scientific documentation rather than a partial revitalization that did not reach its full goal.
