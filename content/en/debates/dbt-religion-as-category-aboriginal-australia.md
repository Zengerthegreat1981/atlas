# Is 'Religion' an Adequate Category for Australian Aboriginal Systems?

A methodological debate within the comparative study of religion and religious anthropology, raised mainly by W. E. H. Stanner beginning with his essays in the 1950s and 1960s. Is the Western category "religion" (with its historical baggage of separating the sacred from the secular, and of presupposing a doctrine separate from social and legal practice) suitable for describing the belief and ritual systems of the Australian Aboriginal peoples? Or does it impose an alien structure that distorts their distinctiveness?

## The First Position: Descriptive Caution (Stanner)

Stanner (who himself wrote *On Aboriginal Religion*, 1959-63, and so did use the term) argued that the concept of "The Dreaming" brings together in a single system what Western categories usually separate into distinct fields: religion, law, myth, moral philosophy and the relationship to the land. He cautioned that translating this system into "religion" in the Western sense (that is, doctrine plus ritual plus an institution separate from daily life) risks obscuring its internal coherence, and he urged attention to the categories of the Aboriginal people themselves before classifying them with imported terminology. He also observed that "Dreaming" is itself a poor English rendering.

## The Second Position: Methodological Continuity (Elkin and the Tradition after Him)

By contrast, A. P. Elkin (whose *The Australian Aborigines*, 1938, was a widely read general account) and many anthropologists after him continued to use the analytical category "religion" (and related terms such as ritual, kinship and totemism) as a comparative tool. A common defence of this practice is that every academic analytical category is a partial external construction, and that abandoning comparison altogether would limit the field's ability to relate Aboriginal cases to other religious traditions.

## Later Echo

This Australian local debate preceded by decades the broader discussion raised later by Talal Asad and Jonathan Z. Smith on the historicity of the category "religion" itself as a historically produced category with roots in modern European history. It is sometimes cited as an early field case of a question that later became central in comparative religious theory.

## Indigenous Australian Contributions

Indigenous writers have approached the question largely on their own terms. Mary Graham, a Kombumerri scholar, describes an Aboriginal worldview in which relations with land and relations among people are fundamental and inseparable, an account in which law, ethics and what outsiders call spirituality do not form separate departments. Legal scholars, including Irene Watson (*Raw Law*, 2015) and C. F. Black (*The Land Is the Source of the Law*, 2011), likewise present law as grounded in land and ancestral relationship, which resists filing it under "religion" alone. Others have retained or adapted religious vocabulary: Anne Pattel-Gray edited *Aboriginal Spirituality* (1996), and the Rainbow Spirit Elders developed an Aboriginal Christian theology (1997). These works do not take a single position, and many do not engage Stanner or Elkin as a debate about academic categories. They show that the choice of terms also concerns who defines the tradition and for what purpose, including claims about land and law.

## Constraint

The older debate set out above took place mainly within anthropological literature written by European-Australian researchers, and the summary of Indigenous contributions here is selective.
