# Psychology of Religion: William James, Freud and Jung

Three founding paths for the psychological study of religion, neither theological nor missionary, emerged in the first decades of the twentieth century and share a single question: what does religious experience or religious belief do to the human psyche, and what is its psychological origin? The three answers differ radically. The field was preceded by an early empirical work, Edwin Starbuck's *The Psychology of Religion* (1899), which collected questionnaires on religious conversion in adolescence, and by G. Stanley Hall, who studied religion in the United States as a developmental phenomenon linked to adolescence.

## The Islamic Heritage: Analysing the Soul Before Psychology

Psychoanalytic analysis of religion was not absent from the Arab-Islamic heritage, although it did not take the form of an independent science. Al-Harith al-Muhasibi (d. 857 CE), in *Al-Ri'aya li-huquq Allah*, described the subtleties of the soul: ostentation, self-admiration and the motives behind actions. He presented the method of *muhasaba*, the reckoning or review of the soul's motives, which in spirit came close to inner analysis. Al-Ghazali (d. 1111 CE), in *Ihya' Ulum al-Din*, addressed the states of the heart, the soul, the intellect and appetite. In the books *Riyadat al-nafs* and *Kasr al-shahwatayn* (books 22 and 23) he drew a conception of the soul situated between appetite, anger and reason. In *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* he showed that the experiential states of the Sufis cannot be attained through demonstrative proof.

In the twentieth century, a current established under the name "Islamic psychology" emerged. Malik Badri, in *The Dilemma of Muslim Psychologists* (1979), argued that Muslim psychologists had been uncritically adopting Western secular assumptions, and called for the discipline to be reconsidered within an Islamic framework. This presence shows that the questions raised by James, Freud and Jung, about motives, religious experience and symbols, were addressed in Arabic culture in a different vocabulary (soul, heart, state). It also shows that reading Islamic Sufism within this framework remains an open task.

## James: Religious Experience as a Psychological Fact

In the Gifford Lectures he delivered in Edinburgh (1901–1902), collected in *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), William James adopted an empirical-pragmatic method. Instead of judging the doctrinal validity of religious beliefs, he studied religious experience as a psychological event that can be described and compared (states of conversion, cosmic consciousness, mysticism). Among his best-known distinctions are those between "healthy-mindedness" and the "sick soul", and between the once-born and the twice-born. He regarded the mystical state as having four characteristics: ineffability, noetic quality, transiency and passivity. He proposed the criterion of "fruits": judging the value of a religious experience by its practical effects on the life of the person who has it, as an alternative to a prior doctrinal judgement. His lectures on mysticism drew on first-person reports from several traditions, including Sufi material, and are an early Western attempt to compare mystical states through their practitioners' own descriptions.

## Freud: Religion as Illusion and Its Defensive Function

In *The Future of an Illusion* (1927), Sigmund Freud regarded religion as a collective "illusion" arising from the helplessness of the first child before the terrifying forces of nature. It projects the child's need for a strong, protective father onto a cosmic "God", which reproduces the childhood relationship with the father on a cosmic scale. He had compared religious rituals with obsessive-neurotic actions in "Obsessive Actions and Religious Practices" (1907), and in *Totem and Taboo* (1913) he traced the origin of religion to the killing of the primal father. He regarded the "oceanic feeling" that his friend Romain Rolland described, in *Civilization and Its Discontents* (1930), as a trace of the child's original state. For Freud, therefore, religion is a collective psychological defence mechanism whose function is to relieve the anxiety of existence, rather than to express a transcendent truth.

## Jung: Religion as Expression of Archetypes

Carl Jung opposed Freud's reduction. He saw religious symbols (God, the cross, the mandala) as expressions of "archetypes" in the "collective unconscious", whose function is not defensive but constructive, helping the psyche to grow and integrate ("individuation"). For Jung, religion is not an illusion to be negated but a symbolic language necessary for psychological health. He delivered the lectures *Psychology and Religion* at Yale University (1937, published 1938), and studied alchemy as a psychological symbol. The clearest direct application of this method to a specific religious text is his late essay "Answer to Job" (1952), in which he reads the Book of Job as a revelation of a duality within the image of God in the collective unconscious, rather than as a theological question about divine justice.

## Legacy and Division

This triple intersection established the academic field of "psychology of religion", but the field was divided from the outset among three paths: an empirical-descriptive path (James, and later Gordon Allport in *The Individual and His Religion* (1950), whose contrast there between mature and immature religion fed into his later distinction between intrinsic and extrinsic religiosity); a reductive analytic path (Freud); and a non-reductive analytic path (Jung). This division reproduces the general reduction debate within comparative religious studies. Later directions emerged from it, including object-relations theory in Ana-Maria Rizzuto's *The Birth of the Living God* (1979), the stages of faith of James Fowler (1981), research on religion and coping by Kenneth Pargament (1997), and the mysticism scales of Ralph Hood (1975).

## Criticism and Limits

James is criticised for relying on extreme cases of individuals, mostly Western and intellectual, and for privileging individual experience over collective and ritual religion, so that he does not explain congregational prayer or sharia. Freud is criticised for building his theory on the Christian-Jewish father in a patriarchal society; for the fact that his claims about the origin of religion are hypothetical history, not supported by the science of human beings (anthropology); and for the fact that most empirical studies do not support the claim that the image of God is merely a copy of the image of the father. Jung is criticised for the difficulty of testing the hypotheses of the collective unconscious and the archetypes, and for reading the religions of others through his own subjective symbolic criteria. The common criticism of all three is that they treat religion as an individual experience, whereas in most cultures religion is at its core collective and social.
