# Epicureanism

A philosophical movement founded by **Epicurus** (341–270 BCE) in Athens in 306 BCE, when he bought a garden and taught in it. Although his name later became, in many cultures, a synonym for someone "immersed in pleasures," genuine Epicureanism is **ethical and spiritual**: it aims to free human beings from **three fears** (death, pain, the gods).

## Context

After the death of Alexander the Great (323 BCE), the Greek cities lost their political independence, and philosophies spread that promised the individual inner "tranquillity" (ἀταραξία). Epicurus offered a "philosophical salvation" that was individual rather than collective.

## Key Figures

- **Epicurus** (341–270 BCE): the founder; he lived a simple life, refused to take part in politics, and said: "Live unnoticed."
- **Metrodorus** (c. 331–278 BCE): his closest friend, who wrote "Against the Sophists."
- **Hermarchus** (c. 325–250 BCE): succeeded Epicurus as head of the Garden.
- **Lucretius** (c. 99–55 BCE; the dates are traditional and approximate): the best-known exponent of Epicureanism in Rome, author of "On the Nature of Things" (De Rerum Natura) in six books.
- **Philodemus of Gadara** (c. 110–40 BCE): a prominent Epicurean philosopher at Herculaneum.

## Central Concepts

- **Pleasure (ἡδονή)**: Epicurus calls pleasure "the beginning and end of the blessed life" (*Letter to Menoeceus*). Not every pleasure is to be chosen: he distinguishes **natural and necessary** desires (for example food, shelter, friendship), **natural but unnecessary** ones, and **empty** ones (for example for fame, wealth, power), and advises choosing among pleasures by calculating their consequences.
- **Static (katastematic) pleasure**: "the absence of pain in the body and disturbance in the soul" (ἀπονία καὶ ἀταραξία). Pleasure is not "satisfaction" but "freedom from disturbance."
- **Death is nothing to us (θάνατος οὐδὲν πρὸς ἡμᾶς)**: while we are alive, death has not come, and when death comes, we no longer perceive. There is no need to fear.
- **The gods do not intervene (ἀπρόνοια)**: the gods exist, but they do not intervene in the universe. The universe is mechanical (atomic).
- **Pain and its limits**: according to a letter to Idomeneus reported by Diogenes Laertius, Epicurus on the last day of his life, while suffering from painful illness, said that the joy of remembering past conversations outweighed his pain.
- **Justice (δικαιοσύνη)**: in the *Principal Doctrines* justice is a pact of advantage not to harm one another or be harmed.
- **Friendship (φιλία)**: in the *Principal Doctrines* (27) Epicurus says that of all that wisdom provides for the blessedness of a whole life, by far the greatest is the possession of friendship.

## Epicurean Physics

- **Atomism (ἄτομοι)**: the universe is composed of atoms and void, atoms moving in the void.
- **The swerve (clinamen)**: atoms occasionally swerve (παρέγκλισις) in their fall, so that not everything is mechanically determined; the doctrine is reported chiefly by Lucretius and Cicero.
- **No immortality of the soul**: the soul is material (atomic particles) and dissolves with the body.

## Ethics

- **Prudence (φρόνησις)**: the ability to distinguish among pleasures and to avoid what causes pain.
- **Self-sufficiency (αὐτάρκεια)**: contentment with what is necessary.
- **Justice (δικαιοσύνη)**: social, not innate: it is a contract among people.
- **Simple living**: modest food and the company of friends are preferred to luxury, since luxury brings anxiety and dependence.

## Historical Influence

- **Lucretius** (De Rerum Natura): republished Epicureanism in Rome.
- **Late antiquity**: Epicureanism faded after the third century CE; the Church Fathers attacked it for denying providence and the immortality of the soul, and "Epicurean" came to mean atheist or libertine.
- **The Renaissance**: rediscovery of Lucretius' poem (Poggio Bracciolini, 1417).
- **The seventeenth century**: Pierre Gassendi revived Epicurean atomism in a Christianised form, and it influenced Boyle and other corpuscularians.
- **Marx**: his doctoral dissertation, *The Difference Between the Democritean and Epicurean Philosophy of Nature* (1841), analyzed Epicurus's concept of the atomic swerve as an assertion of human agency and self-consciousness against Democritean determinism.
- **Nietzsche**: wrote about Epicurus with ambivalent admiration (for example in *Daybreak* and *The Gay Science*).
- **Recent scholarship**: Martha Nussbaum, *The Therapy of Desire* (1994), and others have renewed interest in Epicurean ethics as a form of philosophical therapy.

## Contemporary Criticism

- **The Stoics and Cicero**: criticized Epicureanism on the ground that pleasure cannot be the highest good, because virtue alone is sufficient (Cicero, *De finibus*, books I-II, presents and criticises Epicurean ethics).
- **Christian theology**: "Epicurean" = atheist, especially because of the denial of divine providence.
- **Plutarch**: wrote polemics against the Epicureans, including *Against Colotes*.

## Limits

- "Epicurean" in everyday usage means a lover of pleasure and luxury, a usage that owes much to hostile ancient and Christian polemic.
- Epicurus wrote voluminously (including the 37-book *On Nature*), but most of his work is lost; we have three letters (to Herodotus, Pythocles and Menoeceus), the *Principal Doctrines*, and the *Vatican Sayings*, plus fragments of *On Nature* recovered from the carbonised papyri of Herculaneum, whose reading and editing is still under way. The authenticity of the *Letter to Pythocles* has been questioned.
