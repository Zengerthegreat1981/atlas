# Postmodern Anthropology

Postmodern anthropology is the movement that turned anthropology's attention from the peoples it studied to the texts it produced about them. Its central claim is that an ethnography is not a transparent window onto another culture but a piece of writing — made by a particular author, in a particular historical moment, within relations of power — and that the way an ethnography is written shapes what it can claim to know. It is less a single theory than a critical mood: sceptical of grand narratives, attentive to language and representation, and committed to more honest, more dialogical forms of writing.

## History

The movement's roots lie in the interpretive turn of the 1970s. Clifford Geertz's The Interpretation of Cultures (1973) argued that culture was a web of significance and that the anthropologist's task was to interpret it, producing the "thick description" of a wink, a ritual, or a cockfight. At the same time, decolonization undermined the authority of the Western observer: the people anthropologists had studied as objects were now citizens, scholars, and critics in their own right, and the colonial conditions that had made fieldwork possible could no longer be ignored.

The decisive moment came in April 1984, when a group of anthropologists met at the School of American Research in Santa Fe, New Mexico, for an advanced seminar on ethnographic writing. The resulting volume, Writing Culture: The Poetics and Politics of Ethnography (1986), edited by James Clifford and George E. Marcus, became the movement's founding text. Its essays argued that ethnography was in the midst of a political and epistemological crisis: Western writers could no longer portray non-Western peoples with unchallenged authority, and cultural representation was inescapably contingent, historical, and contestable. Stephen Tyler's contribution, "Post-Modern Ethnography: From Document of the Occult to Occult Document," was the volume's most explicitly postmodern essay. In the 2000s its concerns with voice, authority, and representation were taken up by feminist, Indigenous, and decolonial anthropologists, who pushed them further than the original authors had.

## Key Figures

James Clifford (born 1945), historian of anthropology and cultural critic, is the movement's leading theorist of representation; his introduction to Writing Culture, "Partial Truths," argued that ethnographic truths are inherently partial, committed, and incomplete. George E. Marcus developed multi-sited ethnography and the critique of the single-village study. Clifford Geertz (1926–2006) provided the interpretive foundation on which the movement built, even as its authors criticized his authorial voice. Stephen A. Tyler argued for an evocative, poetic ethnography that abandoned the claim to represent its subjects. Paul Rabinow, whose Reflections on Fieldwork in Morocco (1977) exposed the negotiations and misunderstandings of fieldwork, became a leading theorist of the anthropology of the contemporary. Lila Abu-Lughod, writing from a feminist and "halfie" (partly insider) position, argued in "Writing Against Culture" (1991) that the concept of culture itself froze difference and called for ethnographies of the particular.

## Key Works

Clifford and Marcus's Writing Culture: The Poetics and Politics of Ethnography (1986) is the founding text. Marcus and Fischer's Anthropology as Cultural Critique: An Experimental Moment in the Human Sciences (1986) is its companion volume. Geertz's The Interpretation of Cultures (1973) supplied the interpretive premise, and his Works and Lives: The Anthropologist as Author (1988) examined ethnography as literature. Clifford's The Predicament of Culture: Twentieth-Century Ethnography, Literature, and Art (1988) collected his essays on travel, museums, and the invention of culture. Rabinow's Reflections on Fieldwork in Morocco (1977) is the classic reflexive fieldwork account. Marcus's "Ethnography in/of the World System: The Emergence of Multi-Sited Ethnography" (1995) redefined fieldwork for a globalized world, arguing that ethnographers must follow people, things, and ideas across sites.

## Method

Postmodern anthropology's methods begin with reflexivity: the ethnographer writes herself into the account, acknowledging her position, her relationships in the field, and the limits of what she can know. Fieldwork remains central, but it is understood as a dialogue rather than an observation: informants are collaborators and co-authors, not sources of data. Multi-sited ethnography follows its object — a commodity, a migrant, a policy, a rumour — across the multiple places where it is made and remade, rather than settling in a single village. Experimental writing takes many forms: polyphonic texts that let several voices speak without a single narrator's synthesis, dialogues, fragmented narratives, and ethnographies that include field notes, letters, and the subjects' own writing.

## Influence and Contemporary Debates

Postmodern anthropology changed the discipline permanently, even among anthropologists who reject its label. Reflexivity, attention to writing, and the politics of representation are now part of ordinary ethnographic training, and it is now uncommon for an ethnography to present its author as an invisible observer. At the same time it has been sharply criticized. Critics charged that its focus on texts was a retreat from the world that neglected the study of inequality, violence, and material life. Others argued that its relativism undermined the possibility of reliable knowledge, and that its experimental texts were unreadable outside the academy. Two open questions extend the movement's concerns to the media in which anthropology is now made. Digital ethnography, developed in works such as Christine Hine's Virtual Ethnography (2000) and Sarah Pink and colleagues' Digital Ethnography: Principles and Practice (2016), asks how fieldwork should be done when social life unfolds on platforms and through screens rather than in a shared physical place. Multimodal ethnography, discussed for instance in Collins, Durington, and Gill's 2017 article "Multimodality: An Invitation" in American Anthropologist, asks how text, image, sound, and film can be combined in ethnographic work without one medium dominating the others. Both revisit the movement's founding question — who speaks, and with what authority.
