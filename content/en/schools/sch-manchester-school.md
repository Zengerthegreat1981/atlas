# Manchester School of Anthropology

## Definition

An anthropological school founded by Max Gluckman (1911–1975) at the University of Manchester in 1949. It emerged from within British functionalism but broke with its core assumption of static structural equilibrium, focusing instead on conflict and social change as a permanent structural part of any society rather than a temporary malfunction.

## Historical Context

Gluckman was born in South Africa and was shaped by direct experience there of communities undergoing rapid and violent colonial transformation: the migration of African workers to gold and diamond mines, the emergence of racially mixed industrial towns, and the clash between African customary legal systems and British colonial law. This fieldwork experience led him to reject the prevailing British functionalist model of the day, associated with Alfred Radcliffe-Brown, Evans-Pritchard and Meyer Fortes, which assumed that "traditional" societies were relatively balanced systems whose institutions worked together to maintain stability. In 1949 Gluckman founded the Department of Social Anthropology at the University of Manchester and gathered around him a generation of younger researchers (among them Victor Turner, J. Clyde Mitchell and Elizabeth Colson) whose fieldwork was carried out mainly in south and central Africa (including present-day Zambia, Malawi and Zimbabwe), often in connection with the Rhodes-Livingstone Institute in Northern Rhodesia, which Gluckman had directed in the 1940s.

## Main Ideas and Method

The Manchester School held that conflict and tension are a permanent structural element of every society, and that the mechanisms that contain them, rather than eliminate them entirely, are what reproduce social cohesion. Gluckman expressed this in formulas such as "peace in the feud." The school developed the "extended case method," also known as "situational analysis": tracing a real dispute or social incident in its concrete detail over time (from the beginning of the disagreement to its settlement or escalation), in order to show how real individuals maneuver between multiple and sometimes contradictory social rules, exploiting ambiguity and conflict among customs to pursue their own interests, rather than passively submitting to a single homogeneous social structure. This was a fundamental methodological shift, from describing the "ideal social rule" as narrated by informants to studying "actual practice" in its contested and contradictory cases.

## Key Works and Figures

Max Gluckman remains the founding reference point with his book *Custom and Conflict in Africa* (1955). At Manchester, Victor Turner, who applied the extended case method to the study of ritual among the Ndembu of Zambia, later developed an influential symbolic anthropology, including his elaboration of Arnold van Gennep's concept of "liminality." J. Clyde Mitchell developed the analysis of social networks to study the complex relations in multi-ethnic African industrial cities. Peter Worsley, who was also associated with Gluckman's circle and later became professor of sociology at Manchester, wrote *The Trumpet Shall Sound* (1957), a landmark study of Melanesian cargo cults.

## Impact and Critique

The school paved the way for the study of social change, migration and urbanization in colonial Africa, using rigorous empirical methods decades before the later literature on globalisation. It also influenced the later turn in anthropology toward the study of action, conflict and individual agency rather than static structure alone. Its emphasis on action and choice parallels Fredrik Barth's transactional models and, later, Pierre Bourdieu's theory of practice. Some critics, however, have noted that the school's strong focus on partial, individual disputes may sometimes reduce attention to the broader economic and colonial political structures that shaped these disputes in the first place.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The analysis of conflict and balance in tribal societies in the Manchester School offers a striking theoretical parallel with Ibn Khaldun's theory of *ʿaṣabiyya* (group solidarity). Ibn Khaldun described how states arise out of tribal conflicts and the temporary internal cohesion of tribes. However, Ibn Khaldun focused on the long historical cycle of the rise and fall of states, whereas the Manchester School focused on the analysis of daily, partial disputes within society itself. Ibn Khaldun is a historian theorizing a long-term trajectory spanning generations, while Gluckman and his students were ethnographers observing a single dispute in its immediate detail. This is a methodological difference between comparative historical science and direct field ethnography that deserves attention, although both give conflict an important place in explaining social cohesion and change.
