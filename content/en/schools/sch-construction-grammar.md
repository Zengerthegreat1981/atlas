# Construction Grammar

A linguistic theoretical framework that arose at the University of California, Berkeley in the late 1980s (Charles Fillmore, Paul Kay, George Lakoff) and was later developed by Adele Goldberg. It rejects the strict separation between the lexicon (individual words) and grammar (abstract rules) that generative theory assumed.

## Historical Context and Origins

Construction grammar arose at the University of California, Berkeley in the late 1980s as a direct reaction to the dominance of Chomskyan generative grammar, which posited an autonomous formal grammatical component entirely independent of the lexicon, meaning and usage. Charles Fillmore, together with Paul Kay and Mary Catherine O'Connor, presented an influential case study of the English construction "Let Alone" (1988). They argued that this idiomatic construction has syntactic and semantic properties that do not follow from general rules plus the meanings of *let* and *alone*, and that the pattern itself should be listed as a form-meaning unit. Adele Goldberg then developed argument-structure constructions in her Berkeley dissertation and in *Constructions* (1995). Fillmore and Kay continued to elaborate the Berkeley framework alongside that work.

## The Central Thesis

This grammar holds that **grammatical constructions themselves**, not words alone, are meaning-bearing units in their own right (form-meaning pairings). They vary in their abstraction, from highly specific lexical patterns (a particular idiom such as "Let Alone") to very general syntactic patterns (the passive construction, or the ditransitive "subject-verb-object-object" pattern in sentences such as "gave him a book"). For this framework, language is a network of such constructions at varying degrees of abstraction (a Constructicon). It is not two separate levels (lexicon and grammar) but a single graded scale.

## Goldberg's Argument Structure Constructions

Goldberg put forward a central thesis on "Argument Structure Constructions." It explains the meaning of sentences such as "sneeze the napkin across the table," in which the intransitive verb "sneeze" appears in an unusual transitive use that is not lexically conventional. The thesis assumes that the causal-motion meaning ("causing something to move across a place by some force") does not originate in the verb itself but in the constructional grammatical pattern (the subject-verb-object-locative pattern). That pattern imposes its causal meaning even on verbs that do not carry this meaning in their ordinary lexical use.

## Frame Semantics and FrameNet

In parallel, Fillmore developed a complementary semantic framework, "Frame Semantics," which holds that the meaning of many words can only be understood by reference to a cognitive "frame," a structured body of background knowledge about a typical scene or situation. For example, understanding the word "seller" requires the entire frame of a "commercial transaction": a seller, a buyer, goods and a price. This framework produced the FrameNet project, a huge computational lexical database that classifies English words according to the semantic frames they evoke. It is used as a reference in computational natural language processing.

## Branches and Later Developments

Construction grammar later branched into several sub-schools. "Radical Construction Grammar," associated with William Croft, denies the existence of universal grammatical categories independent of the constructions themselves. "Sign-Based Construction Grammar" integrates more rigorous formal tools drawn from Head-Driven Phrase Structure Grammar (HPSG). "Diachronic Construction Grammar" applies the framework's tools to the study of how constructions arise and develop historically, intersecting with studies of grammaticalization. "Fluid Construction Grammar," associated with Luc Steels, is concerned with computer simulation of how artificial agents learn linguistic constructions through interaction.

## Difference from Generative Grammar and Impact

Unlike generative grammar, which posits an innate, universal grammatical structure independent of meaning and usage, construction grammar links form and meaning directly at every level. It intersects with usage-based approaches to acquisition, which see language learning as a process of gradual abstraction from concrete, repeated examples rather than as the activation of a ready-made innate structure. The framework has been widely used in cognitive and functional linguistics. FrameNet, directed by Fillmore at the International Computer Science Institute in Berkeley, classifies lexical items by the frames they evoke and has been used in natural-language processing. Anatol Stefanowitsch and Stefan Th. Gries (2003) introduced collostructional analysis, a family of statistical tests of association between words and constructions.

## Langacker's Cognitive Grammar

Construction grammar also intersects with "Cognitive Grammar," developed by Ronald Langacker at roughly the same time. It shares the basic assumption that the separation between lexicon and grammar should be rejected, but it starts from analytical tools closer to cognitive psychology (such as schematic diagrams and patterns of perceptual attention). Together, the two frameworks form complementary pillars of what is generally called "cognitive usage-based linguistics."
