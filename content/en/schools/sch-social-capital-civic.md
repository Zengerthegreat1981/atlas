# Social Capital and Civil Society

A sociological current that studies social networks, mutual trust and cooperative norms as "capital" that explains the performance of societies and democratic institutions. It became especially famous in the American context through Robert Putnam's thesis on the decline of civic participation.

## The Central Idea

This current arose in a context of growing academic and political anxiety in the United States and Europe during the 1980s and 1990s about the decline of institutional trust and political participation. It found in the concept of "social capital", used earlier by Lyda Hanifan (1916), Jane Jacobs (1961), Bourdieu and Coleman before it became widespread with Putnam, especially after his study of Italian regions, *Making Democracy Work* (1993), a unifying framework for linking the quality of informal social networks to the health of the broader formal institutions. The current assumes that social networks (associations, clubs, local societies) produce "social capital", meaning trust and norms of exchange, that facilitates collective cooperation and is associated with better performance of democratic and economic institutions. The borrowing from economic capital is deliberate: just as money is invested, presence in social networks is invested and yields a collective return.

## The Functional Version in Coleman and the Conflict Version in Bourdieu

In *Foundations of Social Theory* (1990), building on his 1988 article "Social Capital in the Creation of Human Capital", James Coleman offered a formulation fundamentally different from Putnam's collective-civic one: an individual-functional formulation that views social capital as a resource the rational individual invests within his network of relations to achieve his own goals (such as the mutual trust among students' parents that facilitates collective supervision of children's behavior), consistent with his broader project of applying rational choice theory to social phenomena. Pierre Bourdieu, by contrast, offered a conflict-class formulation entirely different in its political orientation: for him, social capital is the aggregate of resources linked to durable networks of relationships, which members of the dominant classes in particular invest (such as networks of graduates of elite schools) to reproduce their class privileges across generations, not a neutral instrument of civic cooperation whose benefit extends to society as a whole, as the more consensual reading of Putnam and Coleman assumes. This sharp contrast among the three formulations (cooperative-civic in Putnam, individual-rational in Coleman, conflict-class in Bourdieu) explains why the concept of social capital remains one of the most controversial contemporary sociological concepts despite its widespread use.

## Key Figures of the Current

- **Robert D. Putnam** (1941–): author of the thesis of the decline of American social capital in *Bowling Alone* (2000), who built on Alexis de Tocqueville's work on American voluntary associations in the nineteenth century.
- **James Coleman**: formulated a functional-rational version of social capital in *Foundations of Social Theory* (1990).
- **Pierre Bourdieu**: formulated a conflict-class version of social capital as an instrument for reproducing inequality.

## The Bowling Alone Thesis

In *Bowling Alone* (2000), Putnam argued that since the 1960s the United States has seen a steady and measurable decline in civic and social participation: a decline in membership of voluntary associations, unions, political parties and churches; fewer family dinners and visits with friends and less participation in informal collective activities; even a decline in league bowling (hence the book's metaphorical title: more Americans bowl, but fewer in organised leagues). Putnam attributed this decline to several intertwined factors: the spread of television as an isolated home entertainment; pressures of time and money on working families; suburbanization, which lengthened daily commuting times at the expense of time for community participation; and the succession of generations less civically engaged than the "long civic generation" shaped by the Depression and the Second World War. Putnam distinguished between "bonding" social capital, which strengthens ties within a relatively closed, homogeneous group, and "bridging" social capital, which links diverse, different groups to one another, regarding the second type as more important for the health of the pluralist democratic society and for confronting social polarization.

## Tocquevillian Roots and Political Use

Putnam drew explicitly on Alexis de Tocqueville's observations in *Democracy in America* (1835) about the uniquely American tendency to found voluntary associations for diverse purposes, regarding Tocqueville as a classic observer of the link between the density of these associations and the vitality of American democracy, and what Putnam documented as a gradual erosion of this historical Tocquevillian legacy. The influence of this thesis went beyond academic circles to become a direct reference for policymakers in the United States and Europe, as the concept of social capital was used by the World Bank and other international organizations as a development indicator. Later criticisms nonetheless accused the thesis of conservative nostalgia for an idealized American past, and of overlooking how strong "bonding" social capital can feed communal intolerance and exclusion instead of positive civic cooperation.
