# Peripatetic / Aristotelianism

A philosophical movement founded in Athens in 335 BCE when **Aristotle** (384–322 BCE) established his school in the Lyceum (Λύκειον), a gymnasium and sanctuary sacred to Apollo Lykeios. It came to be called the "Peripatetic" school (Peripatos) after the covered walkway (περίπατος) in which its members taught. Aristotelianism remained a major philosophical force in antiquity, especially through its commentary tradition (to about 600 CE), and returned in the 12th century in both Christian Europe and the Islamic world.

## Phases

- **First Greek phase (335–86 BCE)**: Aristotle, Theophrastus (his successor), Strato (the physicist), Demetrius of Phalerum. Then a relative decline with the rise of Rome.
- **Roman phase (86 BCE – 200 CE)**: Andronicus of Rhodes revived the texts, and the **commentators**, such as Alexander of Aphrodisias (c. 200 CE), interpreted Aristotle's texts.
- **Arabic phase (8th–13th centuries CE)**: al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Ibn Sina, Ibn Rushd. The largest Aristotelian heritage in Arabic (the Graeco-Arabic translation movement of the 8th to 10th centuries in Baghdad).
- **Latin European phase (12th–17th centuries)**: Peter of Abano, Thomas Aquinas, Jean Buridan. Then the Renaissance critique of scholastic Aristotelianism.

## Principal Philosophers

- **Aristotle** (384–322 BCE): the founder of the school and a towering systematic philosopher.
- **Theophrastus** (371–287 BCE): his student and successor, who wrote "On Plants" and "Characters" (ethical).
- **Strato of Lampsacus** (c. 335–269 BCE): a philosopher of natural "physics," who neglected logic in favour of natural philosophy.
- **Andronicus of Rhodes** (1st century BCE): reassembled Aristotle's texts into a single edition.
- **Alexander of Aphrodisias** (fl. c. 200 CE): the most prominent ancient commentator on Aristotle.

## Central Concepts

- **The four causes (αἰτίαι)**: the "material cause" (ὕλη), the "formal cause" (εἶδος), the "efficient cause" (ποιοῦν), the "final cause" (τέλος). A full explanation cites whichever of these apply; not every phenomenon has all four (a lunar eclipse, for example, has no final cause).
- **The golden mean (μεσότης)**: virtue is a mean between two vices (for example, courage between recklessness and cowardice).
- **The syllogism (συλλογισμός)**: the "demonstrative syllogism" (apodeictic syllogism) yields certain knowledge from necessary premises.
- **The first mover (πρῶτον κινοῦν)**: the unmoved first cause (ὃ οὐ κινούμενον κινεῖ), that is, the "Unmoved Mover" (God).
- **Nature (φύσις)**: every being has a nature (a principle of motion and rest), and the realization of nature is a natural end (φύσει τέλος).
- **The soul (ψυχή)**: "the first actuality of a natural organic body" (ἐντελέχεια ἡ πρώτη σώματος φυσικοῦ ὀργανικοῦ) — neither pure spirit nor the body.
- **Politics as a science**: man is a political animal (ζῷον πολιτικόν); the state is natural.
- **Virtue ethics**: the "good life" (εὐδαιμονία, εὖ ζῆν) is activity in accordance with reason and virtue, with the virtues as settled dispositions.

## Historical Impact

- **Roman philosophy**: Cicero knew and drew on Aristotle's works, including his lost dialogues.
- **Christian theology**: Augustine's (354–430) reading of Aristotelianism was modest, but Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) made Aristotelian philosophy central to a Christian theology.
- **Arabic philosophy**: al-Kindi, al-Farabi (d. 950 CE), Ibn Sina (d. 1037 CE), Ibn Rushd (d. 1198 CE). The largest Aristotelian heritage in Arabic.
- **Jewish theology**: Maimonides (1138–1204), whose Aristotelian reading shaped a rationalist current in Jewish theology.
- **The European Renaissance**: the Renaissance re-read Aristotle (for example Pietro Pomponazzi, *De immortalitate animae*, 1516).
- **The 17th century**: Francis Bacon, Galileo and Descartes were all "against Aristotle," yet all of them dealt with his legacy.
- **Hegel**: admired Aristotle and gave him a prominent place in his *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*.

## Transmitted Treasures

- **Arabic Aristotelianism**: some Greek commentaries on Aristotle (for example by Alexander of Aphrodisias and Themistius) survive only or more fully in Arabic translation.
- **The "Poetics"**: transmitted into Arabic, and influenced al-Farabi, Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd and Arabic poetics.
- **"On Sophistical Refutations" (Sophistici Elenchi)**: translated, and influenced the Arab logicians.

## Limits

- "Aristotelianism" means: Aristotle + what was added to him (the Peripatetics) + what was attached to him (Thomas Aquinas and Ibn Rushd). This expansion makes "Aristotelianism" an umbrella term.
- Aristotle's surviving treatises are compressed, lecture-like works that depend on interpretation and commentary; only about thirty treatises survive of the many works attributed to him in antiquity, and his published dialogues are lost except for fragments.
- Transmitted Aristotelianism differs from the original Aristotelianism. The early translations and transmission of works such as the *Metaphysics* introduced distortions that later readers had to correct.
- "Thomism" is a Christian synthesis of Aristotelian philosophy with Christian theology, not simply Aristotle's own position.
