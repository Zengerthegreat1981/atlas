# Critical Theory / The Frankfurt School (Frankfurter Schule)

A philosophical and social current founded by the Institute for Social Research (Institut für Sozialforschung) at the University of Frankfurt in 1923. It was distinguished by combining Marxism with German philosophy (Kant, Hegel, Marx), sociology (Weber) and psychoanalysis (Freud) in a critical project directed at contemporary capitalism.

## Historical context

The Institute was founded in 1923 under the patronage of **Felix Weil** (the son of a wealthy grain merchant). In 1930 **Max Horkheimer** (1895–1973) took over the directorship and turned the Institute into a **Marxist intellectual center**, and began publishing the **Journal for Social Research** (Zeitschrift für Sozialforschung, 1932).

With the rise of Nazism, **the Institute went into exile**:
- **Geneva** (1933), with an office in Paris.
- **Columbia University, New York** (1934 onward).
- **California** (from 1941, where Horkheimer and Adorno wrote *Dialectic of Enlightenment*).
- **Return to Frankfurt** (Horkheimer in 1949; the Institute reopened in 1950-51).

This **move from Germany to America** shaped the consciousness of the generation: the merging of **Marxism** with the **American experience** (mass culture and the culture industry).

## The main generations

### The first generation (the founders)

- **Felix Weil** (1898–1975): the material founder.
- **Max Horkheimer** (1895–1973): *Eclipse of Reason* (1947), *Critical Theory* (selections, 1968).
- **Theodor Adorno** (1903–1969): *Dialectic of Enlightenment* (with Horkheimer, 1944), *Minima Moralia* (1951), *Negative Dialectics* (1966).
- **Herbert Marcuse** (1898–1979): *Eros and Civilization* (1955), *One-Dimensional Man* (1964).
- **Erich Fromm** (1900–1980): *The Fear of Freedom* (1941), *The Sane Society* (1955). An audience between social psychology and Marxist psychoanalysis.
- **Franz Neumann** (1900–1954): *Behemoth* (1942), an analysis of the Nazi system.
- **Leo Lowenthal** (1900–1993): literary sociology.
- **Walter Benjamin** (1892–1940) and **Siegfried Kracauer** (1889–1966), close associates of the Institute: Benjamin on art and mass reproduction, Kracauer on film (*Theory of Film*, 1960).

### The second generation

- **Jurgen Habermas** (b. 1929): *Knowledge and Human Interests* (1968), *Theory of Communicative Action* (1981).
- **Oskar Negt** (1934–2024) and **Alexander Kluge** (b. 1932): *Öffentlichkeit und Erfahrung* (1972), on the public sphere.
- **Claus Offe** (b. 1940): critical political sociology of the welfare state.

### The third generation (the extension)

- **Axel Honneth** (b. 1949): *The Struggle for Recognition* (1992). "Recognition" as a model.
- **Nancy Fraser** (b. 1947), an American associate of this tradition: recognition and redistribution.

## Founding concepts

- **Critical theory (Kritische Theorie)**: Horkheimer distinguished it in *Traditional and Critical Theory* (1937). Theory is not a **neutral representation** of reality but an **intervention** that exposes structures of oppression.
- **Negative dialectics (Negative Dialektik)**: for Adorno, dialectics does not arrive at a **final synthesis** but remains in the **negation** of the existing state of affairs. Against "identity" (Identität), which crushes differences.
- **The totalizing Enlightenment (Dialektik der Aufklärung)**: the Enlightenment (modernist rationality) in its essence contains the **seeds of its own regression**: instrumental reason, domination of nature and of people, and ultimately the barbarism of fascism and anti-Semitism.
- **The culture industry (Culture Industry)**: Adorno and Horkheimer (1944) describe how **mass culture** (Hollywood, advertising, popular music) produces **submission** in place of critical thinking.
- **One-Dimensional Man**: Marcuse describes how **advanced capitalism** absorbs every opposition: no opposition remains, only **adaptation**.
- **Eros and Civilization**: Marcuse links **Freud's critique of civilization** with **Marx's critique of capitalism**: the liberation of the "pleasure principle" from the repressive "reality principle".
- **Communicative action (Kommunikatives Handeln)**: Habermas replaces "labor" with "communicative action" as the model of human practice.
- **Recognition (Anerkennung)**: Honneth develops a "theory of recognition": identity is built through mutual recognition.

## The major debates

- **The positivist dispute**: Adorno and **Karl Popper** (with Habermas and Hans Albert) disagreed over the methodology of the social sciences at the 1961 Tübingen conference; the debate was published as *The Positivist Dispute in German Sociology* (German 1969, English 1976).
- **The Habermas-Gadamer debate** (1960s): about **critique** within understanding (see `sch-hermeneutics`).
- **The Habermas-Luhmann debate** (*Theorie der Gesellschaft oder Sozialtechnologie*, 1971): critical theory versus systems theory.
- **Habermas and the French post-structuralists**: in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985) Habermas criticized Foucault, Derrida and others for undermining the rational basis of critique.

## Influence

- **Cultural and media studies**: the analysis of mass culture engaged with, and was criticized by, Stuart Hall and the Birmingham Centre for Contemporary Cultural Studies (founded 1964).
- **Political philosophy**: Habermas on deliberative democracy; Honneth and Nancy Fraser on recognition and redistribution.

## Relation to Earlier Marxism

The Institute's project drew on Georg Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) and Karl Korsch, who revived the Hegelian side of Marx and developed the theory of reification. Unlike Leninist Marxism, the Frankfurt thinkers did not tie critical theory to party organization or to the expectation that the proletariat would be the agent of revolution, and they criticized Soviet Marxism; Lukács himself later attacked them as the "Grand Hotel Abyss."

## Limits

- **The charge of Eurocentrism**: postcolonial critics argue that the current paid little attention to empire and colonialism in its analyses of modernity.
- **The charge of "the impossibility of practice"**: if everything is "dominated", how does change emerge? (a critique pressed by post-structuralist and other theorists).
- **The charge of consensus-orientation**: Habermas's discourse ethics has been criticized (for example by Lyotard and Mouffe) for privileging consensus over irreducible difference and conflict.
