# Arab Developmentalism and State Planning

**Arab Developmentalism and State Planning** is a current of economic thought and policy that dominated most Arab economies between the 1950s and the 1970s. It rests on a central conviction: national industrialization cannot arise automatically through market mechanisms alone in economies that had just emerged from colonialism or semi-colonialism. It requires a **state that plans, owns and protects**, in the same vein as the Latin American structuralism of CEPAL, which made a parallel case in a different context.

No globally agreed name exists for this current: "Arab Developmentalism" is a descriptive analytical label used here to bring together scattered literatures on import-substituting industrialization and state planning, not the name of a school with a single founding declaration.

## Early roots: Talaat Harb and Bank Misr

Decades before this current, Egyptian efforts were led by **Talaat Harb** (1867–1941), who founded Bank Misr in 1920 as the first Egyptian national bank with purely Egyptian capital, and then a network of affiliated industrial and commercial companies (Misr Spinning and Weaving, Misr Airwork, Studio Misr). The project aimed to break foreign capital's monopoly over the Egyptian economy by building a national capitalist class. Harb's project was not centrally planned socialism in the later sense, but it established the idea that **economic independence is a precondition for political independence**, the idea on which the next generation of economists and the Nasserist state would build after 1952.

## The Nasserist peak: nationalization and central planning

After the July 1952 Revolution, and more clearly after the nationalization of the Suez Canal in 1956 and the broad nationalizations of the early 1960s, the Egyptian state adopted a development model based on: nationalization of strategic sectors (banks, insurance, major industries, the Suez Canal); five-year central plans to direct investment; customs protection for infant industries; and an expansion of the public sector in employment and production. Prominent Egyptian economists such as Ismail Sabri Abdallah held direct posts in the central planning apparatus (the Ministry of Planning), so they were part of the planning state rather than outside commentators on it. Other economists, such as Fouad Morsi, offered a Marxist critique of the class structure of this model from a position that was broadly supportive of planning but more insistent in questioning who actually benefited from nationalization.

## Regional diffusion

The model was not limited to Egypt. Syria and Iraq adopted it to varying degrees (particularly after the nationalization of Iraqi oil in 1972), as did Algeria after independence in 1962 (heavy industrialization led by the state), and Tunisia at an early stage. This range of experiences shared one common assumption: that left to itself, the market would keep Arab economies as suppliers of raw materials and importers of manufactured goods within an unequal international division of labour, and that breaking this division required decisive state intervention rather than marginal reforms.

## Opening and retreat after 1974

The model began to retreat with the Egyptian economic opening (*infitah*) launched by Anwar Sadat in 1974, which partly reopened the Egyptian economy to foreign investment and the private sector. It marked a political and economic break with the Nasserist legacy, although it did not dismantle the institutional structure in one stroke. The shift was accompanied by domestic economic criticism from economists such as Fouad Morsi and Ramzi Zaki, who regarded the opening as the re-creation of a new economic dependency more dangerous than the old colonial one. Its supporters, by contrast, saw it as a necessary correction to decades of poor resource allocation and the swelling of an inefficient public sector.

## Later criticism

The model has faced criticism from two opposing directions. A liberal critique holds that protection of infant industries became, over time, a permanent rentier arrangement that rewarded administrative failure rather than efficiency. A leftist critique holds that central planning, as actually implemented, produced a new bureaucratic class that benefited from nationalization more than the workers and peasants in whose name it was justified. The debate over how to weigh these two critiques remains open in the Arab political economy literature to this day, especially over whether the later debt crises and the opening were the inevitable result of structural flaws in planning itself, or the result of poor implementation in an authoritarian and inefficient political context.

## The link to Latin American structuralism

This current shares basic analytical assumptions with the Latin American structuralism of CEPAL, theorized by Raúl Prebisch at about the same time: both hold that the terms of international trade tend structurally to favour the advanced industrial states over raw-material exporters, and that correcting this imbalance requires direct state intervention in industrialization rather than waiting for the free market alone. This intellectual convergence stemmed from a shared global historical context (anti-colonial liberation movements, the rise of the United Nations, and the Bandung Conference of 1955) more than from direct coordination between Arab and Latin American thinkers, although some Arab economists explicitly borrowed Latin American structuralist tools in diagnosing their own economies.
