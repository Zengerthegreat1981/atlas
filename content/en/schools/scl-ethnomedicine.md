# Ethnomedicine

Ethnomedicine is the anthropological study of how different peoples understand health, illness, and healing. It treats medical knowledge not as a universal body of facts but as a cultural system: every society has ideas about why people fall ill, what a sick body means, who is entitled to heal, and which remedies work. Ethnomedicine documents those ideas in their own terms, compares them across societies, and asks how they shape the experience of suffering and recovery. Its subject ranges from herbal pharmacopoeias and spirit healing to the way patients in modern hospitals interpret a diagnosis, so it sits at the meeting point of cultural anthropology, medical anthropology, and the study of knowledge itself.

## History

Interest in other peoples' healing practices is as old as anthropology. Early ethnographers recorded cures, taboos, and spirit beliefs as part of general ethnography, but they usually treated them as folklore or superstition. A decisive shift came with W. H. R. Rivers, whose posthumous book *Medicine, Magic, and Religion* (1924) argued that healing practices follow a coherent logic of their own and should be studied as rational systems rather than as errors. Erwin Ackerknecht carried the argument further in the 1940s and 1950s, insisting that so-called primitive medicine was a genuine medical system with its own theories of disease causation, and his collected essays were published as *Medicine and Ethnology* (1971).

Ethnomedicine took shape as a named field in the 1960s and 1970s, when medical anthropology itself was being institutionalised. The Society for Medical Anthropology was founded in 1967, and ethnomedical research became one of its core strands. George Foster's influential paper "Disease Etiologies in Non-Western Medical Systems" (1976) gave the field its classic typology, distinguishing personalistic systems, in which illness is caused by an agent such as a spirit, a sorcerer, or an ancestor, from naturalistic systems, in which illness results from impersonal forces such as humoral imbalance, heat and cold, or the environment. Horacio Fabrega's *Disease and Social Behavior* (1974) offered a complementary framework, arguing that disease always has both a biological and a cultural dimension, and that the social response to sickness is as important as its physical cause.

The 1980s brought the interpretive turn. Arthur Kleinman's *Patients and Healers in the Context of Culture* (1980) and *The Illness Narratives* (1988) showed that patients and doctors often inhabit different explanatory models of the same condition, and that the gap between them is itself a source of suffering. Byron Good's *Medicine, Rationality, and Experience* (1994) examined how medical knowledge is constructed in clinical practice, including in biomedicine itself, which ethnomedicine increasingly treated as one ethnomedical system among others rather than as a neutral standard.

## Key Figures and Works

Rivers (1924) established the premise that healing beliefs are systematic. Ackerknecht (1971) made the case for the rationality of non-Western medicine and for the historical study of medical ideas. Foster (1976) supplied the personalistic-naturalistic typology that organised a generation of comparative research, and with Barbara Gallatin Anderson he wrote *Medical Anthropology* (1978), one of the field's first textbooks. Fabrega (1974) linked disease to social behaviour and to the cultural shaping of symptoms.

Kleinman (1980, 1988) shifted attention from beliefs about disease to the lived experience of illness, introducing the concepts of explanatory models, illness narratives, and the distinction between disease, illness, and sickness. Good (1994) extended the analysis to biomedicine, showing how clinical reasoning, diagnostic categories, and the language of the body are culturally made. Later scholars such as Margaret Lock, in *Encounters with Aging* (1993), showed that even apparently universal biological processes such as menopause are experienced differently across cultures, while work on traditional medicine in Asia, Africa, and Latin America documented elaborate pharmacopoeias that continue to be tested by modern pharmacology.

## Method

Ethnomedicine relies above all on ethnography: long-term participant observation in homes, clinics, and healing sessions, and careful interviewing about episodes of illness. Researchers record local illness categories and their symptoms, the theories of causation attached to them, the healers consulted, the sequence of treatments tried, and the outcomes as judged by patients and their families. They collect plant specimens and document preparation methods, dosages, and the ritual contexts in which remedies are used. They also study the social organisation of healing: who may practise, how knowledge is transmitted, how payment works, and how healers relate to state medicine.

Comparison is central. The field asks which illness categories recur across societies, which are culturally specific, and how the same plant or practice can carry different meanings in different places. Increasingly, ethnomedical research is collaborative, carried out with indigenous healers as partners rather than informants, and it confronts ethical questions about intellectual property, benefit sharing, and the documentation of knowledge that communities may regard as secret.

## Influence and Legacy

Ethnomedicine changed how medicine understands itself. It supplied the concepts of explanatory models, illness narratives, and cultural syndromes that are now standard in clinical training and public health, and it underpins the field of cultural competence in health care. Its documentation of plant medicines has contributed to drug discovery, while its insistence that healing is social as well as biological has influenced psychiatry, epidemiology, and health policy.

The field also reshaped anthropology's own claims. By showing that biomedicine is itself a cultural system, with its own rituals, metaphors, and blind spots, ethnomedicine helped establish the reflexive study of science within anthropology. Today its questions are urgent in new forms: how global health programmes interact with local healing traditions, how traditional knowledge should be protected, and how patients navigate plural medical systems that mix hospitals, herbalists, and the internet. Ethnomedicine remains the discipline's main way of taking other people's medicine seriously.
