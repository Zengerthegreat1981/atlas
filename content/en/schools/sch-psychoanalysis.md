# Psychoanalysis

Psychoanalysis is the **first established psychotherapeutic school**, founded by Sigmund Freud in Vienna in the late nineteenth century. It rests on a central hypothesis: neurotic symptoms, irrational behavior, and psychological disorders arise from **unconscious conflicts** among instinctual wishes (libido, aggression), the ego's need to adapt to reality, and the demands of conscience (the superego). It does not aim to "fix" outward behavior but to **make the unconscious conscious** through free association, dream analysis, analysis of resistance, and interpretation of transference. Methodologically it is **a psychological theory + a method of treatment + a theory of culture**, and this multiplicity is what made it one of the most influential fields of the twentieth century.

## Intellectual Context

Psychoanalysis was born at the intersection of:

- **Neurology** (Charcot, Breuer, Bernheim): hypnosis, hysteria, catharsis.
- **The Romantic and German-Jewish heritage**: the emphasis on conflict, subjectivity, creativity, and repressed bodily life.
- **Late European modernity**: the anxiety of the Habsburg Empire, the rise of individualism, and the dissolution of traditional values.

A formative case was that of **Anna O.** (Bertha Pappenheim), treated by Josef Breuer in 1880-1882, in which "talking" about hysterical symptoms appeared to relieve them; Breuer and Freud later described this "cathartic method" in *Studies on Hysteria* (1895). Within two decades Freud had turned it into a comprehensive theory and method.

## Major Historical Phases

- **Founding** (1895-1905): *Studies on Hysteria* (1895, with Breuer); Freud first uses the term "psychoanalysis" in 1896; *The Interpretation of Dreams* (1900); the "Wednesday Psychological Society" begins to meet in 1902.
- **Formation and consolidation** (1905-1910): the *case histories* (Dora, Little Hans, the Rat Man) and the elaboration of libido theory (*Three Essays on the Theory of Sexuality*, 1905).
- **The first splits** (1911-1914): Adler (1911, individual psychology) and Jung (1913, analytical psychology).
- **Second theoretical development** (1914-1923): *Instincts and Their Vicissitudes* (1915), *The Unconscious* (1915), and *The Ego and the Id* (1923), which introduced the tripartite structure.
- **Migration** (1930s-1940s): many analysts flee Nazism, and psychoanalysis becomes institutionally strong in the United States, in the American Psychoanalytic Association.
- **British consolidation** (1940s-1960s): the object relations school (Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott, Balint).
- **French structuralism** (1950s-1960s): Lacan re-read Freud through Saussure and hermeneutics.
- **American splits** (1970s-1990s): Kohut (self psychology), Mitchell (relational psychoanalysis), Stolorow (intersubjectivity).
- **Integration and pluralism** (2000s-present): relational, intercultural, feminist, and neuroscientific approaches.

## Founding Concepts

- **The unconscious**: psychological content that is not directly accessible to consciousness but affects behavior. It is broader than consciousness.
- **Repression**: a basic defense mechanism for keeping unacceptable content (instincts, traumatic memories) out of awareness.
- **Libido**: the sexual/vital energy that drives behavior (later widened to include the "life instinct").
- **The tripartite structure (Id / Ego / Superego)**: the id (the instincts), the ego (the mediator), the superego (conscience).
- **Fixation**: the arrest of libidinal development at a particular stage.
- **Developmental stages** (oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital): the theory of psychosexual development.
- **Defense mechanisms** (repression, projection, rationalization, reaction formation, denial, displacement, sublimation...): the ego's strategies for coping with anxiety.
- **Transference**: the transfer of feelings from childhood relationships onto the therapist.
- **Resistance**: everything that obstructs free association and the process of analysis.
- **Sublimation**: the transformation of repressed instinctual energy into socially acceptable activities (art, science, religion).
- **Dreams as the "royal road" to the unconscious**: their distortions reveal the repressed content.

## The Major Sub-Schools (Summarized)

Brief summaries follow; the main sub-schools have their own entries.

- **Classical psychoanalysis** (Classic): Freud, 1900-1939, with instinct theory and the structural model at its center.
- **Jungian analytical psychology** (Jungian/Analytical): Jung, 1913, the collective unconscious, the archetypes.
- **Individual psychology** (Adlerian): Adler, 1911, the feeling of inferiority, the striving for superiority, birth order.
- **The British object relations school** (Object Relations): Klein, Fairbairn, Winnicott, Balint, Guntrip.
- **Ego psychology** (Ego Psychology): Hartmann, Kris, Loewenstein, Rapaport.
- **Self psychology** (Self Psychology): Kohut (*The Analysis of the Self*, 1971), the development of the self through empathic responses from others.
- **Intersubjective theory** (Intersubjective): Stolorow, Atwood, Orange.
- **Relational psychoanalysis** (Relational): Mitchell, Greenberg.
- **The French Lacanian school** (Lacanian): Lacan, 1953-1981, jouissance, the Symbolic/Imaginary/Real.
- **Child psychoanalysis** (Child Psychoanalysis): Klein, A. Freud, Winnicott.
- Further currents include the **Bionian** school, **post-Lacanian** work, and **feminist and queer** psychoanalysis.

## Clinical Applications

Psychoanalysis and psychoanalytically informed therapy are practiced today in various forms (long-term analysis, psychodynamic psychotherapy, short-term dynamic therapy, child psychoanalysis, transference-focused psychotherapy), and have been applied to:

- **Personality difficulties**, including borderline personality disorder (for example transference-focused psychotherapy).
- **Chronic depression and anxiety**.
- **Addiction and eating disorders**.
- **Relationship and marital problems**.
- **Trauma** (as a theoretical framework rather than a treatment for acute trauma, where analytic treatment differs).
- **Community, educational, and cultural applications** (for example Freud's writings on culture and civilization).

## Relation to Neighboring Schools

- **Humanistic therapy** (Rogers): a reaction to analytic "pessimism."
- **Behaviorism** (Watson, Skinner): a reaction to analytic "internal reductionism."
- **Third-wave cognitive-behavioral approaches** (Hayes, Linehan): emphasize acceptance and values rather than interpretation of unconscious conflict.
- **Phenomenological-existential approaches** (Binswanger, Boss): an existential re-reading of Freud.

## Criticism and Limits

- **Contested empirical evidence**: critics, beginning with Eysenck's 1952 review, have questioned the evidence for long-term analysis, while later meta-analyses (for example of psychodynamic therapy by Shedler, 2010, and Leichsenring and colleagues) report benefits for several conditions; the debate continues.
- **Cost and duration**: classical analysis involves several sessions a week over years, which limits access.
- **Contested ideas and history**: parts of Freud's theory, such as his views on female sexuality, the death drive, and childhood sexuality, have been widely criticized, and his clinical methods and case reports have been re-examined by historians.
- **Continuity with revision**: post-Freudian currents (self psychology, relational, intersubjective) modified the core of the theory so that it is no longer "Freudian" in the literal sense.
