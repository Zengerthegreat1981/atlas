# Academic Study of Hinduism and Buddhism (Indology & Buddhology)

A European academic field that emerged in the nineteenth century around the critical edition, translation and study of Indian religious texts (Vedic and Sanskrit) and Buddhist texts (Sanskrit and Pali). It applied the tools of the comparative Indo-European philology then emerging to a vast textual heritage that had been known in the West only in scattered and unsystematic form before that.

## Burnouf: Founding Buddhist Studies

Eugène Burnouf, in *Introduction to the History of Indian Buddhism* (1844) and his posthumously published translation of the Lotus Sutra (*Le Lotus de la bonne loi*, 1852), marked the founding moment of Western academic "Buddhist Studies." He relied on rare Sanskrit manuscripts from Nepal and analyzed their textual strata historically with a philological precision unmatched in Europe at the time.

## Müller: Editing the Veda and the "Sacred Books of the East"

Max Müller, who studied with Burnouf in Paris, carried this project philologically toward the Vedic texts of Hinduism, beginning with his standard edition of the *Rig Veda* (1849–1874). He later supervised at Oxford the series *Sacred Books of the East* (50 volumes), which gathered English translations of many Asian religious texts (Hindu, Buddhist, Jain and Zoroastrian). This large project became a cornerstone for making these texts available to Western readers.

## Impact and Later Expansion

This first generation founded a field that continued to expand through specialized societies and orientalist schools in France, Germany and Britain. For the first time it made the essential Asian religious texts available in European languages, translated directly from their originals rather than from secondary intermediaries.

## Rhys Davids: Specializing in the Pali Tradition

In 1881 Thomas William Rhys Davids founded the **Pali Text Society** in London. He complemented the projects of Burnouf and Müller by editing the Pali tradition of Southeast Asia (Theravada in Ceylon, Burma and Siam), rather than focusing on the northern Sanskrit tradition alone. He came to philology from a background in British colonial administration in Ceylon, a distinction from Burnouf's path, which was more purely philological from the start. His translations later contributed to the formation of "Buddhist Modernism" as they interacted with contemporary Asian Buddhist reform movements.

## The Methodological Constraint: Lopez's Postcolonial Critique

As in the field of Islamic studies, this line of work was later subjected to a postcolonial critique, developed in the volume edited by Donald Lopez, *Curators of the Buddha: The Study of Buddhism under Colonialism* (1995). The search for an authentic textual "essence" of Buddhism or Hinduism through ancient manuscripts sometimes neglected the living religious practice of its adherents. It also reproduced a European image of a homogeneous, textual "religion" that does not necessarily match the complexity of actual practice on the ground.

## Conze: Editing the Perfection of Wisdom Tradition

In the mid-twentieth century Edward Conze continued the project of translation and editing by focusing on the Mahayana "Perfection of Wisdom" (Prajñāpāramitā) tradition. In *Buddhist Thought in India* (1962) he offered a systematic account of three phases of Buddhist philosophy, drawing on Sanskrit, Pali and Tibetan sources.

## Early Arab Precedents: al-Biruni, al-Shahrastani and the Story of Bilawhar and Budhasaf

Centuries before European orientalism, works on Indian religions were written in Arabic. The most important is Abu al-Rayhan al-Biruni's *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind min maqula maqbula fi al-'aql aw mardhula* (circa 1030 CE). He wrote it after living at the Ghaznavid court, traveling to northern India, and learning Sanskrit from its scholars. In it al-Biruni summarized material from Patanjali's *Yoga Sutra* and the *Samkhya*, and included passages from the *Bhagavad Gita*. He described the doctrines of transmigration, liberation and the caste system. He presented Indian ideas in their own terms rather than in the terms of their opponents, noted that the common people of India differ from its scholars, and called for fairness in transmitting what the Indians had. Edward Sachau edited the Arabic original in 1887 and translated it into English in 1888 in London, and the book thus entered the formation of European knowledge. Al-Shahrastani (d. 548 AH / 1153 CE) addressed the doctrines of India, including those of the Buddhists and the Brahmins, in *al-Milal wa-l-nihal*. The story of the Buddha passed into Arabic in the *Book of Bilawhar and Budhasaf*, which drew on earlier Middle Iranian material and later appeared in Islamic versions, including Ibn Babawayh's *Kamal al-Din wa-tamam al-ni'ma*. The same story reached Europe, through Georgian and Greek versions, as the legend of Barlaam and Josaphat. Daniel Gimaret edited the Ismaili version (Droz, 1971). The Arabic word *budd*, meaning idol, is widely held to derive from the name of the Buddha, which suggests that Buddhist images entered Islamic discourse early as images of idols.

## From Philology to Social History

As the field moved in Europe into a new phase, a later generation turned to the systematic history of Buddhism. Hermann Oldenberg wrote *Buddha: Sein Leben, seine Lehre, seine Gemeinde* (1881), and Étienne Lamotte wrote *History of Indian Buddhism* (Louvain, 1958) as a massive reference work on the sources. Gregory Schopen then, in *Bones, Stones, and Buddhist Monks* (University of Hawai'i Press, 1997), showed that the books on which researchers had relied presented an imagined Buddhism of a monk who renounces possessions. Inscriptions and archaeological remains, by contrast, reveal monasteries that owned land, dealt in money, venerated relics and performed rites for the dead. Schopen argued that these material facts had been neglected by text-centred traditions that measured Buddhism by Protestant standards. This extends Philip Almond's thesis in *The British Discovery of Buddhism* (1988), on the construction of a textual Buddhism to suit the image of religion held in Europe.

## Methodological Notes

This line of work shows that the textual approach produced investigations that remain indispensable, but it relied on official records held in monastery archives and gave them weight at the expense of everyday practice. Many questions therefore remained open: how a monk moves between the roles of teacher, property owner and healer, and for whom he reads and teaches the texts. Modern ethnography in Sri Lanka, Thailand and Burma has partly filled this gap. However, many traditions, such as Buddhism in Central Asia where it interacted with Islam, have remained less studied.
