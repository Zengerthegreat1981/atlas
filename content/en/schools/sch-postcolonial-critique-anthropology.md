# Postcolonial Critique of Anthropology (Asad, Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter)

## Definition

A critical current within anthropology itself, crystallized in the 1970s. It examines the structural relationship between the emergence of academic anthropology and European colonialism. Led by the anthropologist Talal Asad and others, it is one of the main sources of the "crisis of representation" that shook American and British anthropology in the 1980s.

## Historical Context

Talal Asad (born 1932), an anthropologist born in Medina to an Austrian-Jewish convert to Islam (Muhammad Asad) and a Saudi mother, who studied at Edinburgh and Oxford, published in 1973 his edited volume *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter*. It gathered essays by several researchers who examined, from differing standpoints, the connections between anthropology (especially British social anthropology) and colonial rule, raising issues such as how fieldwork could be funded by colonial institutions, access to "the field" was facilitated through the colonial administrative network itself, and the researcher's epistemic superiority over the non-European "object" of study could be implicitly assumed. This work was followed a few years later by Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), which offered a parallel critique from the standpoint of literary criticism. Together, the two currents prepared the intellectual ground for a broader wave of self-critique within the discipline that extended through the 1980s.

## Main Ideas and Method

This current argues that classical anthropology (evolutionism, and British functionalism in particular) was not merely a neutral discipline that happened to be studied in a colonial setting. Its tools and core concepts were structurally enabled by the empire, rather than the empire being an incidental background. This does not necessarily mean that every classical anthropologist was a colonizer in personal intent. Rather, the very possibility of the field and its conceptual instruments were shaped within an unequal power relationship that is difficult to separate from the knowledge it produced.

Asad later extended this critique in subsequent works, especially *Genealogies of Religion* (1993), to the central analytical concepts that Western anthropology and the social sciences themselves use, such as "religion" and "secularism." He argued that these are not neutral universal categories but products of a specific European history (especially the Protestant Reformation and the European Enlightenment). When imposed on other cultures, they sometimes distort the understanding of cultural and religious practices that do not share those European historical assumptions about the separation of the sacred and the worldly.

## Key Works and Representatives

Talal Asad's edited volume *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973) remains the foundational reference work of this current. Asad developed his deeper argument about religion and secularism in *Genealogies of Religion* (1993) and *Formations of the Secular* (2003). The critique later extended in several directions taken up by other researchers: James Ferguson, in his analysis of the discourse of "development" as a disguised mechanism of power in *The Anti-Politics Machine* (1990), and Michael Herzfeld, in *Anthropology Through the Looking-Glass* (1987), on the place of Greece and the European margins in the discipline's own hierarchies.

## Impact and Criticism

This critique paved the way for the wave of the "crisis of representation" in the 1980s, which culminated in James Clifford and George Marcus's edited volume *Writing Culture* (1986). It shifted anthropology toward methodological reflexivity, meaning the anthropologist's awareness of their own position and of their influence on the knowledge they produce about the Other. It became a basic reference for all later postcolonial anthropology around the world, from Africa to Latin America to East Asia.

Some critics, however, observed that although this critique is historically valid in general terms, it sometimes overstates the portrayal of all classical anthropology as a pliant colonial tool. It can overlook the varied positions of individual anthropologists, some of whom criticized colonial policies in their own time. This distinction is subtle and worth preserving, to avoid a crude generalization that would drain the critique of its precise analytical force.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

This critique is of particular importance given Talal Asad's partly Arab roots. His father, Muhammad Asad (born Leopold Weiss), was an Austrian-Jewish thinker who converted to Islam. Asad later applied his critical tools to the study of Islam and secularism, including material from Egypt, comparing them with the European context. Others extended the critique to specific Arab cases: Abdellah Hammoudi and Paul Rabinow, for example, reflected critically on anthropological fieldwork and knowledge-making in Morocco. This broadened the original horizon of Asad's critique, which had emerged mainly from the British imperial context, to include the French colonial experience in North Africa, which was administratively and culturally different.
