# Sociology of the Capitalist Ethic and Religion

Max Weber's famous thesis on the connection between the Calvinist Protestant ethic and the rise of modern capitalism in the West, which constituted an implicit response to the one-sided Marxist materialist interpretation of history.

## The Central Idea

In *The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism* (1905), Weber argued that Calvinism, through its doctrine of "predestination", which left the believer in constant anxiety about his or her fate in the afterlife, drove its followers to seek worldly signs of divine grace through success in disciplined worldly work and through saving (not lavish consumption). This behaviour produced, without its practitioners intending it, a "spirit" suited to the rational, systematic accumulation of capital, that is, modern capitalism. This is an example of what Weber called "elective affinity" (Wahlverwandtschaft) between a religious idea and an economic structure, not a deterministic one-directional causal relation.

## Historical Context

Weber wrote this thesis within the framework of his broader project of "comparative sociology of religions", which later included similar studies of Confucianism and Taoism in China, Hinduism and Buddhism in India, and ancient Judaism, with the aim of answering a great comparative question: why did modern rational capitalism arise specifically in Protestant Western Europe, and not in other civilizations that were economically and technically advanced at certain periods of their history (China, the Islamic world)? Weber did not claim that these other civilizations lacked the desire for profit or trade (for these, as he stressed, exist universally in all civilizations), but claimed that they specifically lacked a certain ethical-religious "spirit" that makes systematic disciplined work and continuous saving a moral-religious duty in itself, and not merely a means to a consumer end.

## Main Ideas and Method

Weber focused his analysis on the "ascetic" Protestant denominations (Ascetic Protestantism), Calvinism in particular, together with Pietism, Methodism and the Baptist sects. Central to Calvinism was the doctrine of "predestination": God has determined in advance and finally who will be saved and who will be damned, without the individual having any means of changing this fate through good works (unlike Catholicism, which allows wider mechanisms of atonement and forgiveness). This deep existential anxiety about the unknown fate in the afterlife produced what Weber called the unique "inner loneliness" of the Calvinist believer, who resorted to seeking indirect "signs" of belonging to the saved through continuous success in his worldly "calling" (Beruf/Calling), an entirely new concept of work that turned worldly work from an ordinary worldly activity into a religious duty sacred in itself. The combination of continuous disciplined work with the rejection of luxurious consumption (regarded as sin) produced an accumulated surplus of capital that was continuously reinvested, which is precisely the psychological-social mechanism that produced the "spirit" of modern rational capitalism, without the Calvinists themselves ever intending this worldly economic result.

## Prominent Works and Representatives

Max Weber (1864–1920), one of the three great founders of modern sociology alongside Durkheim and Marx, first published his thesis as two articles in the journal *Archiv für Sozialwissenschaft und Sozialpolitik* (1904–1905); a revised version appeared in his collected essays on the sociology of religion in 1920. Weber developed his comparative project in the studies of China, India and ancient Judaism gathered in the *Collected Essays on the Sociology of Religion* (1920–1921), and wrote a systematic sociology of religion published within *Economy and Society* after his death, applying his comparative method to other civilizations in order to test his thesis negatively and positively across world history.

## Position in the Debate with Marxism and Criticism

The thesis was usually read as the opposite of Marxist economic determinism (the base determines the superstructure), but Weber himself warned against reading it as an inverted opposite (the idea determines the economy), stressing that he offers one causal factor among multiple complex factors that interacted to produce modern capitalism, not a single-cause explanation replacing the Marxist one. Intensive historical criticism was directed at Weber's thesis throughout the twentieth century: economic historians objected that Catholic regions (northern Italy, Flanders) experienced early capitalist commercial prosperity even before the Protestant Reformation, and that the alleged causal relation between Calvinism and capitalism may be reversed or a historical coincidence with no necessary connection between them. Despite this continuing criticism, the thesis remained one of the most read, influential and cited sociological texts of the twentieth century, and a basic tool in any comparative discussion of the relation of religion to economic development, and it continues to be discussed in debates on the effect of cultural and religious values on economic development.
