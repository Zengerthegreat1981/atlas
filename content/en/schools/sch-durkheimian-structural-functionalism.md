# Durkheimian Structural Functionalism

Durkheimian structural functionalism is the school founded by Émile Durkheim (1858–1917), which treats society as a reality in its own right, external to individuals yet shaping their conduct, and explains social institutions by the functions they perform in maintaining social order. Durkheim argued that society is held together by shared beliefs and sentiments, by the division of labour, and by moral regulation, and that sociology's task is to study these "social facts" scientifically, as things. Durkheim did much to establish sociology as an academic discipline in France and founded the journal *L'Année sociologique*. Its concepts — solidarity, anomie, collective conscience, ritual, and the sacred — remain part of the discipline's core vocabulary.

## Historical Origins and Key Figures

Durkheim was born in Épinal in 1858, the son of a rabbi, and studied philosophy at the École Normale Supérieure. After study in Germany he was appointed at Bordeaux in 1887, where he taught some of the first university courses in sociology in France, and he moved to the Sorbonne in 1902, where he later held a chair in education that from 1913 also bore the name of sociology. In 1898 he founded *L'Année sociologique*, the journal around which his school gathered, and his students and collaborators included his nephew Marcel Mauss, the anthropologist of the gift. Durkheim's work was written against the background of the Third Republic, the Dreyfus Affair, rapid industrialisation, and the fear that modern individualism was dissolving moral bonds. He died in 1917, worn down by the death of his son André at the front in the First World War.

## Core Analytical Concepts

The school's foundation is the social fact: a way of acting, thinking, or feeling that is external to the individual and endowed with a power of coercion, such as law, language, custom, or fashion. Social facts must be explained by other social facts, not by individual psychology. In *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) Durkheim distinguished mechanical solidarity, the cohesion of small societies bound by likeness and a strong collective conscience, from organic solidarity, the cohesion of modern societies bound by interdependence between differentiated occupations. The collective conscience is the totality of beliefs and sentiments common to the average members of a society. Anomie is the condition in which norms lose their hold, leaving individuals without moral regulation, a state Durkheim linked to rapid social change. In religion he distinguished the sacred, things set apart and forbidden, from the profane, and argued that collective rituals generate collective effervescence, the shared energy that renews group bonds.

## Key Works and Method

Durkheim's method is set out in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895): treat social facts as things, discard preconceptions, define phenomena by their external characteristics, and explain a social fact by its cause and by its function. *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893) applied the method to the transition from traditional to modern society, arguing against Spencer that contract alone cannot bind a society. *Suicide* (1897) is the school's empirical masterpiece: using official statistics from several European countries, Durkheim showed that suicide rates vary with the degree of social integration and regulation, and distinguished egoistic suicide from weak integration, altruistic suicide from excessive integration, anomic suicide from deregulation, and fatalistic suicide from over-regulation. *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912) argued that the simplest religions reveal the social origins of the sacred.

## Influence and Legacy

Durkheim's influence runs through the whole of twentieth-century sociology and anthropology. In France his school shaped Mauss's anthropology and the sociology of the *Année* group; in Britain, Radcliffe-Brown and Malinowski adapted his functionalism to the study of small-scale societies, creating the British functionalism that is a separate, later development from Durkheim's own school. In the United States, Talcott Parsons built structural functionalism on Durkheim's theory of integration and norms, and Robert K. Merton refined it with the distinctions between manifest and latent functions and between functional, dysfunctional, and non-functional consequences. Durkheim's study of suicide is an early landmark of quantitative sociology and remains a model of sociological explanation.

## Critical Perspectives and Limitations

Durkheimian functionalism has been criticised from several directions. Its emphasis on order and integration leaves it poorly equipped to explain conflict, power, and change: if every institution performs a function, the theory seems to justify whatever exists and to treat domination as consensus. Marxists charge that Durkheim's collective conscience masks class interests, while feminists note that his account of solidarity ignored the gendered division of labour and the subordination of women. Methodologically, critics argue that Durkheim reifies society, treating an abstraction as if it were a thing with needs of its own, and that functional explanations are circular, inferring a function from the very existence of the institution. His study of suicide has been challenged for its reliance on official statistics, which reflect coroners' judgements rather than the acts themselves, and for forcing diverse cases into four types.

## Relationship to Other Schools

Durkheimian functionalism is defined partly by its contrasts with the other classical schools. Against Spencer, Durkheim accepted the organic analogy and the importance of differentiation, but insisted that modern solidarity rests on moral regulation rather than on contract and the market. Against Marx, he tended to treat class conflict as a symptom of abnormal forms of the division of labour rather than as the normal motor of history and located the unity of society in shared morality rather than in the relations of production, though both schools agree that society constrains individuals from outside. Against Weber, he reversed the direction of explanation: Weber began with individual meaning, while Durkheim began with collective representations that shape individual minds. Against Goffmanian dramaturgy, Durkheim's school works at the level of institutions and collective ritual rather than face-to-face performance. The school's enduring claim is that society is more than the sum of its members, and that this "more" is sociology's proper object.
