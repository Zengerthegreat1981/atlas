# German Historical School of Economics

The **German Historical School** was a current in political economy that flourished in Germany between the 1840s and the early twentieth century. It rejected the claim that there exist general economic laws valid for all times and places (as British classical economics had assumed from Smith and Ricardo onward). Instead, it insisted that economic phenomena can only be understood through detailed **empirical historical study** of each nation and each stage of its particular development, and that economic policies must be designed according to a country's stage of development rather than according to a ready-made universal prescription.

## Friedrich List and the Infant Industry Argument

**Friedrich List** preceded the generation of the school's academic founders, but he established its most important political argument in his book *The National System of Political Economy* (*Das nationale System der politischen Oekonomie*, 1841). List criticised Adam Smith's call for free world trade as a recipe that served the interests of the advanced industrial state (Britain), while keeping industrially backward states (such as Germany in his time) trapped in agricultural specialisation forever, because their emerging industries would not survive competition from the established British industry if borders were opened immediately. He proposed instead the **infant industry argument**: temporary and calculated customs protection for new domestic industries until they acquired the technical maturity and scale to compete internationally, after which protection would be gradually removed. This argument later influenced protectionist industrialisation policies in a number of countries seeking to catch up with industrial development.

## Roscher and the Academic Establishment of the Historical Method

**Wilhelm Roscher**, at the University of Leipzig, founded what came to be known as the "older historical school" (with Bruno Hildebrand and Karl Knies), beginning with his book *Outline of Political Economy from the Historical Standpoint* (1843). He called for economics to be studied with the method of the comparative descriptive historian: collecting detailed data on economic institutions across different eras and peoples (systems of agriculture, crafts and trade) in order to derive stages of development, instead of deducing abstract laws from a few theoretical premises as Ricardo had done.

## Schmoller and the Great Dispute with Austrian Marginalism

**Gustav von Schmoller**, the leading representative of the "younger historical school" and a professor of economics at the University of Berlin, led a more radical current in rejecting abstract deductive theory. He demanded that the economist begin by collecting historical and statistical data first, and that he draw no theoretical generalisations until sufficient empirical accumulation had taken place. Schmoller participated in founding the "Verein für Sozialpolitik" (Association for Social Policy), which pushed for practical social reforms (social insurance, the regulation of working conditions) in Bismarckian Germany. In the 1880s, Schmoller engaged in a famous methodological confrontation known as the **"Methodenstreit"** (dispute over methods) with the Austrian economist Carl Menger. Menger defended the possibility of building a general deductive economic theory from fixed individual principles (marginal utility), regardless of historical and institutional detail that changes over time.

## Karl Bücher and the Stages of Economic Development

In the following generation, **Karl Bücher** developed an attempt to classify the development of economies through successive stages defined by the extent of the circle of exchange (the closed household economy, then the urban economy, then the national economy). He continued in the general spirit of the school's historical project, searching for general developmental patterns through the study of comparative economic history rather than through the abstract deductive model.

## Legacy and Decline

The historical school lost its dominant position in German economics after the First World War to the rise of marginal and mathematical analysis. Yet its historical and institutional sense was partly transmitted, through pupils and those influenced by it such as Werner Sombart and Max Weber, into German economic sociology. Its critique of excessive theoretical abstraction also later contributed to the emergence of American old institutionalism, since some of its pioneers were influenced by its empirical method while studying in Germany.

## A National Economic Impulse Within a Broader Methodological Project

The methodological project of the historical school cannot be separated from its political context. It arose in Germany, which was still politically fragmented in the early nineteenth century and later unified under Prussian leadership, and it sought to build a strong national industrial economy that could catch up with British leadership. This made its rejection of the supposedly "neutral" British classical generalisation organically linked to an explicit national economic project. From this perspective, the British classical model was not a neutral science but a reflection of the interest of one particular advanced industrial nation at a particular historical moment, whereas an industrially backward nation such as Germany needed different analytical tools that took account of its own position in the ladder of global economic development.

## An Influence Extending Beyond Germany

The influence of this school went beyond Germany's borders to affect economists and social reformers in other countries seeking to catch up in industrialisation. A number of American economists studied in German universities in the late nineteenth century, absorbed the empirical historical method there, and then returned to found what later became known as American old institutionalism. In this way, the German historical school became an important, if indirect, intellectual link between early European rejection of classical abstraction and the later emergence of the independent American institutional tradition.
