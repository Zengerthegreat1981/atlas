# Mercantilism and Cameralism

**Mercantilism** was an economic and political doctrine that prevailed in Western Europe between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. It rests on a central assumption: that **a nation's wealth is measured by the gold and silver it holds**, and that the principal means of accumulating this wealth is achieving a **permanent trade surplus** (exporting more than importing). This calls for active state intervention: protective customs tariffs, royal trading monopolies, and support for domestic export industries. (Writers such as Mun and Edward Misselden were more nuanced than this simplification suggests, since they stressed the balance of trade rather than bullion hoarding as such.) In the German and Austrian lands a parallel doctrine, developed alongside rather than through documented influence from English mercantilism, was **cameralism**, the science of managing the state's resources and public finances, with a methodology closer to a "science of government administration" than to a theory of international trade.

## Thomas Mun and the classic formulation of the trade balance

**Thomas Mun** (1571–1641), an English merchant and director of the East India Company, provides the most mature formulation of the mercantilist doctrine in his book *England's Treasure by Forraign Trade* (published posthumously in 1664). In it he argues that the ordinary means to increase national wealth is foreign trade, wherein "we must ever observe this rule; to sell more to strangers yearly than wee consume of theirs in value", and that gold sent abroad temporarily to buy cheap raw materials returns many times over when those materials are re-exported as manufactured goods. This distinction between a negative overall trade balance with a particular country and a positive general balance with the world as a whole is considered the most precise mercantilist analysis of international trade of its time.

## Colbert and the directed industrial state

In France, **Jean-Baptiste Colbert** (1619–1683), finance minister to Louis XIV, embodied the most organized applied version of mercantilism ("Colbertism"): establishing royal manufactories (*Manufactures Royales*) for silk and luxury textiles, imposing protective customs tariffs on imports, regulating craft guilds to strict quality standards, and building a national merchant fleet that reduced France's dependence on Dutch and English shipping. Colbert's policies were the clearest application of the idea that the state, not the spontaneous market, is the actor that deliberately builds national wealth through planning.

## Justi and cameralism as the science of the state

In the German lands, **Johann Heinrich Gottlob von Justi** (1717–1771) organized the tradition (earlier shaped by writers such as Veit Ludwig von Seckendorff and Johann Joachim Becher) of *Polizeiwissenschaft* (the science of police or administration) and public finance into an integrated academic system known as cameralism. Its aim was to maximize the state's resources (financial, human and agricultural) through precise central administration, not necessarily through foreign trade as the English mercantilists did, but through organizing the entire domestic economy (agriculture, population, industry) in service of the state's power and wealth together. This tradition is today regarded as an early precursor of modern public administration as much as a theory of economics.

## Classical criticism

Mercantilism was the central target of **Adam Smith's** criticism in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). Smith argued that the true wealth of nations is not the quantity of gold hoarded but their productive capacity, and that mercantilist restrictions on trade impoverish all the parties exchanging rather than enriching them. In doing so he helped found the shift from the doctrine of trade surplus to the case for free trade, which Ricardo's theory of comparative advantage later placed on a more rigorous footing.

## Colonialism and monopoly companies as mercantilist instruments

Mercantilist states did not confine themselves to regulating domestic trade; they extended the same logic to colonies through trading companies holding monopoly privileges granted by the crown (such as the English East India Company, in which Mun served as director, and the Dutch East India Company). Raw materials were imported from the colonies at low prices and European manufactured goods were exported to them exclusively, in a system deliberately designed to maximize the trade surplus of the mother country at the colony's expense. This arrangement is today read as one of the direct economic foundations of the early European colonial system.

## Cameralism and population statistics

A distinctive feature of German-Austrian cameralism was its early interest in population as a source of the state's wealth rather than a burden on it. Justi and his contemporaries linked population density to the productive and military strength of the state, and proposed policies to encourage immigration and organize crafts so as to maximize the productive employment of the population. This approach is sometimes read as an early precursor of economic demography (political arithmetic), which William Petty developed in England at roughly the same time, in parallel.

## The fallacy of bullion accumulation

The central theoretical weakness that classical economics later attacked in mercantilism is its confusion of **money** with **wealth**: accumulating gold and silver does not in itself increase a nation's real productive capacity (land, labour, physical capital). It may raise domestic prices without adding real value if it is not matched by a corresponding expansion of production. This observation was later developed into the quantity theory of money, which the Spanish School of Salamanca had in fact anticipated by more than a century. This confusion between a precious metal and real wealth is what historians of economic thought sometimes call "the mercantilist illusion".
