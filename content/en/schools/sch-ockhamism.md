# Ockhamism / Nominalism

A philosophical-theological tradition deriving from William of Ockham (c. 1287-1347), an English Franciscan friar and Oxford-trained philosopher. Ockham is one of the most influential figures of late scholasticism: he criticized the realist theories of universals and the multiplication of metaphysical entities, developed a sophisticated logic of terms, and defended a strong view of divine freedom. His position is commonly called nominalism (or, more accurately, conceptualism or terminism) because he held that universals are not real things outside the mind but signs in thought and language.

## Founding Context

Ockham studied and lectured at Oxford in the 1310s, commenting on Lombard's *Sentences* around 1317-1319. Some of his teachings were examined at the papal court in Avignon from 1324, and a committee of theologians drew up a list of propositions for censure. In 1328 he left Avignon without permission, together with the Franciscan minister general Michael of Cesena, during the dispute over Franciscan poverty with Pope John XXII; he was excommunicated and took refuge at Munich under the protection of the emperor Louis of Bavaria, where he wrote political works until his death in 1347. He was never formally condemned as a heretic in the sense of a papal condemnation of his philosophy, although restrictions on teaching his ideas were imposed in universities such as Paris in the 1340s.

## Leading Figures

- **William of Ockham** (c. 1287-1347): the central figure.
- **Walter Chatton** (c. 1290-1343): Franciscan contemporary and critic of Ockham at Oxford.
- **Adam Wodeham** (c. 1298-1358): Franciscan who first studied with Ockham and developed his own influential positions.
- **Robert Holcot** (c. 1290-1349): Dominican theologian influenced by nominalist trends.
- **Jean Buridan** (c. 1300-after 1358): Paris arts master and logician who developed terminist logic and the impetus theory of motion; the famous "Buridan's ass" example is not found in his surviving writings.
- **Albert of Saxony** (c. 1320-1390): logician and natural philosopher at Paris and Vienna.
- **Marsilius of Inghen** (c. 1340-1396): taught at Paris and Heidelberg and spread terminist logic in the German lands.
- **Nicole Oresme** (c. 1320-1382): Paris scholar and translator of Aristotle into French.
- **Gregory of Rimini** (c. 1300-1358): Augustinian theologian influenced by the Oxford and Paris debates.

## Central Concepts

### 1. Parsimony

Ockham's name is attached to "Ockham's razor," the principle that plurality should not be posited without necessity (*pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate*). The exact slogan "entities are not to be multiplied beyond necessity" is a later formulation not found in his works in this form, but the spirit runs through his arguments against positing relations, universals, and other entities not required to explain things.

### 2. Universals

Ockham argued against realism about universals: only individual things exist, and universals are concepts or terms that signify many individuals. Against Scotus's "common nature" he held that no real distinction, even a formal one, exists within an individual thing corresponding to universal and individual.

### 3. Supposition

Ockham developed the medieval theory of supposition, concerning how a term stands for something in a proposition. In "Every man is an animal" the term "man" has *personal* supposition (standing for individual men); in "Man is a species" it has *simple* supposition (standing for a concept); in "Man is a noun" it has *material* supposition (standing for the word itself).

### 4. Signification and Connotation

Ockham distinguished absolute terms, which signify things directly (such as "man"), from connotative terms, which signify something primarily and something else secondarily (such as "white," which signifies a white thing and connotes whiteness). This analysis allowed him to dispense with many abstract entities.

### 5. Intuitive and Abstractive Cognition

- **Intuitive cognition**: direct cognition of an existing thing, by which one can judge that it exists or does not exist.
- **Abstractive cognition**: cognition that abstracts from the existence or non-existence of its object.

### 6. Divine Power and Will

Ockham emphasized God's omnipotence and freedom. He distinguished God's absolute power (what God could do without contradiction) from His ordained power (what God has in fact established). Moral obligation depends on God's commands, though scholars debate how far Ockham took the claim that God could have commanded otherwise.

### 7. Politics and Poverty

In the *Opus nonaginta dierum* (c. 1332-1334), the *Dialogus*, and the *Breviloquium de potestate pape* (1341), Ockham defended the Franciscan ideal of poverty and argued that papal authority is limited and that a pope may fall into heresy. He also supported the imperial position against the papacy in temporal matters.

## Ockhamism and "Nominalism"

"Nominalism" is a loose label. Medieval writers spoke of the *nominales* and the *via moderna* in contrast to the *via antiqua*, without implying uniform doctrine; the modern sense of "nominalism" as the denial of real universals applies to Ockham only in a qualified way, since he allowed real qualities and individual things and treated concepts as natural signs in the mind. Many scholars therefore prefer to speak of terminism, or of Ockham's own conceptualist position, rather than of a unified "Ockhamist school."

## Reception

- **Paris and the German universities**: from the 1340s statutes at Paris restricted or targeted "Ockhamist" teachings; Buridan, Albert of Saxony, and Marsilius of Inghen continued the terminist tradition of logic, and it spread to Vienna, Prague, Heidelberg, and Erfurt.
- **Late medieval theology**: Gabriel Biel (c. 1420-1495) at Tübingen represents the late nominalist tradition.
- **The Reformation**: Martin Luther was educated in the *via moderna* at Erfurt and later reacted strongly against its theology of merit; his doctrine of justification by faith is not simply an Ockhamist inheritance.
- **Early modern philosophy**: the focus on individuals and the criticism of scholastic entities are often linked, loosely, to later empiricism, but direct lines of influence are disputed.
- **Logic and science**: Ockham's razor remains a methodological maxim in science, and his analysis of terms is studied by historians of logic and semantics.

## Philosophical Difficulties

- **Knowledge of generals**: if universals are only signs, how can science, which is about general truths, be grounded in individuals? Ockham answered that scientific propositions concern terms that stand for individuals.
- **Divine omnipotence and morality**: if morality depends on God's commands, is anything intrinsically good? Ockham's answer is debated.
- **Relations**: Ockham tried to dispense with real relations as distinct entities, which raised questions about causation and order that later empiricists pressed.
- **Politics**: his ideas on papal authority and poverty brought him into conflict with Pope John XXII and shaped later conciliarist debates.
