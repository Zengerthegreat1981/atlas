# Comparative Religious Gender, Feminist, and Queer Studies

A critical, interdisciplinary field that applies the tools of feminist theory, gender studies and queer theory to the comparative study of religions. It examines how religious traditions produce and regulate gender and sexual identity, and how religious texts, rituals and religious authority are reread from a critical gender perspective. The field took shape within the second wave of Western feminism in the late 1960s and early 1970s, when scholars such as Mary Daly (*Beyond God the Father*, 1973) and Rosemary Radford Ruether began questioning the male images of God and the patriarchal structures of power within Christian theology.

## Islamic Jurisprudence and the Arab Heritage in Gender Studies

The Arab heritage has a place in this field in two ways. The classical jurisprudential and literary tradition is itself material for gender analysis, and contemporary Arabic writing is part of the feminist debate. Islamic jurisprudence addressed ambiguous sex in the discussion of the *khuntha mushkil* (the person of ambiguous sex), and set rules for inheritance, prayer and mixing according to which sex predominated. This material is used in studying how gender is constructed legally. Medieval literary sources (al-Jahiz, al-Isfahani, and al-Tifashi in *Nuzhat al-Albab*) recorded the *mukhannathun* in the early city and patterns of male love poetry that do not match the modern binary of "homosexual/heterosexual." Khaled El-Rouayheb's *Before Homosexuality in the Arab-Islamic World, 1500–1800* (2005) examined how the act, rather than the identity, was viewed. In the modern era, Qasim Amin in *The Liberation of Women* (1899) called for reform of women's status. Nawal El Saadawi wrote *Woman and Sex* in the early 1970s, critiquing circumcision and patriarchal authority. Fatima Mernissi, in *Le harem politique* (1987; English translation *The Veil and the Male Elite*, 1991), detailed how interpretations of the text were used to exclude women from the public sphere.

## Historical Phases

- **First wave (1970s–1980s):** the recovery of women's voice and presence, which had been obscured in religious texts and history, and critical rereading of sacred scriptures from a feminist perspective. Examples include Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza's *In Memory of Her* (1983) on Christian origins, and Judith Plaskow's *Standing Again at Sinai* (1990) on Judaism.
- **From "woman" to "gender" (1990s):** the field moved from studying "women in religion" to studying gender as a broader analytic category. This was influenced by Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) and performance theory of gender, and by the growing recognition that sexual identities are more plural and fluid than early studies had assumed.
- **Islamic feminism:** Amina Wadud, in *Qur'an and Woman* (1992), and Asma Barlas, in *Believing Women in Islam* (2002), offered readings of the Qur'anic text that separate the egalitarian principle from patriarchal interpretation. Leila Ahmed published *Women and Gender in Islam* (1992), a history of gendered discourse in Islamic civilization.
- **Emergence of queer theology:** a queer theory applied to religion branched out from gender studies. It examines the exclusion of gay, lesbian, bisexual and transgender people from traditional religious conceptions of family, marriage and sin, and seeks alternative readings of sacred texts (such as the story of David and Jonathan, or Sodom and Gomorrah) that challenge the prevailing condemnatory readings. In Islam, Scott Siraj al-Haqq Kugle wrote *Homosexuality in Islam* (2010).
- **Transgender studies:** the newest branch, which expanded notably from the 2010s. It examines the experiences of transgender people within religious communities.

## Comparative Across Traditions

Comparative studies document non-binary gender patterns in several traditions: the *hijra* in India (Serena Nanda, *Neither Man nor Woman*, 1990); the "two-spirit" tradition in Native North America (Will Roscoe, on the Zuni "man-woman," 1991); and the Buddhist debate on sex and gender in the collection edited by José Cabezón (1992). These examples show that the gender binary is not a universal premise. They also call for caution: local categories are defined by their context and cannot be reduced to the modern notion of a "third gender."

## Shinto and African Religious Traditions

In **Shinto**, scholars set female figures and offices, such as the sun deity Amaterasu, the imperial princesses who served at the Ise shrine in early and medieval Japan, and the shrine attendants known as *miko*, against restrictions linked to ritual impurity, including that associated with menstruation and childbirth. Carmen Blacker's study of Japanese shamanic practice (1975) documented the prominence of women as mediums, much of it in settings that cross Shinto, Buddhist and folk boundaries. In the modern period the male-dominated priesthood opened to women around the time of the Second World War and after it, though their standing remains a subject of discussion.

For **African traditions**, Ifi Amadiume's *Male Daughters, Female Husbands* (1987) showed that among the Igbo of Nnobi social roles could be separated from sex, with women able to occupy positions coded as male. Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí's *The Invention of Women* (1997) argued that gender was not a primary organizing principle in precolonial Yoruba society, a thesis that has been contested. J. Lorand Matory (1994) analyzed how Oyo-Yoruba possession priesthood uses the imagery of being a wife or mount of a deity for priests of both sexes. These studies concern particular societies and cannot stand for all African religions.

## Institutions and Journals

- *Journal of Feminist Studies in Religion*, a leading feminist journal in religious studies, whose first issues appeared in 1985.
- The "Queer Studies in Religion" unit within the American Academy of Religion (AAR), which provides an annual forum for this field.

## Limits and Critique

The field faces recurring charges from within conservative religious traditions that it imposes secular Western categories on non-Western texts and contexts. Saba Mahmood, in *The Politics of Piety* (2005), offered a critique from within the discipline. She studied the women's mosque movement in Cairo and showed that the Western feminist concept of "agency," based on resistance, does not explain women who find self-realization in submitting to norms of piety. There is also a critique of the bias of academic production toward Anglo-American and Christian-Jewish contexts, which sometimes limits cross-cultural comparison to secondary sources. A further criticism concerns the non-participation of religious women who do not identify as feminists in setting the field's questions.
