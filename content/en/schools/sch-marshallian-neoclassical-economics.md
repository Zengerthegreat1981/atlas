# Marshallian Neoclassical Economics

**Marshallian neoclassical economics** is the British current that reformulated the marginal revolution in a language more amenable to practical application and teaching. It centres on a single pivotal work, the book *Principles of Economics* (1890) by **Alfred Marshall**, which remained the standard reference textbook for teaching economics in English-speaking universities for decades afterwards. The label "neoclassical" was not Marshall's own: it was coined by Thorstein Veblen in 1900 as a critical description of Marshall and his contemporaries, and later became a neutral term in the history of economic thought.

## Partial Equilibrium Instead of General Equilibrium

Unlike Walras, who sought a mathematical model describing all markets together, Marshall chose a more practical method: studying a single market in relative analytical isolation from the rest of the economy, on the assumption that "all else remains equal" (*Ceteris Paribus*). This methodological simplification permitted precise analysis that could be applied directly to particular markets. It made Marshall's tools, the supply and demand curves intersecting on a single simple graph, the most widely recognised image of economics as a whole in university teaching to this day.

## Elasticity: An Analytical Tool Devised by Marshall

Marshall gave **price elasticity of demand** its standard form and name in the *Principles*. (Antoine Augustin Cournot had already written the responsiveness of quantity to price in mathematical terms in 1838.) The measure shows how responsive the quantity demanded of a good is to a relative change in its price. The tool made it possible to distinguish necessities with low elasticity (such as bread) from luxuries with high elasticity. It became one of the most widely used tools of microeconomics in the applied analysis of tax and trade policy until today.

## Time in Economic Analysis: The Short and Long Run

Marshall distinguished the **short run**, in which some factors of production (such as factories and machinery) are fixed and cannot be adjusted quickly, from the **long run**, in which all factors of production become adjustable. This temporal distinction made it possible to explain complex phenomena, such as a firm continuing to produce at a loss in the short run so long as it covers its variable costs, while it tends to leave the market in the long run if price stays below average cost.

## Edgeworth: Mathematical Rigour Within the Marshallian Tradition

Francis Ysidro Edgeworth, Marshall's contemporary (and later professor at Oxford), contributed more abstract mathematical tools within the same tradition. The most prominent of these is the **Edgeworth box** (developed from his *Mathematical Psychics* of 1881 and given its familiar diagrammatic form by later economists such as Pareto and Bowley), which depicts graphically how two parties exchanging goods can both improve their positions through trade until reaching a point at which neither can be made better off without harming the other. This idea would later become the cornerstone of the concept of Pareto efficiency.

## Consumer Surplus: A Tool for Measuring Welfare

Marshall developed the concept of **consumer surplus**: the difference between the maximum price that a consumer was prepared to pay for a good and the actual price that he paid in the market. It serves as an approximate quantitative measure of the net benefit that consumers gain from commercial exchange. It later became fundamental in the cost-benefit analysis of a range of government policies.

## Monopolistic Competition: Softening the Ideal Assumption

Marshall and his successors recognised that real markets are rarely perfectly competitive in the pure sense. Piero Sraffa's 1926 critique of the assumptions of perfect competition, and then Joan Robinson's *The Economics of Imperfect Competition* and Edward Chamberlin's *The Theory of Monopolistic Competition* (both 1933), paved the way for the development of more realistic models that acknowledge degrees of individual market power for each producer without abandoning the essence of the marginal analytical framework.

## The Enduring Educational Impact

Marshall's *Principles of Economics* remained the principal reference text for university teaching of economics in Britain and the United States for more than forty years after its publication. Generations of economists were trained in his method, among them John Maynard Keynes himself, who studied under Marshall in Cambridge before building his own critique on fundamentally different foundations in the twentieth century.

## A Conciliatory Rather Than an Openly Revolutionary Approach

Unlike Jevons, who proclaimed an open break with the classical economists, Marshall deliberately adopted a less contentious conciliatory approach. He wished to show that the new marginal analytical tools (marginal utility on the demand side) complement the heritage of Ricardo and Smith (production costs on the supply side) rather than replacing it entirely. He presented the determination of price as the outcome of the intersection of the "two blades of a pair of scissors", supply and demand together, rather than as a victory for either one. This conciliatory choice made his work more acceptable in the relatively conservative British academic circles of the late nineteenth century, and it helped establish the name "Economics", in place of "Political Economy", for the academic field as a whole, beginning with this very book.

## The Cambridge Institution and the Official School of Economics

Alongside his theoretical contribution, Marshall also built the institutional framework that made economics an independent academic discipline separate from moral philosophy in Britain. In 1903 he succeeded in establishing the "Economics Tripos" at the University of Cambridge as an independent degree programme, after economics had previously been taught as part of the broader moral sciences programme. This institutional separation consolidated the image of economics as an independent technical science with its own analytical tools. It was an institutional shift that accompanied the intellectual transition from the broader classical "political economy" to the more narrowly specialised "economics" that we know today.
