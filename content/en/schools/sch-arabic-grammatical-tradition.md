# Classical Arabic Grammatical Tradition

The classical Arabic grammatical and linguistic tradition is a system of language study that arose in the 2nd century AH (8th century CE), driven largely by religious motives: preserving the recitation of the Qur'an from solecism after the expansion of the Islamic conquests and the entry of non-Arabic speakers into the faith. It developed into a complete theoretical system of grammar, morphology and lexicography, and it remained the principal reference for the study of Arabic for more than a thousand years.

## Origins in Basra and Kufa

The founding of grammar is traditionally attributed to Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī in Basra, but the school proper crystallized under Khalīl ibn Aḥmad al-Farāhīdī (d. c. 170 AH / 786 CE), who is also credited with the first Arabic dictionary, *Kitāb al-ʿAyn*, and the science of prosody. Its systematic formulation was completed by his student **Sībawayh** (ʿAmr ibn ʿUthmān ibn Qanbar, d. c. 180 AH), of Persian origin, who migrated to Basra. In *al-Kitāb*, a comprehensive work on Arabic grammar, morphology and parts of its phonology, he set out the analysis that later grammarians took as their main reference. The *Aṣṭādhyāyī* of Pāṇini is an earlier systematic grammar in another tradition. The Basra school was rivaled by the **Kufan school**, which relied on hearing and poetic transmission with greater flexibility, while Basra relied on logical analogy and rigorous induction from the speech of eloquent Bedouins. Between the two schools arose a long methodological dispute over the foundations of grammatical reasoning (analogy versus hearing), known as the disputes between the Basrans and the Kufans. Modern scholars such as Versteegh note that the neat division into two rival schools was shaped by later biographical literature and should not be taken at face value.

## Method and theoretical tools

Arab grammarians developed original analytical tools. The theory of the governor (*ʿāmil*) explains inflection through the effect of sentence elements on one another. Analogy (*qiyās*) extends the rule of an established case to a new case that shares its operative cause (*ʿilla*). Grammatical reasoning (*taʿlīl*) seeks logical "causes" for linguistic phenomena, reaching in some cases the level of a grammatical philosophy. **Ibn Jinnī** (d. 392 AH / 1002 CE), in *al-Khaṣāʾiṣ*, examined the origin and nature of language and the relation between word and meaning. Among his proposals is the "greater derivation" (*al-ishtiqāq al-akbar*), a claimed correspondence between patterns of sounds and patterns of meaning.

## From grammar to rhetoric

This tradition provided the groundwork on which the Arabic sciences of rhetoric were later built. **ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī** (d. 471 AH; some sources give 474) developed from grammatical data a complete semantic theory of *naẓm* (composition) in speech. He shifted attention from the correctness of inflection alone to the function of constructions in conveying meaning and their rhetorical effect. This later branched into the sciences of *maʿānī* and *bayān*, as a direct extension of grammar rather than a break from it.

## The limits of induction

The grammarians relied on a corpus that was limited in time and place: the "eloquent" speech of the Arabs, namely pre-Islamic and early Islamic poetry and the speech of Bedouins in the desert before the natural language was corrupted by mixing. They deliberately excluded the language of the urban, later-generation speakers (*muwallads*). This was a conscious methodological choice rather than a deficiency. It enabled them to build an internally consistent grammatical system, but it also produced a gap between the grammatical standard and changing everyday usage, which remains a subject of debate among modern Arab linguists.

## Impact and modern scholarship

Western scholars have studied this tradition with the methods of modern linguistics, most notably **Kees Versteegh** in his history of the Arabic linguistic tradition, **Michael Carter** in his work on Sībawayh, and **Jonathan Owens** in analyzing the structure of medieval Arabic grammatical theory and comparing it with modern approaches. Some scholars have pointed to parallels between aspects of Sībawayh's analysis and modern formal theories, while others caution against reading the medieval grammarians anachronistically.

## Later consolidation

The tradition continued well beyond its formative period. Al-Zamakhsharī (d. 538 AH / 1144 CE) wrote the concise grammar *al-Mufaṣṣal*, on which Ibn Yaʿīsh (d. 643 AH / 1245 CE) composed a widely used commentary. Ibn Mālik (d. 672 AH / 1274 CE) summarised grammar in the verse text *al-Alfiyya*, and Ibn Hishām (d. 761 AH / 1360 CE) wrote *Mughnī al-labīb*, a reference on particles and syntax. These works fixed the school curriculum for centuries.
