# History of Memory: Collective Memory and Lieux de Mémoire (Halbwachs, Nora)

A shift in the object of historical study, from "what actually happened" to **how a society collectively remembers its past**, and where this collective memory becomes embodied materially and symbolically.

## Maurice Halbwachs and the Concept of Collective Memory

In *The Social Frameworks of Memory* (*Les Cadres sociaux de la mémoire*, 1925), the sociologist **Maurice Halbwachs** (a student of Durkheim) advanced a foundational thesis: individual memory itself is not an isolated psychological process, but is **socially constructed**. We remember the past through collective frameworks supplied by the family, religion, class and nation, and even intimate personal memories are reformulated in a language and with concepts that society provides. This distinguishes "collective memory" from "history" in the academic sense: the former is lived and constantly changing according to the needs of the present, while the latter is a critical record that seeks permanence.

## Pierre Nora and the Sites of Memory

Six decades later, the historian **Pierre Nora** developed the encyclopedic project *Les Lieux de mémoire* (*Realms of Memory*, 7 volumes, 1984–1992) around a central concept: the **"lieu de mémoire"** (site of memory), any material, symbolic or functional entity (a war memorial, a national flag, an anthem, a school textbook, a museum) in which a collective memory crystallizes after its direct living connection with the past has been broken. Nora opens his project with a sharp thesis: "We talk so much about memory because there is so little of it left." That is, the proliferation of deliberately built "sites of memory" (monuments, commemorations, museums) is precisely the symptom of the **disappearance** of the spontaneous living memory that traditional societies carried without any need to anchor it in objects.

## Method and the Difference from Traditional History

- **Memory as an object of study, not a neutral source**: instead of using oral or collective memory merely as an additional source of facts (as in oral history, section 8), this current makes **memory itself**, how it forms, is forgotten and is politically manufactured, the first object of historical analysis.
- **Memory as a political battleground**: any "site of memory" (a monument, an official history) is the product of a selection and an exclusion that reflect contemporary political balances of power, not an innocent reflection of the past. This is directly connected to the critique of nineteenth-century national history (see section 2 of this chapter).
