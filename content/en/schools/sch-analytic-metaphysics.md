# Contemporary Analytic Metaphysics

A philosophical movement active since the 1970s, which revived metaphysics as a legitimate philosophical project after decades of positivist suspicion of it. The positivist circle had judged metaphysical claims meaningless according to the verification principle; this verdict receded in the second half of the century with Quine's critique of the analytic–synthetic distinction in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), and with the return of ontological questions in "On What There Is" (1948). The present movement began with the contributions of Saul Kripke ("Naming and Necessity", lectures of 1970, published in 1972 and then as a book in 1980), who distinguished between necessity and a priori knowledge and showed that statements such as "Water is H2O" are necessary and known a posteriori. It then took shape in the work of David Lewis, Peter van Inwagen, Theodore Sider and others.

## The central thesis

- **Modal metaphysics**: questions such as "Do numbers exist?" or "What are qualities?" can be examined through metaphysical necessity in Kripke's sense, with possible worlds as a tool for analyzing them.
- **Abstract realism**: universals, natural kinds, sets and numbers exist objectively according to many adherents of the movement.
- **The method of ontology**: Quine set the criterion of ontological commitment, "to be is to be the value of a variable", and the movement based its discussion on what our best theories commit us to.
- **Priority and grounding (Grounding)**: in recent years the concept of grounding has become central to explaining the relation between what is fundamental and what is derivative, as in Kit Fine and Jonathan Schaffer.
- **Scientific realism**: it affirms that unobservable scientific entities exist and that successful theories are approximately true; this is closer to the philosophy of science but intersects with metaphysics.

## Contributions

- **Modal realism** in Lewis's "On the Plurality of Worlds" (1986): possible worlds exist just as concretely as our own, and modal claims are analysed in terms of what holds in them. Kripke, by contrast, treated possible worlds as stipulated ways things might have been rather than as concrete universes, and the two differ on how to understand an individual's existence in other worlds (Lewis's counterpart theory against Kripke's talk of the same individual in other worlds).
- **The theory of universals** in David Armstrong's "Universals and Scientific Realism" (1978) and Lewis's "New Work for a Theory of Universals" (1983).
- **Personal identity and persistence through time**: Derek Parfit in "Reasons and Persons" (1984) argued that identity is not what matters; this is contrasted with the four-dimensionalist theses in Sider (2001) and with perdurantism.
- **Economical ontology**: van Inwagen in "Material Beings" (1990) holds that only living composite beings exist and denies the existence of chairs and tables, and Trenton Merricks in "Objects and Persons" (2001) holds a similar view.
- **Metametaphysics**: "Metametaphysics" (Chalmers, Manley and Wasserman, 2009) discusses whether ontological disputes are substantive or verbal, and Eli Hirsch defends quantifier variance.

## Branches

- The metaphysics of necessity and possibility.
- The metaphysics of properties and universals.
- Personal identity and the nature of the person.
- Philosophy of time: eternalism versus presentism.
- Events and causation.
- Free will: compatibilism and incompatibilism.
- Set theory and numbers.

## Connection to the Islamic heritage

Modal metaphysics today re-poses questions the Muslim philosophers discussed in another language. Ibn Sina's distinction between the necessary being and the possible being is a direct point of contact; Michael Marmura offered a close analysis of Avicenna's proof from contingency in the Metaphysics of the *Shifāʾ*, and some commentators have compared Alvin Plantinga's modal ontological argument in "The Nature of Necessity" (1974) with Avicennian arguments from necessity, although the comparison is contested. The question of essence and existence in Ibn Sina, al-Suhrawardi and Mulla Sadra is likewise compared with the discussions of substance and the universal in Armstrong and Lewis; the question of causation and habit in al-Ghazali with the question of causation in Hume and Lewis; and the kalam arguments about the indivisible part with discussions of continuity and particles.

## Criticism

- Carnap in "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950) cast doubt on "external questions" about the existence of entities, treating them as practical matters of choosing a linguistic framework rather than a factual question.
- Putnam in "Reason, Truth and History" (1981) denied the possibility of a description of the world independent of mind and language and advanced "internal realism", and van Fraassen in "The Scientific Image" (1980) presented a constructive-empiricist position that is wary of commitment to unobservable entities.
- The charge of verbal disputes: the disagreement between two philosophers may be about words, not about reality.
- Objectors hold that the philosopher's intuition about possibility is not valid evidence, and that the price of Lewis's modal realism is a bloated ontology that many find strange.
