# National Historiography and Nation-Building (19th Century)

In the nineteenth century, the writing of history in Europe became a central instrument in **building national identity**. The past was narrated as a connected course leading inevitably to the present nation-state, and events that served this narrative were selected while those that contradicted it were neglected.

## The Phenomenon

With the rise of national movements (German and Italian unification, nationalisms within multi-ethnic empires), universities and ministries of education across Europe funded projects to record "the history of the nation". These were often carried out with the same Rankean source-critical tools (national archives, historical societies, specialised journals), but deployed in the service of a synthetic narrative with a clear political purpose: demonstrating the antiquity and cultural unity of the nation across centuries, and justifying its present political borders by tracing them back to deep historical "roots".

## Mechanisms

- **National archiving**: The establishment of central national archives and libraries to gather and organise the documents of the "nation". This provided vast raw material, but also framed what counts as a "source" deserving preservation according to dominant national priorities.
- **School curricula**: The transformation of specialised historical narrative into standard educational curricula, instilled in collective consciousness from childhood — a direct educational-political function of history.
- **Choosing heroes and founding events**: Spotlighting particular battles, figures or periods as the "founding moments" of the nation (for example, invoking the Germanic Middle Ages in German national discourse, or the French Revolution in French republican discourse), while marginalising periods or groups that did not serve the unified narrative (ethnic and religious minorities, local history in conflict with the centre).

## Limitation and Later Critique

This mode of historiography later became the subject of central criticism in critical philosophy of history and the linguistic turn (see section 5 of this part). The idea that the "nation" is a natural entity extending across history, rather than a modern construct projected backwards onto the past, is precisely what later critical studies of nationalism refuted (such as Anderson's concept of "imagined communities" and Hobsbawm and Ranger's "invention of tradition"). The latter belong to a later period (the twentieth century) outside the scope of this file.
