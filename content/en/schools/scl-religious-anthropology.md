# Religious Anthropology

Religious anthropology — the anthropology of religion — studies how people believe, worship, and live with the sacred. Its subject is the full range of religious life: gods and spirits, rituals and prayers, priests and shamans, magic and witchcraft, sacred texts and sacred places, and the experience of the holy. Where theology asks whether religious claims are true, anthropology asks what religion does: how it binds people together, how it explains suffering and death, how it legitimates power, and how it changes. The field's founding insight is that religion is not a single thing that all peoples share, but a family of human practices that take radically different forms — and that understanding those forms requires taking seriously the worlds in which believers live, rather than reducing their faith to error, ignorance, or psychology.

## Origins and History

The anthropology of religion is as old as anthropology itself. Its founders were Victorian evolutionists: E. B. Tylor, in Primitive Culture (1871), defined religion as belief in spiritual beings and argued that it began in animism, the belief that nature is animated by spirits, and developed through polytheism toward monotheism. James Frazer's The Golden Bough (1890) collected the myths and rites of the world into a vast comparative scheme, arguing that magic preceded religion and religion preceded science. Émile Durkheim, in The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912), rejected the evolutionist program and argued that religion is society worshipping itself: the sacred is the community's own power, projected onto totems and gods, and ritual is what makes a society real to its members. Durkheim's argument made religion a sociological fact rather than a stage of intellectual development.

The field's modern form was made by ethnography. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande (1937) showed that Azande beliefs about witchcraft were not irrational but a coherent system for explaining misfortune: when a granary collapses, the Azande know the termites ate the supports, but they ask why it fell on that person at that moment, and witchcraft answers the question of why. Evans-Pritchard's work established the principle that religious beliefs must be judged by their internal logic, not by Western standards of rationality. In the 1960s Clifford Geertz redefined the field again, defining religion as a system of symbols that creates moods and motivations and gives an order to experience. His essay "Religion as a Cultural System" (1966) made the interpretation of meaning, rather than the explanation of function, the field's central task. The 1980s and 1990s brought the critique: Talal Asad, in Genealogies of Religion (1993), argued that the very category of "religion" was a product of Western history, shaped by the Reformation and the secular state, and that anthropologists had imposed it on peoples for whom belief, practice, and power were not separable.

## Key Figures and Works

Tylor's Primitive Culture (1871) was a founding work of the field. Frazer's The Golden Bough (1890) made it a public sensation. Durkheim's The Elementary Forms of Religious Life (1912) gave it its first sociological theory. Evans-Pritchard's Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande (1937) is the classic ethnography of religious thought, and his later Nuer Religion (1956) showed how a people's idea of God is bound up with their social life. Geertz's "Religion as a Cultural System" (1966) set the interpretive agenda for a generation. Victor Turner's The Ritual Process (1969) developed the concepts of liminality (drawn from Arnold van Gennep) and communitas, showing how ritual creates a space outside ordinary structure in which new social bonds are formed. Mary Douglas's Purity and Danger (1966) showed how ideas of pollution and taboo express a society's sense of order. Talal Asad's Genealogies of Religion (1993) forced the field to question its own categories. More recent landmarks include the work of Saba Mahmood on piety and agency in Egypt's mosque movement, and the ethnographies of Pentecostalism, Buddhism, and new religious movements that study how religions travel and transform in a global world.

## Method

The anthropology of religion is built on long-term participant observation in religious life: attending rituals, praying with believers, learning sacred languages, and living through the religious calendar. The ethnographer records what people do as well as what they say they believe, because practice and doctrine often diverge. The field's methods include the analysis of myth and symbol, the study of ritual as performance, and the recording of religious experience — dreams, visions, trance, and possession — in the believer's own terms. A distinctive method is comparison without reduction: setting the rituals of different peoples side by side to find patterns without claiming that all religions are the same. The field's ethical standard is respect for belief: the anthropologist may not share the faith she studies, but she must describe it as its believers experience it, and she must be honest about the limits of a secular science in accounting for religious experience.

## Influence and Legacies

The anthropology of religion has shaped the study of ritual, symbol, and society far beyond its own boundaries. Durkheim's theory of the sacred underpins the sociology of religion; Turner's concepts of liminality and communitas are used in performance studies, theatre, and the study of pilgrimage; Douglas's work on purity shaped the study of the body and of disgust. The field's critique of its own categories, led by Asad, has influenced the study of secularism, the politics of religious freedom, and the anthropology of the state. The field's current frontier is the study of religion in a secular age: the growth of Pentecostalism in the global South, the revival of Orthodoxy in Eastern Europe, the spread of mindfulness and new spiritualities in the West, and the question of what happens to religion when it leaves the church, the mosque, and the temple and enters the market and the street.
