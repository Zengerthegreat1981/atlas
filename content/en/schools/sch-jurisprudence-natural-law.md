# Natural Law Jurisprudence

A tradition in the philosophy of law holding that the validity of positive law is conditional on its conformity with higher moral standards rooted in human nature, reason, or the cosmic order. This contrasts with legal positivism, which separates the validity of law from its moral content.

## The Major Trajectories

- **Thomas Aquinas and the classical foundation**: In the *Summa Theologiae* (13th century), Aquinas distinguished four kinds of law: eternal (God's governance of the cosmos), divine (revelation), natural (the participation of human reason in the eternal law, apprehended by every person through natural reason), and human (the enactments of rulers, derived from natural law). Aquinas held that a human law contrary to natural law is not law in the full sense but a corruption of law, and that a law contrary to divine good need not be obeyed.
- **Lon Fuller and procedural naturalism**: In the twentieth century, Lon Fuller developed a secular approach to natural law that does not require a substantive moral criterion for the content of law. He held instead that law has a procedural "inner morality": any legal system that aims by its nature to subject human conduct to the governance of rules must satisfy certain formal conditions (clarity, stability, non-contradiction, and the possibility of compliance) in order to count as law in the true sense at all, regardless of the justice of its content.
- **John Finnis and new natural law**: In his book *Natural Law and Natural Rights* (1980), Finnis restated the classical Thomist tradition in contemporary language. He focused on "basic goods" necessary for human flourishing (such as life, knowledge and friendship), and argued that this restatement is consistent with the essential position of Aquinas rather than contradicting it.

## The Debate with Legal Positivism

The disagreement between natural law and legal positivism (represented by Hart and Kelsen) is one of the deepest divisions in the philosophy of law: is an "unjust law" truly law at all? Many natural law theorists answer in the negative in at least some cases (the classical formula being that an unjust law is not truly law), though contemporary writers such as Finnis qualify this considerably. Positivists insist on the analytical separation between "what is in fact law" and "what the law ought morally to be", even when they condemn it strongly on moral grounds.

## Limitations

- The distinction between "substantive naturalism" (Aquinas, Finnis) and "procedural naturalism" (Fuller) is profound, and some scholars question whether Fuller's classification within the classical natural law tradition is appropriate at all.
- This node partly overlaps with the scope of the "modern natural law and social contract" school, which covers specifically the seventeenth-century formulations (Grotius, Hobbes, Locke).
