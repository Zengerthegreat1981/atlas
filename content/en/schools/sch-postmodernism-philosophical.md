# Philosophical Postmodernism

Philosophical postmodernism is a critical stance that does not describe a historical era but doubts the foundations on which European modernity was built: belief in universal reason, in progress, in directed history, and in the possibility of reaching objective truth. It differs from "cultural postmodernism" in art, architecture and literature in being a critique of knowledge and of the narratives that legitimate science and politics. It intersects with post-structuralism, but this page treats "the postmodern condition" as a diagnosis of knowledge, society and culture, whereas the tools of deconstruction and discourse are presented on the post-structuralism page.

## Emergence of the Term

Before it entered philosophy, the term already circulated in the arts and history; it was taken up, for example, by the Egyptian-born critic Ihab Hassan in *The Dismemberment of Orpheus* (1971) and by Charles Jencks in *The Language of Post-Modern Architecture* (1977). Jean-François Lyotard then carried the term into philosophy in *The Postmodern Condition* (1979), a report on knowledge in advanced societies commissioned from him by the Quebec university council. It was translated into Arabic in the 1990s.

## Arab Reception

The term entered Arab culture in the 1980s and 1990s through literary criticism and philosophy, and the debate was between those who saw in it a liberation from the great Western narratives and those who saw in it a justification of nihilism. Abdelwahab El-Messiri and Fathi Triki brought two viewpoints together in *Modernity and Postmodernity* (2003): El-Messiri offered a critique linking postmodernism to materialism and globalization, and Triki offered an interactive approach. Arab scholars hold that the question of modernity itself was never completed in the Arab world, so that talk of what comes "after" it is premature, and that postmodern critique may help in understanding the plurality of identities.

## Major Figures

- **Lyotard** (1924–1998): defined postmodernism as "incredulity toward metanarratives", that is, narratives of emancipation, progress and absolute spirit, and held that knowledge is plural in "language games" (after Wittgenstein) not governed by a single criterion. In *The Differend* (1983) he spoke of how some wrongs have no language.
- **Fredric Jameson** (1934–2024): in *Postmodernism, or, The Cultural Logic of Late Capitalism* (1991) he held that postmodernism is the cultural expression of a new economic phase, marked by the flattening of depth and by pastiche.
- **Jean Baudrillard** (1929–2007): in *Simulacra and Simulation* (1981) he said that images and signs have detached from their referent until we arrive at the "hyperreal", in which there is no origin.
- **Richard Rorty** (1931–2007): in *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) he criticized the idea that the mind is a mirror representing reality. Although sometimes ambivalent about the postmodern label, he acknowledged kinship with Lyotard and Derrida, arguing in *Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989) that language and liberal values are contingent historical creations rather than products of an objective rational foundation.
- **Zygmunt Bauman** (1925–2017): in *Liquid Modernity* (2000) he held that identities and institutions have lost their solidity.
- **David Harvey**: in *The Condition of Postmodernity* (1989) he linked the phenomenon to the transformations of flexible capital and the compression of time and space.

## Founding Concepts

- **Incredulity toward metanarratives**: no narrative can explain the whole or legitimate itself, and knowledge in the information society is measured by performance.
- **Language games and the differend**: types of discourse cannot be translated into one another, so a differend needs recognition rather than resolution.
- **The hyperreal**: the blending of original and copy in the media and consumption.
- **The plurality of identities**: a critique of the reduction that erases sexual, ethnic and cultural difference, a point where Judith Butler's theory in *Gender Trouble* (1990) intersects.

## Reactions

- **Habermas**: in "Modernity: An Incomplete Project" (1980) and then *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985) he accused postmodernism of a new conservatism and of undermining communicative reason, and called for completing the project of the Enlightenment through self-critique.
- **Terry Eagleton**: in *The Illusions of Postmodernism* (1996) he held that it is a culture suited to consumer capitalism.
- **Charles Taylor**: in *Sources of the Self* (1989) he held that its critique rests on an implicit ethics that it does not acknowledge.

## Limits and Criticisms

- **Performative contradiction**: if every narrative is suspect, how is the suspicion itself presented?
- **Political effect**: critics hold that dropping the grand narratives deprives social critique of its basis, an accusation that Lyotard's followers reject, seeing in the "differend" an entry to justice.
- **Neglect of material reality**: when things are described as texts, it is hard to deal with epidemics, famines and wars, and "new realism" trends have tried to return to matter.
- **Generalization**: postmodernism is better described as a spectrum of positions than a school in the strict sense; many of those classed as postmodernists reject the label, and it is hard to separate it cleanly from post-structuralism.
