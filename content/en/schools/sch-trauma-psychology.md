# Trauma Psychology

The field that studies the **effects of overwhelming events** on the psyche, the body, and memory, and develops treatments for them. It includes trauma-focused cognitive-behavioral therapies as well as approaches such as EMDR, somatic experiencing, and sensorimotor psychotherapy.

## A History of Repeated Forgetting

Judith Herman argued that the study of psychological trauma has repeatedly been taken up and then forgotten, in part for social and political rather than purely scientific reasons:

- **Hysteria** in Charcot, Janet, and the early Freud (1880s-1890s), followed by psychoanalysis turning away from the real event toward fantasy.
- **Shell shock and war neurosis** in the First and Second World Wars (for example the work at Craiglockhart War Hospital in 1917), followed by periods of neglect.
- **Institutional recognition in 1980**: **PTSD** was included in DSM-III, following pressure from the **Vietnam veteran** movement and from research and advocacy on sexual and domestic violence associated with the **feminist movement**. In ***Trauma and Recovery*** (1992), Herman argues that the study of trauma depends on a supportive political movement or it is again neglected.

## Theoretical Structure

- **Trauma is not the event** but **what remains of it** in the regulation of the nervous system and in memory: memories that are not integrated into narrative and so return intrusively, present as if happening now.
- **Herman's three stages of recovery**: a widely used framework: **safety and stabilization** first, then **remembrance and mourning**, then **reconnection** with ordinary life. Many clinicians caution against beginning intensive trauma processing before stabilization, although the strict sequencing is debated.
- **The window of tolerance**: the range of arousal within which a person remains able to process; above it is hyperarousal and below it is shutdown.
- **Collective and historical trauma**: what befalls a group across generations cannot be reduced to the symptoms of its individuals. This is the line along which the field meets liberation psychology and Indigenous psychology.

## Treatments and Their Evidence

- **Strongest evidence**: trauma-focused cognitive-behavioral therapies, including **Prolonged Exposure** (Foa) and **Cognitive Processing Therapy** (Resick). They are among the first-line treatments recommended in major clinical guidelines.
- **EMDR**: effective in controlled trials; **its proposed mechanism** (eye movements as the active ingredient) is what is disputed. Dismantling studies suggest that the eye movements may add little beyond the exposure and processing components.
- **Body-based therapies** (Levine, Ogden): accepted in practice and widespread, **but their empirical evidence is weaker** than that of the first group, with fewer and smaller controlled trials.

## Complex PTSD and Developmental Trauma

A major conceptual development within trauma psychology is the differentiation between single-incident trauma and prolonged, interpersonal, or developmental trauma. First formulated systematically by Judith Herman (1992), **Complex PTSD (CPTSD)** captures the psychological sequelae of prolonged, repeated exposure to traumatic events from which escape is difficult or impossible—such as chronic childhood physical or sexual abuse, domestic captivity, torture, or human trafficking.

In developmental contexts, chronic early relational trauma (often termed "developmental trauma") disrupts primary attachment bonds and foundational neurobiological self-regulation. Bessel van der Kolk and colleagues documented how early traumatic caregiver environments compromise somatosensory integration, affective stability, and relational trust, leading to proposals for a distinct diagnosis of Developmental Trauma Disorder (DTD) in children.

This work culminated in formal nosological recognition in the World Health Organization's ICD-11 (code 6B41). ICD-11 distinguishes CPTSD from classic PTSD: while both share the core PTSD triad—re-experiencing in the present, avoidance of traumatic reminders, and persistent perception of heightened threat—CPTSD requires additional pervasive disturbances in self-organization (DSO):
1. **Severe affect dysregulation:** persistent emotional reactivity, explosive anger, chronic emotional numbness, or dissociative states.
2. **Negative self-concept:** persistent beliefs about oneself as diminished, defeated, or worthless, accompanied by profound shame, guilt, and failure.
3. **Interpersonal difficulties:** persistent difficulties in sustaining close relationships and feeling emotional connection.

In contrast, the American DSM-5-TR opted not to create a separate CPTSD diagnostic entity, instead including a dissociative subtype of PTSD; affect and interpersonal disturbances are not captured by a separate diagnosis and may be described through comorbid conditions. Clinical management of CPTSD generally emphasizes a phased, relationally anchored approach prioritizing stabilization, grounded affect regulation, and relational trust prior to trauma-narrative processing.

## Limits

- **The claim "the body keeps the score"**: in its weak formulation, that trauma alters the regulation of the autonomic nervous system and the response to threat, it is **supported and accepted**. In its strong formulation, that the memory is stored in muscles or fascia and retrieved by touch, it is **not established by physiological evidence**, and conflating the two formulations is common in popular literature.
- **Nosological divergence**: the ICD-11 inclusion of Complex PTSD alongside DSM-5-TR's single PTSD construct with a dissociative subtype reflects ongoing diagnostic debates regarding boundary lines with personality pathology.
- **Dilution of the concept**: expanding the word "trauma" to cover every distress weakens its discriminating power, a published critique that is debated in the field.
- **Recovered memory**: the history of the 1990s in this area, and the lawsuits that followed from it, call for caution in any intervention that aims to "retrieve" memories that are not accessible.
