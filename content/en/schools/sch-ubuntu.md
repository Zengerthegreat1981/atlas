# Contemporary Ubuntu Philosophy

Contemporary ubuntu is a philosophical and political reconstruction of the traditional value of "ubuntu" in South Africa after the end of the apartheid regime. The word moved from an oral social custom to a principle cited in the constitution, the judiciary, and national discourse, and to a subject of debate in moral and political philosophy and in management ethics. This page differs from traditional ubuntu in that it deals with contemporary theorizing and institutional use, not the social roots.

## Political Context

The word widened in public discourse with South Africa's democratic transition. The postamble appended to the interim constitution (1993) states that there is a need for understanding but not for vengeance, for reparation but not for retaliation, and for "ubuntu" but not for victimization. In the Constitutional Court's decision in *S v Makwanyane* (1995), which abolished the death penalty, the justices, among them Yvonne Mokgoro and Pius Langa, invoked ubuntu as a value that respects human dignity and limits the cruelty of punishment. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–2002), chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu (1931–2021), was its most prominent application: in *No Future Without Forgiveness* (1999) Tutu linked amnesty and confession to ubuntu, and said that a person is diminished when others are diminished.

## Philosophical Formulations

- Mogobe Ramose: in *African Philosophy through Ubuntu* (1999) he presents it as a conception of being based on "becoming," and from it infers political and moral principles such as the rejection of colonialism and the demand for justice.
- Thaddeus Metz: in "Toward an African Moral Theory" (2007) he proposes that an action is right to the extent that it respects the relation of identity and solidarity among people, that is, it affirms sharing in life and concern for the common good. He compares his theory with utilitarianism and Kantianism to show how it explains rights and duties without relying on the isolated individual.
- Lovemore Mbigi and Jenny Maree: in the book *Ubuntu: The Spirit of African Transformation Management* (1995) they turned the value into a philosophy of institutional management based on the team, consultation, and respect. Their ideas have passed into corporate training programs.
- Other thinkers have focused on the interdependence between the human being and the environment, and on reconciliation and the solidarity economy.

The Linux-based "Ubuntu" operating system (first released in 2004, sponsored by Mark Shuttleworth) took its name from the word and glosses it as "humanity to others", so the word also passed into software culture.

## Influence

Ubuntu has become a cornerstone of the national narrative of the new South Africa, and it has appeared in education, criminal law, restorative justice, and public health practices, and it was often invoked by Nelson Mandela, who explained its meaning in terms of hospitality, such as the traveller who is offered food and shelter without having to ask. It is regarded as an entry point for recovering African philosophical sources in comparative ethics, and it is studied alongside the ethics of care and theories of restorative justice.

## Limits and Criticism

Critics hold that contemporary ubuntu has become a flexible general slogan used to embellish the policies of the state and corporations. Matolino and Kwindingwi wrote an article titled "The End of Ubuntu" (2013) in which they argued that the concept is incapable of confronting poverty and corruption, and that it is closer to a distant moral ideal than to the reality of society. Others have pointed out that the idea may be used to silence protest in the name of harmony, or to oblige victims to reconcile before justice. Christian Gade, in a study of how South Africans of African descent answer the question of what ubuntu is (2012), showed that the concept is understood in divergent ways. The problem of generalizing from the experience of specific societies to an all-embracing "African philosophy" remains.

## Restorative Justice and Law

Many researchers have linked ubuntu to a model of justice that focuses on repairing the relationship after a crime and not on vengeance alone. In the context of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, amnesty was offered in exchange for full disclosure of the facts, so that recognizing the victims and giving them a voice became part of the process. Some jurists held that this model gave the country stability in a fragile phase, while victims held that it deprived them of the prosecution of perpetrators and of fair compensation. Here appears the tension between forgiveness as a moral virtue and justice as a civil right, a tension that theory has not resolved.

## Ubuntu and Comparative Philosophical Perspectives

When ubuntu is compared with Western theories, a difference in starting point stands out. Liberalism begins from the autonomous individual and then builds society by contract, whereas ubuntu begins from the relation and makes the individual its fruit. This is close to ethical collectivism and to the ethics of care in feminism, but it does not end in abolishing rights for most contemporary thinkers; Metz argues that it explains basic rights as conditions of a relation of mutual respect. Researchers in the West and the East have discussed the possibility of using the approach in health ethics, where medical decisions are taken in the family and communal context and not by individual consent alone.
