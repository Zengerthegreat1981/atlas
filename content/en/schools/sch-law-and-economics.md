# Law and Economics

An intellectual movement that originated mainly at the University of Chicago from the 1960s onward. It applies the tools of microeconomic analysis (price theory, the analysis of incentives and efficiency) to legal rules, not merely to describe what courts do (as **American legal realism** did), but to evaluate legal rules by the criterion of **economic efficiency** and to propose their reform.

## The Founding Thesis: Coase's Theory

The movement effectively began with **Ronald Coase**'s article "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960), which argued that, in the absence of transaction costs, it does not matter economically **to whom the law initially grants a right**: the parties will reach, through negotiation, the most efficient allocation of resources regardless of the initial distribution of rights. See **the Coase theorem** for a detailed treatment of this thesis and its main implications. When transaction costs are in fact high (which is the more common situation), **how the law distributes rights in the first place becomes an economically decisive question**.

## Posner: Efficiency as a Comprehensive Standard for Adjudication

**Richard Posner** expanded this framework in his landmark *Economic Analysis of Law* (1973) to cover almost all branches of law, including contracts, torts, property, and even criminal and family law. He proposed that **many historical rules of common law evolved, without conscious intent on the part of judges, toward economic efficiency**, because inefficient rules are litigated and amended more than efficient ones over the long run.

## Juristic Precedents: Consideration of Consequences and Public Interest

Although classical Islamic jurisprudence did not use the vocabulary of modern economics, al-Shatibi's theory of the **maqasid** in *al-Muwafaqat*, and the rule of considering **consequences** (*ma'alat*, meaning attention to the actual results of applying a ruling rather than its abstract form alone), share with the economic analysis of law one methodological demand: that a textual ruling not be considered in the abstract without regard to its real effect on people and their interests. Jurists also discussed, in the chapter on transactions, questions of allocating harm (such as the maxim "liability goes with benefit" (*al-ghurm bil-ghunm*) and the guarantee of destroyed property), which correspond, from a distance, to the logic of distributing accident costs that Calabresi later developed. The essential difference, however, is that Islamic jurisprudence subjects the consideration of public interest and consequences to a fixed sharia ceiling (no recognized public interest may contradict a definitive text), whereas the Chicago school makes **efficiency** itself an independent standard that may at times take precedence over considerations of distributive justice.

## Calabresi and the Analysis of Accident Costs

**Guido Calabresi**, judge and professor at Yale University, provided the third pillar of the law and economics movement alongside Coase and Posner. In his book *The Costs of Accidents* (1970) he developed a framework for analyzing tort law that asks: **who is the "cheapest cost avoider"?** That is, which party to a dispute is best able to prevent the harm at least cost, so that it is held liable regardless of the traditional moral concept of "fault". This framework shifted the analysis of civil liability from a moral question of blame to an engineering question about the optimal distribution of incentives to minimize accidents and insurance costs together.

## Internal Debate and Later Critique: Behavioral Law and Economics

Since the 1990s, a strong internal critique has emerged from **behavioral law and economics**, led by Cass Sunstein, Christine Jolls and Richard Thaler. It challenges Posner's central assumption that individuals are rational actors who maximize their utility through precise calculation. Experimental findings in cognitive psychology showed that individual decisions systematically depart from full rationality (present bias, loss aversion and overconfidence), and that legal rule design should account for these actual deviations rather than an idealized model of *Homo economicus*. The school has also faced a broader critique from schools of distributive justice, which argue that making "efficiency" the central criterion marginalizes questions of fairness and the distribution of wealth among the parties, since a rule may be economically efficient while being distributively unjust by other measures.

## Impact and Legacy

The influence of the law and economics movement has gone beyond academic circles to become an integral part of American judicial training, through programs such as the economics seminars for judges organized by Henry Manne. It has shaped the drafting of laws on antitrust, intellectual property, and the regulation of financial markets. The question "what is the efficient economic effect of this rule?" has become a routine part of legal and legislative debate, even outside the United States.

## The Movement's Relationship to American Legal Realism

The law and economics movement is intellectually descended from the **American legal realism** that preceded it by a generation. Both schools share a rejection of the idea that a legal rule is applied mechanically from an abstract text without regard to its actual social effects. The decisive difference, however, is that the realists largely confined themselves to describing how judges actually decide (a sociological description), whereas the Chicago school went a step further towards an explicit **normative standard**, economic efficiency, used to evaluate existing rules and propose their reform, not merely to describe them. This shift from description to normativity is what made the movement more readily applicable to direct legislative use than its realist predecessor.
