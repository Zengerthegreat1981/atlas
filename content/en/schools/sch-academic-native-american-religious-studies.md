# Academic Study of Native American Religions

This field arose as an independent branch within the comparative history of religions when the Swede Åke Hultkrantz (1920–2011), beginning with his fieldwork among the Shoshone on the Wind River Reservation in Wyoming in the late 1940s, tried to build a comparative framework for the hundreds of diverse tribal religious traditions of North America, rather than being content with a secluded tribal ethnographic description. In his book *The Religions of the American Indians* (first published in Swedish in 1967; English translation 1979), followed by *Belief and Worship in Native North America* (1981), he set out a composite picture of the whole domain. He adopted a method combining European continental phenomenology, close to Eliade's project in classifying structures of the sacred across cultures, with an ecological sensibility that links the form of belief to the mode of subsistence, contrasting the world of hunters and gatherers with that of the agriculturalists of the Southwest and the eastern woodlands.

## Hultkrantz's Central Concepts

Among his best-known contributions is the expansion of the concept of the "guardian spirit", which Ruth Benedict had analysed in her 1923 study of North America (*The Concept of the Guardian Spirit in North America*): an experience of a vision or dream in which a boy or spiritual seeker receives individual protection from a supernatural being, a practice that occupies a central place in religion among the Plains and Plateau peoples. He also distinguished between the "Supreme Being" among some groups and a system of spirits and powers, and studied the Sun Dance rituals, bear worship in the north, and religious revival movements such as the Ghost Dance of 1890, which James Mooney had earlier documented, and the Native American Church, which became associated with the use of the peyote plant.

## The Founding Tension: Who Holds the Right to Study?

Alongside this founding effort from outside, the Indigenous thinker and lawyer Vine Deloria Jr. (1933–2005), of the Standing Rock Sioux tribe, in *God Is Red* (1973) offered a radical critique of the methodology of non-Indigenous scholars. He argued that the academic comparative frameworks impose Christian-centred colonial perspectives on conceptions of place, time and community that cannot be reduced to Western categories of "religion". In his reading, Christianity is a religion of time and history with a salvific arc, whereas Indigenous religions are religions of place, built on sacred geographies and relationships with the land that cannot be transferred. From this tension emerged a continuing debate about the legitimacy of studying "from the outside" and about the criterion of legitimacy in speaking about a tradition, a debate that parallels, without being exactly identical to, the debate about Orientalism in the study of Islam.

## The Legal and Political Dimension

The debate did not remain academic. The American Indian Religious Freedom Act was passed in 1978, followed by the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act (NAGPRA) in 1990. Followers of Indigenous religions lost major cases before the US Supreme Court in *Lyng* (1988) and *Smith* (1990), concerning the protection of sacred sites and the use of peyote. The field's evidence shows that the definition of "religion" (in the sense of belief and worship separated from one another) was used against practices tied to land and community, which made Deloria's critique a practical matter.

## Its Relationship to the Comparative Field of Shamanism

Hultkrantz's classificatory vocabulary (the healing shaman as a mediator with spirits) intersects with the wider debate over the comparative category of "shamanism". The anthropologist Alice Kehoe directed a similar critique in spirit, in her book *Shamans and Religion* (2000), and earlier on the Ghost Dance, against the generalisation of European terms (such as "shaman", originally Siberian) onto an actual diversity of Native American practices that does not necessarily match the Tungusic model.

## The Newer Generation: From Comparative Classification to Constructivist Critique and Historical Narrative

Later scholars expanded the field in two different directions. Sam Gill pushed Deloria's critical logic a step further, but in an inverted form: instead of defending the authenticity of a tradition against an external classification, he himself, as an outside scholar, questioned the authenticity of the common concept of "Mother Earth", arguing that it is a modern construction rather than an ancient unified tribal belief, in his book *Mother Earth: An American Story* (1987). This provoked a sharp debate over who holds the authority to judge the authenticity of a religious tradition. Joel Martin took a third course: a narrative history of specific tribal traditions in their confrontation with colonialism, in *The Land Looks After Us* (1999), avoiding both the general comparative classification and comprehensive constructivist deconstruction. Christopher Vecsey wrote on the Ojibwa and the historical transformations of their religion (*Traditional Ojibwa Religion and Its Historical Changes*, 1983).

## Limits of the Field

The comparative study takes on board that a diverse range of peoples (from the Inuit and Athabaskan to the Hopi and Iroquois) is hard to gather under a single heading, that the early material was collected by mission staff and administrators during the period of subjugation and the erasure of cultures, and that the foreign observer recorded what was allowed to be known. Hultkrantz is criticised for a fixed "typology" scheme that hides historical change and the impact of contact with Europeans. On the other side, it has been said that Deloria's position carries the risk of essentialism: portraying "Indian religion" as a homogeneous bloc in contrast to the West. The contemporary field is moving toward a collaborative method with communities and respect for the sovereignty of knowledge.
