# Contemporary Academic Hindu Studies: Gavin Flood and Julius Lipner

Having been established in the nineteenth century by Burnouf, Müller and Rhys Davids, the comparative philology of Indian and Buddhist texts was followed in the late twentieth century by a generation of British scholars. They produced comprehensive methodological syntheses on "Hinduism" as an object of academic study. They went beyond the mere editing of ancient texts to a historical-methodological description of the diversity of living Hindu practice across eras and social classes, with a critical awareness of the problematic nature of the term itself.

## Gavin Flood: A Methodological Synthesis of Hindu Diversity

In *An Introduction to Hinduism* (1996), Gavin Flood offers an influential academic synthesis. It rejects portraying Hinduism as a single, homogeneous religion with one central doctrine. Instead, it presents Hinduism as a bundle of intertwined historical traditions (Vedic, Upanishadic, Bhakti, Tantric) that share vocabulary and concepts (karma, dharma, moksha) without necessarily agreeing on their interpretation. He adopted a historical-descriptive method, careful to trace the chronological development of practices (ritual, yoga, asceticism) before moving to abstract doctrine. He avoided imposing a theological framework formulated in Christian terms (such as "doctrine" versus "practice") on material that does not necessarily assume it.

## Julius Lipner: Hinduism Between Academic Description and Personal Belonging

Julius Lipner, in *Hindus: Their Religious Beliefs and Practices* (1994), offers a parallel reference work, organized around the image of the banyan tree, whose many trunks remain a single tree. For Lipner, Hinduism is a plurality within a describable unity. In his article "The Rise of 'Hinduism'; or, How to Invent a World Religion with Only Moderate Success" (2006), he discusses the thesis of the "invention of Hinduism," namely that the British administration and orientalists created in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries a single world religion out of scattered local traditions. He concludes that the "invention" was real but only moderately successful. The modern term and its classifications are colonial in origin, but the sense of a shared belonging among traditions that recognize the authority of the Veda, and share concepts and practices older than colonialism, cannot be reduced to an external fabrication.

## The Impact: A Productive Tension Between Synthesis and Deconstruction

Flood and Lipner together represent the two ends of a fruitful methodological tension in the field. The first offers a comprehensive descriptive synthesis that makes Hinduism easy to teach academically as a unit that can be presented. The second examines how the name of this unit was historically formed, and to what extent it withstands the colonial invention thesis. This tension intersects directly with the broader debate, raised from a more general angle by Jonathan Z. Smith, on whether "religion" in general can be defined as an analytic category. In the same generation, Sheldon Pollock represents a parallel philological extension that moves the question from the doctrine and practice of Hinduism to the history of its classical language and its cultural authority. Wendy Doniger represents a third extension, reading Hindu mythology with psychological-analytic tools, which provoked a sharp national controversy over who holds the authority to interpret a living tradition.

## The Name "Hindu" and Its Persian and Arabic Roots

Lipner's discussion starts from the word itself. The word "Hindu" is not a Sanskrit self-designation. It derives from the Sanskrit name of the Indus River, *Sindhu*, which Old Persian rendered as "Hindu," and Persian and Arabic then used it for the land and people beyond the Indus. Arabs came to say "al-Hind" for the country and "al-Hunud" for the people. In this context, Abu al-Rayhan al-Biruni's *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind* (c. 1030 CE) stands as the broadest external description of Indian beliefs and philosophy before the modern orientalist period. He presented texts from the *Yogasutra* and the *Bhagavad Gita* and distinguished between the doctrine of scholars and the practices of the common people. This means the name given to the tradition was born in contact with Muslim conquerors and Persian and Arab traders before the British adopted it. Accordingly, David Lorenzen, in "Who Invented Hinduism?" (*Comparative Studies in Society and History*, 1999), argued that a unifying Hindu identity had formed before British colonial rule, through confrontation with Islamic rule and address to the Muslim Other. This limits the thesis of a purely British invention. Wilfred Cantwell Smith, in *The Meaning and End of Religion* (1962), made Hinduism an example of a word constructed by Western scholarship. Heinrich von Stietencron criticized the term for the same reason in the chapter "Hinduism: On the Proper Use of a Deceptive Term" (1989).

## Flood, the Body, and Tantra

Alongside *An Introduction to Hinduism*, Flood wrote *The Tantric Body: The Secret Tradition of Hindu Religion* (I.B. Tauris, 2006), *The Blackwell Companion to Hinduism* (Blackwell, 2003), and *The Truth Within: A History of Inwardness* (2013). In this work he connects textual traditions with embodied practice, combining a phenomenological approach with historical critique, and stresses the transmission of tradition through ritual and meditation.

## The Debate on Interpretation and Ownership

In this climate, Wendy Doniger published *The Hindus: An Alternative History* (Penguin, 2009). In 2014, the Indian branch of the publisher withdrew it from Indian markets as part of a settlement of a lawsuit filed by a Hindu nationalist group that accused the book of offending the religion. This raised a debate about the limits of academic interpretation and who has the right to speak in the name of a tradition. Sheldon Pollock, in *The Language of the Gods in the World of Men* (University of California Press, 2006), introduced the idea of the Sanskrit cosmopolis: Sanskrit became a language of political and literary expression, not confined to religion, across South and Southeast Asia from roughly the fourth to the thirteenth centuries CE.

## Limits of the Approaches

Flood's approach is criticized on the ground that, despite its reservations, it keeps Hinduism as a unity that can be taught. Critics who support the invention thesis argue that Lipner underestimates the effect of colonial classification, including census categories, in fixing a common identity. Others argue that both approaches still read Indian plurality in the vocabulary of the Western "world religion."
