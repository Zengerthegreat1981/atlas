# New Institutional Economic History (Douglass North)

## Definition

A theoretical framework developed by the American economist and economic historian Douglass North (1920–2015) to explain why some economies grow historically while others stagnate, by focusing on **institutions** — not natural resources or technology alone — as the deepest explanatory variable of economic performance across the centuries. This trend represents a fusion between modern microeconomic theory (transaction costs, property rights) and the quantitative economic history method that had become popular since the 1960s, giving "quantitative history" (Cliometrics) a theoretical explanatory depth beyond merely measuring variables.

## Historical Context

This trend developed from North's work at the University of Washington in Seattle, where he taught for decades, and was carried further at Washington University in St. Louis from the 1980s. He began his career as a researcher in classical economic history concerning American shipping and agriculture, before gradually shifting in the 1970s towards broader questions about the causes of "wealth and poverty among nations" throughout history. This shift came in the context of the crisis of purely technical and geographical explanations of the Industrial Revolution, and it pushed institutional economics (associated with Thorstein Veblen and John R. Commons in the early twentieth century) towards a new form more amenable to historical testing.

## The Central Concept: Institutions as the Rules of the Game

In *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990), North defines "institutions" broadly: the formal rules (laws, constitutions, property rights) and informal rules (customs, shared values) that structure economic and political interactions between individuals. He argues that the differences in long-term economic performance between societies (why Western Europe grew economically from the seventeenth century while other regions stagnated) are rooted mainly in the quality of these institutions — especially the extent to which they effectively protect property rights and reduce **transaction costs**.

## Transaction Costs: The Operational Concept

North's framework rests on the concept of "transaction costs," developed by the economist Ronald Coase: the cost of negotiating contracts, monitoring their performance and enforcing them through the courts. These are unproductive costs that drain the resources of any historical economy. North argues that effective institutions (reliable courts, clearly registered property rights, a stable currency) are essentially mechanisms for reducing these costs. This explains why certain European economies (the Netherlands and England) were able to move from limited personal exchange (between acquaintances who trusted one another) to the wide-scale impersonal exchange required by modern capitalist growth, while other economies remained confined to narrow exchange patterns relying on personal trust and kinship.

## The Emergence of Institutions and the Difficulty of Explaining Their Change

In *The Rise of the Western World* (with Robert Paul Thomas, 1973) and later works, North explains how growth-supporting institutions arose in Western Europe (such as secure property rights after the English Glorious Revolution of 1688) through specific historical factors (the balance of power between the crown, parliament and merchants). He stresses that institutional change is a slow, cumulative process influenced by **path dependence**: early historical decisions constrain the institutional options available later, even when they have become inefficient.

## Methodological Importance

North shared the 1993 Nobel Prize in Economics with Robert Fogel "for having renewed research in economic history by applying economic theory and quantitative methods in order to explain economic and institutional change." North's theoretical framework and Fogel's statistical tools represent two complementary aspects of the same shift of "quantitative history" towards greater theoretical and methodological rigour. The methodological importance of this framework lies in the fact that it provides a bridge between traditional descriptive history (which narrates "what happened") and abstract economic theory (which assumes fixed institutions), by making the "rules of the game" themselves the object of historical explanation that changes over time, rather than an external given outside the analysis.

## Criticism and Later Development

North's framework has faced criticism from later economic historians who regarded it at times as circular (the existence of "good institutions" is inferred from the very growth that it is supposed to explain), or as over-generalising the experience of Western Europe to the rest of the world. Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson later developed a closely related framework (in *Why Nations Fail*, 2012) distinguishing between "extractive institutions" and "inclusive institutions," in the same broad tradition of stressing the centrality of institutions. Meanwhile North's concepts of "path dependence" and transaction costs have remained standard tools in comparative economic history to this day.
