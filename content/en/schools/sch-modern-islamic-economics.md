# Modern Islamic Economics

**Modern Islamic economics** is an intellectual movement that emerged in the mid-twentieth century, peaking in the 1960s and 1970s. It sought to formulate a **complete Islamic economic system** presented as an explicit third alternative to both liberal capitalism and Marxist socialism, rather than as a partial jurisprudential adjustment to either. The school draws on the classical literature on *kharaj* and *hisba* (Islamic fiscal thought, kharaj and hisba), but shifts it from the context of partial applied legal opinion to a project of an "economic theory" or "economic doctrine" with an integrated structure.

## Foundations in Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr

Earlier writers such as Abul A'la Maududi in South Asia had called for an Islamic economic order, and **Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr** (1935–1980) gave the current its most systematic early statement in *Iqtisaduna* (*Our Economics*, 1961). The book opens with a central methodological distinction: Islamic economics is not a "science" in the positivist sense (a theory that explains economic phenomena as they are), but a **"doctrine"** that determines how economic relations should be organized according to the principles of Islamic justice. It thus differs from positive economics, whose analytical tools may be used without adopting its value philosophy. In the book, Sadr critiques the Marxist theory of value and the capitalist theory of income distribution in detail, and proposes an alternative based on multiple forms of ownership (private, public and state) and on the prohibition of riba and the obligation of zakat as the two central instruments of redistribution.

## Institutional and analytical expansion

From the generation after Sadr, **Umer Chapra** developed Islamic economics from a general doctrinal project into an analysis using modern macroeconomic and development tools. He focused on the objectives of the Sharia (particularly the preservation of wealth and progeny) as a normative framework for evaluating economic policies, and discussed the role of the prohibition of riba in the stability of the financial system compared with recurrent capitalist financial crises. The Islamic Development Bank (founded in 1975) and, from 1981, its Islamic Research and Training Institute helped move the discussion out of purely academic debate, alongside the commercial Islamic banks that spread from the mid-1970s.

## Shared features

Despite the diversity of its jurisprudential sources (Shiʿi in Sadr's case, Sunni in Chapra's and others'), the current agrees on a set of shared principles: the prohibition of riba, replaced by profit-and-loss sharing (mudaraba and musharaka); the obligation of zakat as an instrument of redistribution rather than a voluntary charity; rejection of monopoly and of excessive uncertainty (gharar) in contracts; and adherence to **the objectives of the Sharia** (*maqasid al-shariʿa*) as a normative framework higher than mere formal compliance with subsidiary texts.

## Criticism and debate

The current faces criticism from both within and outside. From within, a question is raised about the extent to which the "murabaha" of Islamic banking actually differs, in its real economic effect, from conventional interest-based financing. From outside, economists (including Timur Kuran in *Islam and Mammon*) question whether a distinct Islamic analytical model truly exists beyond the tools of econometrics, arguing that much of "Islamic economics" is a religious relabelling of conventional financial instruments rather than an alternative economic theory.

## Islamic banking as an applied arm

The theoretical propositions of this current became actual financial institutions from the mid-1970s, with the founding of Dubai Islamic Bank (1975) and the Islamic Development Bank (1975), followed by the spread of Islamic banks throughout the Islamic world and beyond. In theory, these banks replace the fixed interest rate with contracts of profit-and-loss sharing (mudaraba, musharaka) or deferred-sale contracts (murabaha, ijara), in an attempt to translate the theoretical prohibition of riba into practical financing tools that can compete with the conventional banking system in efficiency and liquidity.

## Zakat as a redistributive instrument rather than voluntary charity

This current draws a clear distinction between zakat, as a mandatory financial obligation with a legally defined threshold (nisab) and amounts, distributed among eight specified categories of recipients, and voluntary charity (sadaqa), which remains within the sphere of individual ethics. This distinction makes zakat, in this school's literature, a general fiscal policy instrument that can be theorized in the manner of modern redistributive taxes, although its collection mechanisms vary widely by country (largely voluntary in most contemporary Islamic states, but mandated centrally in some, such as Saudi Arabia and Pakistan at certain periods).

## Connection to the classical jurisprudential heritage

Leading figures of this current are careful to present themselves as an extension of the classical literature on kharaj and hisba rather than a rupture with it. When Sadr discusses the question of ownership, or Chapra discusses the objectives of the Sharia, both explicitly invoke the texts of the classical jurists on kharaj, zakat and riba as raw material to be reorganized into a contemporary theoretical structure that mirrors the structure of modern economics (theory of production, theory of distribution, theory of consumption). This accounts for the evolutionary relationship linked to this entry with the classical kharaj and hisba literature, rather than a methodological break with it.
