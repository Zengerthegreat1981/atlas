# The History-of-Religions School (Religionsgeschichtliche Schule)

A circle of theologians and biblical scholars at the University of Göttingen in Germany, formed in the last decade of the nineteenth century around a shared methodological project: the historical and comparative study of early Judaism and Christianity as religious phenomena that arose and developed within a broader cultural context (the ancient Near East, the Hellenistic world and Eastern mystery religions), rather than in isolation from it, and without taking their absolute divine uniqueness as a methodological starting point.

## Origins: The Göttingen Circle

The circle began around Albert Eichhorn, who showed in his study of the Last Supper (1898) that the New Testament carries echoes of its wider religious environment. It included Hermann Gunkel, Wilhelm Bousset, Johannes Weiss, William Wrede and Alfred Rahlfs, and Ernst Troeltsch of Heidelberg joined it. Among its opponents it was first known as the "little Göttingen faculty" (die kleine Göttinger Fakultät) before being called the "History-of-Religions School". Its era was one of archaeological discoveries in Babylon, Egypt and Persia, which made it possible to compare biblical texts with their parallels, including the Babylonian tablets of the *Enuma Elish*.

## The Shared Project

Gunkel published *Schöpfung und Chaos in Urzeit und Endzeit* (1895), comparing the Genesis creation narrative with the Babylonian myth of Tiamat, and then his commentary on Genesis (1901). Bousset investigated the Hellenistic and Persian backgrounds of late Jewish-Christian ideas such as angels, the devil and the afterlife in *Die Religion des Judentums im neutestamentlichen Zeitalter* (1903), and then applied a similar method to the origins of early Christology in *Kyrios Christos* (1913). Troeltsch developed the general philosophical thesis in the principle of "historical relativity", which rejects treating Christianity as a normative exception outside the laws of history that apply to all other religions, and announced it in *Die Absolutheit des Christentums und die Religionsgeschichte* (1902). All of them shared a method that rejects dogmatic apologetic theology as a framework for studying texts, replacing it with philological, historical and comparative tools. They were thus an extension of Max Müller's project of the "Science of Religion" applied to the Jewish-Christian tradition.

## Methodological Contribution: From Written Sources to Oral Layers

The earlier generation, Wellhausen and the Documentary Hypothesis, concentrated on dismantling the written text into its four sources (J, E, D and P). Gunkel and his school developed a complementary question: what oral layers lie earlier than that, what stereotyped "literary forms" (Gattungen) shaped them, such as the lament, the tale and the psalm, and what influences of neighbouring cultures shaped these layers? From this grew "form criticism" (Formgeschichte), which Rudolf Bultmann applied to the Gospels in *Die Geschichte der synoptischen Tradition* (1921), a path that led its author to the program of "demythologization" in 1941. This expansion turned the study of the Bible from an internal theological discipline into a branch of general comparative history of religions.

## The Connection to the Arab Heritage: Wellhausen and the Idols of the Arabs

Julius Wellhausen, professor of Oriental languages at Göttingen from 1892, although not a member of the theological circle, produced in *Reste arabischen Heidentums* (1887) a model of comparative treatment of ancient Semitic religion. In it he relied on the *Book of Idols* (*Kitab al-Asnam*) by Hisham ibn Muhammad ibn al-Sa'ib al-Kalbi (d. c. 819 CE), in which he recorded reports of the idols of the pre-Islamic Arabs such as al-Lat, al-'Uzza, Manat and Hubal, and which is an important source for the history of Arab paganism. The Arabic-Islamic tradition had its own earlier comparative descriptions of religions, in al-Fisal fi al-Milal wa-l-Ahwa' wa-l-Nihal by Ibn Hazm, in al-Milal wa-l-Nihal by al-Shahrastani (1127 CE), and in Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind by al-Biruni. However, its doctrinal framework differed from German historical criticism, and the school does not claim any direct influence from it.

## Impact and Limits

The school exerted a deep influence on later New Testament studies, and on establishing the idea that the great monotheistic religions are a living part of the general "history of religions" that can be studied with the same tools. Its major hypotheses, however, have declined. Carsten Colpe, in *Die religionsgeschichtliche Schule* (1961), challenged the hypothesis of an Iranian "gnostic redeemer myth" on which Reitzenstein and Bousset built part of their interpretation of Christology. Samuel Sandmel, in "Parallelomania" (*Journal of Biblical Literature*, 1962), criticized the obsession with parallels, that is, inferring influence from mere similarity. The "Pan-Babylonism" that exaggerated the reduction of the Bible to Babylon was also criticized in the "Babel und Bibel" debate after the lectures of Friedrich Delitzsch (1902–1905). Conservative theologians also accused the school of dissolving the particularity of revelation in general cultural comparisons that may overlook the theological distinctiveness that believers themselves claim.
