# Stoicism

A philosophical school founded in Athens around 300 BCE by **Zeno of Citium** (c. 334–c. 262 BCE), who taught in the "Painted Stoa" (Στοὰ Ποικίλη), from which the name is derived. It was one of the major schools of the Hellenistic age and the Greek philosophy that most influenced Roman thought. Stoicism offered a unified system of logic, physics and ethics, and a practical way of life aimed at virtue and tranquillity.

## Phases

- **Early Stoa (c. 300–150 BCE)**: Zeno, Cleanthes and Chrysippus, the builders of the system.
- **Middle Stoa (c. 150–50 BCE)**: Panaetius and Posidonius, who modified the doctrine and broadened it to include natural science, history and geography.
- **Roman Stoa (1st–2nd century CE)**: Seneca, Musonius Rufus, Epictetus and the emperor Marcus Aurelius, who stressed ethics as a way of life.

## Key figures

- **Zeno of Citium** (c. 334–c. 262 BCE): founder, of Phoenician origin from Cyprus.
- **Cleanthes** (c. 330–c. 230 BCE): successor of Zeno, author of the *Hymn to Zeus*.
- **Chrysippus** (c. 279–c. 206 BCE): the third head of the school, its greatest systematizer and a creator of propositional logic, said to have written some 700 works, which are lost.
- **Panaetius** (c. 185–c. 110 BCE) and **Posidonius** (c. 135–c. 51 BCE): leading Middle Stoics; Posidonius is known for his scientific and historical work.
- **Seneca** (c. 4 BCE–65 CE): Roman statesman, tutor and adviser of Nero, who was forced to take his own life; author of the *Letters to Lucilius* and philosophical essays.
- **Epictetus** (c. 50–c. 135 CE): a former slave who taught in Nicopolis; his *Discourses* and *Handbook* were recorded by his pupil Arrian.
- **Marcus Aurelius** (121–180 CE): Roman emperor, author of the *Meditations*, a private notebook of Stoic exercises.

## Central concepts

- **Virtue as the only good**: virtue, and the wisdom that underlies it, is sufficient for happiness (*eudaimonia*). The cardinal virtues are wisdom (φρόνησις), justice, courage and moderation. External things such as health, wealth and reputation are "indifferents" (ἀδιάφορα); some are "preferred", but none is good or bad in itself.
- **Living in agreement with nature (ὁμολογουμένως τῇ φύσει ζῆν)**: the goal, understood as living in accord with rational human nature and with the rational order of the cosmos.
- **Logos and providence**: the cosmos is a rational, living whole permeated by divine reason (λόγος), which the Stoics also call God, nature, fate or providence.
- **Fate and responsibility**: everything occurs through a chain of causes, yet the Stoics (especially Chrysippus) argued that human assent remains "up to us" and so is responsible.
- **The passions and *apatheia***: passions (πάθη) are judgments that something good or bad is present or coming. The sage is free of them (ἀπάθεια) but not without feeling, and experiences "good feelings" (εὐπάθειαι) such as joy and caution.
- **The dichotomy of control**: in Epictetus, some things are up to us (our judgments and choices) and others are not (body, property, reputation, office).
- **Cosmopolitanism**: all rational beings share in the same reason and form a single community; the sage is a "citizen of the world", and the doctrine of *oikeiosis* (affiliation) extends concern outward from oneself to humanity.
- **Duties and acceptance of death**: death is not an evil; Seneca and Marcus Aurelius also discuss suicide as permitted in extreme circumstances.

## Logic

Stoic logic was developed chiefly by Chrysippus. It treats propositions (not terms, as in Aristotle) as the units of inference, and recognizes conditional, conjunctive and disjunctive propositions and the basic inference schemata (such as *modus ponens*). Modern historians of logic regard it as an anticipation of propositional logic. The Stoics also discussed signs, impressions and the criterion of truth, the "cognitive impression" (καταληπτικὴ φαντασία).

## Physics

- **Corporealism**: only bodies exist (in the strict sense); God, soul and the virtues are corporeal in some sense. Time, place, void and "sayables" are incorporeals.
- **Pneuma**: a fiery, airy "breath" pervades the cosmos and gives bodies their unity and qualities; the soul is a form of pneuma.
- **Cosmic cycles**: the universe is periodically consumed in a conflagration (ἐκπύρωσις) and then regenerated in the same form.

## Historical impact

- **Roman culture**: Cicero, Seneca and others brought Stoic ideas into Latin literature and political and legal thought, including the idea of a natural law common to all.
- **Early Christianity**: Stoic ideas such as the Logos, providence and natural law influenced Christian writers such as Tertullian and Ambrose; the extent of any Stoic influence on Paul and Augustine is debated by historians.
- **Arabic-Islamic thought**: Stoic doctrines, for example in logic, reached Arabic through Greek commentators and doxographies, and elements of Stoic logical teaching appear in Arabic logical works, although Stoicism was not transmitted as a separate school.
- **Early modern philosophy**: neo-Stoicism (Justus Lipsius, 1547–1606) influenced moral and political thought, and Stoic themes appear in Descartes and Spinoza.
- **Modern psychology and popular revival**: Stoic ideas influenced cognitive-behavioural therapies (Albert Ellis, Aaron Beck) and a modern "Stoicism" movement of self-help.

## Limits and criticisms

- **Fragmentary sources**: nearly all the writings of the early Stoics are lost, and their doctrines are reconstructed from later authors (Cicero, Seneca, Diogenes Laërtius, Plutarch, Sextus Empiricus).
- **Differences among Stoics**: the Middle and Roman Stoics (Panaetius, Posidonius, Seneca) were not always faithful to Zeno and Chrysippus, and differ on the passions, the soul and the strictness of the ideal.
- **Misreadings**: "stoic" in everyday language suggests suppressed feeling; the school's own position is more nuanced.
- **The ideal sage**: critics from antiquity to the present find the ideal of the wise man, who alone is virtuous, unreachable, and the Stoic account of fate and responsibility hard to reconcile.
