# Neoclassical-Keynesian Synthesis

The **Neoclassical-Keynesian Synthesis** is a school of macroeconomics that dominated the teaching of economics in American and British universities from the late 1930s to the mid-1970s. It sought to merge the Keynesian view that the economy can settle at involuntary unemployment in the short run with the traditional neoclassical framework, based on markets that clear themselves automatically in the long run.

## From diagram to model: the contribution of John Hicks

The British economist John Hicks presented in 1937, only a year after the publication of Keynes's *General Theory*, an article that turned Keynes's complex prose argument into a simplified graphical model later known as the **IS-LM model**: two curves representing equilibrium in the goods market (investment and saving) and equilibrium in the money market (liquidity preference and the money supply), whose intersection determines the level of income and the interest rate together. This model became the standard language through which Keynesian economics was taught to generations of students, although Hicks himself later expressed reservations about how faithfully his simplification captured the spirit of Keynes's original thought.

## Paul Samuelson and the formulation of the "synthesis"

The American economist Paul Samuelson carried this project further in his influential textbook *Economics*, which has been in print since 1948 and educated generations of students around the world, by formulating what he and later commentators called the "neoclassical synthesis": in the short run, when there is spare productive capacity and unemployment, Keynesian tools for managing demand apply; as the economy approaches full employment, the traditional neoclassical principles based on price flexibility and market efficiency regain their force. This compromise allowed economists to adopt practical Keynesian instruments without wholly abandoning the mathematical rigour of neoclassical economics, which Samuelson himself developed further in his more theoretical work *Foundations of Economic Analysis* (1947).

## The Phillips curve: a trade-off that seemed stable

In 1958 A. W. Phillips reported a historical inverse relationship, for Britain from 1861 to 1957, between unemployment and the rate of change of money wages, not of prices. Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow, in 1960, recast that scatter as a menu between price inflation and unemployment for the United States. Policymakers were then offered a point on the **Phillips curve**: a higher rate of inflation against a lower rate of unemployment, or the reverse. For a time this seemed to resolve macroeconomic management through a single trade-off. The step from wage change to price inflation was an addition to Phillips's article, and it is the step that later failed.

## Academic and political dominance

Through this synthesis, a modified Keynesian outlook dominated Western economic policy throughout the "thirty glorious years" that followed the Second World War. Western governments adopted the management of aggregate demand through fiscal and monetary policy as a declared goal, believing they had reliable tools to control the cyclical fluctuations of the capitalist economy without sacrificing its market foundations.

## Collapse before stagflation

The synthesis was severely shaken by the oil price shock of 1973 and the phenomenon of **stagflation** that followed: inflation and unemployment rising together, in explicit contradiction to the stable trade-off assumed by the traditional Phillips curve. Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps (in the late 1960s) had already criticised the stable trade-off, and Robert Lucas later pressed a decisive theoretical attack, which led to the decline of the synthesis's dominance in favour of monetarism and then the New Classical school during the 1970s and 1980s.

## Criticism from the left: the betrayal of Keynes's spirit

Economists belonging to what was later called the post-Keynesian school — the most prominent of whom was Joan Robinson — criticized this synthesis sharply. They argued that it emptied Keynes's original thinking about the fundamental uncertainty and instability of capitalism of its radical content, reducing it to a minor technical addition to the traditional neoclassical model rather than a genuine theoretical alternative to it. This interpretive dispute between the two schools has continued to the present day.

## Institutional influence on official economic policy

The synthesis went beyond the boundaries of the university into the decision-making institutions themselves: the US Council of Economic Advisers, under the Kennedy and Johnson administrations, drew on Keynesian macroeconomic reasoning to justify the stimulus tax cuts of the early 1960s. This marked an unusually explicit official use of Keynesian reasoning in peacetime American fiscal policy, reflecting the high confidence of the time in this compromise framework's ability to "steer" the economic cycle with near-engineering precision.

## The spread of the synthesis into global curricula

The synthesis spread through translations of Samuelson's textbook into dozens of languages and its adoption in universities around the world, from Japan to Latin America. For many students outside the specialist journals it was the main framework through which macroeconomics was learned, before curricula widened in response to monetarism and the New Classical school.
