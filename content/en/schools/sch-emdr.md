# Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR)

A psychotherapy developed by Francine Shapiro in 1987 that uses **guided eye movements** (or other forms of bilateral stimulation, such as auditory taps) to relieve the symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and other trauma-related disorders. It is regarded as one of the most controversial treatments in contemporary psychology.

## Historical context

In 1987, Shapiro, then a doctoral student, noticed that her distressing thoughts became less disturbing while her eyes moved rapidly from side to side. She developed this observation into a procedure and in 1989 published a controlled study of 22 people with traumatic memories, reporting reduced distress. The treatment was later extended to other conditions, with the strongest evidence remaining for PTSD.

## The eight-phase protocol (Standard Protocol, Shapiro 2001)

1. **History taking and planning**: gathering the trauma history and identifying goals.
2. **Preparation**: building a safe relationship with the therapist and teaching calming techniques.
3. **Assessment**: identifying the distressing image, the negative belief, the alternative positive belief, and the intensity of distress.
4. **Desensitization**: focusing on the image together with eye movements until the distress diminishes.
5. **Installation**: integrating the processed memory with the alternative positive belief.
6. **Body scan**: exploring any bodily discomfort linked to the memory.
7. **Closure**: returning to calm at the end of each session.
8. **Reevaluation**: checking progress in the next session.

## The theoretical philosophy: the Adaptive Information Processing (AIP) model

Shapiro proposes that traumatic memories are stored in an "unprocessed" state in the brain and continue to trigger the emotional response because they have not been integrated into the broader memory network. Eye movements (or bilateral stimulation) **facilitate reprocessing** and allow the brain to integrate the memory.

## The scientific controversy

### Supporting evidence
- **American Psychiatric Association (2004)**: its practice guideline for acute stress disorder and PTSD listed EMDR among effective treatments.
- **VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guidelines (2010, 2017)**: recommended EMDR among the trauma-focused psychotherapies for PTSD.
- **WHO (2013)**: recommended EMDR, alongside trauma-focused CBT, for adults with PTSD.

### Criticisms
- **McNally (1999)**: in a critical historical analysis, compared the early enthusiasm for EMDR with earlier fads and argued that the distinctive element, the eye movement, had not been shown to be necessary.
- **Herbert et al. (2000)**: a critical review argued that the novel elements of EMDR lacked support and that its benefits could be attributed to exposure and other cognitive-behavioral components.
- **Lilienfeld (1996)**: argued that EMDR's claims exceeded the evidence available at the time.

### Supporters' reply
- **Shapiro and colleagues** replied that dismantling studies were inadequate tests of the model.
- **Lee & Cuijpers (2013)**: a meta-analysis of treatment trials and laboratory studies concluded that adding eye movements contributed to reducing the vividness and emotionality of memories, although this remains debated.

## Applications

- **PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder)**: the main application.
- **Anxiety disorders**: phobias, social anxiety.
- **Depression**: especially trauma-related.
- **Complicated grief**: loss of loved ones.
- **Addiction**: in cases of co-occurring trauma (evidence is limited).

## Empirical impact and methodological controversy

The Cochrane review (Bisson & Andrew, 2007) found EMDR and trauma-focused CBT, including prolonged exposure, to be effective for PTSD symptoms, with no clear difference between them. The most prominent controversy concerns the eye movement itself: studies such as Davidson and Parker (2001), in a meta-analysis, found no significant incremental effect of the eye movements and no significant advantage of EMDR over other exposure techniques, whereas Shapiro and her supporters hold that the neural processing mechanism is broader than mere exposure.
