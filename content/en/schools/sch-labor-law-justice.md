# Labor Law & Justice

## Historical Context

Islamic jurisprudence addressed the employment relationship from its earliest centuries under the heading of "hire" (*ijara*) in transactions. Jurists detailed the rules of contracts for hiring persons and the rights and duties of the hired worker, establishing a central principle drawn from a prophetic hadith (reported in Ibn Majah): "Give the worker his wage before his sweat dries." They expanded on the question of the "wage of equivalent" (*ajr al-mithl*) when the wage is not specified in the contract. Al-Kasani, in *Bada'i' al-Sana'i'*, discussed the rules for hiring a private and a shared worker, while Ibn Taymiyya, in *al-Hisba fi al-Islam*, addressed the role of the state in overseeing markets and crafts to ensure workers are not exploited, through the institution of *hisba*, which functioned as an early regulatory apparatus for controlling employment and trade relations. Al-Qarafi, in *al-Furuq*, discussed questions of liability in hire and the role of public interest (*maslaha*) in commercial rules.

Modern labor law in its institutional sense, however, arose in industrial Europe during the nineteenth century as a reaction to the horrors of the Industrial Revolution. When the harsh working conditions in factories revealed an urgent need for legislation to protect workers from exploitation, the Factory Acts were issued in Britain beginning in 1833 to restrict the working hours of children in textile factories, and were later extended to wider groups of workers and to safety conditions.

## Central Ideas

Modern labor law rests on the idea of the structural inequality between the two parties to the employment relationship, which calls for state intervention to protect the weaker party. This idea intersects with the Islamic principle of *ijara*, which required clarity about the wage and the duration of work to avoid ambiguity (*jahala*) and uncertainty (*gharar*). Among its central ideas are also the right to trade union organization and collective bargaining, the right to strike as a legitimate instrument of pressure, and the principle of distributive justice in sharing the product of production between capital and labor. Marxist-origin socialism offered a radical critique of capitalist labor relations as the exploitation of surplus value, while the Catholic Church, through Pope Leo XIII's encyclical *Rerum Novarum* in 1891, offered a middle-ground vision calling for social justice without abolishing private property.

## Leading Thinkers

Karl Marx is the most prominent theorist of the critique of capitalist relations of production, while Otto von Bismarck in Germany introduced the first comprehensive system of social insurance for workers in the 1880s. In contemporary Islamic jurisprudence, Yusuf al-Qaradawi, in his book *Dawr al-Qiyam wa al-Akhlaq fi al-Iqtisad al-Islami* (The Role of Values and Ethics in the Islamic Economy), discussed the place of ethical values in economic life, including fair dealing with workers, in light of the objectives of the Sharia.

## Impact and Application

The International Labour Organization, founded in 1919, established a global normative framework for workers' rights, through its conventions and recommendations, which many states, including states with Muslim majorities, have drawn on or ratified in their labor legislation, which seeks to reconcile international standards with the rulings of Islamic jurisprudence on *ijara* and *hisba*. The debate continues about the limits of state intervention in regulating the labor market, between advocates of broad social protection and advocates of labor-market flexibility in contemporary liberal economies.

One of the most contested issues in this field is the question of the right to strike and its legitimacy in contemporary Islamic jurisprudence: one group of jurists holds that a strike is a form of legitimate abstention from work when the worker's right has not been secured, while another group objects to a strike when it disrupts public interests that may not be disrupted, such as essential services. There is also a divide between the classical liberal tradition, which tends to see free contracting between individuals as a sufficient guarantee of justice, and the interventionist school, which relies on Karl Polanyi's analysis in *The Great Transformation* on the need to protect society from the commodification of labor as if it were any other commodity. The twentieth century witnessed an intellectual struggle between the socialist model, which makes ownership of the means of production collective, and the contractual capitalist model, in which the state's role is limited to setting a minimum of guarantees. Meanwhile, contemporary Islamic economics offers a middle-ground vision based on the principle of balancing freedom of contract with the prevention of exploitation, drawing on the maxim "no harm and no reciprocal harm" and on the principle of social solidarity that the Sharia established through the system of *zakat* and social mutual support.

Contemporary legal thought also faces the problem of work in the digital economy and online platforms, where the legal classification of the worker is disputed between being a dependent employee who deserves the full protection of labor law and being an independent contractor to whom this protection does not apply. This problem calls for a return to the precise jurisprudential standards that distinguished between the private employee (*ajir khass*), who places his time at the employer's disposal, and the shared employee (*ajir mushtarak*), a craftsman who retains his professional independence. The contemporary jurisprudential debate also continues over the extent to which modern employment contracts, such as remote work and seasonal work, can be adapted within the traditional jurisprudential frameworks of *ijara*, in a way that guarantees workers their rights without undermining the labor market flexibility that the contemporary economy requires.
