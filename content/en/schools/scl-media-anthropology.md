# Media Anthropology

Media anthropology is the study of media — television, radio, film, newspapers, and now digital platforms — as cultural phenomena. It asks how media are produced, how they circulate, and how people make sense of them in their daily lives. Unlike media studies, which often begins with texts or industries, media anthropology begins with people: with the viewers in a village, the journalists in a newsroom, the filmmakers in a community, and the audiences who talk back to their screens. Its premise is that media are not outside culture but part of it, shaping and shaped by the societies that use them.

## History

Anthropologists were slow to take media seriously. For much of the twentieth century, mass media were treated as a corrupting outside influence, something that destroyed authentic culture rather than something worth studying. The shift began in the 1980s, when ethnographers started to document how people actually used television and radio. Eric Michaels's work with Warlpiri people in central Australia, published posthumously as *Bad Aboriginal Art* (1994), showed that Aboriginal communities were not passive victims of television but active appropriators, using video to assert identity and control representation.

The field was named and defined in the early 1990s. Debra Spitulnik's programmatic essay "Anthropology and Mass Media" (1993) argued that media should be studied ethnographically, as part of everyday life, and set out a research agenda that the field has followed ever since. Faye Ginsburg's "Indigenous Media: Faustian Contract or Global Village?" (1991) examined how indigenous peoples use film and video to speak to their own communities and to the wider world, and her work established indigenous media as a central topic.

The 2000s brought consolidation and expansion. *Media Worlds* (2002), edited by Ginsburg, Lila Abu-Lughod, and Brian Larkin, offered a comparative, ethnographic account of media in many societies, and Kelly Askew and Richard Wilk's *The Anthropology of Media: A Reader* (2002) gathered the field's key texts. Abu-Lughod's *Dramas of Nationhood* (2005) showed how Egyptian television serials shape ideas of nation, gender, and modernity, while *Media Anthropology* (2005), edited by Eric Rothenbuhler and Mihai Coman, mapped the field's methods and theories. John Postill's *Media and Nation Building* (2006) traced how media and nationalism intertwine in Malaysia.

## Key Figures and Works

Spitulnik (1993) defined the field, insisting that media anthropology must study production, circulation, and reception together, and in everyday contexts. Ginsburg (1991) made indigenous media a core concern, showing that video and film can be tools of cultural survival and political assertion. Michaels (1994) demonstrated that media use is creative and strategic, even in communities with little access to the means of production.

Abu-Lughod (2005) showed how television melodrama works as a form of national pedagogy, teaching viewers how to be modern citizens, and how audiences interpret serials in ways that reflect their own lives. Larkin's *Signal and Noise* (2008), on Nigerian video films and their infrastructures, showed how media infrastructures — cinemas, video parlours, piracy networks — shape cultural life. Postill (2006) connected media to the state, showing how governments use broadcasting to build nations and how citizens use it to talk back.

## Method

Media anthropology is ethnographic. Researchers spend time with audiences, watching television with families, listening to radio in markets, and following the circulation of films and clips through villages and cities. They also study production: sitting in newsrooms, on film sets, and in community media centres, and tracing how stories are chosen, shot, and edited. Increasingly, they follow media across platforms, from broadcast to social media, and across borders, as diasporic audiences watch the programmes of home from abroad.

The method's distinctive strength is attention to reception. Media anthropologists ask not only what a programme says but what people do with it: how they quote it, argue about it, imitate it, and use it to make sense of their own lives. They also attend to the materiality of media — the devices, the signals, the electricity — because access to media is uneven, and that unevenness is itself a social fact.

## Influence and Legacy

Media anthropology changed both anthropology and media studies. It showed anthropologists that media are not a threat to culture but a part of it, and it showed media scholars that audiences are not passive recipients but active interpreters. Its ethnographies of television, radio, and film have become central texts in the study of globalisation, nationalism, and modernity, and its work on indigenous media has influenced policy on cultural rights and representation.

Today the field's questions have moved online, into social media, streaming, and the algorithmic curation of attention, where its ethnographic methods are more needed than ever. Media anthropology's lasting contribution is its insistence that media must be understood from the ground up, in the living rooms and marketplaces where they are actually used, and that the people who watch, listen, and share are the real experts on what media mean.
