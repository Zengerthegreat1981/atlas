# Humanistic Psychology

Humanistic psychology is the "Third Force" (the "third revolution") in psychology, as Abraham Maslow (1962, 1968) famously framed psychology as three forces: **behaviorism** (the first, "external events only"), **psychoanalysis** (the second, "unconscious internal events only"), and **humanism** (the third, "the whole person, subjective experience, potentials for growth"). Humanistic approaches (Rogerian, existential, and, more loosely, Gestalt) emerged from the climate of postwar American modernity (1950s-1960s). Its slogan: a respect for the person as the best authority on their own experience.

## Intellectual Context

- **Phenomenology** (Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Sartre): a return to consciousness as it is lived.
- **Existentialism** (Kierkegaard, Sartre, Buber, Frankl): authenticity, responsibility, meaning.
- **American pragmatism** (William James, John Dewey): an emphasis on experience and action.
- **A reaction** against the reductionism of psychoanalysis (libido, childhood) and of behaviorism (S-R).

## Historical Phases

- **Pre-founding** (1930s-1940s): Rogers works with children and families in Rochester, New York (1928-1940) and then develops nondirective counseling (*Counseling and Psychotherapy*, 1942). Maslow publishes "A Theory of Human Motivation" (1943) and *Motivation and Personality* (1954).
- **Institutional founding** (1954-1964): Maslow and Anthony Sutich begin organizing a network in the mid-1950s; the *Journal of Humanistic Psychology* appears in 1961 and the Association for Humanistic Psychology is established in the early 1960s (sources give 1961 to 1963; its inaugural meeting was in 1963); the 1964 conference at Old Saybrook, Connecticut, gives the movement a public identity.
- **Countercultural flourishing** (1960s-1970s): the human potential movement (Esalen Institute, founded 1962) and encounter groups (including Rogers's work from the late 1960s) draw on humanistic ideas, and Gestalt therapy becomes part of the wave. The two overlap but are not identical: humanistic psychology was an academic and clinical current, while the human potential movement was a broader, more popular culture of workshops and personal growth.
- **Further development** (1970s-1980s): Maslow and others add a "fourth force," transpersonal psychology (1968-1969), and existential-humanistic therapists such as Bugental and May develop a more depth-oriented strand; Frankl's logotherapy, with its emphasis on meaning, is often grouped nearby.
- **Expansion** (1980s-present): positive psychology (launched by Seligman in the late 1990s), and continuing existential-humanistic work (Bugental, Schneider).

## Founding Concepts

- **Person-Centeredness**: the individual has the capacity to understand themselves if a safe relationship is provided. The client is the best expert on their own life.
- **Positive Orientation**: psychology studies what makes life worth living, not only what makes it ill. Maslow: "the psychology of health."
- **Self-Actualization**: an inherent drive toward growth into the individual's unique potentials. Maslow: "what a man can be."
- **Organismic Trust**: if environmental obstacles are removed, the human organism "moves toward growth" spontaneously (Rogers: the actualizing tendency).
- **Unconditional Positive Regard**: accepting the client as they are, without conditions. The cornerstone of the therapeutic relationship for Rogers.
- **Empathy**: understanding the other's inner world "as if it were one's own," while remaining separate from it.
- **Congruence/Authenticity**: the therapist is genuine, with consistency between their feelings and their outward behavior.
- **Phenomenological Awareness**: a return to "what is happening now" in consciousness.

## Key Figures

- **Abraham Maslow** (1908-1970): the hierarchy of needs, self-actualization, progressive psychology.
- **Carl Rogers** (1902-1987): client-centered therapy, the therapist's core conditions.
- **Rollo May** (1909-1994): existential-humanistic thought, anxiety, freedom.
- **James Bugental** (1915-2008): existential-humanistic therapy.
- **Anthony Sutich** (1907-1976): co-founder, with Maslow, of the *Journal of Humanistic Psychology*.
- **Viktor Frankl** (1905-1997): logotherapy, deep humanistic psychology, the third dimension.
- **Charlotte Bühler** (1893-1974): developmental psychologist who was an early supporter of the movement and a participant in the Old Saybrook conference.

## Major Sub-Currents

- **Client-Centered Therapy** (Rogers).
- **Peak-experience and self psychology** (Self-Actualization, Maslow).
- **Deep humanistic psychology** (May, Bugental, Yalom).
- **Gestalt** (Perls): a fundamental point of intersection.
- **Positive psychology** (Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi, 2000): a later development with a more empirical emphasis.
- **Logotherapy** (Frankl): an existential-spiritual extension.
- **Relational-Cultural Theory** (Miller, Jordan, Surrey): a related development in feminist relational psychology.
- **Focusing-oriented therapy** (Gendlin): guided subjective experience.

## Clinical Applications

- **Individual therapy** in a range of lengths.
- **Counseling** (educational, vocational, marital).
- **Group therapy** (encounter T-groups, NTL, Gestalt sensitivity work).
- **Education and organizational work** (for example Rogers's *Freedom to Learn*, 1969, and the Center for Studies of the Person in La Jolla).
- **Family therapy** (Satir, client-centered family therapy).
- **Patient-Centered Medicine**: a direct influence on medicine.

## Relation to Neighboring Schools

- **Existential therapy**: the overlap is considerable. May is often counted in both camps, and Rogers and Maslow shared existentialism's emphasis on authenticity while being more optimistic about human nature.
- **Gestalt**: a humanistic current that focuses on present wholes rather than a positive orientation.
- **Psychoanalysis**: Rogers, who trained in clinical and educational psychology rather than analysis, criticized directive interpretation in favor of reflection. Humanistic strands within psychoanalysis (Fromm, Horney) are a point of intersection.
- **CBT**: Rogers's relationship conditions have been studied empirically, and some integrative approaches combine person-centered and cognitive methods.
- **Positive psychology**: a more empirical development of humanistic themes, although its founders also distanced it from humanistic psychology.
- **Social constructionism**: critics such as Kenneth Gergen see the movement as excessively individualistic.

## Critique and Limits

- **Methodological subjectivity**: difficulty in measuring empathy and authenticity (even its supporters acknowledge this).
- **Excessive individualism**: it neglects the social context (feminist critique, Gergen).
- **Its conversion into "self-help"**: the slide from an academic psychology into popular self-development.
- **Application to severe cases**: less structured than approaches such as DBT and CBT.
