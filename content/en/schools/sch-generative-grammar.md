# Generative-Transformational Grammar

A research program in syntax begun by Noam Chomsky in *Syntactic Structures* (1957). It rejected the behaviorist and discovery-procedure goals of much post-Bloomfieldian American linguistics and treated a grammar as a formal theory of a speaker's knowledge of language. Over the next decade it became a major part of the emerging cognitive sciences.

## Break with Distributionalism

Rather than describing language through surface distributional procedures, Chomsky posited an **underlying grammar (Generative Grammar)**: a system of generative rules capable of producing an unlimited number of grammatically correct sentences from finite resources, and of explaining the speaker's ability to judge whether sentences they have never heard before are correct or incorrect.

## Core Hypotheses

- **Competence/Performance**: the distinction between the linguistic system latent in the speaker and the intermittent actual use of that system. It is comparable to Saussure's dichotomy (langue/parole), but is framed in terms of individual mental knowledge rather than a social system.
- **Universal Grammar**: the hypothesis that an innate grammatical structure is shared by all humans, which explains how children acquire their language so quickly from limited input (the "Poverty of the Stimulus" argument).
- **Deep and surface structures**: a distinction between the underlying syntactic structure that carries meaning and the overt structure that results after transformation rules are applied.

## Successive Historical Stages

Generative grammar has not remained a single fixed framework since 1957. It has developed through a series of successive theoretical revisions. The first was the Standard Theory in *Aspects of the Theory of Syntax* (1965), which introduced the deep structure level as the input for semantic interpretation. Next came Government and Binding Theory (early 1980s), which unified the scattered transformation rules into a single general movement principle (Move α), constrained by a system of general principles that can be set to variable parameters across languages (the Principles and Parameters theory). Then came the Minimalist Program (from 1993), which sought to reduce the theoretical apparatus to the fewest possible tools, assuming one basic derivational operation (Merge) that builds the grammatical structure bottom-up.

## First Language Acquisition: A Rapid Innate Explanation

Chomsky based his argument partly on the observation that children acquire the grammar of their mother tongue extremely quickly and in a regular sequence across very different cultures, despite differences in the linguistic input they actually receive (which Chomsky characterised as limited and often fragmentary, a characterisation that critics dispute). He treated this pattern as support for an innate capacity that limits the grammars a child entertains. Usage-based researchers dispute both the description of the input as too poor to support learning and the inference to a dedicated innate grammar.

## Method: Linguistic Intuition as Data

Unlike distributional structuralism, which refused to rely on speakers' intuitions, generative grammar relied from the outset on native speakers' judgments about whether a sentence is grammatically acceptable (grammaticality judgments) as a basic empirical datum. The argument was that this intuition is a direct reflection of the internal linguistic competence the model seeks to describe, not merely a subjective, non-objective opinion as its distributionalist opponents claimed.

## The First Break: Criticism of Skinner

Chomsky's review (1959) of B. F. Skinner's *Verbal Behavior* (1957) argued that reinforcement and extinction do not explain how a child produces and understands sentences that were never specifically trained. The review became a standard reference in the move away from behaviorist accounts of language. Historians disagree about how far it, by itself, redirected psychology; behaviorist research continued, and the broader shift in psychology had several sources.

## Impact and Limits of the Claim

The program has been central to theoretical syntax since the 1960s and has influenced work on language in psychology. It did not by itself create psycholinguistics, which already had experimental traditions, but it set many of the questions those experiments later asked. However, the hypothesis of universal grammar and innateness has remained, and still remains, an open scientific debate that has not been settled against competing usage-based and functional schools (such as the usage-based grammar of Bybee and Tomasello). These schools hold that language acquisition can be explained by general learning mechanisms that are not specialized for language, without assuming a specialized innate grammatical structure in advance.

## Philosophical Background

Chomsky presented his linguistic theory as part of a rationalist tradition in the philosophy of mind, in opposition to the behaviorist empiricism that dominated American psychology in the mid-twentieth century. In *Cartesian Linguistics* (1966) he traced precursors of his ideas to Descartes and to the Port-Royal grammarians, a historical reading that some historians of linguistics have questioned.
