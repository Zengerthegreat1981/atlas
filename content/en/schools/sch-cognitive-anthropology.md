# Cognitive Anthropology & Ethnoscience

A branch of the American school that emerged in the late 1950s, led by Ward Goodenough and Harold Conklin. It studies culture as a system of mental knowledge rather than as a set of behaviors or material objects.

## Central Idea

This approach follows Goodenough in defining culture as "whatever it is one has to know or believe" in order to operate in a manner acceptable to the members of a society. Culture is thus a cognitive structure of classification and interpretation rules, not merely an observable pattern of behavior. The approach borrows tools from structural linguistics ("componential analysis") to analyze local classification systems (kinship terms, the names of plants, animals, and colors) with formal precision. Its aim is to extract the implicit classification "rules" as the members of the culture understand them from within (the "emic perspective"), rather than as imposed by the researcher from outside (the "etic perspective").

## Method

This method was applied especially to the study of folk taxonomies of plants and animals and to color-naming systems across cultures, comparing how different languages divide the continuous color spectrum into distinct categories. Brent Berlin and Paul Kay's 1969 investigation of the universality of color classification is a direct extension of this tradition.

## Impact

Cognitive anthropology established a rigorous methodological tradition that influenced the multidisciplinary cognitive sciences. It became a bridge between linguistic anthropology (Sapir–Whorf) and contemporary cognitive studies of classification and perception across cultures.

## Detailed Examples

One of the most prominent applications of this method is Harold Conklin's study of plant classification among the Hanunóo people of the Philippines. He documented roughly sixteen hundred local plant categories, which in some domains were more finely differentiated than the corresponding scientific botanical classification. This demonstrated that "folk knowledge" is not primitive or simplistic, but a sophisticated knowledge system in its own right, serving precise practical purposes.

Berlin and Kay's research on color terms compared multiple languages. They concluded that, despite differences in the number of color categories across languages (some have only two color terms, others eleven basic colors), there is a regular universal sequence in which these categories appear throughout the history of any language. This was considered evidence of shared biological perceptual constraints beneath apparent cultural diversity.

## Critique

The approach was later criticized by interpretive anthropology, Clifford Geertz in particular, for reducing culture to a formal cognitive structure closer to strict grammatical rules. Critics argued that it ignored the rich interpretive, emotional, and contextual dimensions of cultural meaning, which cannot easily be reduced to formal classification lists, however technically precise those lists may be.

## Emic and Etic Perspectives

The distinction between the emic perspective (from within, in the terms of the culture's own members) and the etic perspective (from outside, in general analytical terms set by the researcher) was a central methodological contribution. Its influence extended far beyond cognitive anthropology itself, becoming widely used in cultural anthropology. Kenneth Pike, the linguist who coined the two terms by analogy with phonemic and phonetic, used the distinction to describe the difference between understanding a phenomenon as its members understand it in their own categories, and analyzing it with general comparative tools that allow comparison between cultures. This remained a basic methodological tension in all subsequent debates on anthropological method.

## Continuity of the Field

Despite declining interest in strict componential analysis after the 1970s, cognitive anthropology continued in renewed forms within what is known today as the "new cognitive anthropology." This approach draws on neuroscience and evolutionary psychology to study how shared universal human cognitive structures interact with vast local cultural diversity.

## Kinship Terminology as a Founding Example

One of the earliest applications of this method was the analysis of kinship terminology across cultures. English uses a single word (uncle) for a father's brother, a mother's brother, and a father's sister's husband without distinction, whereas other languages distinguish very precisely between these kin relations with entirely separate vocabulary, reflecting the varying social importance of these relations in each cultural system. Floyd Lounsbury used the unit of "componential analysis" to break these systems down into basic distinctive features (generation, paternal or maternal side, sex), in a manner similar to the analysis of speech sounds into distinctive features in structural linguistics.

The field's lasting contribution is a reminder that understanding another culture begins with learning its own internal categories and concepts, before any attempt at comparison or generalization across cultures.
