# Third World Approaches to International Law (TWAIL)

An academic movement in international law that emerged in the late 1990s (its official name is TWAIL: Third World Approaches to International Law). It shares with **critical legal studies** a skepticism about the neutrality of law, but focuses specifically on **the colonial legacy embedded in the structure of international law itself**.

## Jurisprudential Precedent: International Relations in Classical Islamic Jurisprudence

Centuries before modern European international law took shape, Islamic jurisprudence developed an independent theoretical framework for relations between states and communities of different religions. It divided the world jurisprudentially into the "abode of Islam," the "abode of war," and the "abode of covenant or truce," and codified rules of safe conduct (*aman*), protection (*dhimma*), armistice (*hudna*) and treaties, with the Treaty of al-Hudaybiyya cited as a foundational model in jurisprudence. This formed a comprehensive normative system for regulating relations with the political and religious "other," one framed primarily in religious rather than racial or "civilizational" terms, unlike the later classifications of European international law into "civilized" and "uncivilized" nations. This comparison is not an absolute exoneration of the jurisprudential heritage from all hierarchy, but it shows that models for organizing relations between different political communities were not confined to the European path that ended in modern international law, and that TWAIL's critique of that later path gains additional depth when compared with an alternative path that arose outside Europe early on.

## Central Thesis: International Law Was Born Colonial, Not Neutral

TWAIL theorists reject the traditional narrative that presents international law as a neutral system that developed gradually among sovereign equal states, with its principles later applied equally to Third World countries after their independence. They argue that **the foundational concepts of international law itself** (such as sovereignty, the state, and the "civilized" versus "uncivilized" nations in traditional international law) were originally formulated **to justify and organize European colonial rule**, rather than as a neutral framework preceding the colonial encounter.

## Anghie: Sovereignty as a Product of the Colonial Encounter, Not Its Prior Condition

**Antony Anghie** presents the most influential formulation of this thesis in his book *Imperialism, Sovereignty and the Making of International Law* (2005), showing how European international law scholars in the nineteenth century developed the standards of "sovereignty" and "civilization" specifically **to justify excluding** non-European societies from full legal protection, rather than to describe a neutral legal reality that existed beforehand.

## Rajagopal: Development as a New Instrument of Domination

**Balakrishnan Rajagopal**, in his book *International Law from Below* (2003), extends the critique from the era of direct colonialism to the post-independence era. He argues that the discourse of "development," which dominated international institutions after the Second World War (the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund), reproduced colonial hierarchy in technical economic language instead of the old explicit civilizational language, classifying states as "developed" and "developing" by standards dictated from the Western center itself. Rajagopal emphasizes the importance of "social movements from below" in the Global South as a source for formulating more equitable international legal rules, rather than as passive recipients of law formulated from above.

## Abi-Saab: The Early Question Before the Official Name

**Georges Abi-Saab** belongs to a generation chronologically prior to the official founding of the TWAIL movement. In the early 1960s (1962), he raised structural questions about the position of newly independent states (especially African and Asian ones) with respect to rules of international law that had originally been formulated without their participation. This makes him an early intellectual precedent whom the founding generation of TWAIL later drew upon, although he had not used the same name.

## Critique and Difficulties

TWAIL faces criticism from within the field of international law to the effect that, despite its strength in historical deconstruction, it confronts the same difficulty that critical legal studies faces: formulating a coherent positive institutional alternative to replace the system it criticizes, rather than merely exposing its defects. It is also sometimes criticized for generalizing "the Third World" as a homogeneous category, whereas the interests of its states and their relations to the existing international order vary considerably and are difficult to reduce to a single unified colonial narrative.

## Impact on Contemporary International Practice

TWAIL's analytical tools have inspired contemporary debates on the justice of the International Criminal Court, which critics from the Global South accuse of selectively focusing on African leaders without pursuing similar crimes committed by great powers. They have also found an echo in critiques of the structure of international financing institutions (the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund) as a continuation of economic dependency relations established in the colonial era. These tools have further resonated in debates on reforming the United Nations Security Council and expanding the representation of non-Western states within it, arguing that the current composition of the Council (and the veto power in particular) is a direct extension of the balance of power that followed the Second World War, without revision reflecting subsequent changes in the international order.
