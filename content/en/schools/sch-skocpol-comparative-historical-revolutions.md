# Comparative-Historical Sociology of Revolutions

Theda Skocpol's (b. 1947) rigorous comparative method for explaining the success of the great social revolutions, focusing on the collapse of the state's structure and not on class revolutionary consciousness alone.

## The Central Idea

In *States and Social Revolutions* (1979), Skocpol systematically compared three great social revolutions (France 1789, Russia 1917, China 1949) with contrasting cases of reform or failed revolt (England in the 1640s, Prussia/Germany in 1848, Japan after 1868), using the comparative-historical method of "agreement" and "difference" (borrowed from John Stuart Mill). She concluded that great successful social revolutions require two simultaneous structural conditions: first, a crisis that weakens the capacity of the state's repressive and administrative apparatus to meet challenges (often external military pressure exceeding the capacity of an economically backward state); and second, an independent peasant or rural rebellion already grounded in its own social structure (not imported from a revolutionary urban organization). She thereby departed from earlier Marxist and voluntarist explanations that stressed organized class "revolutionary consciousness" as the essential condition, affirming instead the priority of structural "state collapse" as a necessary precondition preceding any large-scale revolutionary mobilization.

## Comparative Methodology and the Influence of Barrington Moore

Skocpol drew methodologically on the work of Barrington Moore Jr., above all his book *Social Origins of Dictatorship and Democracy* (1966), which compared the paths of political modernization in England, France, America, Japan, China and India, concluding that the nature of the alliances among social classes (especially the stance of the landed agrarian class and the peasants toward industrialization) largely determines whether a national path ends in liberal democracy, fascism or communism. Skocpol developed this comparative-historical tradition, but focused more precisely on the state as an independent actor with its own interests (not a mere reflection of the balance of class forces), and on international geopolitical pressure (wars, military competition among states) as a decisive factor in weakening the old state's capacity to withstand the revolutionary crisis.

## William Sewell's Critique and the Missing Cultural Dimension

The historian William Sewell directed an influential critique at Skocpol's model, holding that her exclusive focus on structure (state collapse, peasant rebellion) neglected the role of ideology and revolutionary discourse in explaining the French Revolution in particular. Sewell argued that the fall of the Bastille and the radical revolutionary transformation that followed cannot be understood without taking into account how the revolutionaries themselves reinterpreted their events through new cultural concepts and frameworks (such as the concept of the "nation" and "popular sovereignty") that changed the course of events themselves rather than merely describing them afterwards. Skocpol later partly acknowledged the importance of this cultural dimension in subsequent works, without abandoning the priority of the structural factor in her basic explanation.

## Impact

This work helped consolidate comparative-historical sociology in American sociology, and it shaped later debates about revolutions, including the Iranian revolution of 1979, despite later criticisms of its neglect of the role of ideology and conscious human agency. Her rigorous comparative method, based on systematically comparing successful and failed cases instead of being content with the study of a single case, is still taught as a basic model of the methodology of comparative historical social science.

## Criteria for Distinguishing Social Revolution from Political Coup

One of Skocpol's most important methodological contributions is her careful distinction between "social revolution", a rapid, radical and simultaneous transformation in both the political structure of the state and the class structure of society, often accompanied by broad popular mobilization from below (as in France, Russia and China), and "political revolution" or the narrower coup, which change the ruling authority without a comparable transformation of the deeper social and class structure. This distinction allowed her to exclude many cases from the general category of "revolutions" (such as the repeated military coups in Latin America) and to focus analytically solely on the historically rare cases that combined state collapse and radical class social transformation, which raised the methodological rigour of her historical comparison.

## Concluding Note

Skocpol's book has remained one of the most cited works in American political sociology over the past four decades, and it helped consolidate the research tradition known today as "comparative historical sociology", which continues to apply her rigorous comparative methodology to broad phenomena beyond revolutions alone, from the emergence of welfare states to the formation of colonial and postcolonial regimes.

The debate between her structural explanation and Sewell's cultural one remains one of the most fertile ongoing methodological discussions in the sociology of revolutions and of large-scale social change.
