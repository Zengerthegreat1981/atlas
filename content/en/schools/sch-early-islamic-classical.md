# Early Islamic & Classical Period

An umbrella for the intellectual fields that took shape from the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 11 AH/632 CE until about 1100 CE. The major juristic and theological schools were largely in place by the fourth/tenth century. In this period the boundaries between kalam (theology), usul al-fiqh (legal theory), Qur'anic exegesis, the hadith sciences and Arabic grammar were not yet those of fully separate disciplines. These inquiries arose together from one question: how revelation is to be understood, and how a ruling and a doctrine are to be derived from it once direct revelation had ended.

## Major Strands

- **Kalam**: It emerged in the first half of the second/eighth century around divine unity (tawhid), divine justice ('adl), human acts and fate in the afterlife. The later tradition presents the Mu'tazila as the first organized theological school and names Wasil ibn 'Ata' (d. 131 AH/748–749 CE) in Basra as the figure who separated them, on a rational account of unity and justice. Modern historians treat those beginnings as gradual.
- **Codification of fiqh and its schools**: The legal schools that still have followers took shape around Abu Hanifa al-Nu'man (d. 150 AH/767 CE) in Kufa, Malik ibn Anas (d. 179 AH/795 CE) in Medina, al-Shafi'i (d. 204 AH/820 CE), who taught in Baghdad and died in Fustat, and Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 241 AH/855 CE) in Baghdad. The tradition attributes to al-Shafi'i's *al-Risala* the first systematic treatise on usul al-fiqh. Wael Hallaq has argued that usul al-fiqh as a full genre belongs to the fourth/tenth century.
- **Qur'anic and hadith sciences**: Exegesis (tafsir), the collection of prophetic hadith and the verification of their chains of transmission developed in parallel, as two tools for extracting rulings and doctrines from the two sources of revelation.
- **Contact with Greek, Persian and Indian traditions**: With the expansion of the conquests into al-Sham, Iraq and Persia, Muslim thinkers came into early contact with earlier philosophical and theological traditions, which fed the translation movement in Baghdad (see: Bayt al-Hikma and the Translation Movement).

## The First Sources of the Religious Sciences

Al-Shafi'i (d. 204 AH/820 CE) wrote *al-Risala*, the book the later tradition treats as the first coherent account of the sources of legal derivation: the Qur'an, the Sunna, consensus (ijma') and analogy (qiyas). It addresses both the People of Opinion (ahl al-ra'y) and the People of Hadith. Before it, Kufan jurists around Abu Hanifa were known for reasoned opinion, and Medinan jurists relied on the practice of Medina, as in Malik's *al-Muwatta'*. Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari (d. 256 AH/870 CE) and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (d. 261 AH/875 CE) then compiled the two *Sahih* collections. Criticism of transmitters ('ilm al-rijal) and the grading of narrators (al-jarh wa-l-ta'dil) were already practiced by critics such as Yahya ibn Ma'in and Ahmad ibn Hanbal. The two *Sahih* books applied that criticism in large collections that later Sunni scholarship treated as canonical.

## Theology and the Sects

Doctrinal debate arose early with the Kharijites, the Murji'a and the Qadariyya. The Mu'tazila then inherited these questions and set out their five principles: divine unity, justice, the promise and the threat, the intermediate position between two positions (al-manzila bayn al-manzilatayn), and enjoining good and forbidding evil. After the *mihna* (the inquisition over the createdness of the Qur'an), begun under al-Ma'mun in 218 AH/833 CE and ended under al-Mutawakkil in 234 AH/848–849 CE, the balance of power shifted. Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. c. 324 AH/935–936 CE) is credited with a middle theological path, and Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 333 AH/944 CE) with a related one in Samarqand, while the school of the people of hadith under Ibn Hanbal remained wary of kalam altogether. The Ash'ari, Maturidi and hadith-centered alignments then began to take shape; they were not yet fully formed schools at the close of the *mihna*.

## Exegesis, History and Compilation

Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari (d. 310 AH/923 CE) wrote *Jami' al-bayan 'an ta'wil ay al-Qur'an* and *Tarikh al-rusul wa-l-muluk*. In the first he gathered the opinions of the early authorities and weighed them, including by linguistic reasoning; in the second he set out historical narrative with chains of transmission. The sciences later arranged as separate disciplines drew, in those centuries, on a shared body of material.

## Between Transmission and Understanding

Reports of fabrication led critics to look at the chain (isnad) and, more selectively, at the wording (matn). The method that was written down and taught centered on the chain and on the reliability of narrators. Biographical registers such as the *Tabaqat al-kubra* of Ibn Sa'd (d. 230 AH/845 CE) became a principal source for the Companions and the Successors.

## The General Methodological Feature

Analogy (qiyas) was used early in jurisprudence, extending the ruling of a stated case to a new one, and in theology as the analogy of the unseen to the seen (*qiyas al-gha'ib 'ala al-shahid*). That shared habit is one reason usul al-fiqh and kalam remained close in this period, before each field developed further tools of its own. Hadith criticism and exegesis used other procedures and were not simply branches of analogy.

## Limitations

- Much of the material on this period was written centuries after the events (the biographical and historical compilations of the fourth and fifth AH centuries), which makes the precise dating of the beginnings of each school a matter of debate among modern historians.
- This school is an umbrella that organizes numerous subfields; it is not a single intellectual school in the strict sense.
