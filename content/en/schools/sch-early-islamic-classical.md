# Early Islamic & Classical Period

An umbrella that gathers the intellectual fields that took shape in the first three centuries of Islam, from the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632 CE to the consolidation of the major juristic and theological schools around the tenth century CE. In this period the boundaries between kalam (theology), usul al-fiqh (legal theory), Qur'anic exegesis, the hadith sciences and Arabic grammatical philosophy were not yet the boundaries of independent disciplines. These sciences arose intertwined from a single question: how the revelation is to be understood, and how a ruling and a doctrine are to be derived from it after direct revelation had ended.

## Major Strands

- **Kalam**: It emerged in the first half of the eighth century CE around questions of divine unity (tawhid), divine justice ('adl), human free will and the human fate in the afterlife. The Mu'tazila, and their founder Wasil ibn 'Ata' (d. 748 CE) in Basra, are regarded as the first organized theological school, resting on a rational defence of divine unity and justice.
- **Codification of fiqh and its schools**: In this period the major legal schools that continue to this day took shape under imams such as Abu Hanifa al-Nu'man (d. 767 CE) in Kufa, Malik ibn Anas (d. 795 CE) in Medina, and later Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 855 CE) in Baghdad, alongside the school of al-Shafi'i, which produced the first systematic theory of usul al-fiqh.
- **Qur'anic and hadith sciences**: Exegesis (tafsir), the collection of prophetic hadith and the verification of their chains of transmission developed in parallel, as two tools for extracting rulings and doctrines from the two sources of revelation.
- **Contact with Greek, Persian and Indian traditions**: With the expansion of the conquests into al-Sham, Iraq and Persia, Muslim thinkers came into early contact with earlier philosophical and theological traditions, which paved the way for the great translation movement in Baghdad (see: Bayt al-Hikma and the Translation Movement).

## The First Sources of the Religious Sciences

Al-Shafi'i (d. 204 AH/820 CE) wrote his *Risala*, in which he is regarded as the first to formulate a coherent theory of the sources of legal derivation: the Qur'an, the Sunna, consensus (ijma') and analogy (qiyas). In it he responded to both the People of Opinion (ahl al-ra'y) and the People of Hadith. He was preceded by a debate between the Kufan school, in which Abu Hanifa was known for reasoned opinion, and the Medinan school, which relied on the practice of the people of Medina, as in the *Muwatta'* of Malik. Then Muhammad ibn Isma'il al-Bukhari (d. 256 AH/870 CE) and Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (d. 261 AH/875 CE) compiled their two *Sahih* collections, and with them the science of the transmitters ('ilm al-rijal) and the evaluation of narrators (al-jarh wa-l-ta'dil) was established as a tool for criticizing chains of transmission.

## Theology and the Sects

Doctrinal debate arose early with the Kharijites, the Murji'a and the Qadariyya. The Mu'tazila then inherited these questions and set out their five principles: divine unity, justice, the promise and the threat, the intermediate position between two positions (al-manzila bayn al-manzilatayn), and enjoining good and forbidding evil. After the *mihna* (the inquisition over the createdness of the Qur'an) begun under al-Ma'mun (833 CE) and ended around 848 CE, the balance of power shifted. Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. c. 324 AH/935–936 CE) founded a middle theological path, and Abu Mansur al-Maturidi (d. 333 AH/944 CE) founded a close one in Samarqand, while the school of the people of hadith under Ibn Hanbal remained wary of kalam altogether. Thus three major circles of Sunni doctrine became established.

## Exegesis, History and Compilation

Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari (d. 310 AH/923 CE) wrote *Jami' al-bayan fi tafsir al-Qur'an* and *Tarikh al-rusul wa-l-muluk*. In the first he gathered the opinions of the early authorities and weighed them by linguistic reasoning; in the second he established the method of historical narrative with chains of transmission. This indicates that the sciences that were later arranged into independent disciplines drew, in those centuries, on a single body of material.

## Between Transmission and Understanding

The spread of fabrication in hadith gave rise to the criticism of the text (naqd al-matn) alongside the criticism of the chain (naqd al-isnad), and to the writing of biographical registers, such as the *Tabaqat al-kubra* of Ibn Sa'd (d. 230 AH/845 CE), one of the broadest sources on which our knowledge of the generation of the Companions and the Successors rests.

## The General Methodological Feature

Analogy (qiyas, extending the ruling of a case addressed in the text to a new case) was used as a central instrument across all these fields from an early date, both in jurisprudence (legal analogy) and in theology (the analogy of the unseen to the seen). This explains the close overlap between usul al-fiqh and kalam in this early period, before each field developed its own tools.

## Limitations

- Much of the material on this period was written centuries after the events (the biographical and historical compilations of the fourth and fifth AH centuries), which makes the precise dating of the beginnings of each school a matter of debate among modern historians.
- This school is an umbrella that organizes numerous subfields; it is not a single intellectual school in the strict sense.
