# Marxist Classical Sociology

Marxist classical sociology is the school founded by Karl Marx (1818–1883) and Friedrich Engels (1820–1895), which explains social life through the organisation of material production and the conflicts it generates. Its central claim is historical materialism: the way a society produces its means of life conditions its social relations, its politics, and its ideas, and history advances through contradictions within each mode of production that are eventually resolved in a new social order. Marx wrote as a philosopher, economist, journalist, and revolutionary, not as an academic sociologist, and his work entered sociology only after his death, yet it became one of the discipline's founding traditions. The school differs from the narrower study of ideology or conflict in general, because it ties both to a specific theory of capitalism, class, and exploitation.

## Historical Origins and Key Figures

Marx was born in Trier in 1818, studied philosophy at Bonn and Berlin, and joined the Young Hegelians, who criticised religion and the Prussian state through the philosophy of Hegel. In Paris in 1844 Marx met Engels, a Manchester industrialist's son whose *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) documented industrial misery first-hand, and the two became lifelong collaborators. Expelled from several countries, Marx settled in London in 1849, where he spent decades in the British Museum reading room working on *Capital*. The revolutions of 1848 provided the occasion for *The Communist Manifesto* (1848), written with Engels, which declared that the history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles. After Marx's death Engels edited the later volumes of *Capital*.

## Core Analytical Concepts

Historical materialism rests on a distinction between the forces of production, tools, techniques, and labour power, and the relations of production, the property relations under which production is organised. Together they form the economic base, on which rises a superstructure of law, politics, religion, and ideas. Classes are defined by their relation to the means of production: under capitalism, the bourgeoisie owns capital while the proletariat owns only its labour power, which it must sell. Exploitation is the extraction of surplus value, the difference between the value workers produce and the wages they receive. Alienation describes how, under capitalism, workers are estranged from the product of their labour, from the labour process, from their fellow workers, and from their own human potential. Class consciousness develops when workers recognise their shared interest, moving from a class in itself to a class for itself.

## Key Works and Method

Marx's method, a materialist version of Hegelian dialectic (the label "dialectical materialism" is not Marx's own and was adopted by later writers), treats social forms as historical and contradictory rather than natural and permanent. *The German Ideology* (written 1845–1846, published 1932) states the materialist conception of history in its first systematic form. *The Communist Manifesto* (1848) applies it as a political programme. The *Grundrisse* (written 1857–1858, published 1939) contains Marx's most searching notebooks on method, money, and pre-capitalist societies. *Capital*, Volume 1 (1867), is the school's central work: it analyses the commodity, the labour theory of value, the working day, machinery, and the accumulation of capital, arguing that capitalism's contradictions, overproduction, falling rates of profit, and the growth of a reserve army of labour, drive it toward crisis. Engels's *Anti-Dühring* (1878) popularised the system, and *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884) extended materialism to kinship and the state.

## Case Studies

Two classic analyses show the method applied to particular events. In the chapter of *Capital* on the working day, Marx used reports of British factory inspectors and parliamentary commissions to trace struggles over hours. Competition pushed employers to lengthen the day, even at the cost of workers' health and children's lives, while organised workers and the state eventually imposed legal limits through the Factory Acts. The case joins an abstract theory of surplus value to documented conflict, and shows law responding to class struggle without simply expressing one employer's wishes. *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte* (1852) examines how Louis Bonaparte moved from elected president of France to seizing power in 1851. Marx distinguished several fractions of the propertied classes and gave attention to parties, the army, the state bureaucracy, and the smallholding peasantry, whom he regarded as unable to organise themselves as a national class. The analysis is considerably more intricate than a two-class model and is often cited against purely economic readings of Marx. Both remain interpretations whose evidence and conclusions historians can dispute.

## Contemporary Empirical Examples

Later researchers have used and revised Marxist concepts in fieldwork and surveys. Michael Burawoy's *Manufacturing Consent* (1979), based on work as a machine operator in a Chicago-area factory, asked why workers cooperate in their own exploitation and found that shop-floor games around output quotas generated consent. Erik Olin Wright's *Class Counts* (1997) used comparative surveys to study class positions defined by property, authority, and skill, including managers and others whose locations a two-class scheme cannot easily place. In China, Pun Ngai's *Made in China* (2005) examined young women migrant workers in an electronics factory, showing how class formation was entangled with gender and rural origin. Ching Kwan Lee's *Against the Law* (2007) compared labour protest in China's declining industrial north-east and export-oriented south, where workers frequently appealed to law and made grievances that did not become a unified class movement. Such studies support the continuing value of examining property, work, and conflict, while indicating that class interests do not translate automatically into class consciousness.

## Influence and Legacy

Classical Marxism has been one of the most influential traditions in sociology. Conflict theory, as in the work of Ralf Dahrendorf and C. Wright Mills, draws on, and also revises, its account of power and domination. Critical theory, from Lukács's *History and Class Consciousness* (1923) through the Frankfurt School, developed its account of reification and ideology. World-systems theory, dependency theory, and much of the sociology of development rest on Marx's analysis of capitalism as a global system. Even sociologists who reject Marxism define their positions against it: Weber's work is often read partly as an answer to Marxism, and Parsons built functionalism as an alternative account of order.

## Critical Perspectives and Limitations

The criticisms of classical Marxism are as extensive as its influence. The predicted collapse of capitalism and the immiseration of the working class did not occur as Marx expected: real wages rose in advanced capitalist countries, a middle class expanded, and revolutions occurred in agrarian societies rather than in the most developed ones. Critics charge the base-superstructure model with economic determinism, reducing politics, culture, and religion to reflections of production, and argue that it leaves little room for human agency or for the relative autonomy of ideas. Feminist critics observe that Marx's theory of labour ignored unpaid domestic work, and that his account of class neglected gender and race as independent sources of domination. The history of states claiming Marx's name in the twentieth century raised a further objection: that the theory's confidence in historical necessity could justify authoritarian rule.

## Relationship to Other Schools

Marxist classical sociology stands in productive tension with the other classical schools. Against Spencerian evolutionism it offers a theory of history driven by contradiction and rupture rather than gradual differentiation, and a critique of the market rather than a celebration of it. Against Durkheimian functionalism it treats order as the product of domination rather than moral consensus, and conflict rather than integration as the normal condition of class society. Against Weberian interpretive sociology it insists that meanings and ideas must be explained by material interests, while Weberians reply that ideas have their own causal power. Against Goffmanian dramaturgy it charges that the study of everyday performance misses the structures that make performances possible. The school's lasting achievement is to have made inequality, power, and the organisation of production questions that no sociology can ignore.
