# Simmelian Formal Sociology

## The Outsider Who Founded a Discipline

Georg Simmel (1858–1918) was one of the founding figures of sociology, and one of its strangest. A brilliant lecturer in Berlin whose courses drew hundreds of students, he was denied a full professorship for most of his career, held back by anti-Semitism and by academic suspicion of his essayistic style. He finally received a chair at Strasbourg in 1914, four years before his death. Instead of a grand system comparable to those of Marx, Durkheim or Weber, he produced penetrating essays and books — *The Problems of the Philosophy of History* (1892), *The Philosophy of Money* (1900), *The Metropolis and Mental Life* (1903) and *Sociology: Investigations on the Forms of Sociation* (1908) — that dissected the smallest forms of social life: the secret, the lie, the stranger, the meal and the letter. His question was deceptively simple: what is society? His answer was that society is not a thing but a process — "sociation" — the sum of the interactions through which individuals become connected to one another.

## Form and Content

Simmel's formal sociology rests on a distinction between the form and the content of social life. The content is the particular interests, drives and purposes that bring people together — hunger, love, profit, faith. The form is the recurring pattern of the interaction itself: domination and subordination, competition, conflict, exchange, secrecy, sociability. The same form can carry many different contents: competition occurs in sport, in business and in love; domination occurs in a household, an army and a church. Sociology's proper task, Simmel argued, is to isolate these forms and study them in abstraction from their contents, just as geometry studies the forms of space. This is why his work moves so freely between the trivial and the momentous: a quarrel between spouses and a war between nations can exhibit the same formal structure of conflict.

## The Dyad, the Triad and the Stranger

Simmel's most influential analyses concern number and social form. Two people — the dyad — form the most intimate and the most fragile of groups: each member is indispensable, and if one leaves, the group ceases to exist. Add a third person and everything changes: the triad introduces mediation, coalition and majority rule, and makes the group independent of any single member. Simmel's essay on the stranger, published in *Sociology* (1908), is his most celebrated study of a social type. The stranger is not the wanderer who comes today and leaves tomorrow, but the person who comes today and stays tomorrow — a member of the group who remains an outsider. The stranger's position, at once near and far, gives him a distinctive objectivity: people confide in strangers precisely because they are not entangled in local loyalties. The essay became a founding text for the sociology of migration and marginality.

## Money, the Metropolis and Modernity

Simmel was also a theorist of modernity. In *The Philosophy of Money* (1900) he argued that the money economy transforms social relations: money, as a pure means that can buy anything, makes all things commensurable and reduces qualitative differences to quantitative ones. It liberates the individual from personal dependence, but it also makes relations impersonal and calculative. In "The Metropolis and Mental Life" (1903) Simmel applied the same analysis to the city. The metropolis bombards the individual with a rapid, unceasing stream of impressions, and the mind defends itself with the intellect and with a blasé attitude — a dulling of feeling in the face of overstimulation. Urban life is dominated by punctuality, calculation and the money economy; the individual gains freedom from the close surveillance of small-town life, but at the price of loneliness and of a sense that objective culture — the accumulated products of human activity — grows ever larger while the individual's capacity to absorb it falls behind. This "tragedy of culture" became one of Simmel's most enduring ideas.

## Conflict, Secrecy and Sociability

Two of Simmel's most original arguments concern conflict and secrecy. Against the common view that conflict destroys society, Simmel argued in *Sociology* (1908) that conflict is itself a form of sociation: enemies are bound together by their antagonism, and conflict can strengthen solidarity, clarify boundaries and produce new norms. Secrecy, likewise, is not merely the absence of communication but a positive social form: the shared secret is one of the strongest bonds of trust. Sociability — conversation, play, the dinner party — is interaction pursued for its own sake, the pure form of sociation, society at play with itself.

## Influence and Criticism

Simmel's influence has been wide but indirect. In Germany he was overshadowed by Weber, who admired him but found his work unsystematic; in America his essays, translated in the 1950s by Kurt Wolff, shaped symbolic interactionism, small-group sociology and network analysis. Lewis Coser's theory of the functions of social conflict (1956) and Erving Goffman's studies of interaction both carry Simmel's imprint, and the Chicago School's urban sociology grew directly from his essay on the metropolis. The criticisms are equally persistent. His formal sociology can seem to float free of history and power: the form of domination looks the same in a family and in a dictatorship, but the difference matters. His writing is unsystematic, and his concepts — form, sociation, the tragedy of culture — are suggestive rather than precise. Yet Simmel's achievement remains distinctive: he showed that the microscopic texture of interaction — a glance, a secret, a third person entering a conversation — is not decoration on the surface of society but its very substance, and that the modern world can be understood through the forms in which people meet, compete, confide and part.
