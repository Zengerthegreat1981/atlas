# Existentialism

A philosophical current that reached the height of its fame in **France** (1945–1960), preceded in Germany by deeper formulations about two decades earlier. It holds that **existence precedes essence**: there is no fixed human nature that determines what a human being is; rather, the human being makes his nature through his choices and actions. Existentialism is not a single doctrine but **a set of shared stances** toward freedom, responsibility, anxiety and death. This page presents the general map of the current, while its two sister pages treat atheist existentialism (Sartre and Beauvoir) and religious existentialism (Marcel, Buber and Tillich) in detail.

## Intellectual Context

Existentialism was born of three legacies:
1. **Phenomenology** (Husserl, Heidegger): a return to "the things themselves" and an analysis of lived experience in place of system-building.
2. **Critique of abstract systems**: Kierkegaard expressed his objection to Hegel by holding that the concrete individual, not the "Absolute Idea", is the locus of truth.
3. **The crisis of modernity**: the First World War crushed confidence in reason, the Second World War and the Holocaust dispelled the claim of moral progress, and then mass consumerism in the 1950s threatened the individual with dissolution.

## Founding Concepts

- **Existence precedes essence** (Sartre): there are no predetermined human constituents; the human being comes first, and then becomes what he becomes.
- **Anxiety (Angst)**: not fear of something specific but a confrontation with possible freedom, since every choice excludes countless possibilities.
- **Being-toward-death** (Sein-zum-Tode, Heidegger): awareness of mortality is what returns the self to its ownmost possibility.
- **Freedom and commitment**: freedom is a burden that cannot be evaded.
- **The Other**: both a limit on my freedom and a source of my subjectivity.
- **Authenticity** as against "the They" (das Man): rejecting social pretense and living from within self-awareness.

## Phases

- **Pre-existentialism**: Kierkegaard (1813–1855), Nietzsche (1844–1900) and Dostoevsky, regarded retrospectively as forefathers.
- **German foundation**: Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927) and Jaspers's *Philosophy* (1932).
- **French flourishing**: *Being and Nothingness* (1943), Sartre's lecture (1945), and the journal *Les Temps modernes*.
- **Transfer to America**: William Barrett's book *Irrational Man* (1958) introduced the current to the American reader; John Wild's *The Challenge of Existentialism* (1955) was another early American treatment.
- **Clinical application**: existential psychotherapy in Binswanger, Medard Boss and Rollo May, logotherapy in Viktor Frankl, and Irvin Yalom in *Existential Psychotherapy* (1980).

## Internal Divisions

- **Atheist existentialism** (Sartre, Beauvoir): there is no God and freedom is absolute.
- **Religious existentialism** (Marcel, Buber, Tillich, Berdyaev): God is present and freedom is created.
- **Absurdism** (Camus): confronting a world without meaning without claiming absolute freedom.
- **Fundamental ontology** (Heidegger): he rejects the label and places "the question of Being" before the human being.
- **Alterity** (Levinas): ethics is first philosophy, preceding ontology.

## Key Figures

- **Kierkegaard**: the putative founder, retrospectively.
- **Heidegger** (1889–1976): fundamental ontology; he joined the Nazi Party and served as rector of Freiburg in 1933-1934, a record that has long clouded his legacy.
- **Jaspers** (1883–1969): "limit situations".
- **Sartre** (1905–1980) and Beauvoir (1908–1986): the public founders of the French movement.
- **Camus** (1913–1960) and Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961).
- **Marcel** (1889–1973), Berdyaev and Unamuno on the religious wing.

## Influence

- **Literature and theater**: Sartre's *Nausea* and *No Exit*, Camus's *The Stranger* and *The Plague*, extending into the theater of the absurd in Beckett and Ionesco, although the playwrights distanced themselves from the label.
- **Psychology**: existential therapy focused on meaning, death, freedom and isolation.
- **Theology**: Paul Tillich and Rudolf Bultmann, who drew on Heidegger's analysis of existence.
- **Cross-cultural reception**: existentialist themes resonated globally, sparking lively philosophical debates across Latin America, East Asia, and the Arab world regarding alienation, political commitment, and cultural authenticity.

## Limits and Critique

- **Adorno**, in *The Jargon of Authenticity* (1964), held that Heidegger's vocabulary of authenticity conceals ideology behind a solemn language.
- **Lukács** regarded existentialism as an escape from history and class structure.
- **Analytic philosophy**: Carnap, in "The Elimination of Metaphysics" (1932), deemed Heidegger's talk of "the nothing" meaningless.
- **Structuralism**: Lévi-Strauss and Foucault held that the central subject in existentialism is an illusion that must be dismantled, and the current's star waned in the 1960s.
- Existentialism is hard to define precisely because its leading figures reject the label and differ over its foundations.

## The Question of the Name and Method

The term "existentialism" (existentialisme) was not a name first given by its proponents; Gabriel Marcel used it, then the Paris press and Sartre's circle, until it stuck. Heidegger, Camus and Jaspers disowned it in different formulations. As for method, existentialist works converge on relying on the phenomenological description of lived experience (mood, shame, boredom, anxiety) and then interpreting it as possibilities of being; they sometimes adopt a literary style in the novel and the theater because abstract philosophy is not enough to express a concrete situation.

## Existentialism and the Arab World

The introduction of existentialism in Arabic began in the 1940s with Abdel Rahman Badawi, who combined German philosophy with literary experience; translators in Beirut and Cairo followed him, and in the 1950s existentialism became a cultural language expressing alienation and commitment, and in the Arabic novel it blended with experiences of defeat and exile. It met with rejection from religious currents that saw in its atheist version a demolition of faith, and from Marxists who saw it as an escape from class struggle.
