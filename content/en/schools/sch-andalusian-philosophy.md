# Andalusian Philosophy (Islamic Averroism)

An Islamic philosophical current that arose in al-Andalus in the eleventh and twelfth centuries CE, combining fidelity in transmitting Aristotle with original independent reasoning in logic, natural science, metaphysics and politics. It reached its peak with Ibn Rushd (d. 595 AH / 1198 CE), who became for the Latins the "Commentator" on Aristotle, and whose readings influenced Thomas Aquinas, "Latin Averroism" and the discussions of reason and faith in medieval Europe. Philosophy in al-Andalus was not an organized scholastic project but the individual activity of physicians, judges and writers who worked under the Umayyad state and then the Almoravids and Almohads.

## Founding context

Cordoba (756–1031 CE) was the capital of the Umayyad state in al-Andalus and a center of learning. There arose Ibn Masarra (d. 319 AH), author of the earliest Andalusian philosophical-mystical attempt, and Ibn Hazm the Zahiri (d. 456 AH), author of *al-Fisal fi al-Milal wa al-Ahwa' wa al-Nihal* and *The Ring of the Dove*. Then Ibn Bajja (d. 533 AH) lived in Saragossa and Fez and wrote *The Regime of the Solitary* on the isolation of the philosopher in a city that is not virtuous. Ibn Tufayl (d. 581 AH) ended the founding stage with the tale *Hayy ibn Yaqzan* about a mind that reaches the truth without a teacher, and he presented Ibn Rushd (born in Cordoba in 1126 CE) to the Almohad caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, who commissioned him to comment on Aristotle's books.

## Major principles

1. **The agreement of wisdom and the Law**: for Ibn Rushd in the *Decisive Treatise* the Law calls for rational inquiry into beings, so philosophy is permitted and obligatory for whoever is able. People have three ways: the demonstrative for the elite, the dialectical, and the rhetorical for the common people, and the interpretation meant for the elite is not to be broadcast to the public.
2. **Proofs of God's existence**: in *al-Kashf 'an Manahij al-Adilla* (*The Exposition of the Methods of Proof*) he set out the proofs from providence and from invention, and criticized the proofs of the theologians.
3. **Internal critique**: he criticized Ibn Sina on the question of existence, because he made existence an accident added to essence, and he criticized emanation and the theory of the Necessary and the possible.
4. **The reply to al-Ghazali**: in the *Incoherence of the Incoherence* he discussed the issues of the *Incoherence of the Philosophers*, and held that the dispute over the eternity of the world is close to verbal, and that for him the world is created with a continuous, eternal creation.
5. **The material intellect**: in his Long Commentary on *On the Soul* he held the unity of the material intellect among human beings, the question that in Latin provoked the controversy over the "unity of the intellect" (monopsychism).
6. **Politics**: he commented on Plato's *Republic* in *Summary of Plato's Republic*, stressed the role of reason in government, and called for the education of women and their participation in public life according to their abilities.
7. **The sciences**: he wrote in jurisprudence *Bidayat al-Mujtahid* and in medicine the *Kulliyyat*.

## Principal figures

- **Ibn Bajja** (d. 533 AH): pioneer of logic and natural science in al-Andalus.
- **Ibn Tufayl** (d. 581 AH): author of *Hayy ibn Yaqzan*, philosopher and physician, translated into Latin by Edward Pococke the Younger in 1671.
- **Ibn Rushd** (d. 595 AH): the Great Commentator.
- **Moses ibn Maymun (Maimonides)** (d. 1204 CE): born in Cordoba, a contemporary of Ibn Rushd, wrote the *Guide of the Perplexed* in Arabic, and combined philosophy with Jewish law.
- **Ibn Arabi** (d. 638 AH) and Ibn Sab'in: from Murcia, who carried Andalusian philosophy into the sphere of Sufism.

## Fates

- In 1195 the Almohad caliph al-Mansur burned the books of philosophy and exiled Ibn Rushd to Alisana (Lucena), then brought him back before his death.
- After the fall of Cordoba (1236 CE) and Seville (1248 CE) the scholars dispersed to the Maghreb, Tunisia and the East.
- Michael Scot translated Ibn Rushd's commentaries into Latin in Toledo in the 1220s; they were read by Albertus Magnus and Thomas Aquinas, and Aquinas then replied to them in *On the Unity of the Intellect against the Averroists* (1270).
- In 1277 the Bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, condemned two hundred and nineteen propositions, including views attributed to the Averroists such as Siger of Brabant.

## Reference works

- *The Decisive Treatise on the Connection between Wisdom and the Law* (*Fasl al-Maqal*).
- *The Incoherence of the Incoherence* (a reply to al-Ghazali's *Incoherence of the Philosophers*).
- *Manahij al-Adilla fi 'Aqa'id al-Milla*, which is the *Kashf 'an Manahij al-Adilla*.
- *Bidayat al-Mujtahid wa Nihayat al-Muqtasid* on comparative jurisprudence.
- The Great, Middle and Short (*Jawami'*) commentaries on Aristotle.

## Influence

- In medieval Europe: "Latin Averroism" in Paris and Padua with Siger of Brabant, Boethius of Dacia and John of Jandun, and then Pomponazzi in the Renaissance. Dante placed Ibn Rushd in the *Inferno* among the sages of Limbo.
- In Judaism: he influenced Levi ben Gershon and others, and his commentaries were translated into Hebrew.
- In the modern Arab era: Farah Antun revived Ibn Rushd in *Ibn Rushd and His Philosophy* (1903) and Muhammad Abduh replied to him, and then Muhammad Abid al-Jabiri presented Ibn Rushd together with Ibn Hazm and al-Shatibi as the current of "Maghrebi rationalism" in *We and the Heritage* (1980), and wrote *Ibn Rushd: Biography and Thought* (1998). Youssef Chahine directed the film *Al-Massir* (Destiny) (1997) about the persecution of Ibn Rushd.
- In contemporary political philosophy: Leo Strauss's studies on esoteric writing in *Persecution and the Art of Writing* (1952).

## Limits and critique

- Many Andalusian works were lost or survive only in Hebrew or Latin translation, among them Ibn Rushd's Long Commentary on *On the Soul*, whose Arabic original does not exist.
- Scholars differ over the degree of the Andalusians' originality relative to the East, and whether they are pure "Peripatetics" or a synthesis with Sufism.
- The term "Averroism" requires caution, for it was coined by the Latins' opponents, and does not necessarily represent Ibn Rushd as he was described.
- Over Ibn Rushd's position on the relation between reason and revelation there is controversy: is it a call for two truths, or for the unity of one truth by different methods?
