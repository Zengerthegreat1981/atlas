# Parsonian Structural Functionalism

## The Architect of American Theory

Talcott Parsons (1902–1979) dominated American sociology for a generation. From his base in Harvard's Department of Social Relations, which he helped to found in 1946, he built the most ambitious theoretical system sociology has produced: a general theory of action covering everything from a single act to whole civilisations. His project began with *The Structure of Social Action* (1937), a synthesis of Weber, Durkheim, Pareto and Marshall, and reached its mature form in *The Social System* (1951) and *Working Papers in the Theory of Action* (1953) with Robert F. Bales and Edward Shils, and in *Economy and Society* (1956) with Neil Smelser. Parsons's question was the classic Hobbesian problem of order: how is it that individuals, each pursuing their own ends, produce a stable, orderly society rather than a war of all against all? His answer was that order rests on shared norms and values, internalised through socialisation and enforced through social control, which align individual action with the needs of the system.

## The Theory of Action

Parsons's starting point is the "unit act": an actor pursuing an end, in a situation, guided by norms and values. Action is voluntaristic — actors choose among means and ends — but it is also constrained by conditions the actor cannot change and by norms defining which means are legitimate. A social system is a system of interaction between actors, organised around shared expectations — roles — and stabilised by institutions. The personality system is the actor's own organised set of needs and dispositions; the cultural system is the shared stock of symbols, values and beliefs; and the behavioural organism supplies energy and capacities. A functioning society requires all four systems to interlock.

## The Pattern Variables

To compare societies and roles, Parsons devised the pattern variables, five pairs of opposed orientations that characterise any act: affectivity versus affective neutrality, self-orientation versus collectivity-orientation, universalism versus particularism, ascription versus achievement, and specificity versus diffuseness. A doctor, for example, is expected to be affectively neutral, universalistic and specific toward patients, while a parent is expected to be affective, particularistic and diffuse toward a child. The pattern variables gave Parsons a vocabulary for describing modernisation: traditional societies favour ascription, particularism and diffuseness, while modern societies favour achievement, universalism and specificity.

## The AGIL Scheme

Parsons's best-known contribution is the AGIL scheme, first formulated with Bales and Shils in *Working Papers in the Theory of Action* (1953). Every social system, Parsons argued, must solve four functional problems if it is to survive. Adaptation (A): the system must secure resources from its environment and distribute them — the function performed above all by the economy. Goal attainment (G): the system must define collective goals and mobilise resources to achieve them — the function of the polity, above all the state. Integration (I): the system must coordinate its parts, manage conflict and maintain solidarity — the function of law, religion and the community. Latency (L), or pattern maintenance: the system must maintain and transmit its values and motivate its members — the function of the family, education and culture. Each function is carried by a subsystem, and the subsystems exchange with one another through generalised media: money in the economy, power in the polity, influence in the community and value-commitments in culture. The scheme was meant to apply at every level, from a small group to a whole society.

## The Sick Role and Socialisation

Parsons's most widely used single concept is the sick role, introduced in *The Social System* (1951). Illness is not only a biological condition but a social role, with defined rights and obligations. The sick person is exempted from normal duties, is not held responsible for the illness, is expected to want to get well, and is expected to seek competent help and cooperate with it. The doctor, correspondingly, is expected to apply universalistic standards, to be affectively neutral and to act in the patient's interest. The analysis showed how functionalism could illuminate a concrete institution — medicine — and it founded the sociology of health and illness. Similarly, Parsons's account of socialisation treated the family as the agency that internalises the values of the wider society. Critics noted that this picture makes the individual a passive recipient of values, and leaves little room for resistance or change from below.

## Criticism and Decline

By the late 1960s Parsons's dominance had collapsed. Critics argued that functionalism was inherently conservative: by treating every institution as meeting a functional need, it legitimated the existing order and could not explain conflict or revolutionary change. C. Wright Mills had already attacked Parsons in *The Sociological Imagination* (1959) for "grand theory" — abstract, jargon-laden and detached from empirical research. Others argued that the AGIL scheme was a classificatory device rather than an explanatory theory, and feminist critics pointed to Parsons's account of the family, with the husband's instrumental role and the wife's expressive role treated as functional necessities, as an apology for gender inequality. Yet Parsons's defenders, and the neo-functionalists who followed Jeffrey Alexander in the 1980s, insist that his questions remain unavoidable: any sociology must explain how order is possible, how values are transmitted, and how the parts of a society hang together. Even his critics still use his vocabulary — system, function, role, socialisation — and the attempt to build a general theory of society that Parsons undertook has not been repeated at the same scale since.
