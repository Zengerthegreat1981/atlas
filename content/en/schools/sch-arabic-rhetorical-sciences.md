# Arabic Rhetorical Sciences: Meaning, Figurative Expression and Embellishment

A classical Arabic body of knowledge that studies and systematically classifies the modes of eloquent linguistic expression. Its central theory of *naẓm* (composition) was set out by ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī, systematised by al-Sakkākī, and taught as a three-branch curriculum through al-Qazwīnī.

## From intuition to codification

These sciences began from the analytical vision of ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī in *Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz* and *Asrār al-Balāgha*, which tied the eloquence of speech to the internal grammatical relations among words (*naẓm*), not to the beauty of isolated words. Abū Yaʿqūb al-Sakkākī (d. 626 AH) turned this vision into a systematic classificatory framework in the third part of his encyclopedic *Miftāḥ al-ʿUlūm*, treating the science of *maʿānī* (meanings) and the science of *bayān* (expression), with *badīʿ* (embellishment) added as a supplement. The later division into three sciences, *maʿānī*, *bayān* and *badīʿ*, became standard through al-Qazwīnī's summaries.

## The three branches

**The science of *maʿānī*** studies the matching of grammatical structure to the requirements of the situation and context (such as the choice between a declarative and an imperative or interrogative construction, and placing a word first or later for a rhetorical purpose). **The science of *bayān*** studies the ways of expressing a single meaning through multiple images (simile, metaphor, metonymy, and figurative language). **The science of *badīʿ*** studies the aesthetic verbal and semantic embellishments (such as paronomasia, antithesis and parallelism) that add splendor to speech without changing its essential meaning.

## Al-Jurjānī's theory of naẓm in more detail

More than a century before al-Sakkākī, ʿAbd al-Qāhir al-Jurjānī (d. 471 AH / 1078 CE, though 474 AH is also reported) argued in *Dalāʾil al-Iʿjāz* that the rhetorical inimitability of the Qur'an, and eloquence more generally, lie not in the beauty of isolated words but in *naẓm*: the arrangement of words according to grammatical meanings such as fronting and postponement, definiteness and indefiniteness, separation and conjunction, and emphasis. On this account, rhetorical effect comes from grammatical relations among words. Scholars such as Margaret Larkin and Kamal Abu Deeb have studied the theory in relation to later discussions of structure and meaning, while warning against reading it as a modern semantic theory.

## Ibn al-Athīr and the competition between two schools

Alongside the al-Jurjānī–al-Sakkākī–al-Qazwīnī line, which eventually dominated the teaching of rhetoric, Ḍiyāʾ al-Dīn Ibn al-Athīr (d. 637 AH) proposed an alternative rhetorical approach in *al-Mathal al-Sāʾir fī Adab al-Kātib wa-l-Shāʿir*. It was closer to practical literary criticism than to abstract theoretical codification, focusing on practical critical taste in evaluating actual literary texts more than on building a comprehensive classificatory system of embellishments as al-Sakkākī did. This made his work a parallel reference that enriched the Arabic rhetorical debate from a complementary applied perspective.

## The connection to the Islamic Arab heritage

These sciences form the core of the Arab-Islamic rhetorical heritage itself. They arose essentially to serve a deeper understanding of the expressive inimitability of the Holy Qur'an, and they were organically linked with the sciences of exegesis and the foundations of jurisprudence (*uṣūl al-fiqh*), which borrowed from rhetoric tools for analyzing the meanings of words, such as the literal and the figurative. They also formed the theoretical basis on which classical Arabic poetry critics relied for centuries, such as al-Jurjānī himself in *Asrār al-Balāgha* when he analyzed metaphor and simile in early Arabic poetry.

## The duality of the literal and the figurative, and brevity and amplification

Among the central analytical tools developed by these sciences is the duality of the "literal" (*ḥaqīqa*) and the "figurative" (*majāz*). The literal is the use of a word in its original, established meaning, and the figurative is its use in another meaning through a relation between them (such as metaphor and metonymy). This duality provoked a long jurisprudential and rhetorical debate about its precise boundaries and its application to the Qur'anic text in particular. Parallel to it, rhetoricians developed the triad of "brevity" (*ījāz*, expressing meaning with the fewest possible words without loss of meaning), "amplification" (*iṭnāb*, adding words for a rhetorical purpose such as emphasis or clarification) and "equality" (*musāwāh*, the words matching the amount of meaning), a basic evaluative standard for the quality of style in a text according to what the situation requires.

## Impact and critique

Al-Qazwīnī's *Talkhīṣ al-Miftāḥ* (d. 739 AH; a summary of the third part of al-Sakkākī's work, later expanded in his *al-Īḍāḥ*) became the standard textbook for teaching Arabic rhetoric in traditional schools until modern times, and this system remained the principal reference framework for analyzing style and rhetoric in Arabic studies. Some modern researchers have criticized the classificatory rigidity into which the science of *badīʿ* in particular fell in later periods, when it sometimes became a mechanical enumeration of verbal embellishments detached from the deep organic vision of the relation between *naẓm* and meaning that al-Jurjānī had originally established. Contemporary scholars called for rereading al-Jurjānī himself with modern linguistic tools, recovering the depth of his original thesis beyond the later classificatory reduction.

## Relation to the principles of jurisprudence and the sciences of the Qur'an

Arabic rhetoric did not develop in isolation from the religious sciences. It intersected with them structurally. Scholars of the foundations of jurisprudence borrowed the rhetoricians' tools (such as the duality of literal and figurative, and the concept of "fitting to the situation") to analyze the meanings of the religious texts and to derive legal rulings from them. The science of Qur'anic inimitability (*iʿjāz al-Qur'ān*), as in al-Bāqillānī (d. 403 AH) before al-Jurjānī and then in al-Jurjānī himself, drew on this growing rhetorical apparatus to offer a systematic explanation of the stylistic superiority of the Qur'anic text over the known styles of the Arabs, instead of relying on the bare assertion of inimitability without technical analysis of the style itself.
