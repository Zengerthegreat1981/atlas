# Socialist Legal Systems

Socialist legal systems form an independent legal family that emerged after the October Revolution of 1917 in Russia. They were distinguished from the two dominant families in the world, the Romano-Germanic civil law and the English common law, by a fundamental ideological premise: that **law is a temporary class instrument**, not a neutral ethical ideal above history, and that it would wither away and disappear once full communism was achieved.

## Origins: Marxism and the Soviet State

The socialist jurisprudence drew on the thesis of **Marx and Engels** (in *The German Ideology*, written 1845–46 and first published in full in 1932, and in Marx's 1859 Preface to *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*) that law, like the state, is tied to a superstructure that reflects the prevailing class relations of production and possesses no real normative independence from them. On this basis, after the Bolshevik Revolution, a project was launched to draft a new Soviet law that would serve the building of socialism, although this project remained strained between two internally opposed currents.

## The Two Internal Currents: Pashukanis versus Vyshinsky

**Evgeny Pashukanis**, in his book *The General Theory of Law and Marxism* (1924), represented the most radical position. For him, law is a phenomenon belonging exclusively to bourgeois commodity-exchange society (the idea of the "legal form of the commodity"), and therefore it was destined to decline ("the withering away of law") gradually as socialist construction advanced and market relations disappeared. However, with the rise of Stalin, the official line of the party turned against Pashukanis's thesis (he was executed in 1937 during the Great Purge). It was replaced by the line of **Andrei Vyshinsky**, the Stalinist Prosecutor General, who instead affirmed that the socialist state needed a strong and centralized law, not one that was withering away, to protect socialist property and suppress enemies of the working class. This position officially dominated Soviet jurisprudence under Stalin and, in modified form, for much of the later Soviet period.

## Structural Features of Socialist Law

Socialist systems shared several structural features that distinguished them from Western families. **First**, the subordination of the judiciary to the direction of the ruling Communist Party, officially negating judicial independence in the liberal sense. **Second**, the abolition of private ownership of the means of production in law, in favour of collective or state ownership, and consequently the reformulation of civil law around the centrally planned economic contracts rather than individual freedom of contract. **Third**, the combination of explicit hostility to the liberal notion of "natural human rights" (regarded as a disguised bourgeois ideology) with the official adoption of the rhetoric of "social and economic rights" (work, housing, education) as the real rights guaranteed by the socialist system.

## Spread and Extension

This model spread, with considerable local variation, to the states of the Eastern Bloc after 1945 (Poland, Hungary, Czechoslovakia and East Germany), and then to China after 1949, to Vietnam and to Cuba. In those countries its ideas mixed with earlier local legal traditions (Confucianism in China, and colonial Spanish law in Cuba), producing hybrid forms rather than literal copies of the original Soviet model.

## Decline and Legacy after 1989

With the collapse of the Soviet Union and the Eastern Bloc system between 1989 and 1991, the socialist legal family disintegrated as a governing model in Europe. Most of its states adopted a rapid transition towards a market economy and the European civil law (often by reviving legal traditions that predated the communist era). China, Vietnam and Cuba remained partial exceptions: they maintained the dominance of a single party over the judiciary while introducing extensive market-economic reforms (the Chinese "socialist market economy"), producing a hybrid model that is difficult to classify definitively either within the traditional socialist family or outside it. Academic comparative debate continues over whether the "socialist legal family" remains a useful analytical category after the disappearance of the Soviet Union, or whether it has become a purely historical category, a matter of continuing disagreement among comparative legal scholars.

## The Position of Socialist Law within the Comparison of the Major Legal Families

Comparative legal scholars (including Konrad Zweigert and Hein Kötz in earlier editions of their classic reference work) have customarily treated the socialist family as a distinct legal family in their classification of the world's legal systems (alongside, in Zweigert and Kötz, the Romanistic, Germanic, Anglo-American and Nordic families, with Far Eastern, Islamic and Hindu law also treated); the third English edition (1998) no longer carries the chapter on socialist law. This classification has provoked methodological debate. While socialist law formally retained many terms and procedural structures inherited from the Romano-Germanic tradition (especially in Russia and Eastern Europe, where European civil codes had been dominant before 1917), the essential difference was not in technical form but in **the ideological function of law** and in the degree of its independence from the political authority of the ruling party. This led some scholars to regard it as a genuinely independent family, while others saw it as merely a politically modified variant of the civil law family rather than a family in its own right.

## Property and the Judiciary: Practical Applications

The structural differences of socialist law were most evident in two principal practical areas. In **criminal law**, the socialist state broadened the concept of the "counter-revolutionary crime" or the "economic crime against the state", an expansion that was used as an instrument of political repression particularly in the Stalinist era, going beyond the traditional limits of the principle of legality in criminal law (*nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege*) familiar in Western families. In **civil and economic law**, binding "planning contracts" between state enterprises replaced the individual freedom of contract that Western civil law presupposes, since economic priorities were set centrally through the state's five-year plans rather than through the mechanism of supply and demand.
