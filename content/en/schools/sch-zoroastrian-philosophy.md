# Zoroastrian Philosophy

The Iranian religious and philosophical tradition attributed to Zoroaster (Zarathustra). It rests on an ethical and existential duality between "truth" and "falsehood" and on the idea that the human being is a free party in a cosmic battle. This tradition has no philosophy in the Greek sense, but in its earliest hymns (the Gathas) and then in the Pahlavi books of the Sasanian era it produced what may rightly be called a "metaphysics of good and evil", and Iranian and Arab thought was later influenced by it, though the forms of influence differ.

## Historical Framework

Seventeen hymns, the Gathas (Gāthā), written in Old Avestan within the Yasna, are attributed to Zoroaster. His date is hard to fix: Zoroastrian tradition places him some two hundred and fifty-eight years before Alexander, that is, around the 6th century BCE, while Mary Boyce and many other modern scholars, on the basis of the archaic language of the Gathas and their description of a pastoral society, place him much earlier, roughly between 1500 and 1000 BCE, in eastern Iran or Central Asia. After the Gathas came the Yasna Haptanghaiti, the Yashts and the Videvdat, which were gathered into the Avesta, and then the Pahlavi texts of the 9th and 10th centuries CE, among them the Denkard, the Bundahishn, and the Shkand-gumanik Vizar, written by Mardan Farrokh in the 9th century, arguing by reason against Islam, Judaism, Christianity and Manichaeism.

## Central Concepts

- **Ahura Mazda** ("the Wise Lord"): the good creator, to whom evil is not ascribed.
- **Spenta Mainyu and Angra Mainyu**: the twin spirits in Yasna 30, the first of whom chose life and the second non-life, and by this choice the world was founded; the duality is ethical before it is cosmic. In the Sasanian era it became an ontological duality between Ohrmazd and Ahriman.
- **Asha and Druj**: truth and order against falsehood and chaos, the key to ethics; truthfulness is the central virtue.
- **The Amesha Spentas**: six holy beings embodying attributes of the deity, among them Vohu Manah (Good Thought) and Khshathra (Dominion).
- **Freedom and responsibility**: the human being chooses his path and is held to account for it, and there is no fate that determines the destiny of the soul.
- **Eschatology**: the dead person crosses the "Chinvat Bridge" and is judged, then the earth is renewed in "Frashokereti" at the hands of the savior (Saoshyant), and the dead are raised.
- **The two worlds, the menog and the getig**: the first, spiritual world precedes the material world created by the deity, and matter is not evil in itself.

## Presence in Arabic Sources

The Arabs wrote about it early. Ibn al-Nadim made Zoroaster (Zaradusht or Zaratusht) the founder of Magianism and described the "Abastaq" and its books in the *Fihrist* (377 AH). Al-Shahrastani presented the Magians in *al-Milal wa al-Nihal*, among them the Kayumarthiyya, the Zurwaniyya, the Zoroastrians and the Mazdakites, and counted them among the adherents of two principles; al-Biruni devoted chapters of *al-Athar al-Baqiya* to the Persian calendars and their festivals. Ibn al-Muqaffa learned Persian and translated into Arabic the *Khwaday-namag* and *Kalila wa Dimna*, and was accused of heresy (zandaqa). The jurists counted the Magians as having a semblance of a scripture and treated them as dhimmis, and the theologians debated them on the question of dualism: the theologians, among them the Mu'tazila, replied to the dualists, and Ibn Hazm wrote refutations of the Magians in *al-Fisal*. In *Hikmat al-Ishraq*, Suhrawardi carried over the language of lights and the dominating lights, and named among the ancient sages the "Khusrawaniyyun", which is one sign of the extension of Iranian influence.

## The Sasanian Era

In the Sasanian era (224–651) Zoroastrianism became a state church; Kartir in the 3rd century introduced an organization of the clergy, and a theological school took shape, represented by Adurbad Mahraspandan in the 4th century. At the court of Khosrow I Anushirvan (531–579) there lived physicians and philosophers from among the Greeks and Syriacs, among them Burzoe the physician, and Agathias related that seven pagan philosophers took refuge at his court after the closing of the Academy of Athens in 529. Among the contentious episodes of that period was the clash of Zoroastrianism with Manichaeism and then with the movement of Mazdak (d. around 528), which called for sharing property and women, and which Khosrow I suppressed; our accounts of Mazdak come largely from hostile sources and should be read with caution.

## Influence

The Zoroastrian eschatological idea (resurrection, judgment and the savior) influenced Judaism after the Persian period from the 6th century BCE, and early Christianity and Islam, although proving direct borrowing requires caution, and the studies of Gnoli and Boyce remain a reference. One fruit of Iranian dualism is Manichaeism, founded by Mani (216–274), which is an independent religion and not a Zoroastrian one.

## Limits of the Study and Its Critique

It is difficult to date the Gathas and to compare them with the later texts, and scholars differ in distinguishing the original religion that Zoroaster called for from the picture later woven by the Sasanian priests (the Magi). At the philosophical level, there are no organized argumentative texts before the *Shkand-gumanik Vizar*, so talk of a "Zoroastrian philosophy" is a broad use of the term. The number of Zoroastrians today is about one hundred thousand or somewhat more, most of them in India (the Parsis) and Iran.
