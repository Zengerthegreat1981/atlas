# Academic Islamic Studies and the Critique of Orientalism

A dual track: on one side, the development of "Islamic studies" (Islamwissenschaft) as an organized European academic field since the late nineteenth century, which applied the tools of philological-historical criticism (borrowed from the study of the Bible) to Islamic texts and heritage; and on the other side, the radical subsequent critique that Edward Said directed at this entire field as a non-neutral body of knowledge formed within colonial power relations.

## Goldziher: Founding Academic Islamic Studies

Ignaz Goldziher, in his philological-historical critique of the hadith literature, represented the methodological founding moment of the study of Islam with tools similar to those applied to the Bible (Wellhausen, Gunkel). This method provoked a debate that continues to this day about the reliability of the traditional hadith isnad, which Joseph Schacht later developed in his study of the origins of early Islamic jurisprudence.

## The Academic Islamic Response from Within: al-A'zami

Muhammad Mustafa al-A'zami, a scholar trained in the same philological method as the Orientalists at Cambridge, devoted an entire book (1985) to refuting Schacht's theory of the "backward growth" of isnads, proposing an alternative explanation of the same historical pattern: improvement in the documentation of the isnad rather than its invention. His work represents a model of what is called the "response from within": the use of the field's own orientalist tools to defend the reliability of the isnad tradition, rather than an external rejection of the philological method as a whole. Preceding him on this path was Mustafa al-Siba'i, who, in the mid-twentieth century, decades before al-A'zami, responded to Goldziher and other Orientalists in his book *al-Sunna wa-makanatuha fi al-tashri' al-islami* (*The Sunna and Its Place in Islamic Legislation*), presenting a method of critiquing the traditional Islamic isnad as a critical tool that had preceded the Orientalist method by centuries.

## Motzki: A More Systematic Formulation within Western Scholarship Itself

The method of "isnad-cum-matn" (analysis of chain and text together) did not remain the exclusive preserve of the Islamic "response from within". The German orientalist Harald Motzki formulated it with greater systematic rigour in *The Origins of Islamic Jurisprudence: Meccan Fiqh before the Classical Schools* (German original 1991, English translation 2002), arriving at a middle position between Goldziher/Schacht and al-A'zami: some early layers of the isnad reflect a genuine transmission of legal knowledge, but Schacht's comprehensive generalization is exaggerated. This shows that the tools of the philological-historical method itself produced multiple positions, not merely a binary division between a skeptical orientalism and a defensive Islamic response.

Motzki's method did not achieve consensus even within German academic Orientalism itself. Tilman Nagel, author of *Mohammed: Leben und Legende* (2008), has taken a more sceptical view of the possibility of dating the earliest layers of the narratives, and the isnad-cum-matn approach has been debated among specialists. This shows that Motzki's tool remained a live methodological debate, not reducible to a confrontation between a uniform orientalism and a uniform Islamic response.

## Said's Critique: Orientalism as an Authoritarian Epistemic Structure

In *Orientalism* (1978), Edward Said argued that the European field of "Orientalism" as a whole, of which the study of Islam is a central part, was not merely neutral academic research but a discourse of knowledge (in the Foucauldian sense) that produced "the East" as a fixed, strange and "other" object, justifying Western colonial domination over it, even when European researchers claimed pure scientific objectivity. This critique, treated in more detail in the separate entry on Said, brought about a major shift in how the entire legacy of European Orientalism is retrospectively evaluated.

## The Middle Position: Goldziher as a Model

Goldziher represents a controversial case within Said's own critique. Although he was a structural part of the European Orientalist establishment that Said criticized, he was known for a relatively rare scholarly respect for the Islamic heritage compared with his contemporaries. This shows that Said's critique, despite its general structural force, does not eliminate individual variations within the field itself.

## Continuity and Rupture with the Early Arab Tradition

This modern track intersects with an Arab tradition several centuries older. Al-Biruni and al-Shahrastani wrote comparative descriptions of religions (Hinduism, and the *Milal wa-l-Nihal*, the religions and sects) without the colonial position that Said criticizes in modern European Orientalism. This is a historical and methodological distinction that deserves attention when comparing the two projects.

## The Arab Reception of the Critique of Orientalism

Arab criticism of Orientalism was not merely an echo of Said. Anouar Abdel-Malek preceded him with the article "Orientalism in Crisis" (*Diogenes*, 1963), which argued that Orientalism presupposes a fixed essence of the East. Najib al-Aqiqi wrote *al-Mustashriqun* (Dar al-Ma'arif, Cairo, 1964–1965) in three volumes, recording the lives of the Orientalists, their contributions and their biases, and it is one of the earliest extensive Arabic works on the subject. After Said, Hasan Hanafi, in *Muqaddima fi 'ilm al-istighrab* (Dar al-Fanniyya, 1991), called on Arab universities to study the West as the West had studied them, so that the Other becomes an object of knowledge rather than the sole source of it. In contrast, Sadik Jalal al-Azm wrote his article "Orientalism and Orientalism in Reverse" (*Khamsin*, 1981) to object that Said relies on a counter-essentialism, granting the West and the East two fixed natures, and ignoring that some Orientalists were independent critics. Aziz al-Azmeh wrote *Islams and Modernities* (Verso, 1993), criticizing essentialism in discourse about Islam among both the Orientalists and the Islamists. Thus Arab positions ranged between total rejection, selective benefit, and a critique of the critique itself.

## Post-Said Developments Within the Field

After 1978 Western Islamic studies were no longer the same. Many researchers revived the theological and legal heritage in Arabic and published critical editions and studies of manuscripts, and academic circles adopted new questions about society, gender, law and cities. Mohammed Arkoun, in *Pour une critique de la raison islamique* (*For a Critique of Islamic Reason*, 1984), argued that the Islamic heritage itself needs to apply the tools of the modern humanities, from linguistics to social history, so that this task is not left to non-Muslims alone.
