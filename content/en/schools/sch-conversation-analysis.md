# Conversation Analysis

Conversation analysis is the study of talk-in-interaction: how people organise conversation turn by turn, moment by moment, to accomplish social actions. It examines the fine detail of ordinary talk — pauses, overlaps, repairs, laughter, intonation — and shows that this detail is not noise but order. Conversation is the primary site where social life happens: relationships are built and broken, identities claimed, decisions made, and institutions enacted in talk. Conversation analysis studies that order rigorously, using recordings of naturally occurring interaction rather than invented examples or recollections of what people think they said.

## Overview

The field's premise is that interaction is orderly at every point. Speakers do not simply exchange information; they perform actions — requesting, inviting, complaining, apologising, agreeing, disagreeing — and each action makes certain responses relevant next. A question expects an answer; an invitation expects acceptance or a polite refusal; a complaint expects sympathy or defence. This sequential organisation is the field's central object of study.

Conversation analysts describe the machinery that produces this order. Turn-taking: speakers take turns with remarkably little gap or overlap, using signals in grammar, intonation, and gaze to project when a turn will end. Adjacency pairs: actions come in pairs, such as greeting and greeting, or question and answer. Repair: when trouble arises — a mishearing, a wrong word — speakers correct it quickly, usually in the same turn or the next. Preference organisation: some responses, such as accepting an invitation, are delivered directly, while others, such as refusing, are delayed, softened, and accounted for. These are not rules imposed from outside but practices that participants themselves display and orient to, moment by moment.

## History

Conversation analysis grew out of ethnomethodology, Harold Garfinkel's programme for studying the methods people use to produce social order. Garfinkel's Studies in Ethnomethodology (1967) argued that social order is an ongoing practical accomplishment, not a given, and that sociologists should study how people do it. Harvey Sacks, who had studied with Erving Goffman and worked closely with Garfinkel in California, took up that challenge for conversation, working with tape recordings of telephone calls to a suicide prevention centre and of everyday talk.

The field's founding statement was Sacks, Emanuel Schegloff, and Gail Jefferson's "A Simplest Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation" (1974), which showed that turn-taking is a systematic, locally managed system. Sacks's Lectures on Conversation, published posthumously in 1992, extended the analysis to storytelling, membership categorisation, and the moral order of talk. Schegloff developed the study of sequence organisation and repair into a systematic programme, summarised in Sequence Organization in Interaction (2007). John Heritage's Garfinkel and Ethnomethodology (1984) connected the field to its ethnomethodological roots. Ian Hutchby and Robin Wooffitt's Conversation Analysis (1998) made the approach accessible to students, and from the 1980s onwards the field expanded from ordinary conversation into institutional settings — courtrooms, clinics, classrooms, helplines, and news interviews.

## Key Figures and Works

Garfinkel provided the philosophical foundation: social order is produced by members' methods, and those methods are available for study. Sacks invented the field, showing that recordings of ordinary talk contain a hidden order worth describing in detail, and that a single episode can reveal general machinery. Schegloff systematised that machinery, above all in the study of repair and sequence, and insisted on grounding every claim in the participants' own displayed understanding.

Jefferson, who developed the transcription system that renders pauses, overlap, and intonation visible on the page, made the field's data analysable — her conventions are now widely used across the field. Heritage connected conversation analysis to ethnomethodology and to the study of institutions, showing how medical consultations and political interviews have their own interactional fingerprints. Hutchby and Wooffitt consolidated the field as a teachable method with applications across sociology, psychology, linguistics, and communication.

## Methods

Conversation analysis has a distinctive method. Researchers record naturally occurring interaction — audio or video — and transcribe it in fine detail, including pauses measured in tenths of a second, overlaps, and changes in pitch and volume. Analysis proceeds through collections: many instances of a phenomenon, such as other-initiated repair, are gathered and compared to identify the practice and its variations. Claims are validated by showing that participants themselves orient to the pattern — for example, that a speaker treats a delayed response as a refusal in the making. The approach is resolutely empirical and deliberately avoids imposing external categories on the data before examining what participants do.

## Influence and Debates

Conversation analysis has influenced sociology, linguistics, psychology, and communication studies, and its findings are applied in medical training, mediation, and the design of human-computer interfaces. Its insistence on naturalistic data has raised standards of evidence across the study of interaction.

Debates include the relationship between conversation analysis and discourse analysis, which shares an interest in talk but admits broader cultural and political interpretation; the question of context, with critics arguing that the field attends too narrowly to the talk itself and too little to the social structures surrounding it; and the challenge of quantification and generalisation. New frontiers include video calls, texting, and interaction with voice assistants and chatbots. Researchers have studied video-mediated interaction in workplaces (for example Heath and Luff, *Technology in Action*, 2000) and the use of voice assistants in family homes (Porcheron, Fischer, Reeves and Sharples, 2018). Cross-linguistic work, such as Stivers and colleagues' comparison of turn-taking timing in ten languages (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 2009), has found both a shared tendency to avoid overlap and cultural variation in response timing. The field's central claim remains: social order is built turn by turn, and close attention to talk reveals how.
