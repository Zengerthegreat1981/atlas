# Contact Linguistics: Pidgins & Creoles

A branch of linguistics that studies languages arising from intensive contact between speakers of mutually unintelligible languages, especially in contexts of colonialism, trade and slavery, and what these emerging languages reveal about the limits of human linguistic capacity.

## From Pidgin to Creole

A **pidgin** is a simplified communication language with limited vocabulary and grammar, which arises when speakers of several languages need limited practical communication (such as trade) without any of them being a native language for anyone. On the classical account, when a generation of children acquires a pidgin as a first language, the language expands into a **creole**. That abrupt scenario is disputed. Expanded pidgins, gradual nativization and ordinary language change have all been proposed as routes to the languages called creoles.

## The Bioprogram Hypothesis

In the 1970s Derek Bickerton proposed a controversial hypothesis: creole languages around the world share certain grammatical features (despite the different languages they derive from) because children, when they lack a sufficiently rich grammatical input from the impoverished pidgin they hear, "impose" a default grammatical structure from their internal linguistic faculty. This has been taken as a possible (disputed) piece of evidence for the innateness of grammatical capacity.

## Historical Context

Early scientific interest in contact languages goes back to the German linguist Hugo Schuchardt in the late nineteenth century, who collected field data on "Creole" languages in the Portuguese and Dutch colonies. The study of language contact was given a modern framework by Uriel Weinreich's *Languages in Contact* (1953), and pidgin and creole studies took shape as a field through the conferences held in Jamaica in 1959 and 1968, and the work of scholars such as David DeCamp and, later, John Holm. Most of the well-known creole languages (Haitian Creole, Jamaican Creole, Tok Pisin in Papua New Guinea, Mauritian Creole) arose in the contexts of the slave trade and European colonialism between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, when enslaved speakers of multiple African languages, who were often mixed with speakers of different languages, had to communicate through a simplified intermediary language built on the vocabulary of the colonizer's language (French, English, Portuguese).

## Competing Hypotheses on Origins

Alongside Bickerton's bioprogram hypothesis, other explanations of creole origins compete. The **Substrate/Superstrate Hypothesis** attributes certain grammatical features of creoles to the influence of the original African languages of their first speakers (the substrate), even though their vocabulary is built mainly on the colonizer's language (the superstrate). The **Relexification Hypothesis**, developed by Claire Lefebvre, holds that the first creole speakers retained the grammatical and semantic structure of their original African language, replacing only its lexical material with words from the colonizer's language. Salikoko Mufwene offered an "ecological-evolutionary" approach (Language Ecology), viewing the emergence of creole as a natural evolutionary process from a "feature pool" in which structures from all the contact languages compete, with selection decided by demographic and social factors (such as the order in which each group arrived, which he called the "Founder Principle") rather than by a single innate mechanism.

## The Creole Continuum and Ongoing Change

In many societies, creoles do not remain an isolated system; they continue to be in contact with the colonizer's language (or its official successor). This produces a "continuum" (Creole Continuum) of gradually shifting forms between the "authentic" creole (Basilect) and the standard colonial language (Acrolect), passing through intermediate forms (Mesolect), in a process known as "decreolization," which David DeCamp studied in Jamaica.

## Critique of Creole Exceptionalism

Michel DeGraff has criticized "creole exceptionalism": the assumption, which he traces in part to colonial description, that creoles are grammatically simpler or less developed than other languages, or that they require a special theory of origin. He argues that they change by the same kinds of processes as other languages. The argument is widely discussed and not universally accepted.

## Impact

Contact languages have been cited, still controversially, as cases in which a new community language can stabilize over a small number of generations. Peter Mühlhäusler's history of Tok Pisin is a standard reference for one such development, from a nineteenth-century Pacific pidgin to a language of Papua New Guinea with millions of users, most of them second-language speakers. It has influenced debates on language acquisition and innateness, and our understanding of linguistic diversity in post-colonial societies. Its tools have also extended to the study of "mixed languages" (Mixed Languages) of bilingual origin, documented by researchers such as Peter Bakker and Yaron Matras.

## Lexical Borrowing and Mixed Languages

Contact linguistics also intersects with the study of "lexical borrowing" (Lexical Borrowing) between neighboring languages without the emergence of a fully new system. This phenomenon is less radical than creolization but widespread in almost all living languages. Researchers such as Peter Bakker, Yaron Matras and Maarten Mous have also studied rare cases of "mixed languages" of genuine bilingual origin, in which the vocabulary of the new language is derived largely from one language and much of its grammatical system from another (such as Michif, a language of the Canadian Métis that combines French-derived noun phrases with Cree-derived verbs). These cases are structurally different from ordinary creoles in how linguistic components are distributed between the two source languages.

## Tok Pisin: A Model Case Study

Tok Pisin, one of the official languages of Papua New Guinea and its main lingua franca, is among the most thoroughly documented cases of creolization in the literature. Peter Mühlhäusler traced its development from a nineteenth-century trade pidgin to a language now used by millions of people in Papua New Guinea, mostly as a second language, with a smaller number of first-language speakers. The record is detailed enough that competing accounts of creolization regularly use it, without agreeing on which account it supports.
