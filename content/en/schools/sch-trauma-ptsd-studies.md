# Trauma and PTSD Studies

This field brings together research on the psychological effects of exposure to events that threaten life or shatter the sense of safety. It runs from the first systematic studies in Paris and Berlin in the late nineteenth century to the inclusion of post-traumatic stress disorder in the American diagnostic manual in 1980, and then in the eleventh revision of the World Health Organization's classification.

## Premodern Arab Medical Views of Fear and Catastrophe

Physicians in the Galenic tradition inherited by Arab-Islamic medicine counted the "psychic affections," namely anger, fear, grief, and excessive joy, among the necessary causes of health and disease. These could give rise to illness if they became severe and the temperament changed. Ibn Sina (Avicenna) is credited with an account of a sheep kept near a wolf, which grew thin and died despite being fed, and this was cited as evidence of the effect of fear on the body. Dols's study of the madman in medieval Islamic society notes that calamity and sorrow were regarded as causes of mental disorder. These are general observations about the effect of intense emotion on the body. They are not a description of post-traumatic stress disorder in its contemporary sense. The modern psychiatric definition of trauma, as an event that overwhelms the capacity of the mind to adapt, remains in memory, and returns in the form of intrusion, is a product of modern psychiatry.

## Foundations: Janet, Oppenheim, and Trench Warfare

In 1889 Hermann Oppenheim named the disorders that followed railway accidents "traumatic neurosis." In the same year Pierre Janet published *L'Automatisme psychologique*, in which he proposed that traumatic experiences are stored as fixed ideas separate from consciousness, and return in the form of attacks and recollection. Janet's work remained on the margins of the twentieth century, overshadowed by Freudian analysis, until it was rediscovered in the 1980s; van der Kolk and van der Hart wrote on it in 1989. At the Salpêtrière, Charcot's school linked hysteria to trauma. In the First World War, Charles Myers described "shell shock" in *The Lancet* in 1915. Physicians disputed whether its origin was organic or psychological, and Rivers and others treated officers through conversation. In 1920 Freud, in *Beyond the Pleasure Principle*, discussed the compulsion to repeat seen in the dreams of war neurotics. Abram Kardiner later published *The Traumatic Neuroses of War* in 1941, based on his work with combat veterans, in which he described chronic fear and its bodily response.

## Clinical Recognition in 1980

After the Vietnam War, the psychiatrists Robert Jay Lifton and Chaim Shatan worked with groups of veterans who demanded recognition of their suffering. In 1973 Lifton, in *Home from the War*, recorded what he called the long-term effects of exposure to death. In the same period, Ann Burgess and Lynda Holmstrom described "rape trauma syndrome" in 1974, which brought sexual violence into the field. In 1980 post-traumatic stress disorder was included in the third edition of the diagnostic and statistical manual (DSM-III), with symptoms grouped as re-experiencing, numbing, and other symptoms such as hyperalertness. DSM-IV (1994) reorganized them into intrusion, avoidance, and hyperarousal. DSM-5 (2013) added a fourth cluster, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and DSM-5-TR (2022) kept that structure. In 1976 Mardi Horowitz had formulated, in *Stress Response Syndromes*, a model in which intrusion and denial alternate. Bessel van der Kolk began working with Vietnam veterans in Boston in the late 1970s, took part in the DSM-IV field trial for the disorder, and in 2014 published *The Body Keeps the Score*. Survey studies in the United States, including Kessler and colleagues' study of 1995, then showed that the disorder is common in the general population and not confined to soldiers.

## Explanatory Models and Treatment

In 1986 Foa and Kozak developed the theory of emotional processing of fear, which founded prolonged exposure therapy. In 2000 Ehlers and Clark published the cognitive model, which holds that persistent disorder follows from a threatening self-appraisal of the event and poorly organized memory. In 1995 van der Kolk and Fisler linked trauma with the fragmentation of memory, and research turned to how sensory and emotional memories are encoded apart from verbal narrative. Reviews, including the study by Brewin, Andrews, and Valentine in 2000, found that the severity of the event, lack of social support, and childhood trauma are among the strongest risk factors. Evidence-based treatments include trauma-focused cognitive behavioral therapy, prolonged exposure therapy, and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (Shapiro, 1989). Some medications, such as serotonin reuptake inhibitors, are also used in treatment guidelines. This entry is educational and descriptive, not clinical advice.

## Complex Trauma and the Dialectics of Memory

In *Trauma and Recovery* (1992) Judith Herman extended the field when she proposed a syndrome of "complex post-traumatic stress disorder" to describe the long-term effects of prolonged, repeated trauma. She described the "dialectics of trauma," in which the survivor swings between acute intrusion, numbing, and withdrawal. She called for treatment in three stages: safety, then remembrance and mourning, then reconnection with life. This diagnosis was included in the eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases, while it remained outside the American manual. In the 1990s the debate over "recovered memories" of childhood intensified between clinicians and experimental memory researchers, such as Elizabeth Loftus, who showed that memory is susceptible to suggestion.

## Limits and Critique

In *The Harmony of Illusions* (1995), Allan Young criticized the diagnosis as a historical construct that changes with social conditions. In 2001 Derek Summerfield, writing in the *BMJ*, argued that turning the suffering of war into a medical category ignores political and cultural dimensions, especially in societies affected by conflict. Others hold that this critique does not negate the real need of survivors for care. Reviews question the independence of the first criterion, which assumes that the event is objectively threatening while the response varies between individuals and cultures. The field is an umbrella for several research traditions and is not a single theory.
