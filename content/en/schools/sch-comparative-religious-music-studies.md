# Comparative Study of Religious and Sacred Music

A subfield within comparative religious studies that examines music and ritual sound (chant, hymns, recitation, ritual rhythm) as a medium of religious experience compared across traditions. It is neither a purely formal musicology nor a musical theology written from within a single tradition.

## Founding: Guy Beck and the Project of "Sonic Theology"

The American scholar Guy L. Beck, a scholar of Hinduism who has taught at Tulane University, helped articulate the field with his book *Sonic Theology: Hinduism and Sacred Sound* (University of South Carolina Press, 1993). In it he examines sacred sound (nada, mantra, Vedic chanting) as a theological category in Hinduism rather than as a mere ritual accompaniment. In other words, for some Hindu schools the sound itself, not the content of the words alone, carries cosmic power.

## Method: Sound as Comparative Theology

Beck later developed his approach in an edited volume (*Sacred Sound*, 2006) that extends the comparison to several major traditions, including Hinduism, Buddhism, Islam, Christianity, Judaism and Sikhism. He proposes that studying how religion is heard, and not only how it is thought about or seen in ritual, reveals latent theological structures that textual or ritual analysis alone would miss. This overlaps with the tradition of comparative ethnomusicology (Curt Sachs, early twentieth century), but it redirects the question from "how is music built formally?" to "what does sound do theologically?"

## Relevance to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

This framework opens a wide field for studying sacred sound in the Arab-Islamic heritage in comparative perspective. Qur'anic recitation, with the rules of tajwid and the maqams (bayati, hijaz, rast), represents a precise sound system developed over centuries to produce a performance that balances legal correctness with aesthetic effect. Scholars have argued (as in the work of Kristina Nelson) that the sharp distinction between recitation (tied to the mushaf and its strict rules) and singing (used in praise of the Prophet and in Sufi chanting) reflects a theological hierarchy of sound. This closely resembles the Hindu distinction that Beck studied between sacred sound (nada/mantra) and ordinary aesthetic music. The Islamic call to prayer (adhan), with its globally uniform form and its local modal variations, is also a comparative sound phenomenon that this entry does not treat in depth.

## Impact and Criticism

The field has broadened comparative religious studies to include the sense of hearing alongside text, image and visual ritual. Yet it remains practically confined to a limited number of English-speaking researchers. It has not yet adequately absorbed the contributions of researchers from within the traditions studied (such as Muslim scholars of tajwid or local musicologists in India), a methodological gap.

## Notable Comparative Applications

Researchers in this field compare the many functions of sound across traditions. Sanskrit Vedic chanting requires strict phonetic precision, since any error in pronunciation is believed to void the ritual efficacy of the mantra. Qur'anic recitation likewise requires precise tajwid that preserves both meaning and sound. Gregorian chant is unaccompanied monophonic song set to a Latin liturgical text. This convergence among traditions that are geographically and doctrinally distant, on the importance of vocal precision for the religious text, supports Beck's central thesis: in many traditions around the world, sound itself, and not only abstract linguistic meaning, carries theological weight.

## Contemporary Issues: Recording and Global Spread

Modern sound recording has changed the nature of the relationship to sacred sound in many traditions. The spread, first by radio and then by the internet, of recordings of leading Egyptian reciters (such as Sheikh Abd al-Basit Abd al-Samad and Sheikh Muhammad Rifat) gave one particular recitation style worldwide reach. It became an audible standard that reciters elsewhere emulate. This reshapes the vocal authority of recitation globally in ways that were not possible before the age of recording. The field studies this phenomenon as a modern extension of its founding question about the authority of religious sound and its transmission across time and space.
