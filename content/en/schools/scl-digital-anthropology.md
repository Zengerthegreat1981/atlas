# Digital Anthropology

Digital anthropology studies how people make social life with and around digital technologies: computers, mobile phones, social media, platforms, games, software, and data systems. It asks what these tools become in particular households, workplaces, and political settings, rather than assuming that a technology has the same effects everywhere. The field includes research conducted online, research on online worlds, and long-term fieldwork about the place of devices in everyday life.

## Formation of the field

Early studies in the 1990s challenged the idea of a separate "cyberspace". Daniel Miller and Don Slater's *The Internet: An Ethnographic Approach* (2000), based on research in Trinidad, showed users fitting the internet into existing concerns about family, religion, business, and national identity. Other researchers treated online settings as field sites in their own right. Christine Hine's *Virtual Ethnography* (2000) examined what ethnography could mean when interaction is mediated. Tom Boellstorff's *Coming of Age in Second Life* (2008) conducted participant observation inside a virtual world on that world's own terms.

## Themes

Research on mobile phones, including work by Heather Horst and Miller in Jamaica (2006), traced how devices alter kin ties, money flows, and the management of distance. Studies of free software, such as Gabriella Coleman's *Coding Freedom* (2012), described the ethical and legal commitments of programmers. Ilana Gershon's work on new media examined the ideas people hold about which medium is proper for a given message. The comparative project Why We Post, led by Miller, placed researchers in field sites in several countries and argued that social media content differs markedly between places. Later work has turned to platform labour, content moderation, algorithms, surveillance, and the infrastructure of cables, servers, and electricity on which digital life depends.

## Methods and ethics

Fieldwork often moves between screens and physical settings, since either one alone can mislead. An account name does not reveal who is typing, and an interview may not show what happens in a private group. Researchers combine participant observation, interviews, the study of interfaces, and attention to company rules and technical design. Material that is technically visible is not necessarily offered for research, so consent, anonymity, searchability of quotations, and the protection of data need decisions case by case.

## Debates

One debate concerns whether digital life is a new object requiring new theory or another domain in which familiar questions of exchange, kinship, language, and power arise. A second concerns the balance between users' creativity and the control exercised by corporations and states. Ethnography can show how people work around designed constraints, but a local account may understate decisions taken far from the field site. Access is also uneven by income, gender, language, disability, and geography, so claims about a connected world need to specify who is connected and on what terms. Rapid change in services dates particular findings, which makes careful description of period and place important.
