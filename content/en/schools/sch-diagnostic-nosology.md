# Psychiatric Diagnostic Nosology

Psychiatric nosology is the field that classifies mental disorders and systematically sets out their diagnostic criteria so that clinicians can apply them consistently. It arose as an attempt to move psychiatric diagnosis from an individual clinical impression to a system of agreed categories, and it then developed through a century and a half of theoretical shifts: from Kraepelin's descriptive classification to the American (DSM) and international (ICD) manuals, and on to contemporary attempts to move beyond fixed categories toward dimensional models such as RDoC and HiTOP.

## Origins and Context

In his textbook *Psychiatrie: Ein Lehrbuch*, which went through nine editions between 1883 and 1927 (the eighth, in 1909-1915, is the best known), the German physician Emil Kraepelin laid the modern foundation of psychiatric classification. He distinguished "manic-depressive insanity" from "dementia praecox" (the conceptual precursor of schizophrenia), relying on the course of the illness and its long-term outcome rather than on momentary symptoms alone. This descriptive method, based on systematic clinical observation, became the model on which the later official classification systems were built.

The American Psychiatric Association published its first Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-I) in 1952, influenced by psychoanalysis and by the concept of "reaction" formulated by Adolf Meyer. DSM-II followed in 1968. The decisive shift came with DSM-III in 1980 under the leadership of Robert Spitzer, which replaced the psychoanalytic framework with specific descriptive diagnostic criteria (checklist criteria), free as far as possible of causal assumptions, in an effort to raise diagnostic reliability among clinicians. DSM-III-R (1987), DSM-IV (1994, with Allen Frances chairing the task force) and DSM-IV-TR (2000) followed, then DSM-5 in 2013, and its revised version DSM-5-TR in 2022. DSM-5-TR introduced limited textual and criterial updates without a major structural change in most categories, along with a new diagnosis: Prolonged Grief Disorder.

In parallel, the World Health Organization developed the chapter on mental and behavioural disorders within the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), leading to ICD-11, which the World Health Assembly adopted in 2019 and which formally came into effect in January 2022. ICD-11 takes a more dimensional approach than its predecessors, especially in its classification of personality disorders, using a single severity model instead of the ten categorical patterns that DSM-5-TR retains in its core text.

## Foundations

- **Descriptive (atheoretical) approach**: Since DSM-III, disorders have been defined by clusters of symptoms and by criteria for duration, severity, and functional impact, rather than by prior assumptions about cause.
- **Categorical versus dimensional**: DSM-5-TR and ICD-11 divide most disorders into separate categories (present or absent), while alternative models propose measuring symptoms on a continuum of severity.
- **Research Domain Criteria (RDoC)**: Developed by the US National Institute of Mental Health from about 2009-2010 as a research framework, not a clinical diagnostic tool, RDoC links symptoms to neurobehavioral functional dimensions (such as emotional valence or working memory) across levels of analysis from genes to behavior, going beyond the boundaries of existing diagnostic categories.
- **Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology (HiTOP)**: A collaborative research initiative whose founding publications began to appear around 2017. It organizes symptoms and psychological traits into a hierarchy of empirical dimensions (such as internalizing and externalizing), statistically derived from the patterns of correlation among symptoms, as a complementary alternative to discrete categories.
- **Diagnostic reliability versus validity**: The original motive for the DSM-III revision was to raise agreement among clinicians on the same diagnosis. The ongoing debate is whether these categories reflect real, causally valid disease entities or are merely procedural agreements.

## Limits

Allen Frances, who supervised DSM-IV itself, criticized the widening of diagnostic thresholds in DSM-5. In his book *Saving Normal* (2013), he warned of the risk of "diagnostic inflation" and of turning ordinary human experiences into medical disorders. David Rosenhan's well-known experiment, "On Being Sane in Insane Places" (1973), exposed the fragility of diagnostic criteria before the DSM-III era. In it, healthy volunteers admitted themselves to psychiatric hospitals claiming fictitious symptoms and were detained for weeks, although they behaved normally afterward. The methodology and findings of that study have since been questioned. Reliability data from the DSM-5 field trials (Regier and colleagues, 2013) were uneven: test-retest agreement was low for major depressive disorder (kappa about 0.28) and generalized anxiety disorder (about 0.20) and higher for some other diagnoses, which fed debate about the criteria. In addition, the sharp categorical separation between disorders does not reflect the high rates of comorbidity seen in clinical practice, which has prompted alternative dimensional models such as HiTOP and RDoC. The latter, however, remain research frameworks that have not been adopted as alternative clinical diagnostic systems.
