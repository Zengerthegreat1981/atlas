# Legalism (Fajia)

A Chinese political-intellectual current that flourished in the fourth and third centuries BCE in the Warring States period. It is called in the Chinese sources "Fajia" (法家, the School of Law), a name given by the historian Sima Tan (father of Sima Qian) in the second century BCE. It made the strict organization of a strong, unified state its highest goal, and relied on promulgated law (fa), administrative techniques (shu) and positional power (shi) rather than the cultivation of virtue. The English rendering "Legalism" is partly misleading because its first concern is political and administrative, not juridical. This thought supported the state that unified China in 221 BCE (Qin), and was then of ill repute for centuries.

## Key Figures

- **Shang Yang** (c. 390–338 BCE): a minister in the state of Qin under Duke Xiao, who implemented reforms in 356 and 350 BCE, abolishing the privileges of the hereditary aristocracy, distributing ranks on the basis of military merit, making families collectively responsible for one another, and encouraging agriculture and war; the *Book of Lord Shang* is attributed to him. His opponents killed him after the death of his patron.
- **Shen Buhai** (d. 337 BCE): a minister in the state of Han, an influential theorist of the art of administration (shu), that is, methods of appointing and verifying officials.
- **Shen Dao** (c. 350–275 BCE): focused on "position" (shi), that is, the power that office gives the ruler, rather than personal virtue.
- **Han Fei** (c. 280–233 BCE): a prince of the state of Han and a student of Xunzi, author of the *Han Feizi*, in which he combined the three pillars (law, technique and power) into a system, and was influenced by Daoism in his concepts of the non-acting ruler (the chapters "Jie Lao" and "Yu Lao"). He died in a Qin prison in 233 BCE after being denounced by his fellow student Li Si.
- **Li Si** (c. 280–208 BCE): also a student of Xunzi, he became adviser to the emperor Qin Shi Huang and prime minister, established the system of commanderies and prefectures and the standardization of writing and measures, and in 213 BCE proposed the burning of private copies of the classical books. He was executed in 208 BCE under the Second Emperor.

## Central Ideas

- **Human nature**: the current proceeds from the view that people are moved by self-interest, seeking profit and avoiding loss, so moral virtue cannot be relied on in managing the state. Here it agrees with Xunzi that nature is bad, but differs from him in not relying on education.
- **Law (fa)**: written, promulgated rules applied to everyone equally, with fixed rewards and punishments. Han Fei says that "the ruler holds two handles", namely reward and punishment (erbing).
- **Technique (shu)**: the ruler's techniques for matching "names" (offices and statements) with "forms" (actual performance) (xingming), and for preventing ministers from manipulating him by concealing his desires.
- **Position (shi)**: the ruler's power comes from his office, not from his person, so even a mediocre ruler can rule if he preserves the mechanism.
- **The non-acting ruler**: he leaves execution to the administrative apparatus and keeps supervision and the final decision.
- **Critique of appeal to the past**: the story of "waiting for the hare" in the *Han Feizi* mocks rigid adherence to the old, and with it the famous story of "the spear and the shield" on logical contradiction.
- **Narrowing intellectual competition**: it held that philosophers, merchants and artisans are "vermin" that weaken agriculture and the army, and so proposed subjecting thought to the benefit of the state.

## History and Reception

- The state of Qin collapsed in 206 BCE after the death of the first emperor, its opponents accused it of cruelty, and this became the verdict of Confucian historians on the current.
- The Han dynasty in practice adopted many Qin institutions under a Confucian cover, and the relation is often summed up in the modern formula "Confucian on the outside, Legalist on the inside"; the dynasty's own ideology is better described as a mixture of the "kingly" and "hegemonic" ways. The "Salt and Iron" debate (81 BCE) took place between the Confucians and the supporters of state economic policy.
- In the modern period Chinese reformers rehabilitated the current as a method for building a strong state, and Mao Zedong praised it in the "Criticize Lin Biao and Confucius" campaign (1973–1974); interest in it then returned in contemporary China.
- Western scholars compare it with Machiavelli and Hobbes, but the Chinese text does not know a contract theory and retains a moral conception of order.

## Limits and Critique

- Scholars such as Paul Goldin (2011) hold that the term "Legalism" is a later creation and that the texts of the figures do not form a single school, being varied strands that Han Fei later synthesized.
- Pines (2009) argued that the thought was not so much against morality as in search of a system that works regardless of the ruler's morality, and that it recognizes the power of office over individuals.
- Its critics charged that it makes the human being an instrument of the state and lacks any mechanism for restraining the ruler himself.
