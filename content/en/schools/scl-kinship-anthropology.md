# Kinship Studies

Kinship studies is the branch of anthropology that asks how people become related to one another — by birth, by marriage, by adoption, by shared substance, or by choice — and what those relations do in social life. For more than a century it was anthropology's central subject, the field in which its theories were made and tested: to understand a society, anthropologists believed, you first had to understand its kinship system, because kinship organized who could marry whom, who inherited land, who owed whom loyalty, and who counted as a person. Kinship studies is therefore not a marginal specialism but the tradition that defined social anthropology itself, and its history is the history of the discipline's changing ideas about what a relationship is.

## Origins and History

The field began with Lewis Henry Morgan, an American lawyer and ethnographer who collected kinship terminologies from Native American peoples and published Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family in 1871. Morgan showed that the words people use for relatives — whether they call their mother's brother by the same word as their father, for example — fall into a small number of systems, and he argued that those systems recorded stages in the evolution of the family. His work made kinship the first subject of comparative anthropology. In Britain, W. H. R. Rivers turned kinship into a field method: his genealogical method, developed on the Torres Strait expedition of 1898 and set out in his work on the Todas, used the collection of family histories to map social organization. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown then made kinship the core of structural-functional theory. In The Social Organization of Australian Tribes (1931) he showed that Aboriginal kinship systems were not exotic curiosities but precise social machines, classifying every person in the community and assigning rights and duties through that classification.

The second great synthesis came from Claude Lévi-Strauss, whose Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) argued that the essence of kinship was not descent but alliance: societies are built by the exchange of women in marriage, and the rules of who may marry whom — the incest taboo and its variations — create the bonds between groups. Lévi-Strauss's alliance theory stood alongside the British descent-based tradition and its great ethnographies of kinship and social structure: Meyer Fortes on the Tallensi of Ghana, E. E. Evans-Pritchard on the Nuer of Sudan, and, with a critical eye on both approaches, Edmund Leach on the Kachin of Burma.

## Key Figures and Works

The field's canon is the canon of classical anthropology. Morgan's Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity (1871) founded it; Radcliffe-Brown's The Social Organization of Australian Tribes (1931) made it structural; Lévi-Strauss's Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) made it a theory of exchange. Meyer Fortes's The Web of Kinship Among the Tallensi (1949) showed kinship as a lived moral order rather than a diagram, and his work on the "developmental cycle" of domestic groups, developed further in a 1958 volume edited by Jack Goody, traced how families grow and divide over time. The field's great crisis came from within: David Schneider, in A Critique of the Study of Kinship (1984), argued that "kinship" was not a universal human reality but a Western folk category — the idea that blood ties are natural — that anthropologists had projected onto other peoples. Schneider's critique threw the field into doubt, but it also helped remake it. The "new kinship studies" that followed, led by Janet Carsten's After Kinship (2004) and by Marilyn Strathern's work on reproductive technology, studies relatedness rather than kinship: how people in Malaysia become kin by sharing food and a house, how donor conception creates new kinds of relatives in Britain, and how same-sex couples build families. The field now studies adoption, surrogacy, migration, and the family forms created by biotechnology.

## Method

Kinship studies gave anthropology several of its basic methods. The genealogical method — collecting family histories person by person — remains the starting point of fieldwork, and the kinship diagram, with its triangles, circles, and lines of descent and marriage, is the discipline's oldest visual tool. Classical method was structural: the anthropologist mapped terminologies, traced descent groups, and worked out the logic of marriage rules. The new kinship studies added ethnography of the everyday: observing who feeds whom, who lives with whom, and how people talk about being related, rather than assuming that a diagram explains a life. The method is comparative in a new sense: rather than sorting societies into types, it asks how different peoples imagine connection itself — through blood, through shared substance, through naming, or through care — and what those imaginations reveal about personhood.

## Influence and Legacies

Kinship studies shaped every other branch of anthropology. Its concepts — descent, alliance, exogamy, the incest taboo — became the vocabulary of social theory, and its ethnographies trained generations of anthropologists to think structurally. Its influence extended into sociology, law, and feminist theory, where the study of the family drew on anthropological work. Schneider's critique had consequences far beyond kinship: it fed into the reflexive turn in anthropology, the argument that the discipline's categories are themselves cultural products. Today the field is one of anthropology's most active areas, because the questions it asks — who counts as family, what makes a parent, how new technologies of reproduction change relatedness — are the questions of contemporary public life. From Morgan's charts of Iroquois terminology to ethnographies of surrogate motherhood, kinship studies has never stopped asking the same question: by what ties are people bound to one another, and what do those ties make possible?
