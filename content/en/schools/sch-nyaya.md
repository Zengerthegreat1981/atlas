# Nyāya (Classical Indian Logic)

Nyāya ("rule" or "method") is an "orthodox" Indian school that made the examination of the instruments of valid knowledge and the rules of debate its foundation, and joined to this a metaphysical realism affirming the existence of the external world, the individual self, and a creator God. It is a major source of logical and epistemological analysis in Indian philosophy; the other schools adopted it or debated it. It is presented here from the classical period to the eleventh century, that is, from the *Nyāya Sūtra* to Udayana, while the later technical development beginning with Gaṅgeśa belongs to Navya-Nyāya.

## Texts and Figures

Tradition attributes the *Nyāya Sūtra* to Akṣapāda Gotama, but scholars hold that it is a cumulative text in five books, which reached its known form around the second century CE, with older layers. Among the most prominent commentators: Vātsyāyana (around the fifth century), author of the *Nyāya Bhāṣya*, the oldest surviving commentary; Uddyotakara (sixth or seventh century) in the *Nyāya Vārttika*, which defended Nyāya against the Buddhist Dignāga; Vācaspati Miśra (ninth century) in the *Tātparyaṭīkā*; and Jayanta Bhaṭṭa (ninth century, Kashmir) in the *Nyāya Mañjarī*. Udayana (around 1000) wrote "the examination of the self" (*Ātmatattvaviveka*) in reply to Buddhism, and the "Bouquet of Nyāya" (*Nyāya Kusumāñjali*), which contains arguments for the existence of God; and Bhāsarvajña (tenth century) wrote the *Nyāya Sāra*. In this period Nyāya merged with Vaiśeṣika (atomism and substances) until they became almost a single school.

## Central Ideas

- **The sixteen categories (padārtha)**: the book opens with a list that includes the instruments of knowledge, the objects of knowledge, doubt, purpose, example, doctrine, the members, reductio ad absurdum, ascertainment, debate, disputation, wrangling, fallacies, quibbling, futile rejoinders and points of defeat; the aim of knowing them is liberation.
- **The instruments of knowledge (pramāṇa)**: four: perception (pratyakṣa), inference (anumāna), comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda), that is, the report of a reliable person, foremost the Veda.
- **Inference with five members**: the claim, the reason, the example, the application and the conclusion, as when we say: there is fire on the mountain (the claim), because it has smoke (the reason), and whatever has smoke has fire, like the kitchen (the example), and this mountain likewise has smoke (the application), so it has fire (the conclusion). They distinguish between inference for oneself and inference for convincing another.
- **Fallacies (hetvābhāsa)**: five kinds of faulty reasons, such as the contradictory reason and the inconclusive reason.
- **Realism**: the external world is real, knowledge reveals things and does not make them, universals have reality, and in perception the sense makes contact with the thing. They explain error as the attribution of a property to an object to which it does not belong (anyathākhyāti).
- **The self and God**: the self is a substance distinct from body and mind, bearing knowledge, will and pain, and Īśvara is the efficient cause of the world, whose existence is inferred from order, purpose and morality.
- **The truth of knowledge**: they said that the truth of knowledge is established from outside it by testing its results (paratah prāmāṇya), in contrast to Mīmāṃsā, which held self-validation.

## Dialogue with the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Scholars have tended to compare Nyāya and Vaiśeṣika with Islamic kalām, because atomism in it (the indivisible part, *al-jawhar al-fard*) among the Mu'tazila and the Ash'aris resembles the atoms of Vaiśeṣika. Shlomo Pines, in *Beiträge zur islamischen Atomenlehre* (1936), proposed the possibility that early Islamic kalām was influenced by Indian sources; other scholars have discussed this and it has not been settled. Udayana's proof of the existence of God also resembles in some respects certain proofs of the theologians and philosophers, though the formulation is different. Al-Biruni wrote in *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind* about the sciences of the Indians, and translated Patañjali's *Yoga Sūtra* and a book on Sāṅkhya into Arabic, and showed that the Indians build debate on principles different from those of the Greeks, but he does not present Nyāya in detail.

## Influence

Nyāya influenced all later schools through its theory of the instruments of knowledge and its art of debate, so that Buddhists such as Dignāga and Dharmakīrti were obliged to formulate their logic in dialogue with it, and the later technical school, Navya-Nyāya, developed it. Bimal Matilal presented studies on "the character of logic in India" (1998) and compared it with Greek logic, holding that the Indian is further from formal logic and closer to a practical logic tied to knowledge.

## Limits

Classical Nyāya has no symbolic system; its inference is an analogical syllogism depending on the example, which led some scholars to liken it to induction more than to deduction. The doctrine combined logical aims with soteriological and metaphysical ends, so some critics held that this mixture weakened the independence of logic. The question of the relation between five-membered inference and the Aristotelian syllogism remained a matter of dispute. Moreover, understanding the details of the school requires recourse to the commentaries, because the sūtras are terse.
