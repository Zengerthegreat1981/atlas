# Bayt al-Hikma and the Translation Movement

The translation movement that carried Greek philosophical and scientific writing (and part of the Persian and Indian) into Arabic in Baghdad, from the late Umayyad period and the beginning of the Abbasid era until the late tenth century CE, and that supplied much of the textual basis on which medieval Islamic philosophy later drew.

## Bayt al-Hikma: The Institution and the Debate Over It

The traditional account links this movement to an institution called "Bayt al-Hikma" (House of Wisdom), founded by the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid or his son al-Ma'mun in Baghdad at the beginning of the ninth century, which some later historians described as a library, an academy and an official translation centre. The classical philologist Dimitri Gutas challenged this picture in *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture* (1998). He argued that the Greek-Arabic translation movement is not described, in the historical reports about it (including the reports of Hunayn ibn Ishaq, the best-known of the translators), as work carried out inside "Bayt al-Hikma". He argues that "Bayt al-Hikma" (also called "Khizanat al-Hikma", treasury of wisdom) was a court library of the Sasanian type, a store of books and not a translation academy in the modern sense. Whatever the verdict on this debate, the translation movement itself is a historical fact documented in great detail, whether it took place within a single official institution or through a network of independent translators and patrons supported by caliphs, viziers and notables.

## Principal Figures and Currents

- **Hunayn ibn Ishaq al-'Ibadi** (d. 260 AH/873 CE): the best-known translator of this movement. He translated works of Galen and Hippocrates and others from Greek into Syriac and Arabic, often with the participation of his son Ishaq and his pupils, and he developed a rigorous method of collating several manuscript copies before translating.
- **The Banu Musa ibn Shakir**: three brothers, astronomers and wealthy engineers, who financed campaigns to collect Greek manuscripts from Constantinople and the Levant and financed translation teams.
- **Ya'qub al-Kindi** (d. c. 870 CE; the year is disputed): often described as the first philosopher of Arab descent in this movement, as distinct from Syriac Christian and Persian colleagues. He supervised a group of translations and attempted a philosophical synthesis of Greek philosophy and Islamic doctrine.
- **The Syriac route**: many Greek texts reached Arabic through Syriac, the scholarly language of East Syrian and West Syrian Christians in Iraq and the Levant. A large part of the movement was therefore a three-stage transfer, from Greek to Syriac to Arabic. Hunayn and other translators also worked directly from Greek.

## Motives and Stages

The movement did not begin with a single decision by al-Ma'mun, as is often told; it extended over nearly two centuries. Under al-Mansur (r. 754–775 CE), Persian books were rendered into Arabic, including the translations of Ibn al-Muqaffa' (d. c. 139 AH/756–757 CE) from Middle Persian (Pahlavi), such as *Kalila wa-Dimna*, and Indian and Persian astronomical texts circulated with the new capital at Baghdad. Under Harun al-Rashid (r. 786–809 CE) and al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833 CE), the transfer widened to include Greek philosophy, medicine and mathematics. Gutas offers a composite explanation: a Sasanian inheritance of translating the sciences; an Abbasid ideology that treated ancient knowledge as support for the state's legitimacy; a class of secretaries who needed arithmetic, astrology and medicine; and the spread of paper manufacture in the Islamic east during the eighth century. The story that al-Ma'mun began the movement after dreaming of Aristotle is, in Gutas's view, a later tale with an ideological point.

## Translation Methods and Generations

Later bibliographers described an early generation, including Yuhanna ibn al-Batriq, as translating word for word. Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 260 AH/873 CE), in his letter to Ali ibn Yahya al-Munajjim on the translations of Galen, described a method of gathering copies, collating them, establishing the sense, and then writing sound Arabic. His son Ishaq (d. 298 AH/910–911 CE) worked in the same manner. Thabit ibn Qurra (d. 288 AH/901 CE) and Qusta ibn Luqa (d. c. 300 AH/912 CE) followed, then Abu Bishr Matta ibn Yunus (d. 328 AH/940 CE) and Yahya ibn Adi (d. 363 AH/974 CE), in the Baghdad circle of Christian logicians where al-Farabi studied. The translations stabilized an Arabic philosophical vocabulary, including *jawhar* (substance), *'arad* (accident) and *hayula* (matter). Philosophers then moved from commentary toward books of their own. Gutas warns that the contrast between a crude early method and a free later method is itself partly a later stereotype.

## Impact

The movement translated Aristotle's logical, natural and metaphysical works, parts of Plato's works (often through summaries), Galen's medical books, and Euclid and Ptolemy in mathematics and astronomy. These became part of the material from which al-Farabi, Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd later wrote. A large part of this Arabic corpus later passed through al-Andalus into Latin in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and became one of the sources of medieval Latin philosophy and science.

## Limitations

- The dispute over Bayt al-Hikma as an actual institution or an organisational figure of speech remains open among historians, although recent research (after Gutas) leans strongly toward caution about assuming it existed as a formal academy in the form portrayed by later accounts.
- Many exact dates for the start of translating a particular text are uncertain, because of the multiple copies and repeated translations of the same work over decades.
