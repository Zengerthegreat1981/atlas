# Bayt al-Hikma and the Translation Movement

The great intellectual translation movement that carried Greek philosophical and scientific heritage (and part of the Persian and Indian) into Arabic in Baghdad, from the late Umayyad period and the beginning of the Abbasid era until the late tenth century CE, and that laid the textual foundation on which medieval Islamic philosophy later rested.

## Bayt al-Hikma: The Institution and the Debate Over It

The traditional account links this movement to an institution called "Bayt al-Hikma" (House of Wisdom), founded by the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid or his son al-Ma'mun in Baghdad at the beginning of the ninth century, which some later historians described as a library, an academy and an official translation centre. The classical philologist Dimitri Gutas, however, strongly challenged this picture in his reference work *Greek Thought, Arabic Culture* (1998). He showed that the Greek-Arabic translation movement is never mentioned, in any of the dozens of historical reports about it (including the reports of Hunayn ibn Ishaq himself, the most famous of the translators), as having taken place within "Bayt al-Hikma". He argues that "Bayt al-Hikma" (also called "Khizanat al-Hikma", treasury of wisdom) was a court library of the Sasanian type, a store of books and not a translation academy in the modern sense. Whatever the verdict on this debate, the translation movement itself is a historical fact documented in great detail, whether it took place within a single official institution or through a network of independent translators and patrons supported by caliphs, viziers and notables.

## Principal Figures and Currents

- **Hunayn ibn Ishaq al-Ibadi** (d. 873 CE): the most famous translator of this movement. He translated works of Galen and Hippocrates and others from Greek into Syriac and Arabic, often with the participation of his son Ishaq and his pupils, and he developed a rigorous method of collating several manuscript copies before translating.
- **The Banu Musa ibn Shakir**: three brothers, astronomers and wealthy engineers, who financed campaigns to collect Greek manuscripts from Constantinople and the Levant and financed translation teams.
- **Ya'qub al-Kindi** (d. c. 873 CE): the first "Arab" philosopher in the precise sense (neither Syriac nor of Persian origin) in this movement. He supervised a group of translations and made the first attempt at a philosophical synthesis between Greek philosophy and Islamic doctrine.
- **The Syriac transmission route**: many Greek texts were not translated directly into Arabic but through a Syriac intermediary (the language of the Nestorian and Jacobite Christian scholars in Iraq and the Levant). This makes the movement a three-layer transfer: Greek, then Syriac, then Arabic.

## Motives and Stages

The movement did not begin with a single decision by al-Ma'mun, as is often told; it extended over nearly two centuries. Under al-Mansur (r. 754–775 CE), Persian books were rendered into Arabic, including the translations of Ibn al-Muqaffa' (d. c. 757 CE) from Middle Persian (Pahlavi), such as *Kalila wa-Dimna*, and Indian and Persian astronomical texts accompanied the founding of Baghdad. Under Harun al-Rashid (r. 786–809 CE) and al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833 CE), the transfer widened to include Greek philosophy, medicine and mathematics. Gutas offers a composite explanation for this expansion: a Sasanian inheritance of translating sciences; an Abbasid ideology that regarded ancient knowledge as evidence of the state's legitimacy; a class of secretaries and state offices that needed arithmetic, astrology and medicine; and the spread of paper, whose manufacture spread after the year 751 CE. The famous story of al-Ma'mun's dream of seeing Aristotle is, in Gutas's view, a later tale that carries more ideological meaning than it records the beginnings of translation.

## Translation Methods and Generations

The first generation, such as Yuhanna ibn al-Batriq, followed literal translation word for word, and their texts came out heavy. Hunayn ibn Ishaq (d. 873 CE), in his letter to Ali ibn Yahya on the translations of Galen, set out a method based on gathering copies, collating them, establishing the meaning, and then formulating it in sound Arabic; his son Ishaq (d. c. 910 CE) followed his method. They were followed by Thabit ibn Qurra (d. 901 CE) and Qusta ibn Luqa (d. c. 912 CE), then Matta ibn Yunus (d. 940 CE) and Yahya ibn Adi (d. 974 CE), in the circle of Christian logicians in Baghdad in which al-Farabi was trained. This work produced a stable Arabic philosophical vocabulary, such as *al-jawhar* (substance), *al-'arad* (accident) and *al-hayula* (matter), after which philosophers moved from commentary to original composition.

## Impact

The movement translated Aristotle's logical, natural and metaphysical works, parts of Plato's works (often through summaries), Galen's medical books, and Euclid and Ptolemy in mathematics and astronomy. These became the raw material from which al-Farabi, Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd later built their medieval Islamic philosophy. A large part of this material later passed through al-Andalus into Latin in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and so became one of the sources of the European scientific and philosophical renaissance.

## Limitations

- The dispute over Bayt al-Hikma as an actual institution or an organisational figure of speech remains open among historians, although recent research (after Gutas) leans strongly toward caution about assuming it existed as a formal academy in the form portrayed by later accounts.
- Many exact dates for the start of translating a particular text are uncertain, because of the multiple copies and repeated translations of the same work over decades.
