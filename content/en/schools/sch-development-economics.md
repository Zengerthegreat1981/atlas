# Development Economics

**Development economics** is a subfield of political economy that emerged directly after the Second World War, alongside the wave of independence in Asia and Africa, to answer a question that prevailing classical and neoclassical economics was not equipped to answer with its usual tools: how do poor, largely rural economies move to modern industrial structures when they lack the capital, institutions and integrated markets that conventional theory assumed as an implicit prior condition?

## The Foundational Break with General Theory

The prevailing neoclassical economics assumed that market laws (competition, relative prices and free trade) work in the same way in every economy, whether advanced or backward, and that development would occur automatically once obstacles to the free market were removed. The pioneers of the new field, above all Gunnar Myrdal, Albert O. Hirschman and W. Arthur Lewis, argued that this assumption ignored structural features of backward economies: the duality between a modern and a traditional sector, weak financial markets, and cumulative, self-reinforcing vicious circles of backwardness. These features, they held, required analytical tools different from the mere application of the standard model to a poorer context.

## Directed Industrialisation and Planning: The Founding Generation

Paul Rosenstein-Rodan's "big push" argument and Ragnar Nurkse's analysis of vicious circles of poverty stressed coordination failures that called for simultaneous, planned investment. Lewis developed the model of the "dual sector," which assumes a surplus of low-productivity agricultural labour that can be gradually moved into a modern industrial sector without raising real wages much, thereby generating an investment surplus that finances industrialisation from within. In *The Strategy of Economic Development* (1958), Hirschman offered an alternative to the idea of "balanced growth" in planning: deliberately unbalanced growth, through pivotal investments that create bottlenecks and pressures forcing the rest of the economy to adjust and to invest in sequence. This approach exploits the scarcity of decision-making capacity in developing economies rather than dissipating it on simultaneous projects. Myrdal linked underdevelopment to circular "cumulative causation" in which poverty weakens education, which in turn perpetuates poverty, and he rejected the idea that markets move automatically towards a fair equilibrium.

## From Comprehensive Planning to Partial Experimentation

By the 1980s, the prescriptions of central planning and directed industrialisation had lost credibility, as many import-substitution programmes faltered and external debt mounted in Latin America and Africa. This opened the way for a market-oriented critique (earlier voiced by Peter Bauer, Deepak Lal and Anne Krueger, and later by William Easterly) that accused foreign aid and central planning of entrenching rent-seeking and dependence rather than solving the problem of development, and that called for more modest, market-based solutions. Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo developed a markedly different methodology in response, in a line of field experiments also associated with Michael Kremer. Instead of the broad theoretical debate over "what causes development," they tested specific, partial policies (incentives for immunisation, school attendance and similar partial interventions) using randomised controlled trials borrowed from clinical medicine. Banerjee, Duflo and Kremer received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2019 for the experimental approach to alleviating global poverty.

## Amartya Sen and the Broadening of the Meaning of Development

Amartya Sen's work marked a profound conceptual shift within the field. Instead of measuring development by growth in gross domestic product alone, Sen proposed understanding development as the expansion of the real freedoms people enjoy to live the life they have reason to value. This capability approach influenced the design of the United Nations Human Development Index, developed from 1990 under Mahbub ul Haq, with Sen among the early collaborators. Martha Nussbaum later set out a related but distinct list of central capabilities; it is not a joint theory with Sen's.

## Arab Applications and Debates

The United Nations Development Programme, in its series of Arab Human Development Reports (beginning in 2002), drew on frameworks close to Sen's approach. The 2002 report focused on three "deficits" in the Arab region (freedom, the empowerment of women and knowledge) as obstacles to development that go beyond a shortage of physical capital. Later reports in the series shifted topic, and the deficit framing was debated by regional specialists.

## The Aid Effectiveness Debate

The disagreement between Jeffrey Sachs, a proponent of a "big push" in international aid to break the poverty trap, and William Easterly, a critic of the effectiveness of large-scale aid who warns against repeating its historical failures, remains one of the most lively debates within the field to this day. It is detailed in its dedicated debate node.

## Institutions and Platforms of the Field

The institutional development of the field was tied to the work of the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund after Bretton Woods. Official development prescriptions shifted over the decades: from financing large infrastructure projects in the 1950s and 1960s, to "structural adjustment" programmes in the 1980s and 1990s, reflecting the market-oriented turn against planning, and then, by the 2000s, to financing partial interventions designed according to evidence from randomised trials. This real shift in official financing was accompanied by a parallel academic debate over who holds the epistemic authority to define "successful development" in the first place.

## Internal and External Criticism

The field faced two distinct critiques. The first came from within the Latin American structuralist school and dependency theory, which argued that the prevailing development economics ignored the structure of unequal exchange between the centre and the periphery of the world economy. The second, a later methodological critique from within the field itself, questions whether small randomised trials can generalise their conclusions to national macroeconomic policy. Its proponents argue that what succeeds in a specific village may not explain why an entire country grows or stagnates.
