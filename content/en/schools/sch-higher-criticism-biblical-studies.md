# Higher Criticism and Critical Biblical Studies

An academic current that emerged in German universities in the nineteenth century. It applies the tools of philological and historical analysis to the texts of the Old Testament (the Hebrew Tanakh) and the New Testament in order to determine their sources, the dates of their composition and their supposed authors. It does so without assuming or rejecting their direct divine revelation, treating them as historical texts analyzable with the same tools of the field that are applied to any other ancient text. The term "higher criticism" is set against "lower criticism" (textual criticism), which compares manuscripts to recover the original wording of the text. Higher criticism asks about the author, date, sources and context.

## Critical Study of Scripture in the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Muslims preceded European scholars by centuries in subjecting the texts of the People of the Book to internal examination, although their purpose was polemical. Ibn Hazm al-Andalusi (d. 1064 CE), in *al-Fisal fi al-milal wa-al-ahwa' wa-al-nihal*, devoted extensive sections to tracing what he regarded as contradictions and geographical, historical and numerical errors in the books of the Torah and the Gospels. He argued that the Torah as it exists in the hands of the Jews cannot be the words of Moses, because it contains an account of his death and burial, and that the scribe Ezra was the one who rewrote it after the Babylonian captivity. Al-Samawʾal al-Maghribi (d. c. 1175 CE), a Jew who converted to Islam, offers in *Ifham al-Yahud* a detailed critique of the Torah texts and their contradictions. In the nineteenth century, Rahmatullah Kairanawi, in *Izhar al-Haqq* (1864), used the results of German criticism to support traditional Islamic critique.

The questions then moved to the Arabic literary and religious text itself. Taha Husayn, in *Fi al-Shi'r al-Jahili* (1926), provoked controversy by applying methodological doubt. Muhammad Ahmad Khalafallah wrote *al-Fann al-Qasasi fi al-Qur'an* (1947) on the literary style of the Qur'anic narratives, and his thesis was rejected. Later, Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd, in *Mafhum al-Nass* (1990), returned to the historical and linguistic interpretation of the Qur'anic text, which led to a legal case against him in the 1990s. These stations show that the relation of historical criticism to the sacred text is a question that crosses religions, not one specific to Western Christianity. Ibn Hazm's approach differs from academic criticism in its explicit doctrinal aim, although their tools, such as comparing narratives and searching for contradictions, are similar.

## The Central Thesis: Multiple Sources

This current holds that the five books of the Torah are not one work written by one author at one time. They are a later compilation of several textual layers distinct in style, vocabulary and theology, composed in widely separated periods and then edited together. The thesis is most clearly formulated by Julius Wellhausen (1844–1918) through the "Documentary Hypothesis," which distinguished four main sources: the Yahwist (J), the Elohist (E), the Deuteronomist (D) and the Priestly (P), abbreviated as JEDP. It was preceded by Jean Astruc (1753), who noted the alternation of the divine name between "Yahweh" and "Elohim" in Genesis; by Eichhorn (1780–1783), who established the term "higher criticism"; by Wilhelm de Wette (1805), who linked the book of Deuteronomy to the reform of King Josiah (around 622 BCE); and by Karl Heinrich Graf (1866) and Abraham Kuenen, who presented the Priestly law as later, after the Exile. Wellhausen completed the structure in *Prolegomena to the History of Israel* (1878). He made the sequence J–E–D–P the basis for reading the development of Israel's religion from a popular cult to a centralized priesthood.

## Method and Tools

This current relies on: (1) analysis of the linguistic style and the distinctive vocabulary of each textual layer; (2) tracing internal repetitions and contradictions (such as the story of creation told in two different versions in Genesis) as indicators of multiple sources; and (3) linking each layer to its probable historical and political context (for example, linking the Priestly source to the period after the Babylonian captivity). This method parallels, from a distance and without a direct line of influence, much older efforts at internal textual criticism in Ibn Hazm. Its purpose, however, differs radically: academic and neutral (or claimed to be) in Wellhausen, polemical and explicitly doctrinal in Ibn Hazm.

## Predecessors Before Wellhausen

About two centuries before Wellhausen, two independent critical attempts were made. Richard Simon, a French priest from the Oratorian congregation, applied philological criticism in *A Critical History of the Old Testament* (1678), suggesting that the Pentateuch was not written entirely by Moses but was revised by later scribes. His book was suppressed by royal order, and he was expelled from his congregation. Baruch Spinoza, in the *Theologico-Political Treatise* (1670), philosophically defended reading the Bible as a historical and moral text rather than as a text of supernatural authority. He started from a rationalist position outside ecclesiastical authority, unlike Simon, who worked from within the Catholic tradition. Neither reached Wellhausen's analytic rigor in disentangling the sources, but both established the principle of subjecting the sacred text to the same tools of historical criticism applied to any ancient text.

## Impact and Controversy

This school created a major rupture in Western theological studies between a conservative current that rejects dismantling the traditional unity of authorship (attributing the five books of Moses in their entirety to Moses), and an academic critical current that adopted the documentary hypothesis or revised versions of it. Wellhausen resigned in 1882 from the theology faculty at Greifswald, explaining that his research was preparing his students for a vocation for which they were not suited. The hypothesis in its Wellhausen form remained the subject of fundamental revisions in the twentieth century: form criticism by Hermann Gunkel (1901), tradition history by Martin Noth and Gerhard von Rad, and the early dating of the Priestly source by Yehezkel Kaufmann. In the last quarter of the century, John Van Seters (1975), Robert Whybray (1987) and Rolf Rendtorff (1977) questioned the possibility of identifying the sources precisely. The "Copenhagen School" (associated with Niels Peter Lemche and Thomas Thompson) called for reading the history of Israel independently of the biblical narrative. On another front, Jon D. Levenson (1993) criticized the tendency of the historical-critical method toward a Protestant model that regards Jewish law as a decline from prophecy. The hypothesis remains the reference point for every discussion of the compositional history of the biblical text.
