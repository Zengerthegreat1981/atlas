# Ethnoecology

Ethnoecology is the study of how peoples perceive, understand, and manage their environments. It asks how a community reads its landscape — its soils, waters, seasons, and species — how that understanding is encoded in language, practice, and belief, and how it guides the use of resources over generations. Where ecology studies the relations between organisms and their surroundings, ethnoecology studies those relations as they are known and lived by people, treating local environmental knowledge as a system of understanding with its own logic and its own evidence.

## History

Ethnoecology grew out of ethnobiology and cultural ecology. Harold Conklin's study of Hanunóo plant knowledge (1954) showed that a people's understanding of nature is detailed and systematic, and Julian Steward's cultural ecology, developed in the 1950s, argued that cultures adapt to their environments through their technologies and social organisation. Ethnoecology took the next step: it treated environmental knowledge itself as the object of study, asking not only how people adapt but how they think about the world they adapt to.

The field took shape in the 1980s and 1990s. Darrell Posey's work with the Kayapó of Brazil, published in the 1980s, argued that Kayapó management — planting, transplanting, and creating forest islands in the savanna — was a deliberate and sophisticated shaping of the landscape, not the accidental by-product of foraging; the scale of the forest-island claim was later debated. Victor Toledo's programmatic essay "What is Ethnoecology?" (1992) set out its scope: the study of how beliefs, knowledge, and practices together link peoples to their environments.

The 1990s brought a series of influential syntheses. Fikret Berkes's *Sacred Ecology* (1999) argued that traditional ecological knowledge is a form of adaptive management, built up over generations of observation and encoded in ritual, story, and taboo. Virginia Nazarea's *Cultural Memory and Biodiversity* (1998) showed how the loss of local crop varieties is also the loss of the knowledge and memory attached to them. Tim Ingold's *The Perception of the Environment* (2000) offered a philosophical foundation, arguing that knowledge of the environment is gained through dwelling in it, not through detached observation.

## Key Figures and Works

Conklin (1954) provided the ethnographic model: fine-grained documentation of how a people know their landscape. Posey's Kayapó research demonstrated that indigenous peoples actively manage and enrich their environments, overturning the image of the forest as pristine wilderness untouched by human hands. Toledo (1992) framed ethnoecology around the linked study of worldview (kosmos), knowledge (corpus), and practice (praxis).

Berkes (1999) made the field's central theoretical claim, that traditional ecological knowledge is a living system of adaptive management, and he documented its role in fisheries, forests, and wildlife. Nazarea (1998) connected biodiversity to cultural memory, showing that seeds, recipes, and stories are preserved together or lost together. Ingold (2000) reframed the whole enterprise, insisting that environmental knowledge is practical, embodied, and inseparable from the experience of living in a place. E. N. Anderson's *Ecologies of the Heart* (1996) added the emotional dimension, arguing that care for the environment is rooted in attachment and moral feeling.

## Method

Ethnoecological method is long-term, collaborative ethnography. Researchers walk landscapes with local experts, recording place names, soil types, indicator species, and seasonal signs. They document calendars of planting, hunting, and gathering, and the rules — taboos, quotas, sacred areas — that regulate resource use. They map resource areas and track changes over time, often combining oral history with satellite imagery and ecological surveys.

A central method is the documentation of traditional ecological knowledge through interviews, participatory mapping, and the recording of stories and songs that encode environmental information. Increasingly, the research is co-designed with communities, who decide what is recorded and how it is used, and the results feed directly into land management, conservation planning, and legal claims to territory.

## Influence and Legacy

Ethnoecology changed conservation. Its documentation of indigenous management helped overturn the idea of wilderness as empty of people, and it underpins the recognition of indigenous and community conserved areas, now a major element of global conservation policy. It supplied the concept of traditional ecological knowledge, which is now cited in international agreements on biodiversity and climate change, and it gave indigenous peoples evidence for their claims to land and resources.

The field also reshaped anthropology's understanding of knowledge. It showed that environmental understanding is not an abstract theory but a practical, lived competence, built through attention, memory, and care. In an era of climate change and biodiversity loss, ethnoecology's questions are urgent: which knowledge is being lost, what it can teach about living sustainably, and how the peoples who hold it can be supported rather than displaced. Ethnoecology is anthropology's contribution to the survival of both cultures and ecosystems.
