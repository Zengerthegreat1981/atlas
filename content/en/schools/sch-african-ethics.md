# African Ethics

## Overview

The term "African ethics" refers to a body of value concepts rooted in the oral and written philosophies of the peoples of sub-Saharan Africa, which contemporary African philosophers have reformulated in systematic philosophical language since the mid-twentieth century. In most of its formulations, this ethics rests on a central idea: a person does not exist as an isolated entity, but becomes fully human to the extent that they engage in the relationships of the community and contribute to its well-being. This is commonly summarized in the popular saying "I am because we are" (Ubuntu). African ethics thus differs from Western liberal individualism, which starts from the independent individual who holds rights, and from extreme communalism, which dissolves the individual entirely into the group. Most of its formulations seek a balance between the individual's entitlement and their duty toward the community.

## Historical Context

Before colonialism, African moral systems were transmitted orally through proverbs, stories, rites of passage, councils of elders, and community conflict resolution, without systematic philosophical writing. During the colonial period, Eurocentric theses were widely promoted, most notably the French philosopher and anthropologist Lucien Lévy-Bruhl's thesis of a "prelogical mentality" among non-European peoples. Such views denied that Africa had any systematic moral philosophy at all, treating what existed as mere "folklore" or a "collective mentality" that did not rise to the level of philosophy. One response came from ethnophilosophical writings, the most prominent of which is the Belgian missionary Placide Tempels's *Bantu Philosophy* (1945). It sought to present the Bantu intellectual system as a complete philosophy based on the concept of "vital force," but it was criticized as a European reconstruction of African thought rather than a critical philosophical analysis.

With the independence of African states in the 1950s and 1960s, professional philosophers trained in Western universities took up the critique of ethnophilosophy and called for a critical, argumentative African philosophy in the academic sense. The most prominent of them was the Beninese philosopher Paulin Hountondji, whose *African Philosophy: Myth and Reality* (French original 1976; English translation 1983) criticized ethnophilosophy and refused to regard "collective wisdom" as philosophy in the precise sense unless it was formulated in written texts of individual authorship that are open to critique and argument. In this critical context, contemporary African ethics matured as a philosophical project that draws inspiration from the oral heritage but reformulates it with the tools of analytic and argumentative philosophy.

## Central Concepts: Ubuntu and Communal Personhood

The concept of "Ubuntu," drawn from the Nguni languages of southern Africa such as Zulu and Xhosa (with cognate notions such as *unhu* in Shona), lies at the heart of most contemporary formulations of African ethics. Augustine Shutte, in *Ubuntu: An Ethic for a New South Africa* (2001), developed the idea philosophically for the post-apartheid context, and the Kenyan philosopher John Mbiti, in *African Religions and Philosophy* (1969), gave a widely quoted formula of the communal view of the self: "I am, because we are; and since we are, therefore I am." It is often contrasted with the Cartesian cogito, "I think, therefore I am." The point is that personal identity itself, not ethics alone, is socially constituted through the network of family, clan, and community relations.

The Nigerian philosopher Ifeanyi Menkiti, in "Person and Community in African Traditional Thought" (1984), argued that "personhood" is not a biological given but a normative status acquired gradually through participation in the community and the fulfilment of its moral roles. On this view, someone whose conduct is selfish and corrupt may be said to fall short of full personhood in the moral sense, despite being biologically human. In response to the worry that such communitarianism could justify the subordination of individuals to "communal unity," the Ghanaian philosopher Kwame Gyekye proposed a "moderate" communitarianism, developed in *Tradition and Modernity* (1997) and earlier work, which preserves individual rights that may not be sacrificed for the community.

From another direction, the South African-based philosopher Thaddeus Metz, in "Toward an African Moral Theory" (2007), formulated a systematic normative theory that aims to express a core of ubuntu in a principle open to argument. He held that an act is right insofar as it positively relates to others by prizing communal relationship, which consists of identity (sharing a way of life and sense of belonging) and solidarity (caring about others' good). On this account, the rightness of an act is measured by the extent of its contribution to the fabric of positive communal relations, not by its individual utility, nor by its conformity to an abstract rational duty.

## Objections and Replies

This framework faces several objections. The first is the objection of "suppression of the individual": if the individual becomes a full human being only through engagement in the community, does this not open the door to subjugating women, minorities, or dissenters in the name of "the collective interest"? Ubuntu rhetoric has in fact been used as a political instrument in some contexts to justify the suppression of opposition. Advocates of moderate communitarianism, such as Gyekye, respond that the correct form of communitarianism does not cancel the rights of the individual but constrains excessive individualism, and that any despotic use of it is a distortion rather than a correct application.

The second is the objection of conceptual vagueness. Critics argue that concepts such as "harmony" or "community" are so loose that it is difficult to derive decisive practical guidance in disputed cases. Metz and others reply that identity and solidarity can be spelled out with some precision, and that the principles of utility or duty in other theories also face problems of interpretation. A related objection asks how far "African ethics" can be treated as one body of thought given the continent's diversity, a point some philosophers press against generalizing from particular cultures.

## Applied Example: Restorative Justice after the Rwandan Genocide

After the genocide of 1994 in Rwanda, the state faced a very large number of suspects and a formal judicial system unable to try them all promptly. From the early 2000s it adapted a traditional form of community dispute resolution, *gacaca*, into a system of community courts in which confession and apology could lead to reduced sentences, with the stated aim of promoting reconciliation as well as accountability. The case is sometimes discussed in connection with communal and restorative conceptions of justice, though *gacaca* is a Rwandan institution rather than an expression of Nguni *ubuntu*, and it has also been criticized on grounds of due process and coercion. It illustrates the contrast between restorative and purely punitive models of justice rather than a straightforward application of any one African ethical theory.

## Impact and Contemporary Application

In South Africa after apartheid, *ubuntu* was invoked in the postamble of the 1993 interim constitution, in the Constitutional Court's judgment in *S v Makwanyane* (1995), and in the discourse of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu, where it was cited in support of amnesty and reconciliation in place of revenge. Debates over applying African ethics have also borne fruit in fields such as environmental ethics, where the moral community is extended to include ancestors, future generations, and the natural environment, and in critiques of individualistic Western development models in favor of participatory development models that respect social cohesion.
