# Al-Tabari and Islamic Historiography

Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Jarir al-Tabari (224/225–310 AH / 839–923 CE) was a polymathic historian, Quranic exegete, and jurist whose work became a leading model of universal Arabic-Islamic historiography. Born in Amul in the mountainous province of Tabaristan along the southern Caspian coast, al-Tabari conducted extensive scholarly travels (*rihla*) across Rayy, Baghdad, Basra, Kufa, Syria, and Fustat (Cairo), studying with leading traditionists and jurists before settling permanently in the Abbasid capital of Baghdad. There, he founded an independent school of Islamic law (*al-madhhab al-jariri*) and produced major works in both historical chronicle and scriptural commentary.

## *Tarikh al-Rusul wa-l-Muluk*

Al-Tabari's historical magnum opus, *Tarikh al-Rusul wa-l-Muluk* (History of the Prophets and Kings), completed around 303 AH / 915 CE, became a standard model of universal Islamic history. Commencing with the theological creation of the cosmos, the work incorporates pre-Islamic biblical prophetology, ancient Persian kings of the mythical and Sasanian dynasties, and pre-Islamic Arabian tribal lore, before detailing prophetic biography (*sirah* and *maghazi*), the early Caliphate, and the Umayyad and Abbasid dynastic eras down to 302 AH.

## Methodological Principles

Al-Tabari's historical method is defined by four structural conventions:

- **The *Isnad* as Evidentiary Backbone**: Adapting the chain of human transmission (*isnad*) developed in Hadith scholarship, al-Tabari generally prefaces historical accounts with chains of named authorities, often reaching back to an eyewitness, anchoring historical authority in transmission rather than deductive speculation.
- **Annalistic Framing (*Hawliyyat*)**: From the Prophet's Hijra to Medina (1 AH / 622 CE) onward, al-Tabari organizes events chronologically year by year. This annalistic framework disciplined historical narrative into strict sequential time, preventing retroactive teleological distortions.
- **Narrative Multiplicity without Forced Harmonization**: When dealing with contentious political disputes—most notably the First and Second Civil Wars (*fitnas*)—al-Tabari records multiple, competing, and often mutually contradictory accounts of the same event alongside their respective chains of transmission. He deliberately refrains from synthesizing these reports into an artificial single narrative, placing the raw, competing testimonies before the reader.
- **Preservation of Lost Early Corpuses**: By acting as a meticulous compiler, al-Tabari preserved vast excerpts from early historical collections that have not survived independently, including the works of Ibn Ishaq (d. 151 AH / 768 CE), Abu Mikhnaf (d. 157 AH / 774 CE), Sayf ibn Umar (d. 180 AH / 796 CE), al-Waqidi (d. 207 AH / 823 CE), and al-Mada'ini (d. c. 228 AH / 843 CE).

## Epistemological Stance and Historical Scope

In his programmatic introduction, al-Tabari explicitly articulates his historical epistemology: knowledge of past events cannot be deduced through speculative reason (*'aql*), but can only be known through transmission (*khabar*) and eyewitness testimony. Consequently, if a reader encounters accounts that seem improbable or troubling, the responsibility rests on the original transmitters rather than the compiler who faithfully preserved the record.

Complementing his *Tarikh*, al-Tabari authored *Jami' al-Bayan 'an Ta'wil Ay al-Qur'an*, the monumental commentary based on transmitted tradition (*tafsir bi-l-ma'thur*). Together, these works reflect a scholarly approach combining philology, legal deduction, and transmitter criticism.

## Historiographical Impact

Al-Tabari's annalistic synthesis served as a principal reference for subsequent generations of medieval Muslim historians:
- **Continuators and Abridgers**: Historians such as Miskawayh (d. 421 AH / 1030 CE), Ibn al-Athir (555–630 AH / 1160–1233 CE) in *Al-Kamil fi al-Tarikh*, and Abu al-Fida' (672–732 AH / 1273–1331 CE) utilized al-Tabari's text as their primary chronological baseline.
- **Vernacular Transmission**: In 352 AH / 963 CE, the Samanid vizier Bal'ami translated and adapted the *Tarikh* into Persian (*Tarikhnama-yi Bal'ami*), which became one of the earliest monuments of New Persian prose.

Modern historians note that al-Tabari's method offers little internal critique of the plausibility of reports, a concern later raised by Ibn Khaldun; his preservation of transmission variants nonetheless makes the *Tarikh* one of the most important sources for early Islamic history.
