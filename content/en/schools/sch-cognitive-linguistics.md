# Cognitive Linguistics

A current in theoretical linguistics that arose in the late 1970s and early 1980s in the United States, as an explicit reaction against Chomskyan generative grammar and its assumption that syntax is autonomous from meaning and general cognition. This current assumes the opposite: language is not an isolated cognitive faculty but a direct reflection of general cognitive abilities, and meaning is **embodied**, that is, rooted in the sensorimotor experience of the body in the world rather than in abstract symbols detached from perception.

## Split from Generativism: The Linguistics Wars

Cognitive linguistics arose partly out of the so-called "Linguistics Wars." In the late 1960s, researchers who adopted "Generative Semantics" broke away from Chomsky. The leading figure was George Lakoff, who called for integrating semantics into the core of grammar rather than treating it as a subsidiary interpretive component. As generative semantics declined as a grammatical school by the 1970s, Lakoff, together with **Ronald Langacker** and Leonard Talmy, redirected this objection toward a new project that goes beyond generative formalization as a whole.

## Conceptual Metaphor

With the philosopher Mark Johnson, Lakoff presented the thesis of "Conceptual Metaphor Theory" in their seminal book *Metaphors We Live By* (1980). Metaphor, on this account, is not rhetorical ornament but a basic cognitive mechanism through which we understand abstract concepts via concrete sensory domains: time through space, importance through size, and states through containment, for example. These "mappings" between domains structure a large part of human thinking itself, not language alone.

## Langacker's Cognitive Grammar

Ronald Langacker developed in parallel an alternative grammatical model, "Cognitive Grammar." It rejects the separation between lexicon and grammar, and treats all linguistic units, from the phoneme to the complete sentence, as symbols with both a phonological pole and a semantic pole. Among its central tools are **construal**, the way a speaker chooses to frame a single scene; and **profile** and **base**, respectively the highlighted part of a concept and the broader cognitive background against which it is understood.

## Prototype Theory and Cognitive Semantics

Cognitive linguistics borrowed from the psychologist Eleanor Rosch the "prototype theory." This holds that categories of meaning are not sharp logical boundaries but are organized around "prototypical" examples that are more representative than others (a sparrow is closer to the prototype of "bird" than an ostrich is). The theory explained classificatory patterns that traditional formal semantics finds difficult to account for.

## Influence and Extension

The current has expanded in many directions. Related work by **Stephen Levinson** studies the relationship between language and spatial cognition across cultures, though he is a critic of some cognitivist assumptions rather than a member of the school. **Vyvyan Evans** and **Melanie Green** wrote a widely used introductory textbook. Research on sound symbolism (such as the bouba-kiki effect) looks for non-arbitrary links between sound and meaning, and is often discussed in relation to the principle of the arbitrariness of the linguistic sign, associated with Saussure.
