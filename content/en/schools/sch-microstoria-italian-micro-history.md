# Italian Microhistory (Microstoria: Ginzburg and Levi)

An Italian current that reverses the logic of historical generalisation: instead of inferring general social structures from broad collective statistics (as in Braudelian Annales history or quantitative history), it examines **a single exceptional individual or local case** closely, in order to illuminate complexities that do not appear in the wider statistical generalisation.

## The Founding Work

*The Cheese and the Worms* (*Il formaggio e i vermi*, 1976) by **Carlo Ginzburg** reconstructs the world of an Italian sixteenth-century miller named Menocchio (Domenico Scandella), who was burned for heresy after developing a distinctive religious cosmology (he claimed that the universe arose as cheese does, out of which worms form) from a strange mixture of popular readings and peasant oral tradition. Ginzburg uses the records of two Inquisition trials as a dense source that reveals a stratum of peasant "popular culture" which rarely leaves any direct written trace.

## Method

- **The exceptional normal (*l'esemplare normale*, or "exceptional normal"):** an individual case recorded in exceptional detail (often because of a trial or legal inquiry) is not a worthless anomaly, but a dense window onto broader cultural patterns that would have remained hidden without the archival accident that documented it in such detail.
- **Reduction of the scale of observation:** changing the scale of analysis from society or nation to the individual or village reveals internal complexities and contradictions (individual agency, negotiation with authority, cultural hybridisation) that disappear when a phenomenon is reduced to statistical averages.
- **Giovanni Levi**, *Inherited Power* (*L'eredità immateriale*, 1985): applies a similar logic to a Piedmontese village, arguing that individual actors manoeuvre within real margins of freedom even within rigid coercive structures (such as the feudal system), rather than being merely passive victims of a deterministic structure.

## Impact and Limitations

This current raised a central methodological question: is it valid to generalise from a single individual case? Ginzburg answers that the goal is not statistical generalisation but the **revelation of a hidden possibility** (that a peasant culture of authentic heresy was possible), not a claim that Menocchio is statistically representative of all peasants.
