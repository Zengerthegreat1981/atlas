# Migration Anthropology

Migration anthropology is the study of human movement across borders and within societies, and of the social worlds that movement creates. It examines why people leave home, how they travel and settle, how they maintain ties across distances, and how states, labor markets, and communities respond to their presence. Unlike approaches that treat migrants as a problem to be managed, anthropology begins from migrants' own experiences: the journeys they undertake, the families they sustain across borders, and the identities they remake in new places.

## History

Anthropologists have studied movement since the discipline's beginnings, but migration became a distinct field only in the late twentieth century. Early work focused on rural-to-urban migration within colonized and postcolonial societies, and on the labor migrations that fed industrial economies. Oscar Lewis's *The Children of Sanchez* (1961) brought the lives of poor urban migrants in Mexico City to a wide audience. The decisive shift came in the 1990s with the theory of transnationalism. Nina Glick Schiller, Linda Basch, and Cristina Szanton Blanc argued in *Towards a Transnational Perspective on Migration* (1992) that many migrants do not simply assimilate into a new country but build lives that span borders, maintaining family, economic, and political ties in two or more societies at once. Their book *Nations Unbound* (1994) developed this argument and challenged the assumption that societies coincide with nation-states. Arjun Appadurai's *Modernity at Large* (1996) placed migration within global flows of people, media, money, and ideas, arguing that the imagination itself had become a social force in migrant lives. In the 2000s the field turned to the politics of borders, illegality, and deportation. Nicholas De Genova's work on the production of "illegality" showed how states create the category of the undocumented migrant. Ruben Andersson's *Illegality, Inc.* (2014) traced the industry that profits from policing migration across Europe and Africa. Today the field also studies refugees, climate displacement, and the digital infrastructures through which migrants communicate and move.

## Key Figures and Works

A key early text is the volume edited by Glick Schiller, Basch, and Blanc-Szanton, *Towards a Transnational Perspective on Migration* (1992), followed by *Nations Unbound* (1994), which developed the concepts of transmigrants and transnational social fields. Arjun Appadurai's *Modernity at Large* (1996) remains influential for its framework of global cultural flows. Leo Chavez's *Shadowed Lives* (1992) documented the lives of undocumented Mexican immigrants in the United States with ethnographic depth. Aihwa Ong's *Flexible Citizenship* (1999) showed how wealthy Chinese migrants navigate multiple passports and national loyalties. Susan Coutin's *Legalizing Moves* (2000) examined how Salvadoran immigrants in the United States negotiated immigration law and belonging. Nicholas De Genova's *Working the Boundaries* (2005) analyzed how Mexican migrant labor is racialized and rendered deportable. Ruben Andersson's *Illegality, Inc.* (2014) exposed the border industry in Europe. More recently, Catherine Besteman's *Making Refuge* (2016), on Somali Bantu refugees in Lewiston, Maine, and the anthropology of humanitarianism have connected migration to war, aid, and the politics of rescue. These works share an attention to how large structures — states, economies, empires — are experienced in the intimate details of migrant lives.

## Method

Migration anthropology is built on multi-sited ethnography: following people, objects, and relationships across the places that migration connects. A researcher may conduct fieldwork in a village of origin, a transit city, and a destination neighborhood, tracing the same families and networks across those sites. Life histories and migration narratives are central, revealing how decisions to move are made within families and over years rather than in a single moment. Participant observation takes place in workplaces, border crossings, detention centers, remittance offices, and religious congregations. The field also attends to documents — visas, passports, asylum applications — as ethnographic objects that shape who can move and who cannot. Increasingly, anthropologists study digital life: messaging apps, social media, and money-transfer platforms that sustain transnational families. This methodological flexibility reflects the field's central insight that migration cannot be understood from a single place.

## Influence and Debates

Migration anthropology transformed the study of movement by rejecting the assimilation model, which assumed migrants gradually abandon their origins. Transnationalism showed that maintaining ties to home is not a failure to integrate but a normal feature of migrant life. The field also challenged "methodological nationalism," the habit of treating the nation-state as the natural container of society, a critique developed by Andreas Wimmer and Nina Glick Schiller in 2002. Its ethnographies have influenced debates about citizenship, showing that belonging is practiced through care, remittances, and political participation rather than granted by papers alone. The study of illegality has shown how immigration law produces the very categories — the "illegal alien," the "economic migrant" — that public debate treats as natural. A continuing debate concerns the balance between structure and agency: how much room do migrants have to shape their lives within systems designed to control them? Feminist scholars have emphasized gendered dimensions of migration, from domestic workers to the "left-behind" children of migrant mothers. As displacement reaches record levels, migration anthropology provides some of the most detailed accounts of what borders do to human lives.
