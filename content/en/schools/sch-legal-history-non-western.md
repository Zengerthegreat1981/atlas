# History of Non-Western Law

## Historical Context

The history of Islamic law heads this field as the broadest non-Western legal system in terms of geographic spread and historical continuity. From the first century AH it took shape through two primary sources, the Qur'an and the Sunna of the Prophet. The science of usul al-fiqh then developed as a methodology for deriving rulings through analogy (qiyas), consensus (ijma'), juristic preference (istihsan), and unrestricted public interest (maslaha mursala). Imam al-Shafi'i, in his *al-Risala*, laid out the first systematic formulation of the science of legal theory. Afterward a number of legal schools emerged, including the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali schools, in addition to Ja'fari jurisprudence among the Imami Shi'a. Alongside the Sharia, other non-Western civilizations developed venerable legal systems: Confucian Chinese law, based on the concept of *li* (proper ethical conduct) in contrast to *fa* (strict penal law); Hindu law, drawn from the *Dharmashastra* texts and the Laws of Manu; and traditional African customary laws, which rested on mechanisms of communal reconciliation.

## Central Ideas

Islamic law rests on the idea that legislation belongs exclusively to God, and that the role of the jurist is to exercise ijtihad in understanding the intent of the Lawgiver. This is what fundamentally distinguishes it from the Western view of law as a purely human creation. The theory of the objectives of the Sharia, elaborated by al-Shatibi, limits the aims of legislation to the preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny, and property. Confucian Chinese law, by contrast, prefers social harmony and moral education over severe penal punishment. Traditional African systems rest on the principle of collective restorative justice rather than individual punishment. This diversity amounts to an implicit critique of the centrality of Western law, which dominated comparative legal studies for decades.

## Key Thinkers

Imam al-Shafi'i is regarded as the founder of the science of usul al-fiqh. He was followed by Imam al-Juwayni, in *al-Burhan*, and al-Ghazali, in *al-Mustasfa*, who developed the methodology of legal reasoning. Then al-Qarafi, in *al-Furuq*, developed the study of legal maxims and distinguished the juristic roles of ruling, fatwa and governance. Ibn Taymiyya rooted siyasa shar'iyya as a framework for assessing the public interest. In the modern period, Sheikh Muhammad Abu Zahra and Dr. Muhammad Salim al-Awa contributed to restating Islamic political jurisprudence within the frameworks of the modern state. The orientalist Joseph Schacht is regarded as the most prominent scholar to have studied the history of Islamic legislation from a critical orientalist perspective, a work that provoked wide debate among researchers. Wael Hallaq offered a methodological critique of Schacht's theses, arguing for an earlier and more continuous development of Islamic legal thought than Schacht allowed.

## Impact and Application

Islamic jurisprudence remains a principal source of legislation in personal status laws in most Muslim-majority states. It also inspired modern civil codes, such as the Ottoman *Majalla*, which codified Hanafi transactional rules in the form of legal articles, and the Egyptian civil code drafted by Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri, which uniquely combined Islamic jurisprudence with comparative law. The field remains an arena of scholarly debate over how to study non-Western legal systems apart from Western classificatory frameworks, and over whether Islamic jurisprudence and positive law can be integrated in the modern state. Recent studies compare dispute-resolution mechanisms in Islamic law, African customary law, and traditional Indian law as alternative models to the state's monopoly on justice.

Comparisons among non-Western systems are also significant. Traditional Chinese law favored family and community mediation over formal litigation in order to avoid tearing the social fabric. Islamic jurisprudence offers a similar principle in preferring reconciliation (sulh) over litigation in financial disputes, while the right to resort to the courts remains fully guaranteed. Studies in legal orientalism, from Ignaz Goldziher through Schacht, sought to understand the Sharia from the perspective of the historical development of its texts. This approach met sharp criticism from Muslim and Western scholars alike for neglecting the internal coherence of the discipline of usul al-fiqh. The central question in this field remains whether non-Western legal systems can be understood with the very analytical tools of Western law, or whether these systems require an independent legal epistemology drawn from their own sources. This question intersects with post-colonial debates in comparative legal studies.

European colonialism also reshaped non-Western legal systems. Colonial administrations in Egypt, India, and Nigeria imposed civil and criminal codes derived from the European tradition, while largely retaining Islamic jurisprudence or local customs in the sphere of personal status. This produced a legal dualism whose effects remain visible in many contemporary Arab and African constitutions. The legal historian Wael Hallaq studied this phenomenon in depth in *Shari'a: Theory, Practice, Transformations*. He showed how the replacement of the traditional judicial structure by the modern nation-state stripped the Sharia of many of its independent institutional mechanisms, such as the plurality of legal schools and the decentralized system of legal opinion (ifta').
