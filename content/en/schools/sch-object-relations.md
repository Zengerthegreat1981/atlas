# Object Relations Theory

A British psychoanalytic school in which the focus shifted from Freud's theory of motivation (libido seeking discharge) to the idea that the fundamental motive of the psyche is **the search for relationship** with "objects": persons who have been psychically internalized, not material things. Melanie Klein developed this line of theory from the 1920s, and her book *The Psycho-Analysis of Children* (1932; German original *Die Psychoanalyse des Kindes*) presented the results of applying play technique to the analysis of infants and young children and describing their inner world in terms of "part" objects (such as the good/bad breast) before the perception of the whole person has formed. W. R. D. Fairbairn developed the line independently from Scotland in the 1940s and 1950s, proposing in his collected essays *Psychoanalytic Studies of the Personality* (1952) that libido is "object-seeking" rather than "pleasure-seeking," as Freud had assumed, a radical reversal of Freudian metapsychology.

## Core Assumptions

(1) The self does not form in isolation but through the internalization of early relationships with caregivers, so that the "external object" becomes part of the internal psychic structure. (2) Early defense mechanisms such as splitting and projective identification, which Klein described within the "Depressive Position" (1935, 1940) and the "Paranoid-Schizoid Position" (1946), keep the internal representations of objects separated as "good" or "bad," and their later integration is a sign of psychological maturity. (3) D. W. Winnicott, a pediatrician and later psychoanalyst, developed the concepts of the "good-enough mother," the "holding environment," and the "transitional object" (an object such as a blanket or toy that the child uses in the passage from total dependence toward separateness; Winnicott 1951, and in *Playing and Reality*, 1971), focusing on the environment and not on instinctual drives alone.

## Development and Splits

Disagreements between Melanie Klein and Anna Freud (Sigmund Freud's daughter) over child analysis led to the "Controversial Discussions" in the British Psychoanalytical Society between 1941 and 1945, which ended in an arrangement dividing the Society's training into three streams: the Kleinians, the contemporary Freudians (followers of Anna Freud), and the Middle Group, later the "Independents," to which Winnicott and (as a Scottish-based analyst closely associated with the group) Fairbairn are usually assigned. The tradition later extended to the United States through Otto Kernberg, who from the 1960s and 1970s integrated object relations with American ego psychology in his theory of borderline personality organization.

## Current Status

The school remains a cornerstone of British psychoanalytic training (the Institute of Psychoanalysis in London) and directly influenced John Bowlby's attachment theory. Bowlby began his training within the Kleinian circle before diverging from it theoretically from the 1950s on, developing an attachment theory based on behavioral observation rather than purely symbolic interpretation.

## Criticism

Klein's early hypotheses were accused of lacking direct empirical observational grounding (inferring a complex inner world in infants from the play of two- and three-year-old children). Bowlby, after diverging from the Kleinians, stressed direct observation of attachment behavior rather than reconstruction of infants' fantasy life.
