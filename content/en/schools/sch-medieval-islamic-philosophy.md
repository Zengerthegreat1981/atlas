# Medieval Islamic Philosophy (Falsafa)

An umbrella that gathers the current of "philosophy" (*falsafa*, from the Greek *philosophia*) in the Islamic world, from the 9th century CE, building on the translation movement, until the decline of Peripatetic philosophy in al-Andalus with the death of Ibn Rushd in 1198 CE, followed by a limited continuation in the East. The school was built on translated Greek texts (chiefly Aristotle, and Plato as mediated through Neoplatonic sources), and it sought either to reconcile them with Islamic belief or to critique them from within their own logical tools.

## Central Figures

- **al-Farabi** (d. 339 AH / 950 CE): called "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle in the later tradition. He set out a political and metaphysical system that links prophecy to the Active Intellect and to the virtuous city.
- **Ibn Sina** (370–428 AH / 980–1037 CE): a central philosopher of the medieval Islamic world. In the *Ilahiyyat* of his encyclopedia *al-Shifa'* he formulated a distinction between **quiddity** (*mahiyya*, "what a thing is") and **existence** (*wujud*, "whether it is"). He held that every contingent being is possible in itself: its existence is additional to its quiddity and comes to it from an external cause, unlike the Necessary Existent, in whom existence is not an added attribute. This distinction became a point of reference in Latin scholastic metaphysics, through translations that included the Toledo version of the *Ilahiyyat*.
- **Ibn Rushd** (520–595 AH / 1126–1198 CE): philosopher of Córdoba and physician at the Almohad court, known for his commentaries on Aristotle (he is "the Commentator" in the Latin tradition). In *Tahafut al-Tahafut* he answered al-Ghazali's critique in *Tahafut al-Falasifa*, and in *Fasl al-Maqal* he argued that demonstration and the Sharia do not contradict one another.
- **Ibn 'Arabi** (560–638 AH / 1165–1240 CE): usually classed with philosophical Sufism rather than with the Peripatetics. He wrote of the self-disclosure of the Real (*tajalli*). Later readers summed up his metaphysics as **unity of being** (*wahdat al-wujud*); the phrase itself was fixed in the commentarial tradition more than in his own usage.
- **al-Suhrawardi** (d. 587 AH / 1191 CE): formulated **Illuminationist philosophy** (*hikmat al-ishraq*). Against a purely Peripatetic account of knowledge he argued for "presential knowledge" (*al-'ilm al-huduri*), knowing by immediate presence rather than by a concept abstracted from a thing.

## The Great Debate: Quiddity and Existence

The dispute between Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd over the relation of quiddity to existence is a famous controversy in medieval Islamic philosophy. On the usual reading, Ibn Sina held that in contingent beings existence is additional to quiddity. Ibn Rushd objected that this confused a logical distinction with a real composition. Related questions were taken up in thirteenth-century Latin scholastic philosophy, notably by Thomas Aquinas and John Duns Scotus.

## Intellect, Prophecy, and al-Ghazali's Critique

A related set of arguments concerns the soul and the intellect. al-Farabi distinguished the potential, actual, acquired and active intellects, and he linked prophecy to the imaginative faculty. Ibn Sina, in *al-Shifa'*, *al-Isharat wa-l-Tanbihat* and related works, set out the "proof of the truthful" (*burhan al-siddiqin*), the thought experiment of the "Flying Man," and a classification of the faculties of the soul. al-Ghazali, in *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (written about 488 AH / 1095 CE), discussed twenty questions. He charged the philosophers with unbelief on three: the eternity of the world, the denial that God knows particulars, and the denial of bodily resurrection. The other seventeen he treated as heretical innovation rather than as unbelief. Ibn Rushd replied in *Tahafut al-Tahafut* and in *Fasl al-Maqal*. A famous image in the latter calls philosophy and the Sharia milk-sisters.

## Suhrawardi, Ibn 'Arabi, and Mulla Sadra

al-Suhrawardi, in *Hikmat al-Ishraq*, criticized Peripatetic epistemology and grounded a part of knowledge in unveiling (*kashf*) and in light. Ibn 'Arabi, in *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya* and *Fusus al-Hikam*, wrote of being as the self-disclosure of the Real (*tajalli al-haqq*). Sadr al-Din al-Shirazi (Mulla Sadra; d. 1045 AH / 1635–1636 CE; some sources give 1050 AH / 1640 CE) drew on Peripatetic philosophy, illumination and gnosis in *al-Asfar al-Arba'a*, and argued for the primacy of existence (*asalat al-wujud*). Philosophy continued in Iran and the East long after Ibn Rushd. The claim that it ended with him does not fit that later history. The dates of this entry mark the medieval Peripatetic arc, not that later continuation.

## Legacy

Through translations made in Toledo, Sicily and Italy in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle reached European universities and were taken up by the Latin Averroists. Ibn Sina's metaphysics remained a major source for Christian scholasticism after those commentaries became current. Avicennism was not simply displaced.

## Caveats

- The line "al-Farabi – Ibn Sina – Ibn Rushd" is a pedagogically simplified line. Medieval Islamic philosophy also included illuminationist, Sufi and theological currents that overlapped with it and cannot be reduced to Peripateticism alone.
- This is an umbrella grouping many subfields, not a single homogeneous school of thought.
