# Medieval Islamic Philosophy (Falsafa)

An umbrella that gathers the current of "philosophy" (*falsafa*, from the Greek *philosophia*) in the Islamic world, from the 9th century CE, building on the translation movement, until the decline of Peripatetic philosophy in al-Andalus with the death of Ibn Rushd in 1198 CE, followed by a limited continuation in the East. The school was built on translated Greek texts (chiefly Aristotle, and Plato as mediated through Neoplatonic sources), and it sought either to reconcile them with Islamic belief or to critique them from within their own logical tools.

## Central Figures

- **al-Farabi** (d. 950 CE): called "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle, he produced the first integrated political-metaphysical system, linking prophecy to the Active Intellect and to the theory of the virtuous city.
- **Ibn Sina** (980–1037 CE): the most influential philosopher of the medieval Islamic world. In his encyclopedia *al-Shifa'* he formulated a key distinction between **quiddity** (*mahiyya*, "what a thing is") and **existence** (*wujud*, "whether it is"). He held that every contingent being is possible in itself: its existence is additional to its quiddity and comes to it from an external cause, unlike the Necessary Existent (God), in whom quiddity and existence coincide. This distinction later became one of the pillars of Latin scholastic metaphysics through the Toledo translations.
- **Ibn Rushd** (1126–1198 CE): philosopher of Córdoba and physician to the Almohad court, known for his precise commentaries on Aristotle (he is "the Commentator" in the Latin tradition). In *Tahafut al-Tahafut* he answered al-Ghazali's critique of the philosophers in *Tahafut al-Falasifa*, defending the possibility of reconciling philosophical demonstration with the Sharia.
- **Ibn 'Arabi** (1165–1240 CE): although usually classed as philosophical Sufism rather than pure Peripatetic philosophy, he developed the doctrine of **unity of being** (*wahdat al-wujud*), which deeply influenced later Islamic philosophy and Sufi thought.
- **al-Suhrawardi** (d. 1191 CE): founder of **Illuminationist philosophy** (*al-ishraqiyya*), which replaced the Aristotelian logic of the Peripatetics with an epistemology based on "presential knowledge" (*al-'ilm al-huduri*) and luminous illumination.

## The Great Debate: Quiddity and Existence

The dispute between Ibn Sina and Ibn Rushd over the relation of quiddity to existence is among the most famous controversies in medieval Islamic philosophy. Ibn Sina held that existence in contingent beings is an accident additional to quiddity. Ibn Rushd objected that this was a logical confusion rather than a genuine metaphysical reality, a position close to a direct reading of Aristotle. Related questions were taken up in thirteenth-century Latin scholastic philosophy, notably by Thomas Aquinas and John Duns Scotus.

## Roots of the Question in Ibn Sina and al-Ghazali

The first discussion emerged from the problem of the soul and the intellect. al-Farabi (d. 339 AH/950 CE) distinguished four intellects (potential, actual, acquired and active) and linked prophecy to imagination. Ibn Sina (d. 428 AH/1037 CE), in *al-Shifa'*, *al-Isharat wa-l-Tanbihat*, and related works, formulated the "proof of the truthful" (*burhan al-siddiqin*), the thought experiment of the "Flying Man," and a classification of the faculties of the soul. al-Ghazali, in *Tahafut al-Falasifa* (1095 CE), set out to refute twenty issues and declared the philosophers unbelievers on three of them: the eternity of the world, God's knowledge of particulars, and the denial of bodily resurrection. Ibn Rushd replied in *Tahafut al-Tahafut* and in *Fasl al-Maqal*, arguing that wisdom and the Sharia are two sisters nursed at the same breast.

## Suhrawardi, Ibn 'Arabi, and Mulla Sadra

al-Suhrawardi (d. 587 AH/1191 CE), in *Hikmat al-Ishraq*, critiqued Peripatetic logic and grounded knowledge in unveiling (*kashf*) and light. Ibn 'Arabi, in *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya* and *Fusus al-Hikam*, built a conception of being based on the self-disclosure of the Real (*tajalli al-haqq*). Then Sadr al-Din al-Shirazi (Mulla Sadra; d. 1050 AH/1640 CE) combined Peripatetic philosophy, illumination and gnosis in *al-Asfar al-Arba'a*, arguing for the primacy of existence (*asalat al-wujud*). Philosophy thus continued after Ibn Rushd in Iran and the East, a reality that the claim that Islamic philosophy ended with Ibn Rushd tends to obscure.

## Legacy

Through the Toledo and Sicilian translations (12th and 13th centuries), Ibn Rushd's commentaries on Aristotle reached European universities and formed the Latin Averroist school. Ibn Sina's philosophical synthesis (Avicennism) was one of the most important sources of Christian scholastic philosophy before a more direct Aristotelian reading replaced it in the thirteenth century.

## Caveats

- The line "al-Farabi – Ibn Sina – Ibn Rushd" is a pedagogically simplified line. Medieval Islamic philosophy also included illuminationist, Sufi and theological currents that overlapped with it and cannot be reduced to Peripateticism alone.
- This is an umbrella grouping many subfields, not a single homogeneous school of thought.
