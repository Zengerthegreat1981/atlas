# Ethnophilosophy

A current in African philosophy that tried to present a "distinctive" philosophy of Africans, relying on ethnographic data: proverbs, languages, rituals, myths and tales, on the assumption that every community carries an implicit collective worldview that can be extracted and formulated as a philosophical system. The term was established, with critical intent, by the Beninese philosopher Paulin Hountondji in the 1970s (the term was also used by Marcien Towa), and then became the name of an entire school.

## Context

The current began in its well-known form with:
- **Placide Tempels (1906–1977)**: a Belgian Franciscan missionary who spent years in Katanga in the Belgian Congo, and who presented in "La philosophie bantoue" (1945, republished in French by Présence Africaine in 1949) the idea of "Vital Force" as the principle of being among the Bantu: a being is a force, everything interacts by an exchange of influence, and evil is a diminution of force. The motive was also missionary and administrative: he wanted to build a Christian order founded on understanding Africans' beliefs rather than ridiculing them.
- **Marcel Griaule**: "Conversations with Ogotemmêli" (1948) on the Dogon of Mali, which presented a detailed cosmology based on the data of a blind elder, and which later theorists drew on.

## Central Ideas

- **African philosophy = a shared worldview**: every community has a unified vision of being, representing the implicit philosophy of the people.
- **Bantu ontology**: in Tempels and Alexis Kagame, the categories Muntu (human being), Kintu (thing), Hantu (place and time) and Kuntu (modality and manner), a system of categories that parallels Aristotle's system.
- **Language as ontology**: the grammar and vocabulary of a language reveal a view of the world, as Kagame showed in "The Bantu-Rwandan Philosophy of Being" (1956).
- **Practical wisdom**: proverbs, traditions and rituals are the source of values.
- **Person and community**: in John Mbiti, the phrase "I am because we are," which stresses the priority of the community in constituting the person.
- **African time**: Mbiti said that time among the peoples of East Africa extends into the past (zamani) and the present (sasa), and the distant future almost does not exist, a view that many have criticized.

## Figures

- **Placide Tempels**: the pioneer of the current.
- **Alexis Kagame (1912–1981)**: a Rwandan priest and philosopher who worked on the Kinyarwanda language.
- **John Mbiti (1931–2019)**: Kenyan, "African Religions and Philosophy" (1969).
- **Janheinz Jahn (1918–1973)**: "Muntu: African Culture and the Western World" (1961; German 1958).
- **Léopold Senghor**: poet and president of Senegal, author of Négritude, whose philosophical outlook is close to this tendency.
- **Okot p'Bitek**: "African Religions in Western Scholarship" (1971), in which he criticized, from within East Africa, the imposition of Greek concepts on Africans.

## Impact

- It paved the way for the emergence of academic African philosophy as a response to it and a going beyond it (Kwasi Wiredu, Peter Bodunrin, Paulin Hountondji).
- It influenced philosophical anthropology through Marcel Griaule and Germaine Dieterlen, and the question of "local knowledge systems" in postcolonial studies.
- It gave Africans material to affirm that they have an intellectual tradition in the face of colonial theories that denied them reason, and contributed to the discourse of recognition in the Négritude movement and in Kwame Nkrumah's writings in "Consciencism" (1964).

## Limits and Critique

- **The critique of the professionals**: Hountondji in "African Philosophy: Myth and Reality" (1976) held that ethnophilosophy is ethnography disguised as philosophy, because it attributes a collective philosophy to a whole people without presenting individuals who discuss and argue.
- **Bodunrin's critique**: "The Question of African Philosophy" (1981) affirms that philosophy is individual critical thinking, written or open to discussion.
- **The objection to generalization**: Africa is a continent of enormous diversity in languages and cultures, and no single philosophy can be valid for it; Wiredu also objected to projecting the categories of one language onto the whole epistemic reality.
- **The effect of the European intermediary**: critics pointed out that Tempels and others wrote with the concepts of scholastic philosophy and imposed them on their sources, so that the echo of Aristotle and Thomas Aquinas returned in the form of a "Bantu philosophy."
- **The response**: Henry Odera Oruka replied with the "sage philosophy" project (1990), which gathered interviews with individual sages, in order to go beyond collectivism without abandoning the oral tradition.

## Arab and Islamic Reception

Ethnophilosophy had no direct relation to the Arab world, but its discussions touched Muslim Africa: in West Africa there is a long tradition written in Arabic, represented for example by Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti (1556–1627), which shows, when "African philosophy" is discussed, that the African community is not purely oral and that Africa has written scholarly traditions. Researchers have drawn on this evidence against the conception that confines African thought to proverbs and rituals.
