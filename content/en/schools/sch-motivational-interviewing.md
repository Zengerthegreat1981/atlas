# Motivational Interviewing

A goal-directed therapeutic interview that focuses on **evoking and strengthening the individual's motivation to change**. It was first described by William R. Miller in 1983 in work with problem drinkers, and then elaborated with Stephen Rollnick in a series of books: *Motivational Interviewing: Preparing People to Change Addictive Behavior* (1991), the second edition *Motivational Interviewing: Preparing People for Change* (2002), and the third edition *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change* (2013). It is supported by a large body of trial evidence in addiction and health behavior and is used widely in clinical and medical settings.

## Historical Context

Motivational interviewing arose from an observation by Miller, a clinical psychologist at the University of New Mexico (Albuquerque), that in his work with problem drinkers confrontational approaches did not succeed and sometimes increased resistance. He was influenced by Carl Rogers (therapeutic empathy, authenticity, unconditionality) and by the **Transtheoretical Model** of Jim Prochaska and Carlo DiClemente. Miller and Rollnick describe the **spirit of motivational interviewing** (MI Spirit) in terms of collaboration instead of confrontation, evoking instead of teaching, and honoring the individual's autonomy instead of coercion; the third edition (2013) summarizes it as four elements, partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation (PACE). The third edition also presented the four processes (engaging, focusing, evoking, planning) and drew on research on how change talk predicts change.

## Core Theoretical Concepts

### "Change Talk" and "Sustain Talk"
The most important distinction in motivational interviewing: **"change talk"** is client speech that expresses desire, ability, reason, need, or commitment toward change ("I want...", "I can...", "because...", "I have to..."). **"Sustain talk"** is speech that justifies the status quo ("Yes, but...", "I can't because..."). **The therapeutic aim**: to evoke and amplify change talk.

### Self-Perception Theory
The theoretical basis: Miller, drawing on Daryl Bem's self-perception theory, holds that **people persuade themselves** more than they are persuaded by others' arguments. The therapist's role is not to present arguments but to **draw out what the client says in favor of change**. This runs contrary to the usual confrontational style.

### Rolling with Resistance
Instead of **confronting resistance** (which increases it), it is "rolled" (reframed) along with the individual. Resistance is not a problem in the patient but a **natural response to a therapeutic environment that deprives him of autonomy**. The solution: collaboration, not confrontation.

### Developing Discrepancy
Change is more likely when the individual perceives a **gap** between current behavior and his own values and goals, an idea related to (but not identical with) cognitive dissonance. **Developing discrepancy** is a core aim of motivational interviewing: not the therapist's indoctrination, but helping the client see the contradiction himself.

## The Four OARS Foundations

These skills are considered the **core of motivational interviewing**:

- **O — Open Questions**: instead of closed (yes/no) questions, questions beginning with "how", "what", "tell me about..." are used. They evoke change talk.
- **A — Affirmations**: acknowledging the client's abilities and efforts, strengthening self-efficacy.
- **R — Reflections**: repeating the content by emotional or simple rephrasing. It shows listening and reveals deeper meaning.
- **S — Summaries**: gathering what the client has said into one paragraph, highlighting change talk.

## The Four Processes

The third edition (2013) describes **four overlapping processes** (not separate stages):

1. **Engaging**: building a therapeutic relationship founded on empathy and trust. A necessary condition for everything that follows.
2. **Focusing**: defining the therapeutic goal by agreement between therapist and client. In addiction it may be "reducing drinking", "complete cessation", or "abstinence with consequences".
3. **Evoking**: drawing out change talk from the client through OARS questions and specialized techniques (such as importance and confidence rulers).
4. **Planning**: moving from "I want to change" to "how will I do it". Setting measurable steps.

## Clinical Techniques

### Evoking Change Talk
- "Positive hypothetical" questions: "If you succeeded in quitting, what would change in your life?"
- Looking back: "What was your life like before this became a problem?"
- Looking forward: "How do you imagine your life in 5 years if you do not change?"
- "Values Clarification": "What matters more to you than this behavior?"

### Importance and Confidence Rulers
The client rates the **importance of change** and **confidence in ability to change** on a 0-10 scale. The therapist asks: "Why did you give 6 instead of 2?", "What would need to happen for you to move from 6 to 7?".

### Decisional Balance
A simple table: **advantages of change, disadvantages of the status quo** versus **disadvantages of change, advantages of the status quo**. It is used to amplify change talk.

### DARN-CAT
A mnemonic for the types of change talk: preparatory change talk (**D**esire, **A**bility, **R**easons, **N**eed) and mobilizing change talk (**C**ommitment, **A**ctivation, **T**aking steps).

### The ELICIT-PROVIDE-ELICIT Style
- **ELICIT**: "What do you know about this option?"
- **PROVIDE**: the therapist offers the information.
- **ELICIT**: "What do you think about that?"

## Evidence Base

Motivational interviewing has been tested in hundreds of randomized controlled trials, and meta-analyses generally find small-to-moderate benefits, with the strongest evidence in substance use and health behaviors:

- **Alcohol addiction**: reduces drinking, with the clearest effects in brief interventions.
- **Drug addiction**: evidence is more mixed but generally supportive, for example for treatment engagement.
- **Smoking cessation**: modest effects on quit rates.
- **Medication adherence**: studied in several chronic conditions, with mixed results.
- **Health behavior change**: weight management, physical activity, and diabetes self-management have been studied.
- **Brief therapy**: it shines in short settings (1-4 sessions), such as emergency rooms and addiction units.
- **Heterogeneity**: effects vary with the quality of delivery, the target behavior, and the comparison condition.

## The Spirit of MI

Motivational interviewing avoids confrontation, argument, and blaming the client, and tries to resist the "righting reflex," the urge to fix the person's problem for them. Through OARS skills, the therapist helps the client explore the discrepancy between his current actions and his deeper values and goals, and elicits "change talk" that comes from the client himself. MI shares with Carl Rogers' humanistic school the principle of client-centeredness and reflective listening, but adds a deliberate direction toward change.

## Versus Contingency Management

Unlike contingency management, which reinforces abstinence with external material rewards, MI bets on evoking the client's own internal motivation without external incentives, and the two techniques are often combined in addiction treatment programs.

## Relation to Neighboring Schools

- **CBT**: overlap in cognitive-behavioral foundations, but MI differs in that it **does not assume the individual is ready for change**, and instead focuses on increasing motivation.
- **Solution-Focused Brief Therapy (SFBT)**: considerable similarity in brevity and in the focus on "drawing out" the client's resources, but MI gives the therapist a wider role (especially in OARS).
- **Brief Intervention**: a natural complement; MI is considered a **common basis for brief interventions in primary care**.
- **Family therapy**: MI-based approaches such as the Family Check-Up (Dishion and colleagues) have been used in family-focused prevention work with adolescents.
- **Psychiatry** (in managing treatment adherence): MI is used as an adjunct to promote engagement and adherence in a range of mental health conditions, with varying evidence.

## Criticism and Limits

- **Training quality**: MI is sensitive to the quality of delivery. Brief workshops alone are generally not enough to produce reliable skill, and some practitioners use MI techniques while departing from its spirit.
- **Fidelity measures**: the MITI (Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity) was developed, but its application in training remains uneven.
- **Effectiveness with psychosis**: less well documented in psychotic conditions, and adaptations are usually needed.
- **Cultural limits**: MI was developed in a Western context, and its transfer to high-context cultures (such as Arab societies) needs adjustments (the influence of family dynamics, for example).

## Legacy and Influence

Motivational interviewing influenced: (1) the **shift in addiction treatment** away from confrontational counseling toward a more collaborative model; (2) the founding of **MINT** (Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers), an international training network; (3) wide adoption in **primary care** and psychiatry; (4) applications in **public health** (for example smoking cessation and physical activity); (5) use in educational and organizational settings.
