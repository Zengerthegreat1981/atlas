# New Institutional Economics

**New Institutional Economics** is a school of political economy that began to take shape in the mid-1970s. It restored institutions (laws, contracts, property rights and corporate governance structures) to the centre of economic analysis. Unlike Old American Institutionalism, however, it did not start from rejecting the rational, utility-maximising individual. Rather, it extended the neoclassical model itself to include a fact it had ignored: **gathering information, negotiating and enforcing contracts are costly processes**. This cost explains why economic transactions take different institutional forms: an open market, a long-term contract, or a vertically integrated firm.

## Ronald Coase and the Origin of the Question

The school's intellectual origin lies in Ronald Coase's article "The Nature of the Firm" (1937), which posed a question that seemed simple but went without a satisfactory answer for decades. If markets are an efficient coordination mechanism, as neoclassical theory assumes, why do firms exist at all, rather than coordinating everything through individual contracts in the open market? Coase answered that **transaction costs** (the cost of searching for contracting parties, negotiating with them and monitoring their performance) sometimes make it cheaper to organise production within a single administrative hierarchy (the firm) than to coordinate it through repeated market contracts. The boundaries of the firm are drawn at the point where the cost of internal organisation equals the cost of external contracting. Coase's later article "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960) argued that if transaction costs are zero and rights are well defined, bargaining leads to an efficient use of the resource whichever party holds the right at the outset, abstracting from income effects. Where transaction costs are positive, the initial assignment of the right does matter. George Stigler later labelled the zero-cost result the **Coase theorem**.

## Oliver Williamson and the Economics of Transaction Costs

Oliver Williamson developed Coase's answer into a complete systematic analytical framework known as "Transaction Cost Economics," which he set out in *The Economic Institutions of Capitalism* (1985). Williamson argued that the choice of the most suitable governance structure for any transaction (a market, a hybrid contract, or full vertical integration within a firm) depends on three main dimensions. The first is the degree of **asset specificity**: the more the assets invested in a transaction are specialised for a single purpose and difficult to redeploy, the greater the risk of opportunistic "holdup" by the other party after the investment is made. The second is the degree of uncertainty, and the third is the frequency of the transaction. When asset specificity rises and transactions recur in an uncertain environment, vertical integration within a single firm becomes less exposed to opportunistic behaviour than a repeatedly renegotiated market contract.

## Douglass North and Institutions as the Rules of the Game

Douglass North extended the scope of the analysis from the individual firm to the economy as a whole. He defined "institutions" in general terms as the **"rules of the game"**: the formal rules (constitutions, laws and property rights) and informal ones (customs and shared values) that determine the structure of incentives in any society. He distinguished these from the **"players"** (organisations) who operate within those rules. North argued that the quality of these institutions, especially how effectively they protect property rights and reduce transaction costs across the broad market rather than only inside the firm, is the deepest determinant of divergent long-run economic performance between countries, more so than differences in natural or technological resources alone.

## From the Firm to Public Governance

Later economists extended the tools of new institutional economics well beyond the private firm. They examined how employment contracts can be designed to address information asymmetry between employer and employee (the principal-agent problem), and how local communities govern their shared resources. The latter line of work was developed by Elinor Ostrom, starting from similar questions about the costs of collective coordination, although it later formed a school of its own. What unites all these branches is the view that institutions, whatever their form, are rational responses, though not always efficient ones, to the problem of reducing the costs of exchange and coordination among parties with limited information and conflicting interests.

## Critiquing Hyper-Rationality Without Full Abandonment

This current occupies a precise middle position in the history of economic thought. It does not go as far as rejecting the neoclassical model radically, as Old American Institutionalism did. It keeps the assumption that individuals seek to maximise their interests rationally, but relaxes the standard accompanying assumptions (complete information, contracting without cost) that made this maximisation appear instantaneous and frictionless. This limited modification, rather than a complete break, allowed New Institutional Economics to integrate relatively easily into the mainstream of academic economics. Old Institutionalism, by contrast, remained for decades in a relatively marginal position.

## From the Private Firm to Public and International Governance

The application of this school's tools gradually expanded from analysing the boundaries of the private firm to broader fields. These include designing government procurement contracts to reduce the risks of corruption and opportunistic holdup; analysing the effectiveness of international organisations as institutional responses to transaction costs that cross national borders and that no single state can handle alone; and explaining why developing countries adopt institutional reforms imported from abroad (such as corporate governance reforms or intellectual property laws) without always achieving the expected economic impact. These reforms sometimes conflict with deeply rooted informal local customs that are hard to change by merely amending an imported legal text.
