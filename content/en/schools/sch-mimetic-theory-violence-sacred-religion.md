# Mimetic Theory and the Scapegoat Mechanism as a Theory of Religion (René Girard)

An anthropological-philosophical proposal put forward by the French-American René Girard (1923–2015). It moves from a literary critique of desire in the European novel (1961) to a comprehensive theory of the origin of religion, violence and culture, through two central works: *Violence and the Sacred* (La Violence et le Sacré, 1972) and *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* (Des choses cachées depuis la fondation du monde, 1978). The central thesis is that religion, in its ritual origin rather than its doctrinal one, arises from a specific social mechanism, not from an abstract metaphysical need.

## Mimetic Desire and the Sacrificial Crisis

Girard holds that human desire is not spontaneous but **imitative**: an individual desires something because another "model" or "mediator" desires it, not because of the object's own qualities (a triadic structure of subject, model and object). When the model comes close to the subject within the same social space, the model turns from an exemplar into a competing rival. The "mimetic crisis" (crise mimétique) then escalates into a generalized competitive violence that threatens the cohesion of the entire community. According to Girard, this cycle is the condition that precedes the emergence of every primitive religion.

## The Scapegoat Mechanism and the Origin of Sacrifice

According to *Violence and the Sacred* (1972), the crisis is resolved through an automatic and unconscious gathering of the community's violence onto a single victim (an individual or a minority), to whom the responsibility for the crisis is falsely attributed. Killing or expelling the victim suddenly restores social peace. Inside the community, this is interpreted as a supernatural power of the victim, who is then deified after death and becomes the origin of the ritual (the symbolic re-enactment of the founding killing) and of the taboos (preventing the repetition of the act that produced the crisis). In this way Girard traces **sacrifice, ritual and taboos together** to a single origin: the concealment by a founding community of its founding violence from itself, through mythologizing and sacralizing it. This intersects directly with the theory of sacrifice of Hubert and Mauss (`con-sacrifice-theory-hubert-mauss`), but goes further by making the mechanism of collective violence, not the ritual function of sacrifice alone, the explanation of the origin of religion itself.

## The Biblical-Gospel Text: Revelation, Not Repetition

In *Things Hidden Since the Foundation of the World* (1978), Girard argues that primitive myths conceal the innocence of the victim, so that the fabricated accusation is believed. The texts of the two Testaments, especially the narratives of the Passion of Christ, reveal the victim's innocence explicitly, from the perspective of the victim rather than the persecuting community. For Girard, this distinction makes the biblical-Gospel text a unique cognitive event that dismantles the scapegoat mechanism instead of reproducing it. This thesis is the subject of sharp debate between his theological readers, who adopt it, and secular anthropological readers, who reject the claim of religious uniqueness for any particular text.

## The Theological Application: Schwager and the Conference on Violence and Religion

While Girard presents his reading of the biblical-Gospel text (above) from an anthropological-literary angle that he takes to be descriptive, his most prominent theological disciple, Raymund Schwager (`thk-raymund-schwager`), went further in his central work *Must There Be Scapegoats?* (German original *Brauchen wir einen Sündenbock?*, 1978; English translation 1987; `wrk-must-there-be-scapegoats-schwager`). Schwager traced a gradual path through the Old Testament, from Cain and Abel to the Songs of the Suffering Servant of the Lord, in which revelation, in his view, unveils the scapegoat mechanism step by step before it reaches its peak in the Passion of Christ. On that basis he built a complete normative theology (a "dramatic theology") that presents redemption as a divine disclosure of purely human violence, not as divine punishment. In 1990 Schwager was among the founders of the Colloquium on Violence and Religion (COV&R). He thereby helped transform Girard's theory from an individual proposal into an organized interdisciplinary research field that brings together literature, theology and anthropology annually.

## Reception and Critique

Research on the theory has continued through the Colloquium on Violence and Religion (COV&R), which has met annually since 1990 and brings together researchers in comparative literature, theology and anthropology. The most prominent criticisms, as Wolfgang Palaver (`thk-wolfgang-palaver`, 2013) documents in his reference study, charge the theory with excessive, unfalsifiable comprehensiveness: any text or myth can be read as a representation of the mechanism, without a clear criterion for exclusion. Field anthropologists also object to Girard's generalization, which comes from literary criticism rather than direct ethnography, drawn from textual readings of diverse primitive societies.

More precise than this general objection is a specific critique made directly by Jonathan Z. Smith at the Stanford debate of 1983 (`crt-jonathan-z-smith-critique-girard-scapegoat-mechanism`). Smith argued that Girard theorizes from a literary-mythic exception and generalizes it to actual ritual sacrifice, which is controlled and "domesticated" in the ethnographic record, not chaotic and random as Girard's scenario assumes. The same debate brought Girard together with a third, competing party: Walter Burkert (`thk-walter-burkert`). In *Homo Necans* (1972), Burkert proposed an alternative origin for sacrifice that is biological-evolutionary rather than social-psychological, namely an inherited anxiety about killing from the stage of human hunting. His proposal rests on the direct Greek ritual record rather than on a cross-cultural literary generalization.
