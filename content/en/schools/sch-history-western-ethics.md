# History of Western Ethics

## Overview and Methodological Note

This entry traces the broad course of Western moral philosophy from ancient Greece to the twentieth century, as a sequence of successive answers to a single question: what is the good life, and what makes an action right? Before turning to this history, it should be noted that restricting the account to the "Western" tradition does not mean that moral thought in the Islamic heritage had no parallel and independent history. Theologians (the Mu'tazila and the Ash'ari), philosophers (al-Farabi and Ibn Sina), and writers on ethics (Miskawayh and al-Ghazali) developed comprehensive systems on questions of good and evil and the refinement of the soul. These systems interacted with the Greek heritage, especially Aristotle, through the translation movement, yet they produced distinctive original syntheses of their own. That history deserves separate treatment running parallel to the Western account, not treated as subordinate to it.

## Ancient Greece: From Nature to Virtue

Western moral philosophy began with the Sophists, who raised the question of relativism (Protagoras: "man is the measure of all things"). Socrates (470–399 BCE) then set out a counter-project, seeking universal and stable definitions of the virtues (justice, courage, piety) through dialectical questioning. On the view Plato attributes to him, virtue is a kind of knowledge and no one does wrong knowingly. His pupil Plato (428–348 BCE) developed this foundation in the theory of Forms, holding that "the Good" is a transcendent paradigm separate from the sensible world. He argued that the just soul is one in which its three faculties (rational, spirited, and appetitive) are in harmony under the guidance of reason, as elaborated in the dialogue *The Republic*. Plato's pupil Aristotle (384–322 BCE), in the *Nicomachean Ethics*, established the tradition of virtue ethics. He defined happiness (eudaimonia) as the ultimate end of human action, and virtue as a mean between excess and deficiency, acquired through habit and practice.

After Aristotle's death, two major schools emerged in the Hellenistic age. Stoicism (Zeno the Stoic, then Epictetus and later Marcus Aurelius) made virtue the sole true good and called for living in accordance with universal reason (logos), with indifference to the blows of fortune. Epicureanism (Epicurus) made tranquil pleasure (ataraxia, the freedom of the soul from anxiety) the goal of life, not the boisterous sensual pleasure with which it was popularly and wrongly associated.

## The Middle Ages: Theological Ethics

With the rise of Christianity, Augustine (354–430) reformulated Platonic ethics within a theological framework. He made the love of God the supreme end and linked virtue to divine grace rather than to rational effort alone, because of the doctrine of original sin. In the thirteenth century, Thomas Aquinas achieved a vast synthesis of Aristotelianism, which reached Latin Europe partly through Arabic-Latin translations and the commentaries of Ibn Rushd (Averroes), and Christian doctrine, in his *Summa Theologiae*. In it he founded the theory of natural law, which holds that human reason can grasp general moral principles (such as preserving life, knowledge, and society) rooted in the nature created by God. This framework remains influential in Catholic moral theology.

## Early Modernity: From Contract to Duty

From the seventeenth century, theories of the social contract emerged. Hobbes, in *Leviathan* (1651), saw political obligation as the product of a rational agreement to leave the "state of nature," which he regarded as a condition of war of all against all. Locke, in the *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), grounded natural rights to life, liberty and property prior to the state. In the eighteenth century, British ethics divided between the "moral sense" school (Shaftesbury and Hutcheson), which held that humans possess an intuitive faculty, akin to the aesthetic sense, that perceives the good, and David Hume. Hume reversed the traditional equation by making the passions, rather than reason, the basis of morality ("reason is the slave of the passions"). He also formulated his well-known dilemma on the impossibility of deriving "ought" from "is".

The next decisive development came with Immanuel Kant (1724–1804). In the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) and the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), he founded deontological ethics, making autonomous practical reason the source of moral law through the "categorical imperative," and rejecting the grounding of ethics in consequences or feelings. In opposition, Jeremy Bentham laid the foundations of classical utilitarianism in the eighteenth century, and John Stuart Mill developed it in the nineteenth. Both made the maximization of total pleasure and the minimization of pain the criterion of moral rightness. The tension between Kantianism and utilitarianism thus became the dominant axis of modern normative ethics, and it remains so today.

## The Nineteenth and Twentieth Centuries: Deconstruction and Revival

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) posed a radical challenge to both camps. Proclaiming the "death of God," he called in *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886) and *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887) for a "revaluation of all values," and he traced, in his view, the origin of Christian "slave morality" to resentment and weakness. At the start of the twentieth century, G. E. Moore helped found analytic metaethics in *Principia Ethica* (1903), arguing that "good" is a simple, non-natural property and charging naturalist definitions of it with the "naturalistic fallacy." A counter-linguistic challenge then arrived with logical positivism and the "emotivist" ethics of A. J. Ayer and Charles Stevenson, which reduced moral judgments to expressions of approval rather than genuine propositions. The mid-century saw a revival of virtue ethics with G. E. M. Anscombe's article "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958), which called for a return to Aristotle. Anscombe argued that the concept of "duty" was historically tied to the idea of divine law, which had lost its philosophical support in a secular age.

## Worked Example: The Question of Lying Through History

The question "Is it permissible to lie to save the life of an innocent person?" illustrates the transformations of this history. An Aristotelian virtue ethicist asks what a wise and courageous person would do in that situation. Thomas Aquinas holds that lying is wrong by its nature, but that one may sometimes conceal the truth, and some later writers permitted verbal devices such as equivocation or mental reservation. Kant, in his most controversial position (in the 1797 essay "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy"), insists that lying is forbidden even to a murderer asking where an intended victim is hiding, because a lie cannot be willed as a universal law. Utilitarians permit it where it produces the best outcome. The advocate of the ethics of care holds that the answer depends on the concrete relationship with the persons concerned, not on a general, abstract rule.

## Contemporary Impact

This long history forms the groundwork on which contemporary applied ethics rests, whether medical, environmental, or political. Specialists still use Kantian, utilitarian, and Aristotelian tools side by side in analyzing contemporary issues such as abortion, artificial intelligence, and climate change. Familiarity with this history is therefore a prerequisite for understanding any contemporary moral debate in Western philosophy.
