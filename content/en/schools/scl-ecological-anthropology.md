# Ecological Anthropology

Ecological anthropology studies human beings as part of ecosystems: how populations adapt, how subsistence and ritual regulate the use of resources, and how human activity transforms the world it depends on. Its core idea is adaptation: cultures are ways of living in particular environments, shaped by obtaining food, water, shelter, and energy, and by sustaining those resources over time. Where other branches begin with meaning or power, it begins with the material conditions of life — calories, rainfall, soil, and seasons.

It should not be confused with environmental anthropology, its younger and broader sibling. Ecological anthropology is the older tradition, rooted in the 1950s and 1960s, centred on adaptation, energy flow, and the ecosystem as a unit of analysis, and built on the work of Julian Steward, Roy Rappaport, and Andrew Vayda. Environmental anthropology, which emerged from the 1980s onwards, is wider: it takes in political ecology, conservation, environmental justice, and climate change, treating ecological anthropology's adaptation framework as one strand among several.

## History

Its origins lie in Julian Steward's cultural ecology. Steward had worked among the Shoshone of the Great Basin in the 1930s, where small, mobile family groups tracked the seasonal availability of seeds and game. In Theory of Culture Change (1955), he argued that cultures adapt through a "cultural core" of subsistence technology and social organisation, and that similar environments could produce similar adaptations — a multilinear evolution rejecting nineteenth-century unilinear evolutionism and Boasian particularism.

In the 1960s the school took a second, more ecological turn. Andrew Vayda and Roy Rappaport, working at Columbia University, argued that anthropologists should study human populations as parts of ecosystems, using biological concepts such as energy flow and carrying capacity. Rappaport's Pigs for the Ancestors (1968) became the school's classic demonstration. Among the Tsembaga Maring of Papua New Guinea, the ritual cycle of pig feasts regulated the pig population, distributed protein, relieved pressure on gardens, and timed cycles of warfare and truce — a religious system that functioned as an ecological regulator, and one of the first studies to measure energy flows through a human community.

The 1970s brought expansion and crisis. The energy crisis and the environmental movement gave the field new urgency, but critics attacked its functionalism: rituals were explained by their ecological effects, whether or not the people performing them understood those effects, and human agency, history, and power seemed to disappear into systems models. In the 1980s and 1990s the field responded by absorbing political ecology and taking indigenous knowledge seriously as environmental understanding.

## Key Figures

Julian Steward (1902–1972) is the school's founder, the author of cultural ecology and the concept of the cultural core. His work on the Shoshone and comparative analysis of early irrigation civilizations established the study of adaptation through subsistence and technology. Roy Rappaport (1926–1997) is its most famous practitioner: his Tsembaga Maring work fused ecology, cybernetics, and ritual theory, and his later Ecology, Meaning, and Religion (1979) argued that religion and ecology could not be separated, because ritual regulates human relations with the world.

Andrew Vayda, Rappaport's collaborator at Columbia and later at Rutgers, was the school's methodologist and its most persistent internal critic. He argued that anthropologists should study concrete human actions and their environmental consequences rather than assuming cultures were adapted systems, and his later work on forest fires in Indonesia showed how local actions, state policies, and global markets combined to produce environmental change. Emilio Moran, working in Amazonia, documented how farmers, ranchers, and indigenous peoples use and transform the rainforest.

## Method

Its method combines ethnography and ecological measurement. Researchers live in a community for a year or more, mapping land use, weighing harvests, counting calories, and tracing flows of energy and materials through households and ecosystems. Time allocation studies, dietary surveys, and ecological surveys document how people spend their days, what they eat, and the plants, animals, and soils they depend on. The method is comparative and often quantitative, increasingly using satellite imagery and GIS mapping.

## Influence and Contemporary Debates

Its influence has been wide. It supplied the framework for human ecology, the anthropology of subsistence, and much of environmental archaeology. Its attention to local environmental knowledge influenced development policy and conservation. Its demonstration that ritual could have ecological functions changed the study of religion.

Two frontiers dominate its present debates. The first is climate change: the field's tools — long-term observation, local knowledge, and the study of adaptation — are now applied to communities facing warming and sea-level rise, from Arctic hunters to Pacific islanders, and the field debates whether adaptation is adequate to a crisis produced by global systems rather than local environments. The second is indigenous knowledge: the peoples once studied as objects of adaptation are themselves sophisticated ecologists, whose knowledge must be treated as a partner in research rather than data to be extracted. The school's future lies in this partnership — studying adaptation not from above, but with the people who practise it.
