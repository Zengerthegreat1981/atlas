# Cultural Ecology & Neo-Evolutionism

A branch of the American school, founded by Julian Steward in the late 1930s, that studies the adaptive relationship between the natural environment and the social, economic and technological organization of human societies.

## Central Idea

This orientation holds that certain features of social and economic organization (the "cultural core": patterns of work, property and family organization) arise as adaptations to specific environmental constraints (climate, resource availability, terrain), while other elements of culture (art, religion, language) are less directly constrained by the immediate environment. It differs from classical unilinear evolutionism by assuming "multilinear evolution": societies in similar environments may independently reach similar adaptive solutions, without a single universal evolutionary law.

## Central Works

Steward, *Theory of Culture Change* (1955): in it he formulated the method and applied it to the societies of the Great Basin in North America, linking the scattering of social organization to the scarcity of desert resources.

## Impact

This orientation paved the way for the emergence of "cultural materialism" in the work of Marvin Harris, who was influenced by Steward, and for "neo-evolutionism" in the work of Elman Service and Marshall Sahlins. It established an ecological-economic tradition that remained influential in American anthropology until the late twentieth century.

## Detailed Examples

Steward applied his method in detail to the Shoshone societies of the Great Basin desert of the American West. He showed how severe seasonal shortages of food caused social organization to break down into small, mobile family units instead of the large tribal gatherings found in more abundant environments. By contrast, the salmon-rich societies of the Northwest Coast, with abundant and storable food, developed ranked social hierarchies and craft specialization. Such contrasts between adaptations on the same continent illustrated the thesis of "multilinear evolution."

## Later Development: Neo-Evolutionism

*Evolution and Culture* (1960), a volume edited by Marshall Sahlins and Elman Service, drew an important distinction between "general evolution" (the overall trend toward increasing complexity of human social systems over history) and "specific evolution" (the diverse adaptive paths of each particular society within its specific environment). The aim was to reconcile Steward's multilinear vision with the need for a general evolutionary model that does not fall into the pitfalls of the older classical unilinear evolutionism. The idea of "general evolution" drew on the earlier work of Leslie White (*The Science of Culture*, 1949), who held that cultural evolution is measured by the amount of energy harnessed per capita per year, a more strictly universal scheme than Steward's multilinear one.

## Institutional Context

After early university teaching, Julian Steward worked at the Bureau of American Ethnology of the Smithsonian Institution. He edited the multi-volume *Handbook of South American Indians* (1946–1950), which gathered comparative data on dozens of indigenous societies across the continent. This experience deepened his conviction in the importance of systematic comparison between different environments, rather than the isolated single-case studies that dominated the Boasian tradition. Steward then taught at Columbia University and the University of Illinois. His students at Columbia included Eric Wolf and Sidney Mintz, and his ideas also influenced Marvin Harris; these scholars went on to develop various directions (cultural materialism, political economy) from the ecological foundation laid by their teacher.

## Limits of the Approach

Steward's method is sometimes criticized for its strong focus on the economic-ecological "cultural core," which may marginalize important historical and political factors that cannot be reduced to direct environmental adaptation, such as conquest, migration and long-distance trade. These shaped many societies as much as their immediate local environment did. This was addressed later, in more detail, by the political economy school of Eric Wolf.

## Comparison with British Functionalism

Steward's approach differs fundamentally from the British functionalism that prevailed at roughly the same time. Whereas the functionalists (Radcliffe-Brown and Malinowski) focused on how the elements of a single society integrate to produce internal social stability, with little interest in cross-cultural comparison or historical change, Steward was specifically concerned with the systematic comparison of multiple societies in different environments, and with understanding the mechanisms of change and adaptation over time rather than merely describing a static state of equilibrium at a single ethnographic moment. This interest in history and change made Steward's work an important bridge between the historical concerns of classical evolutionism and the relatively static functionalism of the British school.

Steward's legacy remains strongly present in contemporary environmental anthropology and studies of climate change, which continue his tradition of linking local social patterns to concrete environmental constraints, although they now add a broader historical and political dimension that was not present to the same degree in Steward's original formulation of the method. The concept of the "cultural core" also remains a basic teaching tool for training students to think about the relationship between economy, environment and social organization as an interconnected system, rather than as separate elements that can each be studied in isolation.

Thus this orientation offers an early and precise model of how to combine ethnographic sensitivity to local detail with a broader comparative ambition that seeks patterns generalizable across contexts, without falling into the hasty generalization that classical evolutionism had fallen into before.
