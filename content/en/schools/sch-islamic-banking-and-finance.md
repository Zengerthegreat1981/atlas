# Islamic Banking and Finance

**Islamic banking and finance** is the applied arm of contemporary Islamic economics. It comprises the financial institutions and contractual forms designed to offer banking and financing services as alternatives to lending at fixed interest. Its basis is the prohibition of riba (usury, or interest) in Islamic jurisprudence, and it operates through contracts based on profit-and-loss sharing or on Shariah-compliant deferred sales.

## The Pioneering Experiment: The Mit Ghamr Local Savings Bank

**Ahmad al-Najjar** (1931–1996) is regarded as the first practical pioneer of this school. In 1963 he founded the Mit Ghamr Local Savings Bank in the city of Mit Ghamr in Egypt, the first documented modern Islamic banking experiment. It was modelled on German cooperative savings banks, but with forms compatible with the prohibition of riba. It offered Egyptian farmers savings and small-scale financing services based on profit sharing instead of fixed interest. The experiment was closed after a few years for administrative and political reasons, but it established a practical model that was later resumed on a wider scale.

## Institutional Expansion from the 1970s

Islamic banking then expanded institutionally with the founding of Dubai Islamic Bank and the Islamic Development Bank in 1975, followed by a network of Islamic banks in Egypt, Saudi Arabia and the Gulf. This was accompanied by a parallel theoretical effort, developed by economists such as Muhammad Anas Zarqa and Monzer Kahf, to formulate the jurisprudential and economic foundations of Islamic finance contracts and to extend their scope from small savings to corporate financing and international trade.

## Core Contractual Forms

Islamic banking rests on a set of contractual forms that replace the loan bearing riba:

- **Murabaha**: the sale of a commodity at a deferred price that includes a known profit margin, instead of interest on a cash loan.
- **Mudaraba**: a partnership in which one party provides the capital and the other provides the labour. Profit is divided at an agreed ratio, while the capital provider alone bears the financial loss.
- **Musharaka**: a partnership in which both parties contribute capital and labour, sharing profit and loss according to each party's share.

These forms seek to move the financing relationship from a creditor-debtor relation with guaranteed return to a partnership in the actual risk of investment.

## Later Instruments: Sukuk and Takaful

Islamic banking later developed to include instruments broader than deposits and individual loans. The most prominent are **sukuk** (certificates of ownership in income-generating assets, replacing conventional bonds based on debt and interest) and **takaful** (a mutual cooperative insurance system based on reciprocal donation, in place of the conventional commercial insurance contract based on selling risk). This extended the school's scope from narrow banking to an integrated Islamic financial system encompassing both debt markets and the insurance industry.

## Criticism and the Debate over Convergence with the Conventional System

This current faces an influential critique, most notably that put forward by **Timur Kuran** in *Islam and Mammon* (2004). According to Kuran, many Islamic finance forms, above all murabaha, produce in actual economic terms (risk distribution, effective rate of return, repayment structure) results very close to conventional lending at riba, despite the difference in formal contractual wording. This raises the question of whether the distinction between the two systems is economically real or primarily a formal jurisprudential one.

From within the current itself, a more sympathetic criticism holds that in practice the predominance of murabaha (the least risky form for the bank) over mudaraba and musharaka (which are most committed to the spirit of profit-and-loss sharing) departs from the founding vision of this current, as formulated by Muhammad Baqir al-Sadr and others.

## Global Spread and Regulatory Oversight

Islamic banking spread from Egypt and the Gulf to Malaysia, Southeast Asia, Europe and North America. Specialized regulatory bodies were established to oversee its Shariah compliance, most notably the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) and the Islamic Financial Services Board. They aim to standardize Shariah and accounting standards across different jurisdictions, in which the rulings of banking Shariah boards on the permissibility of a particular form sometimes differ considerably.

## Islamic Microfinance and Financial Inclusion

Alongside large commercial Islamic banking, a specialized branch of Islamic finance has developed for microfinance, recovering the founding spirit of the Mit Ghamr experiment. It offers small savings and financing services to poor or rural populations whom large commercial banks often exclude, because the transaction sizes they require are relatively small. The aim is to widen financial inclusion in relatively poor Muslim communities in South Asia and Africa. The field is growing, supported by international institutions such as the Islamic Development Bank, which regards it as a tool of social development rather than merely a profit-oriented banking product.
