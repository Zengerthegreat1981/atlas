# Existential Phenomenology

Existential phenomenology is the direction that carried the phenomenological method from the analysis of transcendental consciousness to the analysis of human existence as lived in the world. It made the ontological question prior to the epistemological one. This turn began with Martin Heidegger in "Being and Time" (1927), and continued in France with Sartre, Merleau-Ponty, Beauvoir and Marcel. This page treats this existential turn itself; the original Husserlian method is dealt with on the page on phenomenology in general.

## From Husserl to Heidegger

Heidegger (1889–1976) was Husserl's assistant in Freiburg, and succeeded him in his chair in 1928. In his early lectures (such as the 1923 course "Hermeneutics of Facticity") he began to recast the method: the starting point is not consciousness but the being for whom its own being is at issue, which he called "Dasein". The question of Being, that is, what it means for a being to be, is the one that had been neglected since the Greeks. "Being and Time" appeared in the "Yearbook for Philosophy and Phenomenological Research" (1927) and was dedicated to Husserl. Its structure contains the categories that became the language of the school: being-in-the-world, care, mood (Befindlichkeit), falling into the "they", anxiety, being-toward-death, and authenticity. Each of these structures is drawn out of a description of everyday life, not from theoretical inference.

## The French formulation

The French carried these ideas into another domain: freedom, history and the body.

- **Sartre** (1905–1980): in "Being and Nothingness" (1943) he distinguishes being-in-itself from being-for-itself, holds that consciousness is a nothingness independent of things, and that man is condemned to be free. The phrase "existence precedes essence" he presented in the lecture "Existentialism Is a Humanism" (delivered in October 1945, published 1946).
- **Merleau-Ponty** (1908–1961): in "Phenomenology of Perception" (1945) he made the lived body, rather than pure consciousness, the axis of experience, and criticized the duality of subject and object.
- **Simone de Beauvoir** (1908–1986): in "The Ethics of Ambiguity" (1947) and "The Second Sex" (1949) she recast existential concepts in an analysis of the historical situation of women, and formulated her saying "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman".
- **Gabriel Marcel** (1889–1973): he presented "Christian existentialism" in the "Metaphysical Journal" (1927) and in "Being and Having" (1935), and distinguished between problem and mystery.
- **Karl Jaspers** (1883–1969): he approached the subject from the "philosophy of existence" (1932), although he was not a phenomenologist in the methodological sense.

## Founding concepts

- **Being-in-the-world**: man does not first exist and then enter a world; from the outset he is open to a world of meanings and concerns.
- **Thrownness (Geworfenheit)**: we find ourselves in a factual condition we did not choose, with a history, a language and a body.
- **Authenticity**: that Dasein takes over its existence as its own, rather than dissolving into what "people" do. For Heidegger it carries no moral content but is an existential structure.
- **The Other**: for Sartre the relation to the other is based on the "look" and on conflict, and for Heidegger on "being-with".

## Existentialism and Existential Phenomenology

The two labels overlap but are not identical. "Existentialism" is a broader cultural and philosophical category that includes figures such as Kierkegaard, Jaspers, and Marcel, who were not phenomenologists in Husserl's sense, and writers such as Camus, who rejected the label. "Existential phenomenology" more narrowly designates the use of Husserl's descriptive method to analyze lived existence, as in Heidegger, Sartre, and Merleau-Ponty. Heidegger himself rejected the existentialist label, and the literature does not use the terms uniformly.

## The later turn

After 1930 Heidegger began to speak of the "turn" (Kehre): man became the site of the disclosure of Being, and interest shifted to the history of Being, poetry and technology. In the "Letter on Humanism" (1947) he replied to Sartre and refused to have his philosophy count as existentialist in the Sartrean sense. The debate remains open about the degree of continuity between the early and the late Heidegger.

## Influence

- **Existential psychotherapy**: Ludwig Binswanger and Medard Boss founded "Daseinsanalysis" on the basis of Heidegger, and Rollo May introduced the approach to the United States in "Existence" (1958).
- **Arab thought**: Abd al-Rahman Badawi was an early and influential introducer of existentialism in Arabic, in "Existential Time" (1943). Fathi al-Meskini translated "Being and Time" (2012), which opened a new debate about the terminology of Being in Arabic.
- **Psychology and medicine**: the phenomenological description of the patient's experience (anxiety, depression, the ill body) became a later reference in phenomenological psychiatry and in studies of chronic illness.
- **Social critique and feminism**: the concepts were employed in studies of gender, race and disability.
- **Translation**: the English translation of "Being and Nothingness" appeared in 1956 and that of "Being and Time" in 1962, and the difficulty of the terminology contributed to misunderstanding.

## Limits

- Analytic phenomenology and realism reject the obscure language and doubt the possibility of combining description with ontology.
- Heidegger's membership of the Nazi Party (1933) raises questions about the relation of his philosophy to politics, and the "Black Notebooks", published since 2014, reopened the debate.
- Levinas accused Heideggerian ontology of putting Being before ethics and the Other, and Marxists accused existentialism of subjectivism.
