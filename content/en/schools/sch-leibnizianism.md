# Leibnizianism

A philosophical tradition deriving from **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646-1716), German philosopher, mathematician, diplomat, and librarian. Leibnizianism is among the most ambitious systems of **Continental Rationalism**, combining mathematics (Leibniz developed the differential and integral calculus, independently of Newton), logic, metaphysics, theology, and law. Its best-known doctrines are the theory of monads, pre-established harmony, the principle of sufficient reason, and the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds. It shaped Christian Wolff and German university philosophy and provoked Kant's critical reaction; Voltaire satirized its optimism.

## Founding Context

Leibniz was born in Leipzig, the son of a professor of moral philosophy, and was largely self-educated in an encyclopedic range of subjects: law, mathematics, philosophy, theology, history, and diplomacy. A diplomatic mission to Paris (1672-1676) brought him into contact with Huygens and Arnauld and with the mathematics from which he developed the calculus. From 1676 he served the court of Hanover as counsellor, historian, and librarian. He promoted the founding of the Berlin Society of Sciences (1700) and advised Peter the Great on an academy in St Petersburg, which was established in 1724-1725 after his death.

## Leading Figures

- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646-1716): the central figure.
- **Samuel Clarke** (1675-1729): Newton's defender in the *Leibniz-Clarke Correspondence* (1715-1716).
- **Christian Wolff** (1679-1754): systematizer of Leibniz's rationalism in German, beginning with the *German Metaphysics* (1719); his teaching dominated German universities for decades.
- **Christian August Crusius** (1715-1775): a Pietist critic of the Leibniz-Wolff system.
- **Immanuel Kant** (1724-1804): trained in the Leibniz-Wolff tradition, whose pre-critical works (such as the *Monadologia physica*, 1756) engage Leibnizian themes before the critical turn.
- **Nicolas Malebranche** (1638-1715): an influence on Leibniz and also an opponent on occasionalism.
- **Johann Bernoulli** (1667-1748): a leading mathematical correspondent and defender of Leibnizian calculus.

## Central Concepts

### 1. The Monad

In the *Monadology* (written 1714) Leibniz describes monads as simple substances without parts, which cannot come into being or perish naturally; each mirrors the universe from its own point of view. He writes that monads have no "windows" through which anything could enter or leave (section 7).

### 2. Degrees of Perception

Monads differ in the distinctness of their perception: bare monads have only minute, unconscious perceptions; animal souls add sensation and memory; rational souls (spirits) have reason and self-awareness. God is the supreme, original simple substance.

### 3. The Principle of Sufficient Reason

Nothing happens without a reason why it is so and not otherwise. With the principle of contradiction, it is one of the two great principles of Leibniz's reasoning.

### 4. The Identity of Indiscernibles

No two distinct substances can be exactly alike in all their intrinsic properties. Leibniz uses this against atomism and absolute space in the correspondence with Clarke.

### 5. Pre-established Harmony

God created each substance so that its states unfold in exact correspondence with those of every other, without causal interaction between them. This was Leibniz's solution to the problem of mind-body relation left by Descartes and his alternative to Malebranche's occasionalism.

### 6. The Best of All Possible Worlds

God, being perfectly wise and good, chose the best of the infinitely many possible worlds. Evil is permitted not as a good in itself but as a consequence of the best overall order. Voltaire mocked this "optimism" in *Candide* (1759).

### 7. Necessary and Contingent Truths

Necessary truths (such as mathematical truths) have contrary statements that imply contradiction and hold in all possible worlds; contingent truths of fact, though certain, could have been otherwise.

### 8. Theodicy

In the *Théodicée* (1710), written partly in reply to Pierre Bayle, Leibniz argues that faith and reason are compatible and distinguishes three kinds of evil: metaphysical (finitude), physical (suffering), and moral (sin).

## Leibniz, Wolff, and Wolffianism

The relation between Leibniz and the Leibniz-Wolffian school is historically contested. Wolff transformed Leibniz's scattered essays and letters into a systematic, demonstrative, German-language philosophy, dropping or reinterpreting doctrines such as monads and pre-established harmony in some of his writings. Later critics, including Kant and Crusius, often attacked Wolffian scholasticism, which they identified with Leibniz himself; many scholars therefore distinguish Leibniz's own philosophy from the school that bears his name.

## Relation to Other Schools

- **Cartesianism**: an extension and critique, rejecting Descartes's identification of matter with extension and his interactionist dualism.
- **Spinozism**: Leibniz rejects the unity of substance in favour of a plurality of substances, though he engaged closely with Spinoza's work.
- **Occasionalism**: a critique of continuous divine intervention; pre-established harmony is the alternative.
- **Empiricism**: Locke argued against innate ideas, and Leibniz replied in the *New Essays*, written c. 1704 and published in 1765.
- **Scholasticism**: Leibniz drew on scholastic notions such as substance and substantial forms.

## Later Influence

- **In mathematics**: Leibniz's notation for the calculus is the one still used; the priority dispute with Newton, pressed by Newton's supporters after 1710, was acrimonious.
- **In German philosophy**: Wolffianism dominated German universities in the early eighteenth century, and Kant's critical philosophy developed in reaction to it.
- **In logic and computing**: his project of a universal characteristic and a calculus of reasoning anticipated modern symbolic logic, and his binary arithmetic (1703) and stepped reckoner are early steps toward computing; Russell's *A Critical Exposition of the Philosophy of Leibniz* (1900) helped bring his logic back to attention.
- **In metaphysics and semantics**: the idea of possible worlds, used in modal logic from the twentieth century, goes back to Leibniz.
- **In contemporary philosophy**: Gilles Deleuze re-read Leibniz in *Le Pli: Leibniz et le Baroque* (1988).

## Tensions and Criticism

- **With Newtonian science**: in the Clarke correspondence Leibniz opposed absolute space and the idea that God must repair the world-machine, while Clarke defended Newton's position.
- **With theology and experience**: the problem of evil remained pressing; Voltaire's response to the Lisbon earthquake (1755) turned optimism into a target of ridicule.
- **With individuality**: monads "without windows" raise the question of how substances interact or know one another, which pre-established harmony answers only by appeal to God.
- **With Kant**: Kant argued that the principle of sufficient reason and the concept of monads exceed the limits of possible experience.
- **With physics**: monads are metaphysical substances and should not be confused with physical atoms or particles.
