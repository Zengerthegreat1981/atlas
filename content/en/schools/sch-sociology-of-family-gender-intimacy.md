# Sociology of Family, Gender & Intimacy

The field's shift from the Parsonsian functionalist conception of the "stable" nuclear family to Giddens's analysis of how intimate relationships in late modernity move toward more negotiated and fluid forms.

## Central Idea

In *Family, Socialization and Interaction Process* (1955), Talcott Parsons and Robert Bales offered a functionalist analysis of the American nuclear family of the 1950s. They divided roles between an "instrumental" role for the husband (earning income, linking the family to the outside world) and an "expressive" role for the wife (emotional care, internal cohesion). They regarded this division as functionally necessary for family stability, although it was later sharply criticized as an ideological justification of an unequal gendered division of labor rather than an objective functional necessity. Anthony Giddens later developed a radically opposed analysis in *The Transformation of Intimacy* (1992): as rigid traditional gender roles recede, contemporary intimate relationships move toward the "pure relationship", one that continues only as long as each partner finds continuing mutual satisfaction in it, and not by virtue of a permanent external institutional commitment (religious marriage, social pressure). This makes such relationships potentially more egalitarian but also more fragile and liable to end.

## Historical Context

Parsons and Bales's 1955 analysis came at the height of the flourishing of the American middle-class nuclear family after the Second World War. Economic prosperity and relative job stability made the "male breadwinner/housewife" model look like a "natural," historically stable pattern. Giddens's work came four decades later, after radical transformations: women's wide entry into the labor market, the spread of effective contraception, rising divorce rates, and the emergence of the women's liberation movement and gay-rights movements. In Giddens's view these were structural transformations far deeper than a mere superficial change in values.

## Related Historical Event

A pivotal historical event is linked to this shift: the US Supreme Court decision in *Loving v. Virginia* (1967), which struck down the American laws that had prohibited marriage between different races. The decision reflected a deep change in American society's conception of the limits of "legitimate intimacy," paving the way for a comparable later expansion that included legal recognition of same-sex marriage decades afterward, along a path that intersects directly with Giddens's thesis of intimacy's gradual liberation from traditional external institutional and legal constraints.

## Prominent Figures

Alongside Giddens, scholars such as Arlie Hochschild (who studied the "Second Shift", the double burden of housework that falls on the working woman after she returns from her formal job) and Judith Stacey (who studied the diversity of postmodern, non-nuclear family forms) helped extend this field toward a more complex analysis of the persistence of real gender inequality despite the official discourse of the egalitarian "pure relationship" in Giddens.

## Impact and Critique

These two successive frameworks presented a picture of a radical transformation in the structure of the family and of intimate relationships across the second half of the twentieth century, influencing the sociology of gender and of contemporary marriage. The Parsons–Bales analysis was sharply criticized by the feminist movement as a scientific-looking ideological justification of an unequal gendered division of labor, falsely presented as an objective functional necessity. Giddens's optimism about the "pure relationship" has been questioned by later scholars of gender and work, who argue that it overstates the equality actually achieved and passes over persistent gaps in the distribution of housework and child care between partners. Hochschild's *The Second Shift* (1989), based on interviews and observation of dual-earner couples in the United States, argued that many employed women effectively worked a "second shift" of unpaid domestic labor after their paid job, while their partners typically did less of it. The study rested on a small, non-representative sample, and later time-use research has shown that the gap has narrowed over time without disappearing. The term "second shift" has become a standard reference in studies of gender and work and has been taken up in research on many other societies.
