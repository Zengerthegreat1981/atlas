# Historical Anthropology

Historical anthropology is the study of societies across time: it brings the anthropologist's attention to culture, meaning, and everyday life together with the historian's attention to change, documents, and long-term process. Where classical ethnography froze its subjects in an "ethnographic present," historical anthropology insists that every society has a history, that the present is made by the past, and that anthropology without history is a snapshot without a story. Its central question is how cultural meanings shape historical change — and how historical change, in turn, remakes culture.

## History and Emergence

Historical anthropology took shape in the 1960s and 1970s as a reaction against the timelessness of structural-functionalism. E. E. Evans-Pritchard's 1961 lecture "Anthropology and History" argued that social anthropology was closer to historiography than to natural science, and that ethnographers should treat their societies as historical products. In Britain, the Manchester school's studies of Central Africa and the work of historians such as E. P. Thompson showed how ordinary people made history from below. In the United States, the decisive influence was Eric Wolf, whose *Europe and the People Without History* (1982) argued that the peoples anthropologists studied — peasants, tribal societies, enslaved laborers — had always been part of world history, drawn into global processes of trade, conquest, and capitalism. There were no "people without history," only people whose history had been written out of the record.

The field matured in the 1980s alongside the new cultural history and the anthropological turn in history itself. Sidney Mintz's *Sweetness and Power* (1985) traced how sugar, a Caribbean plantation crop, became a staple of the English working-class diet, linking the plantation, the slave trade, and the industrial revolution in a single story of consumption and power. Marshall Sahlins's *Islands of History* (1985) showed how Hawaiians understood Captain Cook's arrival through their own cosmology — and how that understanding itself changed the course of events. Bernard Cohn's studies of colonial India demonstrated how British rule invented traditions, codified caste, and turned fluid social practices into fixed administrative categories.

## Key Figures

Eric Wolf (1923–1999), an Austrian-born American anthropologist, gave the field its program: to write the history of the world's peoples as participants in global processes rather than as isolated "cultures." Sidney Mintz (1922–2015) pioneered the anthropology of commodities and of the Caribbean plantation world. Marshall Sahlins (1930–2021) developed the theory of the "structure of the conjuncture" — the idea that historical events are structured by cultural categories and that cultural categories are transformed by events. Bernard Cohn (1928–2003) was a pioneer of the historical anthropology of colonial India, showing how colonial knowledge produced the very societies it claimed to describe. Jean Comaroff (born 1946) and John Comaroff (born 1945) developed the study of colonialism, Christianity, and consciousness in southern Africa, combining ethnography with archival research. William H. Sewell Jr. (born 1940), a historian working close to anthropology, supplied the influential theory of events and structures that many historical anthropologists still use.

## Key Works

The landmarks are Wolf's *Europe and the People Without History* (1982), Mintz's *Sweetness and Power: The Place of Sugar in Modern History* (1985), and Sahlins's *Islands of History* (1985) — three books that defined the field's ambitions. Cohn's *An Anthropologist Among the Historians and Other Essays* (1987) collected his studies of colonial knowledge in India. The Comaroffs' *Ethnography and the Historical Imagination* (1992) set out a method for joining ethnography and history. Evans-Pritchard's "Anthropology and History" (1961) remains a classic statement of the kinship between the two disciplines. Together these works established a shared premise: that culture is historical through and through, and that history is cultural through and through.

## Method

Historical anthropology's method is a deliberate hybrid. From anthropology it takes long-term ethnographic attention to meaning — to ritual, to cosmology, to the categories through which people understand their world. From history it takes the archive: colonial records, missionary reports, court cases, letters, and newspapers, read critically as products of the power relations that produced them. The method is often called ethnohistory when it reconstructs the past of peoples without written records of their own, using oral tradition alongside documents. Its distinctive move is to read sources against the grain — to find the voices of peasants, enslaved people, and colonized subjects in records written by their rulers — and to treat the ethnographic present as one moment in a longer story rather than as a timeless condition.

## Influence and Debates

Historical anthropology transformed both of its parent disciplines. It forced anthropology to abandon the timeless ethnographic present and to reckon with colonialism, capitalism, and globalization as forces that shape every society it studies. It gave historians a richer account of culture and meaning, and a way of writing the history of people who left few documents of their own. Its influence is visible in the anthropology of colonialism, in world-systems approaches, in the study of memory and heritage, and in the growing field of the anthropology of history itself.

Debates persist. Some ask how far cultural categories can explain historical change without reducing history to a clash of worldviews; others worry that the emphasis on meaning neglects material forces such as demography, ecology, and the economy. There is also an enduring tension between the historian's demand for documentary evidence and the anthropologist's reliance on oral tradition and ethnographic analogy. What the field has established beyond dispute is its premise: no society stands outside history, and no history can be understood without attending to the meanings people make of it.
