# Latin American Structuralism (CEPAL)

**Latin American structuralism**, known in short as the "CEPAL" school after the United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (CEPAL in Spanish), is a current in development economics that emerged around the Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch, whose 1949 report for the Commission (founded in 1948) became its founding manifesto; he was its Executive Secretary from 1950 to 1963. Other leading figures include the Brazilian economist Celso Furtado. It offered a structural explanation of Latin America's underdevelopment that rests on the continent's position within the structure of international trade, not on a shortage of domestic capital or savings alone.

## Challenging classical comparative advantage

The CEPAL school challenged a central assumption of classical trade theory: that specialization according to comparative advantage (the periphery exporting primary commodities and importing manufactured goods from the industrial "centre") is mutually beneficial in a way that, over time, supports the periphery's development. Prebisch argued that this model overlooks an unequal structure in the distribution of the gains from trade between the industrial centre and the primary-exporting periphery.

## The Prebisch–Singer hypothesis: deterioration of the terms of trade

Developing a parallel line with the economist Hans Singer, Prebisch formulated an influential empirical hypothesis: the terms of trade (the ratio of export prices to import prices) tend to deteriorate over the long term to the disadvantage of primary-commodity exporters. The reason is that demand for primary commodities is less responsive to income than demand for manufactured goods, and that labour unions in industrial countries are better placed to keep productivity gains as higher wages, while agricultural productivity gains in the periphery pass to consumers in the centre through lower prices. This hypothesis reversed the classical expectation that specialization in primary exports is a guaranteed route to development.

## Import-substituting industrialization

From this diagnosis the CEPAL school drew a radical policy recommendation: Latin American countries should protect their nascent industries with customs tariffs and import restrictions, in order to build a domestic industrial base that replaces imported manufactured goods instead of continuing to specialize in primary exports. This policy was actually applied in Brazil, Argentina and Mexico during the 1950s and 1960s, and achieved tangible rates of industrialization in its early decades, before facing recurrent balance-of-payments crises, chronic inflation, and a continuing dependence on imports of capital goods to operate the very protected industries.

## From structuralism to dependency theory

The structuralism of CEPAL formed an intermediate step between early development economics and the more radical dependency theory later developed by thinkers such as André Gunder Frank and Samir Amin. The latter went further than Prebisch's empirical diagnosis, asserting that underdevelopment is not a stage preceding development but a direct structural product of the incorporation of the periphery into a global capitalist system that continually reproduces dependency. This is a more comprehensive sociological position, treated in the entry on dependency theory.

## Debt crisis and decline

Structuralist policies faced a sharp credibility crisis with the Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s, when protected, industrialized economies were unable to service their accumulated external debts. This opened the way for the "Washington Consensus" wave of neoliberal trade liberalization and privatization, a competing alternative that dominated Latin American economic policy for the following two decades.

## An enduring institutional legacy

The Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (CEPAL) itself remains an active research institution to this day. It later moved away from some of its early protectionist recommendations toward a "neostructuralism" more open to trade while retaining a directive role for the state. This synthesis influenced similar debates about industrialization and trade in other developing countries outside Latin America, including some experiences of import-substituting industrialization in Egypt during the 1960s.

## Prebisch himself: from technocrat to institutional leader

Raúl Prebisch was not a purely academic theorist. He effectively led the Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean from its early years, and became the first Secretary-General of the United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) in 1964, carrying his structuralist ideas to a global platform addressing the South as a whole rather than Latin America alone, and calling for preferential trade arrangements for developing countries against the advanced industrial states.

## Distinguishing the analysis from its policy recommendation

It is important to distinguish Prebisch's analytical diagnosis (the structural deterioration of the terms of trade) from the policy recommendation derived from it (protective tariffs and import-substituting industrialization). Many economists later accepted the empirical diagnosis in part while rejecting the remedy, arguing that it generated internal distortions (inefficient domestic monopolies, currencies overvalued above their real worth) that harmed growth more than they helped. This methodological disagreement remains present in the history of development economic thought.
