# Internet Anthropology

Internet anthropology is the ethnographic study of life online. It asks how people inhabit the internet — how they form relationships, build identities, argue, play, mourn, and organise — and how online life connects to offline life. Its premise is that the internet is not a separate, virtual world but a part of the social world, woven into everyday routines, kinship, work, and politics. Internet anthropologists study websites, platforms, and virtual worlds the way earlier ethnographers studied villages: by spending time there, learning the local norms, and taking the people they meet seriously.

## History

The field emerged in the 1990s, as the internet moved from universities and laboratories into homes. Howard Rheingold's *The Virtual Community* (1993) described the WELL, an early online community, and argued that people could form genuine social bonds through screens. Sherry Turkle's *Life on the Screen* (1995) examined how users of early virtual environments experimented with identity, presenting different selves in different windows and questioning the idea of a single, unified self.

Internet anthropology took shape as an ethnographic field around 2000. Christine Hine's *Virtual Ethnography* (2000) set out the method: how to do fieldwork on the internet, how to treat online interaction as real social action, and how to follow connections between online and offline life. Daniel Miller and Don Slater's *The Internet: An Ethnographic Approach* (2000), based on fieldwork in Trinidad, showed that the internet is not a global, homogenising force but a technology that is appropriated differently in different places, and that Trinbagonians made the internet their own.

The 2000s and 2010s brought the study of virtual worlds and social media. Tom Boellstorff's *Coming of Age in Second Life* (2008) was a landmark: two years of fieldwork inside the virtual world Second Life, conducted entirely through an avatar, demonstrating that virtual worlds are real places where people live meaningful social lives. *Ethnography and Virtual Worlds* (2012), by Boellstorff and colleagues, codified the method. Heather Horst and Daniel Miller's *Digital Anthropology* (2012) consolidated the field, and Miller's *Tales from Facebook* (2011) and the comparative project *How the World Changed Social Media* (2016), by Miller and colleagues, showed how social media are used differently across eight countries, from Trinidad to China.

## Key Figures and Works

Rheingold (1993) popularised the idea of the virtual community and argued for its social reality. Turkle (1995) showed that online identity is fluid and experimental, and her work became a touchstone for debates about the self in digital life. Hine (2000) gave the field its method, insisting that the internet must be studied ethnographically, as a place where people act, not as a text to be read from outside.

Miller and Slater (2000) established the field's comparative principle: the internet is always local, shaped by the culture that adopts it. Boellstorff (2008) argued that ethnography could be conducted entirely inside a virtual world and that avatar life is real social life. Horst and Miller (2012) mapped the field's scope, from mobile phones to gaming, and Miller and colleagues (2016) demonstrated the power of comparative, multi-sited ethnography in showing that social media are not one thing but many, used for kinship in one place, for commerce in another, and for politics in a third.

## Method

Internet anthropology adapts classic ethnographic method to online life. Researchers create accounts, join communities, and participate over months or years, observing how people talk, share, and organise. They follow links, threads, and networks, and they conduct interviews online and offline, tracing how digital and face-to-face life intertwine. In virtual worlds, the ethnographer works through an avatar, attending events, building relationships, and learning the norms of the world from inside.

The method raises new ethical questions. Online spaces feel public but are often experienced as private, and the boundary between observing and intruding is contested. Internet anthropologists debate questions of consent, anonymity, and the quoting of online material, and they have developed norms for protecting the people they study — norms that are still evolving as platforms, algorithms, and norms of privacy change.

## Influence and Legacy

Internet anthropology changed how the social sciences understand digital life. It showed that the internet is not a placeless, cultureless space but a set of places with their own norms, histories, and politics, and that online behaviour must be understood in its local context. Its ethnographies have informed debates about identity, community, privacy, and the effects of social media, and its methods are now used across the social sciences.

The field's influence extends to policy and design. Its findings about how people actually use platforms have informed discussions of moderation, misinformation, and digital inclusion, and its insistence that users are active interpreters rather than passive consumers has shaped how technologists think about their audiences. As artificial intelligence, virtual reality, and new platforms reshape online life, internet anthropology's questions remain central: who are we when we are online, how do we live together there, and what does the internet do to the societies that adopt it?
