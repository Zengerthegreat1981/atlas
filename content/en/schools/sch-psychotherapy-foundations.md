# Foundations of Psychotherapy

This entry covers the major theoretical and therapeutic currents from which modern psychotherapy was built, from the birth of psychoanalysis at the hands of Sigmund Freud in late nineteenth-century Vienna to cognitive behavioral therapy and contemporary integrative approaches.

## Precedents in the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Practices in Islamic civilization that treated the soul through speech and regimen preceded psychotherapy in its European form. Abu Zayd al-Balkhi, in *Masalih al-abdan wa-al-anfus* (The Welfare of Bodies and Souls), addressed sorrow, anxiety, and obsessive thoughts, and recommended reformulating painful ideas and gradually confronting fears. Contemporary scholars compare this to cognitive therapy, while cautioning against projecting the modern term onto an older text. Miskawayh, in *Tahdhib al-akhlaq* (The Refinement of Character), wrote on treating diseases of the soul such as anger, fear, and grief through the correction of dispositions. Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, in *Ihya' 'ulum al-din* (The Revival of the Religious Sciences), regarded diseases of the heart as an ailment requiring a physician, struggle, and discipline. The Adudi bimaristan in Baghdad and the Mansuri bimaristan in Cairo maintained wards for people with mental illness. Michael Dols describes hospital care, regimen, and diversion in that tradition. Later accounts also mention music. These practices are not a modern school of psychotherapy. They show that attention to the soul as an object of care has a long tradition in Islamic culture.

## Psychoanalysis

Modern talking treatment began with studies of hysteria published by Josef Breuer and Freud in 1895, which opened its case histories with that of "Anna O.," who herself named the method "the talking cure." Freud then developed free association, dream analysis, and transference, reaching his first full formulation in *The Interpretation of Dreams* (published at the end of 1899 but dated 1900). The method rests on the premise that symptoms express unconscious conflicts, and that therapeutic work uncovers them through interpretation and transference. Alfred Adler and Carl Jung broke away from it. Later figures followed, including the proponents of object relations theory and self psychology, and some extended dynamic therapy to personality disorders. Shedler reviewed the empirical evidence for psychodynamic therapy in a 2010 paper in *American Psychologist*.

## Humanistic and Client-Centered Therapy

Carl Rogers published *Counseling and Psychotherapy* in 1942 and *Client-Centered Therapy* in 1951. In a 1957 paper he identified six conditions necessary and sufficient for therapeutic change, the most prominent of which were unconditional positive regard, empathy, and congruence (authenticity). Rogers was among the first to record and analyze therapy sessions empirically, which helped bring psychotherapy into the domain of quantitative research. Abraham Maslow and Rollo May joined him in the humanistic movement, which was influenced by existential philosophy.

## Behavioral and Cognitive Therapy

Behavior therapy emerged in the 1950s from learning theories. Joseph Wolpe published *Psychotherapy by Reciprocal Inhibition* in 1958 and introduced systematic desensitization. In the 1960s Aaron Beck formulated cognitive therapy for depression on the basis of observed negative automatic thoughts, and published *Cognitive Therapy and the Emotional Disorders* in 1976. Albert Ellis proposed rational emotive therapy in *Reason and Emotion in Psychotherapy* in 1962. The two lines merged into cognitive behavioral therapy, which has been tested in randomized controlled trials and has become one of the most prominent approaches in clinical guidelines.

## The Third Wave and Integration

In 2004 Steven Hayes introduced the term "third wave" for therapies built on acceptance and mindfulness, including acceptance and commitment therapy (Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson, 1999) and Marsha Linehan's dialectical behavior therapy (1993). Many therapists favor integrative practice, drawing on more than one school according to the patient.

## What All Approaches Share

Rosenzweig presented the idea of common factors shared by different therapies in 1936. Jerome Frank developed it in *Persuasion and Healing* in 1961, identifying hope, a persuasive treatment narrative, and a relationship of trust as shared elements. Luborsky and colleagues found in 1975 that compared therapies produced similar results. The meta-analysis by Smith and Glass in 1977 found that psychotherapy was associated with clear benefit compared with no treatment. In 1979 Edward Bordin identified three components of the therapeutic alliance: agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and the emotional bond. Wampold and Imel argue from meta-analytic evidence that common factors such as the alliance account for more of the variation in outcome than differences between specific therapies, a contested position; other researchers point to evidence that particular treatments work better for some disorders.

## Limits and Critique

Eysenck published a skeptical assessment in 1952 arguing that psychotherapy does not outperform spontaneous recovery. Later scholars considered this overstated, but it stimulated research. Adolf Grünbaum criticized the epistemic foundations of psychoanalysis in 1984, arguing that its clinical evidence is contaminated by therapist suggestion, and Karl Popper linked the unfalsifiability of Freudian theory to its weakness as a science. Critics from cultural psychology argue that Western models assume an independent, autonomous self and need adaptation when applied in collectivist societies, including Arab societies, where family and religion play a central role in recovery.
