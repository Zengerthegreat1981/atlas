# Radcliffe-Brownian Structural Functionalism

A second branch of British functionalism, founded by the English anthropologist Alfred Radcliffe-Brown (A. R. Radcliffe-Brown, 1881–1955) in parallel with the functionalism of Bronisław Malinowski. Its direct theoretical source is the sociology of Émile Durkheim rather than the individual psychology underlying Malinowski's work. Radcliffe-Brown taught at several universities (Cape Town, Sydney, Chicago and Oxford) and built a school of wide influence that spread across the British Empire.

## Definition

Structural functionalism is a theoretical orientation that views society as a system of stable, interrelated social relations (social structure). It holds that the function of any cultural practice or institution, from rituals to kinship rules, is measured by its contribution to the continuity and overall cohesion of this structure, not by its satisfaction of individual or psychological needs.

## Historical Context

The school took shape in the early decades of the twentieth century, when British anthropology was seeking a rigorous scientific method that would free it from Victorian evolutionary speculation. Radcliffe-Brown published his first field study, *The Andaman Islanders*, in 1922, drawing on his extended residence there before the First World War. He then moved to Australia to study Aboriginal tribes and their systems of kinship and totemism. The school remained dominant in British social anthropology from the 1920s until about the mid-1950s.

## Main Ideas and Method

Society is viewed as resembling a living organism whose parts (the institutions of kinship, economy, religion and law) are integrated to achieve overall functional equilibrium. This is an "organic analogy" that Radcliffe-Brown inherited from Durkheim through the concept of "social solidarity." He modified it so that the central analytical unit became "social structure," meaning the actual network of relations among persons and groups and their reciprocal roles, rather than Durkheim's mental "collective representations." Radcliffe-Brown applied this framework to small societies without a centralized state, analyzing how rituals, kinship rules and social relations maintain the stability of the social system and avoid tension between groups in the absence of a coercive central authority. Examples include "joking relationships," in which playful mockery is permitted between certain in-laws, and strict "avoidance" between others, such as the avoidance of a mother-in-law.

## Major Works and Representatives

His book *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952), a collection of essays drawn from his lectures, is the clearest theoretical statement of the school, alongside his field study *The Andaman Islanders* (1922). Radcliffe-Brown influenced a whole generation of British anthropologists, most notably E. E. Evans-Pritchard and Meyer Fortes, who developed his structural approach into the study of kinship, law and politics among stateless societies (the work often called "descent theory"). The two co-edited *African Political Systems* (1940). His ideas also provided the foundation from which the "Manchester School," led by Max Gluckman, later analyzed conflict and social change.

## Impact and Critique

This school remained the dominant framework of British social anthropology until the mid-twentieth century. It was later subjected to sharp criticism for its excessive ahistoricism, meaning its tendency to portray the studied societies as balanced systems outside history, change and the colonialism to which they were in fact subject at the time of the study, and for its neglect of internal conflict and individual agency. This criticism came primarily from the Manchester School itself, led by Max Gluckman, which reintroduced the concepts of conflict and historical change into structural-functional analysis rather than merely describing static equilibrium. It also came from later interpretive anthropology, notably Clifford Geertz, who criticized the reduction of cultural meaning to a purely structural function.

## Comparison with Malinowskian Functionalism

The precise distinction between Radcliffe-Brown's functionalism and Malinowski's helps explain the division of early British anthropology into two adjacent currents, despite their similar names. Malinowski, through his fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands, focused on how cultural institutions satisfy the basic biological and psychological needs of the individual (food, security, reproduction). Radcliffe-Brown turned the focus entirely toward the continuity of the collective social structure itself, regardless of the individual. This methodological difference is why Radcliffe-Brown's direct students (rather than Malinowski's) were the ones who later developed the analysis of legal and political systems in stateless societies.

## Joking and Avoidance Relationships

One of the best-known examples Radcliffe-Brown studied in detail is the phenomenon of "joking relationships." These are customary relationships between individuals of two particular kinship groups (such as those between a man and his wife's kin in some societies, or between different clans) in which public teasing and mockery are permitted without being considered an insult. This contrasts with other, stricter "avoidance" relationships, such as a man's avoidance of direct speech with his mother-in-law. Radcliffe-Brown explained this apparent contradiction by arguing that both patterns serve the same functional purpose: channelling the latent tensions produced by complex relations such as affinity in a regulated way that preserves the stability of the social structure as a whole, whether by releasing tension through permitted joking or by avoiding it altogether.
