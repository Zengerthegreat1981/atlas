# Linguistic Typology & Language Universals

A branch of linguistics that compares the structures of the world's diverse languages in search of recurring patterns and linguistic universals. Its nineteenth-century precursors classified languages by word structure (isolating, agglutinative, inflecting), and the modern approach was launched by Joseph Greenberg's landmark 1963 study of word-order universals.

## The importance of a balanced language sample

Serious typological studies take care to avoid "genetic and geographic bias" in the selection of languages. Choosing a large number of languages that are historically or geographically close (such as many European languages, for example) can statistically inflate the weight of a single structural feature inherited from a common ancestor or acquired through geographic contact. This can produce a spurious "universal" that does not reflect a genuine cognitive or functional tendency across languages. For this reason, the major typological projects adopt samples that are balanced, representing independent language families across all continents in proportions that are as equal as possible.

## Method: induction across samples

This current works by studying broad samples of the world's languages that are balanced geographically and genetically (rather than relying on a few European languages), and by recording the statistical patterns that recur in their structure, such as the order of subject, verb and object, and its relation to the position of prepositions, adjectives or question words.

## The methodological difference from generative grammar

Typology differs from generative grammar in the direction of its inference. Generative grammar posits an innate universal structure (Universal Grammar) and looks for its manifestations in languages. Typology, by contrast, starts from **comparative inductive observation** across wide samples, and formulates probabilistic (not absolute) universals. The two are competing methods for explaining the similarities among languages, and the relationship between them remains a continuing methodological debate.

## Absolute and implicational universals

Greenberg distinguished two kinds of universals. Absolute universals hold across all the world's languages without exception (though they are very rare in practice). Implicational universals, which are more common, express a relationship of co-occurrence (often statistical) between two structural properties. For example, the order of verb before object in a language is statistically linked to the order of preposition before its noun rather than after it. Implicational universals have proven to have strong predictive power across wide samples of languages that are not historically related. This has led many typologists, such as John Hawkins, to favor functional-cognitive explanations (such as ease of processing) over purely innate grammatical explanations, though the question remains debated.

## From Greenberg to the major projects: WALS and beyond

This current later expanded through the efforts of many researchers (Bernard Comrie, Martin Haspelmath, Matthew Dryer) into the construction of large typological databases covering many of the world's languages. A central reference is **The World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS)**. The 2013 online edition, edited by Matthew Dryer and Martin Haspelmath, maps on the order of 190 structural features (sound systems, word order, case, and others) across more than a thousand languages, so that typological hypotheses can be checked against a large comparative sample.

## Central typological phenomena: ergativity and numeral classifiers

Among the most prominent phenomena that typology has helped describe and compare across languages are the following. The distinction between the nominative-accusative pattern (dominant in English and Arabic) and the ergative pattern, in which the subject of an intransitive clause is treated like the object of a transitive clause, as in Basque and many Caucasian languages. **Numeral classifiers** are obligatory words that accompany numbers to specify the category of the noun being counted, as in Chinese and Japanese. **Evidentiality** is an obligatory grammatical marking that specifies the source of the speaker's knowledge of what they are saying, as in many South American languages.

## The critique of innate universals

Martin Haspelmath, one of the leading typologists of the newer generation, is known for his explicit critique of the generative hypothesis of innate grammatical universals. He argues that much of what is presented as absolute grammatical universals collapses when examined across broad and adequate typological samples, and that the patterns that genuinely recur across languages are better explained by functional factors (communicative efficiency, ease of mental processing, and shared historical development) than by a biologically inherited universal grammatical structure. He has also contributed to other global documentation projects, such as the World Loanword Database for lexical borrowing across languages.

## Legacy

Greenbergian typology shaped the contemporary comparative study of language universals, supported large documentation projects such as WALS, and influenced functional linguistics, which explains recurring structure through use and processing as well as through inherited grammar. Typological sampling has also been one reason for fieldwork on lesser-described languages, since a lost language is a lost point in a comparative sample.
