# Archaeology & Anthropology

Archaeology is the branch of anthropology that studies human societies through their material remains — the tools, settlements, burials, art, and landscapes people leave behind. Where the other branches of anthropology study living people, archaeology studies the deep past, reconstructing ways of life that no written record preserves. In the United States it has long been counted as one of anthropology's four fields, alongside cultural anthropology, biological anthropology, and linguistics; in Britain and much of Europe it developed as a more independent discipline, closer to history and classics. Either way, its central claim is anthropological: that material things carry social meaning, and that the past can be understood through the same questions about kinship, economy, power, and belief that ethnographers ask of the present.

## History and Emergence

Archaeology began in the nineteenth century as antiquarian collecting and the excavation of spectacular sites — the tombs of Egypt, the cities of Mesopotamia, the ruins of the classical world. It became a science when it acquired chronology: the three-age system of stone, bronze, and iron, stratigraphic excavation, and, in the twentieth century, radiocarbon dating. In Britain, Augustus Pitt Rivers (1827–1900) turned excavation into a systematic method, insisting that the ordinary objects of everyday life mattered as much as treasure. In Australia, V. Gordon Childe (1892–1957) gave archaeology its first grand theory of social evolution, arguing in *Man Makes Himself* (1936) that the Neolithic and urban revolutions were transformations in the organization of production, not merely new technologies.

In the United States, archaeology grew inside anthropology. Franz Boas's students applied his historical particularism to the American past, and the culture-historical approach — defining archaeological "cultures" from styles of pottery and tools and tracing their movements in space and time — dominated the first half of the twentieth century. The decisive rupture came in the 1960s with the New Archaeology, or processual archaeology, led by Lewis Binford. Binford's essay "Archaeology as Anthropology" (1962) demanded that archaeology explain cultural processes with testable hypotheses, using systems theory and the scientific method, rather than merely describing and classifying artifacts. In the 1980s, post-processual archaeology, led by Ian Hodder, answered that material culture is symbolic and meaningful, and that the past must be interpreted, not only explained.

## Key Figures

Augustus Pitt Rivers established stratigraphic excavation and the typological study of artifacts as a scientific practice. V. Gordon Childe linked archaeology to Marxist social theory and to the great transitions of prehistory. Lewis Binford (1931–2011) founded processual archaeology and insisted on hypothesis testing, systems thinking, and ethnoarchaeological research among living peoples. Ian Hodder (born 1948) led the post-processual turn, arguing that objects are active in social life and that interpretation is inseparable from archaeology. Colin Renfrew (born 1937) reshaped the archaeology of Europe and of cognitive and social change, and with Paul Bahn wrote the field's standard textbook. Bruce Trigger (1937–2006) gave archaeology its most comprehensive history and defended a comparative, historically grounded approach. In the Americas, Gordon Willey (1913–2002) pioneered settlement-pattern archaeology, showing that the layout of houses and fields reveals social organization.

## Key Works

The field's landmarks map its theoretical shifts. Childe's *Man Makes Himself* (1936) remains the classic statement of archaeology as social history. Willey and Philip Phillips's *Method and Theory in American Archaeology* (1958) codified the culture-historical program that Binford would attack. Binford's "Archaeology as Anthropology" (1962) launched processualism. Hodder's *Symbols in Action* (1982) demonstrated, through ethnoarchaeological work in Africa, that material culture carries symbolic meaning that cannot be reduced to function. Trigger's *A History of Archaeological Thought* (1989) is the standard history of the discipline. Renfrew and Bahn's *Archaeology: Theories, Methods and Practice* (1991) became the most widely used textbook, synthesizing survey, excavation, dating, and interpretation for a generation of students.

## Method

Archaeological method has three stages: finding sites, excavating them, and interpreting what is found. Survey — walking the landscape, using aerial photography, geophysics, and now lidar — locates settlements and monuments. Excavation recovers artifacts, ecofacts such as bones and seeds, and features such as walls and hearths, recording their exact position in three dimensions and in stratigraphic sequence. Dating combines relative methods, such as stratigraphy and typology, with absolute methods, above all radiocarbon dating, dendrochronology, and luminescence. Interpretation then moves from things to people: reconstructing diet from animal bones, trade from the sourcing of obsidian and pottery, social hierarchy from the distribution of grave goods, and belief from the treatment of the dead. Contemporary archaeology also works closely with descendant communities, and with Indigenous archaeology it has adopted collaborative and community-based methods, including repatriation of human remains and sacred objects.

## Influence and Debates

Archaeology's influence reaches far beyond the past. It provides the only evidence for most of human history — for the origins of agriculture, the emergence of cities, the spread of languages, and the collapse of states. Its findings feed directly into debates about climate change, inequality, and sustainability, as archaeologists document how past societies responded to environmental crisis. Within anthropology, archaeology is the discipline's memory: it tests ethnographic generalizations against the long term and reminds cultural anthropology that the present is a thin slice of the human story.

The field's enduring debate is between explanation and interpretation — between Binford's program of testable, law-like generalizations and Hodder's insistence that meaning, agency, and history cannot be reduced to systems. A second debate concerns ownership: who controls the past, who may excavate, and who decides what happens to what is found. Both debates keep archaeology close to anthropology's core concerns: how to know other people, across the distance of time as well as of culture.
