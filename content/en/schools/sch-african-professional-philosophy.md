# Professional/Academic African Philosophy

A philosophical movement that helped establish African philosophy as a professional academic field in African universities, with professors, journals, conferences and curricula, and which applies standards of argument and criticism to the heritage. It regards itself as a response to ethnophilosophy, which held that African philosophy is a collective worldview expressed in languages and myths, and to colonial approaches that denied the existence of an African philosophy altogether.

## The founding controversy

The controversy began with the Belgian priest Placide Tempels's book "Bantu Philosophy" (1945), which regarded "vital force" as the essence of African thought, and which Africans received both with warmth and with criticism. Then came Paulin Hountondji, from Benin, with his article "Remarks on Contemporary African Philosophy" (1970) and his book "On African Philosophy" (1976), attacking ethnophilosophy because it ascribes a collective philosophy to peoples and answers the West's questions rather than Africans' questions; he said that philosophy is a written critical activity, not a mere creed. In reply, those who insisted on particularity held that imitating Western standards is a new dependency.

## Central ideas

- **Philosophy is an independent field**: not a mere description in the science of humanity.
- **Critique of ethnophilosophy**: African ideas are not to be reduced to a "philosophy of the tribe".
- **Rational criticism of the inherited**: examining African beliefs with logical tools. Kwasi Wiredu in "Philosophy and an African Culture" (1980) analyzed Akan concepts and called for "conceptual decolonization", to exclude unexamined foreign categories from African thought.
- **African and universal**: Peter Bodunrin in "The Question of African Philosophy" (1981) discussed whether African philosophy is to be counted as African in its subject matter or in its method, and acknowledged that philosophy has universal standards.
- **Linguistic analysis and conceptual experiment**: Barry Hallen and J. Olubi Sodipo studied the concepts of knowledge, belief and witchcraft among the Yoruba in "Knowledge, Belief and Witchcraft" (1986).
- **Analysis of concepts**: Hallen and Sodipo showed that the Yoruba distinguish between "imo" (knowledge based on direct witness) and "igbagbo" (belief based on assent), and held that this distinction parallels the epistemological distinctions in analytic philosophy.
- **African languages**: some call for philosophizing in them, a demand that has not been realized on a wide scale.

## Key figures

- **Paulin Hountondji** (Benin, 1942–2024): critic of ethnophilosophy and author of *African Philosophy: Myth and Reality*.
- **Kwasi Wiredu** (Ghana, 1931–2022): worked at the University of Ghana and at the University of South Florida.
- **Peter Bodunrin** (Nigeria, 1936–1997): one of the founders of academic philosophy at Ibadan.
- **Kwame Gyekye** (Ghana, 1939–2019): wrote *An Essay on African Philosophical Thought: The Akan Conceptual Scheme* (1987).
- **Barry Hallen** (United States, b. 1941): worked with the Yoruba scholar J. Olubi Sodipo on Yoruba epistemology.
- **Henry Odera Oruka** (Kenya, 1944–1995): author of *Sage Philosophy* (1990).
- **Sophie Oluwole** (Nigeria, 1935–2018): one of the most prominent African women philosophers, who analyzed Yoruba philosophy.
- **Souleymane Bachir Diagne** (Senegal, b. 1955): wrote on Islamic thought, history of logic, and *The Ink of the Scholars: Reflections on Philosophy in Africa* (2016).

## The Islamic and Arab dimension

Part of the African philosophical heritage is connected with Islamic education: the schools of Timbuktu and elsewhere left books in jurisprudence, logic and kalam in Arabic, including the works of Ahmad Baba al-Timbukti (d. 1627). Diagne draws attention to this African writing in Arabic and calls for its integration into the history of African philosophy, which is neglected by approaches that confine philosophy to the oral heritage.

## Positions within the movement

The movement was not a single bloc: the universalists (Hountondji and Bodunrin) said that philosophy is a critical activity with no particularity, and the particularists (among them, in different ways, Gyekye) said that every tradition has its own questions and concepts, while Wiredu took a middle position that separates the universal in logic and knowledge from the particular in culture and beliefs, and calls for distinguishing local belief from universal truth.

## Impact

- Establishing philosophy programs in African universities, including Ibadan, Ife, Nairobi and Accra.
- Publishing specialized journals, including "Second Order" (Ife, 1972) and "Thought and Practice" (Nairobi).
- Dialogue with Western philosophy and analytic philosophy in particular.

## Limits and criticism

- Supporters of ethnophilosophy hold that the standards of written argument exclude venerable oral traditions.
- Postcolonial critics hold that this philosophy remained captive to Western logic, and that its proclamation of universality conceals judgments of European origin.
- It is faulted for the limited size of its academic audience, the difficulty of publication, and the paucity of writing in languages other than English and French.
