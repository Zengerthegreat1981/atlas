# Sāṃkhya

Sāṃkhya ("enumeration" or "counting") is one of the oldest "orthodox" Indian philosophical schools (that is, those that nominally accept the authority of the Vedas). It is a doctrine of real dualism based on the distinction between puruṣa (spirit or pure consciousness) and prakṛti (nature or primal matter). It holds that suffering arises from confusing the two, and that liberation is clear discrimination (viveka), by which the spirit is freed. It is a theoretical, rationalist doctrine that influenced Yoga, Indian medicine and Vedānta, and interacted with Buddhist thought.

## Sāṃkhya in Arabic Sources

Al-Bīrūnī (973–1048) had a unique role in transmitting this doctrine into Arabic. In his *Verifying What India Says, Whether Acceptable to Reason or Rejected* (c. 1030) he devoted chapters to explaining the Indians' belief about God, the soul and liberation, drawing on the "Book of Sānk" and the "Book of Patanjal", which he translated, and comparing their teachings with Sufism and Greek philosophy. Edward Sachau translated al-Bīrūnī's book into English (1888), and Shlomo Pines and Tuvia Gelblum studied and translated al-Bīrūnī's Arabic version of the *Yogasūtra* (1966 and later). Al-Bīrūnī reports that the Indians see liberation in knowledge of the distinction between spirit and matter. The account bears witness to an early Arab-Islamic reading of Indian philosophy in a language intelligible to its reader.

## Textual Authority

Tradition ascribes the doctrine to the legendary Kapila, and the oldest complete text is the *Sāṃkhya Kārikā* of Īśvarakṛṣṇa (around the fourth or fifth century CE) in about seventy verses. Commentaries were written on it, among them that of Gauḍapāda, the *Tattva Kaumudī* of Vācaspati Miśra (tenth century), and the *Yukti Dīpikā*. The *Sāṃkhya Pravacana Sūtra* attributed to Kapila is a late text, and its commentary is by Vijñānabhikṣu (sixteenth century). The doctrine has echoes in the Upaniṣads, the *Mahābhārata*, the *Bhagavad Gītā* and the medical *Caraka Saṃhitā*.

## The Twenty-Five Principles

The doctrine counts twenty-five principles (tattva):

- **Prakṛti**: undifferentiated primal matter, eternal and active, composed of three qualities (guṇa): sattva (light and balance), rajas (activity and passion) and tamas (heaviness and darkness).
- **Mahat or buddhi**: the first thing to arise from it, intelligence or the discriminating faculty.
- **Ahaṃkāra**: the principle of ego that ties experience to an "I".
- **Manas**: the inner sense.
- **Ten senses**: five of knowledge (hearing, touch, sight, taste, smell) and five of action (speech, grasping, movement, excretion, procreation).
- **Five subtle elements (tanmātra)** then **five gross elements (mahābhūta)**: ether, air, fire, water and earth.
- **Puruṣa**: the witnessing consciousness, which neither changes nor acts, and which is plural: each being has its own spirit.

## Basic Ideas

- **Sat-kārya-vāda**: the effect exists in the cause potentially, so the world is an evolution (pariṇāma) from prakṛti, not a creation from nothing.
- **Means of knowledge**: it accepts three: perception, inference and testimony (reliable speech).
- **The cause of suffering**: a person thinks that pain and joy are states of the spirit, when they are really changes of nature. In the well-known example prakṛti is likened to a dancer who displays herself before the witnessing puruṣa and then withdraws once he has seen her as she truly is.
- **Liberation (kaivalya)**: attained through discriminating knowledge, and the spirit separates from matter completely.

## Influence

- Yoga, as systematized in Patañjali's *Yogasūtra* (compiled between roughly the second and fourth centuries CE), took over the same dualist framework, adding "Īśvara" as a special God for meditation, and so it was called "Sāṃkhya with God".
- Vedānta, the Purāṇas and Tantric schools absorbed the terminology of the guṇas and tattvas, although Vedānta opposed its dualism with monism.
- It influenced Indian medicine (Āyurveda) and the theory of humors.
- The West has studied it since the nineteenth century, and it was treated in the studies of Richard Garbe in *Die Sāṃkhya-Philosophie* (1894), Surendranath Dasgupta (1922) and Gerald Larson (1979).

## Limits and Criticisms

- **The problem of relation**: how does the inactive puruṣa connect to the unconscious prakṛti if there is no link between them? This is the most important objection raised by rival schools, among them Śaṅkara's Advaita Vedānta (c. eighth century CE), which saw in prakṛti an unconscious system that does not explain purpose.
- **The plurality of spirits**: the Advaitins doubt their plurality, and critics ask how a number of inactive spirits can be distinguished.
- **Doctrinal atheism**: the classical formulation includes no creator God, which separates it from theistic Hindu schools although it is counted as "orthodox".
- **Variation in the sources**: little survives of the earliest formulations, and what we know of the ancient formulations comes from late texts, so it is hard to date the development of the doctrine.
