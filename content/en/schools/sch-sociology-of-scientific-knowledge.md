# Sociology of Knowledge & Scientific Knowledge

A subfield that examines the social roots of knowledge itself, rather than being content with studying society in isolation from how it produces its scientific and intellectual conceptions, extending from the classical ideological questions to the most radical philosophical positions on the objectivity of science itself.

## Definition

The "sociology of knowledge and science" is a field that studies how human knowledge, including apparently "objective" scientific knowledge, is shaped by social and historical factors, extending from the classical foundation in Karl Mannheim in Weimar Germany to the radical "Strong Programme" in British science studies in the 1970s.

## Historical Context

The field arose in two phases distinct historically and geographically. The first was German, between the wars, when the Hungarian-German sociologist Karl Mannheim (1893–1947) put forward his project in an intellectual climate dominated by Marxism and the critique of ideology. The second was British, arising in the "Edinburgh School" at the University of Edinburgh in the 1970s, around the Science Studies Unit established there in the 1960s, in a context of rising doubts about the simple cumulative, progressive picture of science after Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962). That work argued that shifts between scientific "paradigms" are not purely logical processes, but involve historical, psychological and communal factors within the scientific community.

## Main Ideas or Method

The sociologist Karl Mannheim founded the classical sociology of knowledge in *Ideology and Utopia* (Ideologie und Utopie, 1929), arguing that all human thought, including political and social thought, is tied to the "social position" (Seinsverbundenheit) of its holder (class, generation, group). He distinguished between "ideology" (thought that justifies the existing situation in favor of a dominant group) and "utopia" (thought that envisages transcending the existing situation in favor of a rising group), but he exempted natural-scientific knowledge from this analysis as more "objective" and not tied to a class social position in the same way. David Bloor and Barry Barnes of the Edinburgh School later came with the "Strong Programme," which Bloor formulated in *Knowledge and Social Imagery* (1976) and which is more radical (Barnes had published *Scientific Knowledge and Sociological Theory* in 1974). They demanded applying social analysis to all knowledge, including scientific theories accepted as true, not only errors or rejected beliefs, through four tenets: causality (explanations should be causal), impartiality (with respect to truth and falsity, rationality and irrationality, success and failure), symmetry (the same types of cause should explain true and false beliefs) and reflexivity (the explanations should apply to sociology itself). In particular, it rejects the assumption that true beliefs are explained by "the truth itself" while only error needs a social explanation.

## Major Works or Representatives

Besides Bloor and Barnes, researchers from the "Bath School" took part in developing the field, such as Harry Collins, who studied empirically how scientific controversies are settled, through interviews and fieldwork on cases such as replication disputes and the search for gravitational waves, and who developed the "empirical programme of relativism" (see his *Changing Order*, 1985). Laboratory ethnography was pursued separately, notably in Latour and Woolgar's *Laboratory Life* (1979). In 1975 the Society for Social Studies of Science (4S) was founded, which gave the field an institutional home alongside the history and philosophy of science.

## Impact and Critique

The "Strong Programme" provoked a sharp controversy later known as the "Science Wars," which peaked in the "Sokal affair" (1996), when the physicist Alan Sokal published a deliberately parodic article in the cultural studies journal *Social Text* to expose what he considered intellectual laxity in writing about science. Its main targets were postmodern and cultural-studies writers, though it was also read as a blow to science studies in general, including the Edinburgh programme, whose position differs from theirs. The broader field of "Science and Technology Studies" (STS) continued to develop, including through Bruno Latour's actor-network theory, which departs from the Strong Programme in important ways. A philosophical critique was also directed at the Strong Programme: that explaining all scientific knowledge, even what is empirically certain to be true, by purely social factors may slide into epistemic relativism that makes it hard to distinguish a good scientific theory from a poor one on grounds that are not purely social. Nevertheless, the field remained influential in debates over scientific objectivity and in neighboring areas such as laboratory studies and the sociology of technology.
