# New Religious Movements Studies and the Cult Debate

A subfield within comparative religious studies that arose in the 1970s in response to the emergence in the West of many new religious and spiritual movements (the Unification Church of Sun Myung Moon, ISKCON/Hare Krishna, Scientology, and others), and to the intense media, legal and academic controversy surrounding these movements and the ways in which people joined them. Interest intensified after the tragedies of Jonestown (1978, roughly 900 dead), Waco (1993), Heaven's Gate (1997) and the Solar Temple (1994–1997), which made the "cult" a headline term for an imminent danger.

## Naming and Terminology

Academic researchers prefer the term "new religious movement" (NRM) to "cult", the common popular and media term, because the latter carries an implicit negative value judgement (the presumption of manipulation and danger) that precedes any empirical study of each movement. The word "cult" itself is not semantically fixed. As a sociological category it derives from the church-sect typology of Max Weber and Ernst Troeltsch (the latter distinguishing church, sect and mysticism in 1912), to which Howard Becker added the "cult" in 1932. Stark and Bainbridge (1985) defined the cult as a new movement with no roots in an established tradition, before the word became a pejorative popular label. In Arabic, translation requires care: the Arabic word commonly rendered as "sect" (ṭā'ifa) denotes a recognised religious community (such as the Maronite and Druze communities) rather than a suspect group. This makes the more precise equivalent of "cult" in scholarship a "new religious community" or "new religious movement", depending on context. Movements that arose in or near Islamic environments are sometimes studied in this category, such as the Ahmadiyya (1889), the Nation of Islam in America (1930), and the Bábī movement (1844) from which the Bahá'í Faith emerged; Bahá'ís regard their faith as an independent world religion. These cases show that the notion of "new" is defined by the movement's relationship to the dominant tradition, not by the date of its emergence.

## The Central Debate: Voluntary Conversion or "Brainwashing"?

The most contested axis of this field is the following question: do members join these movements of their own free will (though driven by understandable psychological or social needs), or through techniques of "mind control" or "brainwashing" that nullify their volition? The psychologist Margaret Singer presented "thought reform" in *Cults in Our Midst* (1995) and endorsed the coercion model, and "deprogramming" practices emerged under Ted Patrick. By contrast, the leading academic researchers concluded, on the basis of field and statistical studies, that the deterministic "brainwashing" model lacks sufficient empirical support. Eileen Barker, in *The Making of a Moonie: Choice or Brainwashing?* (1984), observed that very few of those who attended the movement's workshops joined it, and that many of those who joined left after a few years. Thomas Robbins developed a methodological critique of the absence of precise operational criteria in the concept of "brainwashing" itself. The American Psychological Association rejected, in 1987, a task force report on "coercive persuasion" for its methodological shortcomings. The process of joining is therefore closer to a gradual religious conversion, similar to other historically documented conversion patterns, than to coercive "programming". This prevailing academic position remains contested by Anti-Cult Movement organisations, which hold the opposite view. It is a live disagreement that has not been settled by consensus. Benjamin Zablocki revisited the "brainwashing" question in the late 1990s, using the measure of the "cost of exit".

## Atrocity Tales: Deconstructing the Apostate's Narrative

David Bromley, together with Anson Shupe in *Strange Gods: The Great American Cult Scare* (1981), added an analytical lens that complements Barker's empirical scepticism: the concept of "atrocity tales". This concept analyses how the dramatic accounts of former members about their earlier movements (brainwashing, abuse) are socially shaped after leaving, often with the help of anti-cult organisations, rather than being a raw, neutral reflection of their experience at the time it occurred. This does not mean that these accounts are judged false. It means dismantling the social mechanism by which they are produced.

## From the Individual to the Institution: Beckford's Comparative Contribution

James Beckford completed Barker's empirical question (is the individual manufactured through brainwashing?) with a comparative structural question: why do the institutions of one society (the state, the official church, the media) react with greater panic than the institutions of another society to the same movement? In *Cult Controversies* (1985), he compared the reception of the Unification Church in Britain, France and the United States, showing that the intensity of the "moral panic" reflects the existing institutional structure (the centralisation of the state, and the official church's relationship to it) rather than the characteristics of the movement itself. This analysis explains the emergence of the anti-cult movement: a coalition of voluntary organisations and affected families that adopted the "brainwashing" model, and some of which practised coercive "reprogramming". This practice receded legally in the 1980s, and its most prominent organisation, the Cult Awareness Network (CAN), collapsed in 1996 following lawsuits.

## Boundaries and Criticism

Critics of the academic school, including Stephen Kent and former activists, argue that it is very close to the movements it studies, because of the conditional access it depends on and, at times, the funding or hosting it receives from them. They also argue that it minimises the harm suffered by victims, and it is accused of being "apologetic towards cults". Researchers reply that accurate empirical description, not prior judgement, is what protects against both panic and encroachment on religious freedom. The literature is criticised for its Anglo-American character and for neglecting groups outside the West. It is also faulted for applying the field's tools to exceptional cases of violence without a statistical plan for the actual rates. A broader field emerged in the 1990s that studies "new religious movements" through reference encyclopaedias and works such as *The Oxford Handbook of New Religious Movements* (2004) and Lorne Dawson's *Comprehending Cults* (1998).
