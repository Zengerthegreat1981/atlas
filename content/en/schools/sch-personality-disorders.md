# Personality Disorders

Personality disorders are stable, deeply rooted patterns of cognition, affect, interpersonal relationships, and impulse control. They deviate markedly from the expectations of the individual's culture, begin in adolescence or early adulthood, show relative stability over time and across situations, and cause distress or functional impairment. DSM-5-TR (2022) keeps a categorical model of ten disorders distributed across three "clusters" (A, B, C). Its Section III includes an alternative hybrid dimensional model (AMPD) that has not yet been adopted as the primary official diagnostic system. ICD-11 (in force since 2022), by contrast, made a radical shift: it replaced the discrete categorical patterns with a single severity scale for personality disorder, with optional trait qualifiers.

## Origins and Context

The three-cluster classification traces back to DSM-III (1980), which placed personality disorders on a separate "Axis II," distinct from acute clinical disorders, to emphasize their chronic and enduring nature. Theodore Millon, a member of the DSM-III personality disorders advisory committee and later of the DSM-IV work group, developed a theoretical model of personality patterns that influenced the manual's descriptions. He elaborated it in *Disorders of Personality* (1981), later revised with Roger Davis as *Disorders of Personality: DSM-IV and Beyond* (1996). DSM-5 (2013) abolished the five-axis system, so personality disorders were listed alongside other diagnoses on a single list. The ten-category classification, however, remained essentially unchanged in the core text.

## Diagnostic Foundations

- **Cluster A (odd or eccentric)**: Schizoid, schizotypal, and paranoid personality disorders, characterized by eccentric thinking or behavior and social withdrawal.
- **Cluster B (dramatic or emotional)**: Borderline, antisocial, narcissistic, and histrionic personality disorders, characterized by excessive emotionality and instability in relationships and self-image.
- **Cluster C (anxious or fearful)**: Avoidant, dependent, and obsessive-compulsive personality disorders, characterized by chronic anxiety and an excessive need for reassurance or control.
- **Shared general criteria**: To diagnose any personality disorder, DSM-5-TR requires an enduring pattern that deviates from cultural expectations in at least two of four areas (cognition, affectivity, interpersonal functioning, and impulse control); stability across multiple situations; onset traceable to adolescence or early adulthood; and exclusion of alternative explanations (another disorder, a substance, or a medical condition).
- **Alternative model (AMPD)**: Proposes a two-part diagnosis. The first is a dimensional assessment of impairment in self and interpersonal functioning (Criterion A) on a five-point scale. The second is the matching of a pattern of five pathological trait domains (negative affectivity, detachment, antagonism, disinhibition, and psychoticism), distributed across 25 facets (Criterion B). It retains only six specific personality disorder types (borderline, antisocial, narcissistic, avoidant, schizotypal, and obsessive-compulsive), along with a "personality disorder–trait specified" category for other cases.
- **ICD-11 position**: Replaces the ten patterns with a single diagnosis, "personality disorder," whose severity is specified (mild, moderate, or severe). Its traits are described optionally across five domains (negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, and anankastia, meaning obsessive-compulsive), plus an optional sixth qualifier, the "borderline pattern." This brings it closer to the logic of AMPD than to the traditional categorical model.

## Notable Figures and Contributions

Otto Kernberg developed an influential psychodynamic framework for borderline personality organization and developed transference-focused psychotherapy for borderline personality disorder and severe narcissistic personality disorder. Marsha Linehan developed dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) in the late 1980s as an evidence-based treatment protocol for borderline personality disorder. It combines acceptance and behavioral change techniques, and it is among the most extensively studied psychological treatments for this disorder.

## Limits

The categorical versus dimensional debate raises a fundamental question. Critics of the categorical model argue that the boundaries between the ten patterns are artificial and that overlap among them is very high, since many patients meet the criteria for more than one personality disorder. Supporters of the categorical model argue that it is simpler for everyday clinical use and historically more stable for research and health insurance purposes. DSM-5's adoption of the AMPD model only as a research tool, without making it a primary diagnostic standard, reflected this divide within the drafting committees themselves. ICD-11 went a step further by formally adopting the dimensional model, which creates a gap in alignment between the two most widely used systems worldwide.
