# Ethnography of Religion and Fieldwork

A methodological line that runs from the early anthropological foundation of "participant observation" to the contemporary method of "lived religion", sharing one principle: actual religious practice cannot be understood from books and texts alone, but must be observed and participated in on the ground, where it is actually lived.

## Arab Precedents: Observation Before the Academy

Modern field observation was preceded by models in the Arab heritage. Abu Rayhan al-Biruni spent years in India with the expeditions of Sultan Mahmud of Ghazni, learned Sanskrit, sat with the Brahmins and compared what he heard with what he read, and wrote *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind* (c. 1030 CE), declaring that a description must adhere to the words of the people of the faith. Ibn Battuta recorded the rituals of Muslims and others in Africa and Asia in his *Rihla* (1355 CE) through personal observation, and Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddima* (1377 CE), built a science of human social organization that analyzes nomadism and collective religiosity (*asabiyya* and religious da'wa) from his observation of the Maghrib. In the twentieth century, the Finnish scholar Edward Westermarck produced *Ritual and Belief in Morocco* (1926) after a long stay among the tribes of Morocco, and Clifford Geertz compared religiosity in Morocco and Indonesia in *Islam Observed* (1968) using the method of "thick description". Religious ethnographies of the Arab world followed: Vincent Crapanzano studied the Hamadsha in Morocco (1973), and Paul Rabinow wrote *Reflections on Fieldwork in Morocco* (1977). This experience showed that fieldwork in Muslim societies poses particular problems, including the relationship of the non-Muslim researcher to the sacred, and the intrusion of "official" religiosity and popular religiosity into the description.

## Malinowski: Founding "Participant Observation"

In his fieldwork among the Trobriand Islands (Papua New Guinea, 1915–1918), and in his book *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922), Bronisław Malinowski is widely credited with establishing the method of "participant observation": living for a long period within the studied society, mastering its language, and taking part in its daily life instead of relying on the reports of intermediaries or short visits. He applied this method to the study of magic and religious myth among the Trobrianders, affirming that religious and magical practices perform concrete psychological and social functions (relieving anxiety and maintaining group cohesion) that can be understood only through direct immersion. The publication of his personal diary in 1967 provoked debate about the gap between the ideal methodological picture and the researcher's personal experience.

## Evans-Pritchard: The Internal Coherence of Belief

Edward Evans-Pritchard developed this method further in his fieldwork among the Azande and the Nuer, showing how extended fieldwork reveals a coherent internal logic in systems of belief that appear "irrational" from the outside.

## Orsi: From the Distant "Other" to Nearby "Lived Religion"

Robert Orsi transferred this ethnographic method, in *The Madonna of 115th Street* (1985), from the study of "other" societies distant geographically and culturally to the study of popular religious practice within contemporary Western urban societies themselves (Italian Catholic immigrants in New York), helping to establish the approach of "lived religion": a focus on the daily bodily and ritual practice of ordinary believers rather than the official doctrine of the religious institution. Nancy Ammerman and Meredith McGuire built on this in *Everyday Religion* (2007) and *Lived Religion* (2008).

## Hall: An Early Historical Counterpart to Vernacular Religion

In parallel with Orsi, David D. Hall developed a similar approach in *Worlds of Wonder, Days of Judgment* (1989), applied to historical material rather than contemporary ethnography: the popular religiosity of the Puritan settlers of New England, which often went beyond the theological doctrine recorded in sermons. He described this gap between popular practice and clerical doctrine by drawing on alternative historical sources (such as diaries, almanacs and court records) instead of live field observation, and later edited *Lived Religion in America* (1997).

## Critical Ethnography: From Participation to Ethics

After the 1980s, ethnography underwent self-criticism. The book *Writing Culture* (1986) by James Clifford and George Marcus argued that ethnographic texts are products of rhetoric and authority rather than transparent copies of reality, and Johannes Fabian in *Time and the Other* (1983) showed that anthropology places its subject in a "primitive" time different from the time of the researcher. In the study of religion, Saba Mahmood embodied this critique in *Politics of Piety* (2005): she lived in Cairo among women of the mosque movement, analyzed the concept of "agency" presupposed by Western feminist criticism, and argued that submission to the standards of piety may, for these women, be a training of the self rather than passive submission. Charles Hirschkind, in *The Ethical Soundscape* (2006), examined Egyptians' listening to cassette sermons as a moral-sensory training. These works opened the question: can the liberal model accommodate notions such as agency and freedom?

## The Generational Debate

The difference between the distant colonial location of Malinowski's and Evans-Pritchard's ethnography and the local "lived religion" location of Orsi and Hall is not merely an incidental geographic difference. It touches on the balance of power in the field and on the object of observation itself: the local researcher may confront his own community with foreign tools, and the outsider may conceal his colonial position.

## Impact and Methodological Continuity

This ethnographic thread provides the shared methodological tool on which most fields in comparative religion that rely on field description are based (the symbolic anthropology of religion, the study of traditional African religions, and the study of new religious movements). It also poses a constant methodological question: how does the researcher balance sufficient sympathetic immersion to understand meaning from within (Emic) with sufficient critical distance to analyze from without (Etic)?
