# Sociology of Culture and Lifestyles

A field that studies culture not as a homogeneous inherited system of values but as a practical "toolkit" that individuals use flexibly to solve everyday problems of living, and that links it to patterns of consumption and taste differentiated by class.

## The Central Idea

In her pivotal article "Culture in Action" (1986), Ann Swidler argued that the traditional conception of culture as fixed "values" that directly guide behaviour (as if a particular value "causes" a particular action) is inaccurate. She proposed instead a conception of culture as a "toolkit": a varied set of symbols, stories, rituals and practical skills that individuals use with flexible selectivity to build "strategies of action" suited to particular situations, rather than mechanically following a single unified value. In parallel, Richard Peterson developed the concept of the "Cultural Omnivore" (1992). Contrary to Bourdieu's conception of exclusive class taste (the elite prefers only high art), Peterson showed that the contemporary cultural elite tends toward wide-ranging consumption that includes both "highbrow" and "popular" forms (for example classical music alongside country or blues), while real class distinction has become one between the wide-ranging "omnivore" and the "univore", limited to a single cultural genre.

## Historical Context

Ann Swidler published "Culture in Action" in the *American Sociological Review* in 1986, where it became one of the most cited articles in the history of American sociology of culture. Her main target was the account, drawn from Max Weber and developed by Talcott Parsons, of values as the ends that guide action. Her approach is often compared with the French sociology of Pierre Bourdieu, which had become influential in the study of culture and taste from the 1970s (especially *Distinction*, La Distinction, 1979), and which assumed that cultural taste is a nearly automatic reflection of class position (the "habitus"), working silently to reproduce the existing social hierarchy. Later commentators have contrasted this emphasis on class dispositions with Swidler's emphasis on flexible repertoires. In parallel, Richard Peterson (1932–2010), a sociologist of culture and music at Vanderbilt University, developed his observation of the "cultural omnivore" on the basis of actual American survey data on patterns of cultural consumption (attendance at arts events and musical tastes, from large national surveys of arts participation), which showed a decline in the classic "exclusive" class distinction in favour of a new distinction between the breadth and the narrowness of the culture consumed.

## Main Ideas (in Detail)

Swidler elaborated the mechanism by which "cultural toolkits" work. In "settled lives", culture operates silently and almost unconsciously, guiding routine, repeated behaviour without any need to invoke explicit values; in "unsettled lives", such as revolutions, major personal crises or radical institutional transformations, explicit ideologies and coherent symbols become more important, because individuals need clear interpretive frameworks to guide their actions in an unfamiliar context that lacks a ready-made cultural routine. For his part, Peterson distinguished between the "omnivore" (who consumes a wide range of cultural genres, from classical music to country or rock) and the "univore" (whose consumption is limited to one narrow cultural genre), proposing that wide cultural openness has become the mark of the new highest social status in post-industrial societies, in place of the narrow cultural exclusivity that formerly distinguished traditional elites.

## Impact

These two frameworks offered an important correction to Bourdieu's French conception of taste when applied to the American context, influencing the sociology of contemporary cultural consumption. Swidler's "toolkit" and Peterson's "omnivore" became among the most widely used frameworks in later empirical research on culture, class and consumption, and they are often taught alongside Wendy Griswold's "cultural diamond", a framework for relating cultural objects, creators, receivers and the social world.

## Criticism and Evaluation

Peterson's "omnivore" framework was criticized by researchers who held that wide cultural openness may itself conceal a newer and subtler hierarchy (for example Bethany Bryson's 1996 study found that educated Americans' broad tastes still excluded genres associated with lower-status groups), while other researchers criticized the generalization of Swidler's thesis beyond the American context in which it was formulated, asking how far the "settled/unsettled" dichotomy applies to societies with more homogeneous or more rigidly traditional cultural structures. Nevertheless, the two frameworks together remained among the most influential contributions in freeing American sociology of culture from exclusive dependence on the French Bourdieusian model, and gave the field analytical vocabulary better suited to studying diverse consumer societies whose context is changing, and a solid foundation on which later generations of American sociologists of culture built.
