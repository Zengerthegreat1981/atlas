# Positive Psychology

Positive psychology is a **scientific movement** launched in 1998 when Martin Seligman, as president of the American Psychological Association (APA), made it the theme of his presidency, and crystallized in a special issue of *American Psychologist* (Seligman & Csikszentmihalyi, 2000). Its central idea: **psychology had concentrated on illness** (disorders, helplessness, pain), and so produced theories and treatments mainly "for illness." The new mission: **to study what makes life worth living**, using the same empirical methods. Happiness, virtues, personal strengths, meaning, engagement, positive relationships, and accomplishment are all subjects for research.

## Intellectual Context

- **Humanistic psychology** (Maslow, Rogers, May): the "positive human being" as a subject. Positive psychology presents itself as more empirical than its predecessor.
- **Positive mental health** (Marie Jahoda, 1958, *Current Concepts of Positive Mental Health*): defining mental health not only by the absence of illness.
- **Greek philosophy** (Aristotle, *eudaimonia*): flourishing and the good life.
- **Personality and motivation research** (Allport, Erikson, and later work on well-being and optimism).

## Historical Phases

- **Precursors** (1950s-1990s): Maslow's work on self-actualization, Rogers's emphasis on growth, Jahoda (1958), and Csikszentmihalyi's work on flow (*Flow*, 1990); Seligman's own work on learned helplessness and optimism (*Learned Optimism*, 1991).
- **Founding** (1998-2000): Seligman's APA presidency (1998) and the special issue of *American Psychologist* (2000); the University of Pennsylvania's Positive Psychology Center became a hub of the field.
- **Theoretical development** (2000s): Peterson and Seligman's classification of character strengths and virtues (2004); Ryff's model of psychological well-being (1989) and Keyes's work on flourishing; Seligman's *Authentic Happiness* (2002) and, later, the PERMA model in *Flourish* (2011).
- **Global expansion** (2000s to present): the International Positive Psychology Association (founded 2007), cross-cultural research, and applications in education and organizations.
- **Criticism and revision**: Barbara Ehrenreich's *Bright-Sided* (2009) criticized "positive thinking" culture; scholars within the field proposed a "second wave" (for example Paul Wong, and Lomas and Ivtzan) that gives more attention to suffering, meaning, and context.

## Founding Concepts

- **PERMA (Seligman, 2011)**: five elements of well-being:
  - **P** (Positive emotion)
  - **E** (Engagement): absorption, including flow
  - **R** (Relationships)
  - **M** (Meaning)
  - **A** (Accomplishment)
- **VIA Classification of Character Strengths (Peterson & Seligman, 2004)**: six broad virtues (wisdom and knowledge, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence) comprising 24 character strengths.
- **Flow (Csikszentmihalyi)**: the optimal state of full engagement in an activity, in which skill matches challenge.
- **Flourishing (Keyes)**: a state of high emotional, psychological, and social well-being, treated as more than the absence of mental illness.
- **Psychological well-being (Ryff)**: six dimensions, namely self-acceptance, positive relations, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth.
- **Hope (Snyder)**: the capacity to envision pathways to goals and the motivation to pursue them.
- **Learned optimism (Seligman)**: the way people explain good and bad events (their explanatory style) is learnable and changeable.
- **Broaden-and-build (Fredrickson)**: positive emotions broaden attention and thought-action repertoires and so build lasting personal resources.
- **Post-traumatic growth** (Tedeschi and Calhoun) and **resilience**: positive change and recovery in the face of adversity.

## Key Figures

- **Martin Seligman** (1942-): the movement's founding figure; learned helplessness and learned optimism; PERMA.
- **Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi** (1934-2021): flow.
- **Christopher Peterson** (1950-2012): character strengths and virtues (VIA).
- **Charles Snyder** (1944-2006): hope theory.
- **Barbara Fredrickson** (1964-): broaden-and-build theory of positive emotions.
- **Ed Diener** (1946-2021): subjective well-being.
- **Corey Keyes**: the flourishing model of mental health.
- **Carol Ryff**: the psychological well-being model.

## Subfields

- **Classical positive psychology** (Seligman, Peterson).
- **Well-being psychology** (Diener, Keyes, Ryff).
- **Character strengths and virtues** (Peterson, Seligman, and later Niemiec).
- **Cross-cultural positive psychology**.
- **Positive organizational scholarship** (Cameron, Dutton, and others).
- **Positive education** (school-based programs such as the Penn Resiliency Program).
- **Second-wave positive psychology** (Wong; Lomas and Ivtzan).

## Clinical Applications

- **Positive psychology interventions**: for example gratitude letters and visits, "three good things," and using signature strengths in new ways (Seligman, Steen, Park, & Peterson, 2005).
- **Positive psychotherapy** (Seligman, Rashid, & Parks, 2006): a protocol that builds strengths and positive emotion alongside symptom reduction.
- **Hope-based approaches** (Snyder).
- **Resilience and well-being programs** in schools and workplaces.

These programs are not substitutes for evaluation and treatment of mental illness by qualified clinicians.

## Relation to Neighboring Schools

- **Humanistic psychology**: the direct roots, though positive psychology is more empirical and less philosophical in method.
- **Client-centered therapy** (Rogers): shares an emphasis on growth and strengths.
- **CBT**: shares an interest in cognition, and several positive interventions are used alongside CBT methods.
- **Evolutionary psychology**: some authors link character strengths to adaptive functions.

## Criticism and Limits

- **Reduction to "happiness"**: the risk of turning a broad psychology of well-being into a prescription for how to be happy.
- **Western cultural bias**: models such as PERMA and the VIA classification arose in a Western context, and their cross-cultural validity is debated.
- **Neglect of social context**: relatively little attention, especially in early work, to injustice, poverty, and discrimination.
- **Commercial application**: the field's ideas have been absorbed by a large self-help and wellness industry, a trend Ehrenreich criticized in *Bright-Sided* (2009).
- **Modest and variable effects**: meta-analyses of positive psychology interventions (for example Sin & Lyubomirsky, 2009; Bolier et al., 2013) find small-to-moderate effects on well-being and depression (Sin and Lyubomirsky reported mean r of about .29 and .31; Bolier and colleagues reported smaller standardized differences of about 0.20 to 0.34), with concerns about study quality; a 2019 reanalysis (White, Uttl and Holder) concluded that the effects are much smaller than previously reported.

## The Ehrenreich Critique and Its Reception

In *Bright-Sided: How the Relentless Promotion of Positive Thinking Has Undermined America* (2009), investigative journalist Barbara Ehrenreich delivered a prominent critique of positive psychology and the cultural ideology of mandatory optimism:

- **Core arguments of the critique**: Drawing partly on her experience as a breast cancer patient, Ehrenreich argued that the positive thinking movement promotes a culture of mandatory optimism, in which individuals are blamed for their own suffering or illness under the guise that illness reflects a failure of optimism. Economically and politically, she contended that positive psychology was co-opted by corporations and financial institutions to mask structural inequality, pacify anxious or laid-off workers, and encourage the reckless, uncritical optimism that contributed to the 2008 financial crisis.
- **Reception by critical psychologists and sociologists**: Some critics shared Ehrenreich's concerns. Scholars like Barbara Held pointed to the "tyranny of the positive attitude," arguing that pressuring people to feel happy stigmatizes realistic grief and legitimate anger. Some health-psychology researchers (e.g., James Coyne) likewise questioned claims that a positive attitude improves cancer survival, and the evidence for such claims is weak.
- **Counter-replies by positive psychologists**: Seligman and fellow researchers responded by distinguishing academic, empirical positive psychology from the unscientific, commercial "positive thinking" tradition of Norman Vincent Peale. They maintained that positive psychology does not advocate denial of reality, reckless optimism, or the suppression of negative emotions (acknowledging realistic assessments).
- **Catalyst for "Second-Wave" Positive Psychology (PP 2.0)**: The debate accelerated a dialectical maturation within the field. Theorists such as Paul Wong, Tim Lomas, and Itai Ivtzan formulated "Second-Wave Positive Psychology," which moves beyond simplistic positive/negative binaries to integrate existential suffering, tragic optimism, contextual nuance, and meaning-making directly into the study of human well-being.

## Institutional and Clinical Extensions

- **The US Army's Comprehensive Soldier Fitness program** (launched 2009), a large institutional application that was also controversial because of questions about evidence and about its premises.
- **Positive education** and school well-being programs.
- The influence of the field on the **measurement of well-being** in public policy.
