# Ash'ariyya

A Sunni Islamic theological school founded by Abu al-Hasan Ali ibn Isma'il al-Ash'ari (d. 324 AH / 936 CE), which offered a middle course between the rationalism of the Mu'tazila and the literalism of the people of hadith. It became the dominant theology of the Sunni majority (Ahl al-Sunna wa al-Jama'a) in most Islamic urban centres from the fifth and sixth centuries AH, and its presence has remained strong at al-Azhar and in the institutions of religious education in Egypt, the Levant, the Maghreb, West Africa and Southeast Asia.

## Founding Context

Al-Ash'ari grew up in Basra and studied under the Mu'tazili master Abu Ali al-Jubba'i, then announced in the mosque of Basra around 300 AH his departure from Mu'tazilism. The sources report that the cause was dream visions and a disagreement with al-Jubba'i over the famous question of "the good and the better" (al-salah wa al-aslah). After that he moved to Baghdad and wrote books refuting the Mu'tazila and other sects, among them "Al-Luma' fi al-Radd ala Ahl al-Zaygh wa al-Bida'" ("The Flashes in Refutation of the People of Deviation and Innovation"), "Maqalat al-Islamiyyin wa Ikhtilaf al-Musallin" ("Opinions of the Muslims and the Differences among Worshippers"), and "Al-Ibana an Usul al-Diyana" ("The Elucidation of the Principles of Religion"). Scholars have differed over the order of these books and over how close "al-Ibana" is to the Hanbali position: some held that he wrote it at an early stage after his repentance, while others saw that he combined in it the text with a method of moderate rational inference.

## Major Principles

1. **Affirmation of the divine attributes**: affirming the attributes established by the text (knowledge, life, power, will, hearing, sight and speech) as attributes subsisting in His essence, held to be neither identical with nor separate from it, and denying anthropomorphism, including the phrase "without asking how" (bila kayf).
2. **The Speech of God is uncreated**: God's speech is an attribute subsisting in His essence, and the later authorities distinguish between the eternal inner speech (al-kalam al-nafsi) and the temporal recited words.
3. **The narrated attributes (al-sifat al-khabariyya)**: such as the hand, the face and istiwa' (settling upon the throne), which al-Ash'ari at first affirmed, whereas the later authorities after al-Juwayni resorted to figurative interpretation (ta'wil) or deferral of meaning (tafwid) while denying corporeality.
4. **Destiny and acquisition (kasb)**: God creates the actions of servants, and the human being "acquires" them, a middle solution between determinism and delegation that remains a matter of debate.
5. **Faith (iman)**: heartfelt assent is the root of faith, and works are its perfection, unlike the Atharis' saying that it is speech and action.
6. **Ethics**: good and evil are determined by revelation, not by reason, so what God commanded is good and what He forbade is evil; this is known as divine voluntarism, in contrast to the Mu'tazila's affirmation of rational good and evil.
7. **The vision of God**: affirmation that the believers will see their Lord on the Day of Resurrection without asking how and without direction.

## The Natural and Epistemic Dimension

The later members of the school, beginning with al-Baqillani, formulated a theory of the world based on atomism and accidents: the world is composed of indivisible parts (jawahir farda) and accidents that do not endure for two moments, which God re-creates moment by moment. The consequence is that habit (al-ada) is what God brings about by way of conjunction between events, with no causal necessity between them, which is the question al-Ghazali discussed in "The Incoherence of the Philosophers" when he defended the possibility of miracles by the example of cotton burning on contact with fire.

## Prominent Figures

- **Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari** (d. 324 AH): the founder.
- **Abu Bakr al-Baqillani** (d. 403 AH): author of "al-Tamhid" and "I'jaz al-Qur'an," who introduced the rational and atomistic premises into the school's system.
- **Abu al-Ma'ali al-Juwayni** (d. 478 AH): Imam al-Haramayn, author of "al-Irshad" and "al-Shamil."
- **Abu Hamid al-Ghazali** (d. 505 AH): author of "al-Iqtisad fi al-I'tiqad," "Tahafut al-Falasifa" and "Iljam al-Awamm an Ilm al-Kalam."
- **Ibn Asakir** (d. 571 AH): author of "Tabyin Kadhib al-Muftari" in defence of al-Ash'ari.
- **Al-Shahrastani** (d. 548 AH): author of "Nihayat al-Iqdam" and "al-Milal wa al-Nihal."
- **Fakhr al-Din al-Razi** (d. 606 AH): author of "al-Matalib al-Aliya" and "Muhassal Afkar al-Mutaqaddimin wa al-Muta'akhkhirin," who merged kalam with Avicennan Peripatetic philosophy.
- **Sayf al-Din al-Amidi** (d. 631 AH), **Adud al-Din al-Iji** (d. 756 AH), author of "al-Mawaqif," **al-Taftazani** (d. 792 AH), author of "Sharh al-Maqasid," and **al-Sanusi** (d. 895 AH), author of the "Small Creed."

## Spread and Patronage

The school enjoyed influential political patronage. Nizam al-Mulk founded the Nizamiyya madrasas in Baghdad (1067), Nishapur and elsewhere, in which Shafi'i jurisprudence and Ash'ari kalam were taught; Saladin adopted its creed in Egypt and the Levant; the project of Ibn Tumart of the Almohads in the Maghreb was influenced by it, and from there it took root in the Islamic West through the Malikis. It remained the official creedal school at al-Azhar, and the Maturidiyya is considered its counterpart in Central Asia and the Hanafi world.

## Ash'arism and Philosophy

The relationship was dialectical: al-Ghazali attacked the philosophers and declared them unbelievers on three questions (the eternity of the world, God's knowledge of particulars, and bodily resurrection), while accepting Aristotelian logic as an instrument of inquiry. Al-Razi and al-Amidi then came and absorbed the doctrines of Ibn Sina and criticized them. From this came what is called "later kalam," which became mixed with philosophy so that it was no longer possible to separate the two.

## Limits and Criticism

- The people of hadith, foremost among them Ibn Taymiyya in "Dar' Ta'arud al-Aql wa al-Naql," faulted it for interpreting some of the narrated attributes figuratively and for using Greek logic in creed.
- A philosophical criticism was directed at it because "acquisition" is an obscure theory that leaves moral responsibility without a clear ontological basis.
- The theory of continuous re-creation was criticized because it makes natural science merely probable in its logic, which led Ibn Rushd to attack it in "Tahafut al-Tahafut."
- Some of the later Ash'aris were faulted for rigidity and for preoccupation with glosses.
