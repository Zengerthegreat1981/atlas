# Engineering Ethics

## Historical Context

Before modernity, the ethics of craftsmanship in Islamic civilization was tied to the concepts of "excellence" (*itqan*) and "trust" (*amana*). Building and manufacturing were regarded as a trust owed to the user and to society. Much earlier, the Code of Hammurabi (c. 1750 BCE) prescribed severe penalties for a builder whose house collapsed and killed its owner. Islamic jurisprudence developed rules on the liability of hired craftsmen (often discussed under *daman al-sunna'*), who may be held responsible for damage resulting from negligence or breach of the contract, with differences among the schools.

Modern institutional engineering ethics emerged in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries in the United States and Europe. The Industrial Revolution and a rising number of bridge collapses and boiler explosions led professional societies, such as the American Society of Civil Engineers (founded 1852) and the American Society of Mechanical Engineers (founded 1880), to adopt codes of professional conduct in the early twentieth century (for example, in 1912 to 1914). Concern grew after later disasters, most notably the loss of the space shuttle *Challenger* in 1986, which showed how schedule and management pressure could override engineers' technical warnings.

## Central Idea: Public Safety Above All

The core of engineering ethics rests on a single founding principle: the engineer should place "the safety, health and welfare of the public" above any other obligation, including loyalty to the employer or the client. This principle, stated in similar terms in the codes of the NSPE and the IEEE, translates into several subsidiary duties:

- the duty of competence, meaning the engineer should not accept work outside their field;
- the duty of honesty in technical reports, and not falsifying test data;
- the duty to disclose conflicts of interest;
- the duty to blow the whistle (whistleblowing) when the engineer discovers a danger threatening the public and their managers refuse to address it.

The literature distinguishes between "micro-ethics," meaning the individual engineer's conduct in everyday decisions, and "macro-ethics," concerning the collective responsibility of the profession on issues such as military technology, climate change, automation and job loss.

## Models of Ethical Failure and Test Cases

Engineering students are usually taught standard cases that have become reference points:

- **The Ford Pinto (1970s)**: the car's fuel tank design made it vulnerable to fires in rear-end collisions. Internal cost-benefit documents that weighed the cost of design changes against estimated costs of injuries and deaths became public in litigation and press coverage. The case is widely cited as an example of subordinating safety to cost calculation, although some of its factual details and the legal outcomes have been disputed.
- **Roger Boisjoly and the *Challenger* (1986)**: Boisjoly, an engineer at Morton Thiokol, and colleagues warned on the eve of the launch that the booster O-ring seals might fail in the cold weather forecast. After a teleconference with NASA, Thiokol management reversed its initial recommendation against launching. The shuttle broke apart 73 seconds after liftoff, and all seven crew members were killed. Sociologist Diane Vaughan's *The Challenger Launch Decision* (1996) analyzes the organizational "normalization of deviance" behind the decision. The incident is used today as a classic example of the dilemma between organizational loyalty and professional integrity.

## Key Thinkers and Institutions

Mike W. Martin and Roland Schinzinger, in their influential textbook *Ethics in Engineering* (first edition 1983), helped establish engineering ethics as a teaching field. They treated engineering as a form of "social experimentation" and emphasized case-based reasoning alongside ethical theory. Major professional bodies also played a pivotal role: the National Society of Professional Engineers (NSPE) in the United States, the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE), and the Accreditation Board for Engineering and Technology (ABET). ABET's accreditation criteria expect engineering programs to prepare students to understand professional and ethical responsibility, and the professional codes are enforced through licensing boards and society disciplinary procedures to varying degrees.

## Contemporary Challenges

Emerging technologies raise questions that were not posed in the last century. How should the software of a self-driving vehicle be designed to make a decision in an unavoidable collision (the trolley problem in its engineering form)? Who bears responsibility when an artificial intelligence system causes harm that no particular engineer explicitly programmed? And what are the limits of the responsibility of engineers who build autonomous weapons? These issues show that engineering ethics is continually expanding from the traditional model of individual responsibility to a model of responsibility distributed across teams, companies and algorithms together.

*Hypothetical example:* A software engineer discovers that an automatic braking system in cars already in production fails in 1 out of every 100,000 cases under certain rainy conditions. The company intends to launch the product before fixing the flaw in order to meet a marketing deadline. She escalates the matter internally and, when her concern is rejected, reports it to the competent regulator, although doing so risks her job. Her action follows the principle that public safety takes precedence over institutional loyalty.

## Professional Licensing and Legal Accountability

The engineer differs from many other professionals in that practice is often subject to formal licensing (the Professional Engineer License). Licensing requires passing rigorous technical and ethical examinations and completing years of experience under a licensed engineer, just as a license is required to practice medicine or law. This licensing is not merely a bureaucratic procedure. It reflects the fact that the engineer holds technical authority that directly affects public lives (designing a bridge, a reactor or an aircraft). For this reason regulatory bodies hold engineers to strict legal accountability for their errors, unlike an ordinary worker who carries out instructions without independent technical decision-making authority. Licensing duties and professional codes give the engineer a recognized basis for refusing to sign a design considered unsafe, although legal protection against retaliation varies by jurisdiction.

## Toward an Institutional Safety Culture

Recent literature has moved beyond the view that engineering ethics is merely a matter of individual conscience. It stresses the need to build a "safety culture" across the entire organization. Internal reporting channels for risks should be rewarded rather than punishing those who raise them, and safety reviews should be a mandatory step at every stage of design rather than a formality at the end. Post-disaster analyses of major failures, from *Challenger* to other major accidents, show that the root cause is rarely a purely engineering error. More often it is an organizational failure to heed clear technical warnings issued in time, which were overwhelmed by pressures of schedule and cost.
