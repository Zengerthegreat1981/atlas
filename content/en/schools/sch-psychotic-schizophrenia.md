# Psychotic Disorders and Schizophrenia

This category groups together disorders whose core is loss of contact with reality (psychosis), manifested as positive symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, disorganized speech or behavior) and/or negative symptoms (blunted affect, poverty of speech, lack of drive). Chief among them is schizophrenia, the chronic psychotic disorder most studied and theorized in the history of modern psychiatry.

## Origins and Context

Emil Kraepelin, in his *Psychiatry: A Textbook for Students and Physicians* (editions from 1883 to 1927; the sixth edition of 1899 set out the dementia praecox concept), formulated the concept of *dementia praecox*, grouping under it hebephrenia, catatonia, and dementia paranoides on the basis of what he took to be a deteriorating course and an early onset. This contrasted with "manic-depressive insanity," which has a periodic course that can improve. The Swiss physician Eugen Bleuler opposed the inevitability of this deterioration. He proposed the term "schizophrenia" (*Schizophrenie*, from the Greek for "split mind") in 1908 and set out the concept in his 1911 monograph, focusing on the "fundamental symptoms" (association, affect, ambivalence, autism) rather than on the course alone. Kurt Schneider, from the late 1930s, added the concept of "first-rank symptoms" (such as hearing voices that comment on the patient's actions or converse among themselves, or feeling that thoughts are withdrawn or broadcast) as practical criteria to assist differential diagnosis, although it was later shown that these symptoms are not specific to schizophrenia to the degree Schneider originally assumed.

## Diagnostic Foundations

- **Schizophrenia (DSM-5-TR)**: requires at least two of five symptoms (delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior, negative symptoms) for a significant portion of time during one month, at least one of which must be delusions, hallucinations or disorganized speech. Continuous signs of the disorder must persist for at least six months, with clear functional decline.
- **Schizoaffective Disorder**: combines the symptoms of schizophrenia with a major mood episode (depressive or manic) within a single continuous illness period. Delusions or hallucinations must be present for at least two weeks without prominent mood symptoms at some point in the course of the illness, which distinguishes it from a mood disorder with psychotic features.
- **Schizophreniform Disorder**: matches the criteria of schizophrenia in terms of symptoms but lasts between one and six months. It is often used as a provisional diagnosis until the duration of the illness becomes clear.
- **Brief Psychotic Disorder**: psychotic symptoms that last at least one day and less than one month, with full return to previous functioning.
- **Delusional Disorder**: one or more delusions lasting one month or more without meeting the other criteria for schizophrenia, with relatively preserved functioning outside the direct sphere of the delusion.
- **Catatonia**: distinctive motor symptoms (such as rigidity, abnormal postures, and motor or verbal echo) that may be diagnosed as a specifier accompanying another disorder, or as an independent diagnosis associated with a medical condition.
- **ICD-11 position**: adopts a parallel classification for schizophrenia and other primary psychotic disorders, with essentially similar symptom criteria. It abandons the traditional subtypes of schizophrenia (paranoid, hebephrenic, catatonic) that DSM-IV and ICD-10 retained (DSM-5 had already removed them in 2013), in line with the weak evidence for their diagnostic stability and predictive value.

## Impact on Research and Treatment

Nancy Andreasen's empirical distinction between positive and negative symptoms in schizophrenia, through her systematic scales (SAPS and SANS) published in the early 1980s, guided neuroimaging and drug-response research toward the understanding that these two dimensions follow partly different neurophysiological pathways. Positive symptoms generally respond better to traditional and modern antipsychotics through blockade of D2 dopamine receptors, while negative symptoms remain more resistant to drug treatment and are more closely linked to poor long-term functioning. The simplified dopamine hypothesis, which links positive psychotic symptoms to dopamine hyperactivity in the mesolimbic pathway, formed the theoretical basis for the first generation of antipsychotics since the discovery of chlorpromazine in the 1950s. Subsequent decades, however, showed that the neurochemical picture of schizophrenia is far more complex than a single-neurotransmitter model.

Since the 1990s part of clinical research has shifted toward the stage before full psychosis emerges, namely "high-risk" or "prodromal" mental states, seeking early interventions that reduce the likelihood or severity of transition to a first psychotic episode. In parallel, the definition of "recovery" from schizophrenia has changed. It is no longer measured only by the disappearance of clinical symptoms but includes broader functional, social and personal dimensions (personal recovery). This shift was influenced by the psychiatric consumer/survivor movement, which questioned the presumption of inevitable deterioration that the medical literature inherited from Kraepelin's original concept of dementia praecox.

## Limits

First-rank symptoms, despite their long clinical use, were shown in later studies to also appear in other disorders (such as bipolar disorder with psychotic features), and they are not decisive for diagnosis on their own. For this reason, modern classification systems treat them as aids rather than definitive criteria. Likewise, the distinction between schizoaffective disorder and schizophrenia, and between mood disorders with psychotic features, remains a recognized difficulty for diagnostic reliability, given the difficulty of determining clinically the precise time ratio between psychotic and mood symptoms.
