# Logical Atomism

A philosophical doctrine that appeared in Cambridge between roughly 1910 and 1925, associated with the work of Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) and his student Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951). It holds that the world is composed of logically independent facts, that a precise language is composed of independent atomic propositions that picture these facts, and that logical analysis reveals the structure of reality beneath the structure of ordinary language. Russell introduced the name in 1911 and developed the doctrine in his 1918 lectures ("The Philosophy of Logical Atomism"); the atomism here is logical, not physical, since the process of analysis stops at it even if physical atoms were not known. The doctrine arose in reaction to British idealism (Bradley), which holds that reality is one whole and that relations are unreal, and in this way it was one of the founders of analytic philosophy.

## Background

- **The rebellion of Moore and Russell** around 1898–1903 against Bradley's idealism: Moore defended the reality of the external world, and Russell defended the plurality of things and the reality of relations.
- **Frege's logic**: it made possible the analysis of the sentence by function, variable, and quantifier, and offered a tool for analyzing the language of mathematics.
- **Russell and Whitehead's *Principia Mathematica*** (1910–1913): an attempt to reduce mathematics to logic, including the theory of types to avoid Russell's paradox.
- **The theory of descriptions** in the article "On Denoting" (1905): the analysis of sentences containing definite descriptions ("the present king of France is bald") into logically complex propositions, so that there is no need to posit non-existent entities. Russell regards it as a model of philosophical analysis.

## The Theses

- **Russell** (1918 lectures): the world is composed of "logical atoms", which are simple particulars (sense-data, in his epistemology) that we know by "knowledge by acquaintance"; everything else is complex "logical constructions" out of them (tables and persons). The ideal language must have a structure matching the structure of the world.
- **Wittgenstein in the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*** (written 1918, published in German in 1921 and in English in 1922): "The world is the totality of facts, not of things" (1.1); the proposition is a "picture" (Bild) of reality; elementary propositions consist of names that represent simple "objects", and all complex propositions are truth-functions of elementary propositions. What cannot be said is shown by logic, and what lies beyond the limits of language, such as ethics and meaning, is that "whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent" (7).
- **The difference between the two authors**: Russell made the atoms out of sensory experience and adopted a theory of knowledge, whereas Wittgenstein built his doctrine on the structure of language and logic and did not specify what the simple objects are, holding that analysis requires their existence without his knowing them.

## Method: Logical Constructions

Russell adopted what he called the "philosophical Ockham's razor": instead of positing new entities to explain phenomena, complex entities (numbers, classes, material bodies, and persons) are built as "logical constructions" out of simpler known entities, so the question of their existence becomes a question of how to analyze the sentences that speak about them. In *Our Knowledge of the External World* (1914) he tried to construct material bodies from sense-data. This method is what the Positivists after him took as a model for their project of rebuilding scientific knowledge on the basis of experience.

## Logic and the Theory of Types

Russell's *Principia Mathematica* used a ramified theory of types to block paradoxes such as his own. Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* held that such a theory was unnecessary, since the logical syntax of a symbolism already shows which combinations of signs make sense (3.331-3.334): no proposition can say anything about itself, because a sign cannot be part of itself. The disagreement over this point was one source of the divergence between the two thinkers.

## Influence

- It inspired logical positivism in Vienna: the circle of Schlick, Carnap, and Neurath read the *Tractatus* in 1925–1926, and Carnap built the *Logical Structure of the World* (1928) on the idea of logical constructions from the data of experience.
- It contributed to the flourishing of symbolic logic and the philosophy of language and mathematics.
- These ideas entered Arabic culture through Zaki Naguib Mahmoud in *Logical Positivism* (1951), and Azmi Islam translated the *Tractatus* into Arabic (1968) with the review of Zaki Naguib Mahmoud; the translation contributed to the discussion of the philosophy of language and science in the Arab world.

## Criticism and Decline

- In the article "Some Remarks on Logical Form" (1929), Wittgenstein acknowledged that the problem of color and of what resists composition (the propositions "this is red" and "this is green" cannot be combined) undermines the independence of elementary propositions; he then abandoned atomism entirely in the *Philosophical Investigations* (1953), holding that meaning is use in language games, not a single formal structure.
- Quine, in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), rejected the sharp distinction between analytic and synthetic propositions, on which analysis rests.
- Peter Strawson ("On Referring," 1950) and later ordinary-language philosophers challenged the theory of descriptions, and affirmed that ordinary linguistic usage requires an understanding that goes beyond logical analysis.
- There is still disagreement: is logical atomism a metaphysical theory of the structure of the world or an analytic method? Some scholars say it is both for Russell and only a method for Wittgenstein.
