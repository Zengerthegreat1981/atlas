# Subaltern Studies as a Critique of Elite Historiography (Guha, Chakrabarty)

The Subaltern Studies project emerged in the early 1980s as an intervention in South Asian historiography, challenging the dominant paradigms of modern Indian historical writing. Originating among a circle of historians led by **Ranajit Guha**—including Shahid Amin, David Arnold, Partha Chatterjee, David Hardiman, and Gyanendra Pandey—the collective adopted the term "subaltern" from Italian Marxist theorist Antonio Gramsci's *Prison Notebooks* to designate non-hegemonic social groups: peasants, tribal communities, agricultural laborers, women, and urban workers whose consciousness and actions had been marginalized by elite narratives.

## The Dual Critique of Colonialist and Nationalist Historiography

In the programmatic preface and inaugural essay to *Subaltern Studies I* (1982), Guha launched a two-pronged critique of South Asian historiography:

1. **Colonialist Historiography**: British imperial administrators and modern academic historians of the Cambridge School framed the development of Indian political consciousness as an outcome of colonial institutional reform, English education, and elite administrative tutelage, depicting popular unrest as irrational fanaticism or law-and-order disturbances.
2. **Bourgeois-Nationalist Historiography**: Mainstream Indian nationalist histories, centered on the leadership of the Indian National Congress and prominent figures like Mohandas K. Gandhi and Jawaharlal Nehru, treated the achievement of national independence as the achievement of elite vanguard mobilization. In this view, popular movements were recognized only insofar as they followed Congress directives, reducing the history of anti-colonial resistance to a monophonic national epic.

Guha contended that both traditions shared an elitist bias, viewing the making of the Indian nation as the exclusive achievement of elites—whether foreign or indigenous. Against this consensus, Guha asserted that there existed an autonomous domain of subaltern politics running parallel to elite politics, animated by its own structural dynamics, idioms of resistance, and collective consciousness. In his monograph *Elementary Aspects of Peasant Insurgency in Colonial India* (1983), Guha argued that nineteenth-century peasant insurgencies were not blind, spontaneous outbursts of economic despair, but deliberate, highly organized political confrontations structured through codes of negation, inversion, and solidarity.

## Methodological Innovations: Reading the Archive Against the Grain

Because subaltern groups rarely produced or preserved their own written archives, their experiences were recorded primarily through the documentary apparatus of colonial counter-insurgency: police dispatches, judicial records, military diaries, and intelligence reports. To recover subaltern agency, the collective became closely associated with the method of reading colonial sources "against the grain" (*à rebours*). By analyzing the linguistic anxieties, silences, and rhetorical displacements in official reports, historians could reconstruct the autonomous political initiatives that colonial documentation had sought to suppress or mischaracterize.

## The Postcolonial Turn and Provincializing Europe

By the late 1980s and 1990s, Subaltern Studies underwent a profound epistemological reorientation, absorbing poststructuralist, deconstructive, and psychoanalytic approaches through engagements with scholars like Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak and **Dipesh Chakrabarty**. In her influential essay "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988), Spivak cautioned against romantically recovering an unmediated subaltern voice, arguing that ideological and imperial epistemic violence structurally silences the subaltern subject, particularly gendered subalterns.

In *Provincializing Europe: Postcolonial Thought and Historical Difference* (2000), Chakrabarty extended the critique to academic historical methodology as a whole. Modern historical consciousness, he argued, relies on concepts forged in European intellectual history—such as secular time, the modern state, citizenship, and abstract human agency—and treats them as universal standards. Third World histories were routinely relegated to a figurative "waiting room of history," judged as incomplete or belated transitions toward modernity. Chakrabarty proposed "provincializing Europe": recognizing European thought as both indispensable and inadequate for narrating non-Western modernities, while creating room for diverse ways of being in the world that resist reduction to secular-historicist teleologies.

## Debates and Historiographical Impact

The evolution of Subaltern Studies provoked significant scholarly debate. Early Marxist contributors, such as Sumit Sarkar, criticized the group's shift toward discourse analysis and culturalism, arguing that abandoning materialist social analysis led to a disavowal of class struggle and universal political emancipation. Later, political theorist Vivek Chibber (*Postcolonial Theory and the Specter of Capital*, 2013) challenged the postcolonial premise that universal categories of political economy and human rights are inherently Eurocentric. Despite these disagreements, Subaltern Studies has had wide influence on global historiography, including on Latin American Subaltern Studies, African postcolonial criticism, and histories from below worldwide.
