# Social Ethics

Social ethics studies the norms that govern relations among individuals and groups within social structures. It moves beyond the question of the virtue of the isolated individual to the question of justice, solidarity, and the distribution of resources and rights across society as a whole.

## Foundations in Islamic Thought

The Islamic conception of social ethics rests on two mutually reinforcing pillars: faith-based brotherhood and social solidarity (*takaful*). The Holy Qur'an established the principle of *zakat* as an institutional obligation rather than a merely voluntary act of individual charity, for the purpose of redistributing wealth and meeting the needs of the poor and the needy. Jurists, among them Abu Yusuf in *al-Kharaj*, elaborated the rules of financial justice between the ruler and the ruled. Al-Mawardi, in *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya*, presented the imamate as established by a contract-like arrangement (*'aqd*) that ties the ruler's legitimacy to the performance of his duties toward the subjects. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah*, offered a pioneering social analysis of the concepts of *'asabiyya* (group solidarity) and *'umran* (human civilization), showing how collective morality is shaped by economic and political conditions. Al-Farabi, in *Mabadi' Ara' Ahl al-Madina al-Fadila* (*Principles of the Views of the Citizens of the Virtuous City*), linked individual happiness to the soundness of the social and political order as a whole, regarding the human being as political by nature, who cannot attain his perfection except within a community.

## The Western Historical Context

In Western philosophy, the roots go back to the Aristotelian concept of the human being as a "political animal," whose good can be realized only within the city. The field took modern shape with the theories of the social contract in Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau, which explained the legitimacy of society by a hypothetical consent among free individuals. It was then reshaped through the critiques of Hegel and Durkheim of the individualism of these theories, as they stressed that the individual is formed from the outset within a social fabric that precedes them.

## Central Ideas

The field addresses central questions about distributive justice: how wealth, opportunities, and rights are distributed among members of society. What are the limits of the obligation of solidarity toward the poor and the marginalized? What is the relationship of the individual to intermediate institutions such as the family, trade unions, and the state? Among the pressing issues are also the ethics of racial and gender discrimination, the limits of government interference in individual freedoms in the name of the public interest, and the ethics of migration and national belonging.

## Leading Thinkers

Alongside al-Mawardi, Ibn Khaldun, and al-Farabi in the Islamic tradition, contemporary thought is marked by John Rawls, who in *A Theory of Justice* offered two principles of distributive justice derived from an "original position" behind a "veil of ignorance"; Michael Sandel (*Liberalism and the Limits of Justice*, 1982), who criticized Rawls's conception of the self from a communitarian perspective that stresses the individual's rootedness in traditions and communities prior to them; and Amartya Sen, who developed the "capabilities approach" as an alternative to measuring well-being by income alone.

## Impact and Contemporary Application

These debates led to the design of social welfare policies and progressive tax systems in many countries, and to the formulation of international human rights standards that assume collective social obligations. A practical example is the debate over the "universal basic income," which proposes an unconditional financial guarantee for every citizen, as a contemporary application of the principle of social solidarity that *zakat* established long ago in an institutional form.

## The Individual and the Community: A Renewed Debate

The tension between the rights of the individual and the demands of the community remains a constant focus in social ethics. Liberal currents emphasize the priority of individual rights and their protection from domination by the majority or the state, while communitarian and socialist currents insist that the individual is necessarily a social being, and that neglecting the collective dimensions of justice produces an abstract and false picture of the human being. This debate has historically been reflected in the discussion of the role of the state in the economy, between advocates of minimal intervention and advocates of the comprehensive welfare state, and in the contemporary debate over corrective justice, which seeks to redress historical wrongs inflicted on particular groups, such as colonialism and slavery, through affirmative action policies or symbolic and material compensation. Some scholars hold that the optimal solution lies in a reconciling conception that respects the autonomy of the individual while recognizing that justice is achieved only within a just institutional structure that guarantees a fair distribution of opportunities from birth. This makes social ethics a field closely and inseparably connected with political philosophy and welfare economics.

## Ethics of Intermediate Institutions

Social ethics pays particular attention to the intermediate institutions that connect the individual with the state, such as the family, the school, trade unions, and voluntary associations. These are regarded as the space in which civic virtues such as cooperation and mutual trust are formed before they pass into the wider political sphere. The sociologist Émile Durkheim observed that the weakening of these intermediate institutions in modern societies can lead to what he called "anomie," a state of weakened collective moral regulation that he linked, in *Suicide* (1897), to certain forms of suicide. Hence the importance of rebuilding these institutions in contemporary policy, whether by supporting the family as the primary nucleus of moral upbringing, or by enabling civil society organizations to play an independent oversight and solidarity role, free from the power of the state and the dominance of the market. The aim is a moral balance between the freedom of the individual and the requirements of social cohesion.
