# Law and Society Studies

## Historical Context

The field of law and society rests on a basic premise: law cannot be adequately understood apart from the social structure in which it arises and operates. In the Islamic juristic tradition, **custom** ('urf) was among the recognized subsidiary sources of usul al-fiqh. Jurists established the maxim "custom is binding" (al-'adah muhakkama), and the **Hanafis** and **Malikis** in particular held that many subsidiary rulings (such as the amounts of maintenance, the forms of customary contracts, and the determination of what counts as a defect entitling a party to rescind a sale) are built on the customary practice of people rather than on a specific text, provided that custom does not contradict a definitive scriptural text. This is an early recognition that the legal rule is woven into a social fabric that determines its actual content. The modern academic field, by contrast, took shape in the West from the convergence of legal sociology in **Max Weber**, who studied the modes of legitimacy and rational legal authority, with the American legal realism movement. The Law and Society Association was founded in the United States in 1964 to institutionalize this interdisciplinary field academically.

## Central Ideas

The field distinguishes between "law in books" and "law in action," holding that the gap between them is the real subject of study: how rules are actually applied in courts, streets and institutions, and who in practice reaches justice and who is effectively excluded from it despite formal equality before the text. Another central concept is "legal pluralism," which tracks the coexistence of multiple legal systems (the state, local custom, Sharia, and the rules of community groups) within a single social space, making "law" a social phenomenon broader than state legislation. The field also studies "legal mobilization": how social actors use legal rights as a tool for political change.

## Key Thinkers

**Max Weber** offered an analytical framework for types of legitimate authority (traditional, charismatic, and rational-legal), holding that modern law is an expression of the rationalization of Western bureaucratic society specifically. **Karl Llewellyn**, although originally aligned with legal realism, made a central contribution to this field through his joint anthropological research with E. Adamson Hoebel on dispute-resolution practices among the Cheyenne people, published as *The Cheyenne Way* (1941). This work established an empirical method for studying "law" in societies outside the formal Western framework.

## Impact and Application

This field has shaped the design of public policy by encouraging "empirical legal studies," which measure the actual effects of laws rather than relying solely on abstract normative analysis. Studies of access to justice, for example, revealed how costs and procedural complexity limit the ability of the poor to use the judicial system despite their formal legal equality. This approach has also informed studies of legal pluralism in postcolonial societies, where official courts coexist with parallel customary or religious dispute-resolution mechanisms.

## Criticism and Problems

The field is sometimes criticized for being closer to describing reality than to offering a normative standard for what law should be. It is also said that its methodological plurality (combining sociology, anthropology and economics) may cost it the unified theoretical coherence that distinguishes the parent disciplines from which it borrowed its tools.

## Legal Pluralism in the Contemporary Islamic Context

Legal pluralism is observed with particular clarity in societies with Muslim majorities or Muslim minorities, where state courts coexist with fatwa councils or Sharia arbitration bodies. Their rulings are often applied informally in personal status matters, without formal legal authority. This creates what the anthropologist Sally Falk Moore called "semi-autonomous social fields" (1973), in which religious and customary groups negotiate their actual authority with that of the official state. Studying these fields reveals that "law enforcement" is not a simple hierarchical process from top to bottom, but a continuous negotiation among multiple sources of legitimacy.

## Field Research Methodology

Researchers in this field tend to use field tools (interviews, participant observation, and statistical analysis of court decisions) rather than limiting themselves to textual analysis of legal codes. Their conviction is that understanding law in practice requires moving out of the courtroom and into the street, the institution and the family, where rules are translated into daily practices. Those practices may contradict the literal wording of the rules, or empty them of content in practice without any formal repeal.
