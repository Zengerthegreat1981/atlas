# Bourdieu's Distinction & Cultural Reproduction

Pierre Bourdieu's large empirical study of how cultural "taste" (in art, music, food, dress) works as a hidden instrument for distinguishing social classes and reproducing inequality across generations.

## Definition

The "sociology of distinction and cultural reproduction" is the empirical and theoretical project developed by the French sociologist Pierre Bourdieu (1930–2002). It starts from the hypothesis that the "taste" practices of a society (the choice of music, food, dress, leisure) are not a free expression of individual aesthetic sensibility but a hidden social instrument for distinguishing classes and entrenching symbolic domination across generations, above all through the educational system.

## Historical Context

In this project Bourdieu relied on survey questionnaires conducted in France in the 1960s and early 1970s, covering a little over a thousand respondents about their cultural tastes, together with other statistical sources and observation, and whose results were published in his central book *Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste* (La Distinction: Critique sociale du jugement, 1979). This work followed up his earlier project on education, begun with the sociologist Jean-Claude Passeron in two books: *The Inheritors: French Students and Their Relation to Culture* (Les Héritiers, 1964) and then *Reproduction in Education, Society and Culture* (La Reproduction: Éléments pour une théorie du système d'enseignement, 1970). It arose in a French context that had seen a great expansion of university education after the Second World War, in which the question "does education achieve equality of opportunity?" was posed with renewed political urgency.

## Main Ideas or Method

In *Distinction* (1979), relying on this survey material, Bourdieu argued that "taste", which appears to be a purely free, natural individual choice, is closely correlated statistically with class position, and that this correlation is not coincidence but a systematic social mechanism: the dominant classes use their "refined taste" (a preference for abstract art, classical music, "light" rather than "heavy" cuisine) as a marker of symbolic distinction that legitimizes their social superiority as an inherent, natural "refinement" rather than an acquired class privilege, while the tastes of the popular classes ("popular taste") are stigmatized as coarse or lacking refinement. This is a process of hidden "symbolic violence" (Violence Symbolique) that leads the victims themselves to accept this hierarchy as natural rather than perceiving it as a social arbitrariness. To this end Bourdieu developed an interrelated conceptual triad: the "field" (Champ) as a social space of competition over different kinds of capital; the "habitus" (Habitus) as a system of dispositions acquired early that guide tastes and practices without explicit awareness of them; and "capital" in its three kinds: economic, cultural (knowledge, credentials, modes of speech) and social (networks and relationships). In *Reproduction* (1970, with Passeron) he analyzed the educational system as the main instrument of this process: education appears to be a neutral "merit" mechanism that rewards the most intelligent, but in fact it rewards the cultural capital that well-off families have already transmitted to their children (through "pedagogic symbolic violence," which imposes the culture of the dominant class as a neutral general standard), and so reproduces class hierarchy disguised as neutral individual achievement. This gives inequality a doubled legitimacy, because it appears to be the result of personal merit rather than social inheritance.

## Major Works or Representatives

Besides Bourdieu himself and his partner Jean-Claude Passeron, this current branched out in France through many students and followers, among them Luc Boltanski, who later developed an internal critique of Bourdieu's approach in the "sociology of critique." Internationally, the American sociologist Paul DiMaggio tested Bourdieu's hypotheses empirically in the American context (for example, "Cultural Capital and School Success", 1982), and Annette Lareau applied the concept of cultural capital to class differences in child-rearing in *Unequal Childhoods* (2003).

## Impact and Critique

This work provided a highly influential analytical tool in the sociology of culture and education, directly influenced later debates on educational justice and social mobility across different educational systems worldwide, and its concepts (habitus, cultural capital, field) became standard tools in the social sciences generally. However, critics, most prominently Raymond Boudon from the standpoint of methodological individualism, objected that Bourdieu's model is excessively deterministic: it portrays social actors as if they were predetermined by their class habitus, with no real margin for rational choice or upward mobility, and that his data from 1960s France may not generalize easily to other societies with different class and cultural structures. Even so, Bourdieu's conceptual tools (field, habitus, capital in its kinds) remained among the most used sociological frameworks in contemporary empirical studies around the world, applied by researchers to many national contexts outside France, from the United States to Latin America and the Arab world, with adjustments that take account of the specificity of each local context.
