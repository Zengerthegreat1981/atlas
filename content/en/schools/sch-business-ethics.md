# Business Ethics

Business ethics studies the ethical standards that should govern the conduct of individuals and institutions in trade, production, and exchange. It is an applied field at the intersection of moral philosophy, economics, and management science. It examines the foundations on which practices of profit, competition, employment, advertising, and responsibility toward consumers, the environment, and society are justified.

## Roots in Islamic Thought

Islamic civilization was not unfamiliar with reflection on the ethics of earning and commerce. Jurists, hadith scholars and moralists produced a rich legacy in this domain. The prophetic tradition emphasizes honesty in dealings and prohibits fraud, and the Qur'an, in Surat al-Mutaffifin (83), warns against giving short measure. Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, in his *Revival of the Religious Sciences* (*Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*), included a book on the etiquette of earning and livelihood. Islamic law restricts riba (usury), hoarding for price manipulation (ihtikar) and gharar (excessive uncertainty) in contracts, so that property is not consumed wrongfully. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah* (1377), offered a social analysis of trade, labor and economic life. Jurists of all the schools developed a detailed law of transactions (*mu'amalat*) distinguishing lawful from unlawful contracts, and contemporary Islamic finance draws on this jurisprudence.

## Historical Context in Western Thought

The field of business ethics emerged as an independent academic specialization in the United States during the 1970s, following major financial and industrial scandals that led universities to teach dedicated courses. Its intellectual roots, however, are much older. They go back to Adam Smith's discussions in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) and *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) on the relationship between self-interest and social virtue, to the Marxist critique of capitalist exploitation, and to labor movements that demanded workers' rights and fair working hours.

## Central Ideas

The field revolves around fundamental questions. What are the limits of a company's social responsibility? Is its sole purpose to maximize profit for shareholders, as Milton Friedman argued in his 1970 essay "The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits," or does it have obligations to all stakeholders, including workers, customers, and local communities, as proposed by the stakeholder theory developed by R. Edward Freeman? Other central issues include the ethics of advertising and the limits of manipulating consumers; the fairness of wages and the income gap; the environmental responsibility of multinational corporations; and the ethics of governance and financial transparency in the face of corruption and conflicts of interest.

## Key Thinkers and Theoretical Approaches

Theoretical approaches fall into three major streams. The first is utilitarian, which measures the rightness of a business decision by the aggregate benefits it yields to everyone affected. The second is Kantian (deontological), which stresses respect for the dignity of the individuals who work for or deal with the firm, so that they may not be used merely as a means to profit. The third is virtue-based, drawing on Aristotle and concerned with building an institutional culture that values integrity, justice, and moderation. Robert Solomon contributed to developing a virtue-based model of business. Archie Carroll proposed a "pyramid" of corporate social responsibility (1991), with economic, legal, ethical and philanthropic responsibilities.

## Contemporary Impact and Application

The field has given rise to institutional codes of conduct, standards for sustainability and social responsibility (ESG), and ethics committees within large corporations, as well as international legislation regulating the fight against corruption and the protection of workers' rights. A prominent example is the Enron scandal of 2001. It revealed accounting manipulation that led to the collapse of the company and the loss of many employees' jobs and retirement savings, and it has since become a classic case study in the failure of ethical governance. The field's questions remain strongly present in debates over the ethics of artificial intelligence in hiring and the fairness of global supply chains.

## Additional Contemporary Issues

The field is now expanding its scrutiny of the ethics of economic globalization. Sharp questions are raised about the fairness of relocating factories to countries with weak labor legislation, and about the responsibility of large corporations for supply chains that may involve child labor or harsh working conditions. Digital privacy ethics has emerged as a modern extension of the field, as technology companies are questioned about the limits of their use of consumer data for marketing or surveillance purposes.

Researchers distinguish three levels of ethical analysis in business. The individual level concerns the conduct of an employee or manager in particular situations. The organizational level concerns company policies and internal culture. The systemic level concerns the fairness of markets and of economic legislation itself. Recurring financial crises, most notably the crisis of 2008, have added fresh momentum to calls for an ethical culture in the financial sector. Such a culture would go beyond formal legal compliance to proactive responsibility for systemic risks that may inflict wide damage on the global economy.
