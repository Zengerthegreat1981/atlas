# Madhyamaka (Prāsaṅgika and Svātantrika)

Madhyamaka ("the Middle Way doctrine") is one of the two major schools of Indian Mahayana Buddhist philosophy (with Yogācāra), traditionally traced to Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE). It holds that all phenomena are "empty" (śūnyatā) of fixed inherent nature (svabhāva), and that this emptiness is not nothingness but is the very same as dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda), so that the Middle is between affirming a permanent substance (eternalism) and denying existence altogether (annihilationism).

## Origins and Context

The school appeared in the midst of a sharp debate among the Abhidharma schools, foremost among them the Sarvāstivāda, who held that the "dharmas" (the primary elements of experience) possess intrinsic existence in the three times. Nāgārjuna saw that this position contradicts the Buddha's teachings on dependent origination, because what has an intrinsic nature needs no causes and does not change. Statements about the emptiness of phenomena also appeared in the literature of the "Prajñāpāramitā" (the Perfection of Wisdom), which prepared the ground for his philosophical interpretation. Traditional accounts attribute to him a life in southern India, but his earliest biography is mixed with legends, and nothing is known of it with certainty beyond the fact that he is the author of the pivotal text "The Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way" (Mūlamadhyamakakārikā).

## Central Ideas

- **Emptiness (śūnyatā)**: nothing possesses an independent inherent nature; the existence of everything depends on its causes, its parts, and the designation by which it is identified.
- **The two truths**: a conventional truth (saṃvṛti) in which everyday practice proceeds, and an ultimate truth (paramārtha), which is emptiness. In chapter twenty-four Nāgārjuna states that whoever has not understood the two truths does not understand the Buddha's teaching, and elsewhere in the text he teaches that emptiness itself is empty, so that it does not become a new doctrine to be clung to.
- **Dependent origination**: Nāgārjuna equates emptiness with dependent origination, dependent designation and the Middle Way (24:18), so emptiness means nothing but the absence of inherent nature.
- **The tetralemma (catuṣkoṭi)**: he examines the four positions: a thing exists, does not exist, both, neither, and negates them all with regard to inherent nature. The "Verses" open with the eightfold negation: no cessation and no arising, no annihilation and no permanence, no unity and no plurality, no coming and no going.
- **The dialectical method**: he analyzes concepts such as causality, motion, time, the self and nirvana to show that belief in inherent nature leads to contradictions.

## Works and Figures

- **Nāgārjuna**: author of "The Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way," of "The Dispeller of Disputes" (Vigrahavyāvartanī), in which he answers the objection of the Naiyāyikas that emptiness undermines the very instruments of knowledge, of the "Sixty Verses on Reasoning," the "Seventy Verses on Emptiness," and the "Precious Garland" (Ratnāvalī).
- **Āryadeva**: his student, author of the "Four Hundred Verses" (Catuḥśataka).
- **Buddhapālita**: a commentator of the late fifth or early sixth century who was content with refuting the opponent's theses.
- **Bhāviveka** (c. 500–570 CE): criticized Buddhapālita and insisted on the use of independent inferences; author of the "Lamp of Wisdom" and the "Blaze of Reasoning."
- **Candrakīrti** (7th century): defended Buddhapālita's position in the "Clear Words" (Prasannapadā) and the "Entry into the Middle Way" (Madhyamakāvatāra).
- **Śāntarakṣita** (c. 725–788) and his student Kamalaśīla: combined the Middle Way with Dharmakīrti's logic and Yogācāra ideas in the "Ornament of the Middle Way," and contributed to the establishment of Buddhism in Tibet.

## The Two Branches: Prāsaṅgika and Svātantrika

The two names were coined by Tibetan doxographers of a later period and were not common among the school's own members. The "Prāsaṅgikas" (from prasaṅga, a necessary consequence) are those who content themselves with showing the contradictory consequences of the opponent's thesis without committing to a positive thesis of their own, foremost among them Candrakīrti. The "Svātantrikas" (from svatantra, independent reasoning) hold that debate between the two sides requires a syllogism acceptable to both parties, foremost among them Bhāviveka. These divide into "Sautrāntika-Madhyamaka" in Bhāviveka, who accepts external reality at the conventional level, and "Yogācāra-Madhyamaka" in Śāntarakṣita and Kamalaśīla. The Tibetan Gelug school, led by Tsongkhapa (1357–1419), favoured the Prāsaṅgika position.

## Spread and Influence

The text passed to China through Kumārajīva's translation (c. 400 CE), from which arose the Sanlun school, "the Three Treatises school," and its scholar Jizang, and it left an influence on Chinese Chan. In Tibet the Middle Way became the pillar of philosophical education in the monasteries. In the modern era Jay Garfield and others re-read it in comparison with analytic philosophy. Likewise Kyoto School thinkers, notably Nishitani Keiji, engaged with the Buddhist idea of emptiness (śūnyatā) in dialogue with Western philosophy.

## Limits of the Doctrine and Its Critique

Its Indian opponents, including the Naiyāyikas, object that negating the means of knowledge itself demolishes the argument that does the negating, and Nāgārjuna replied that emptiness does not entail a fixed thesis. Some modern commentators hold that the tetralemma involves an acceptable contradiction (Garfield and Priest), while others see it as mere subsequent negation without contradiction. There is also a historical dispute over how far "emptiness" is to be interpreted nihilistically, which led other Buddhist schools such as Yogācāra to accuse the Middle Way of nihilism. The question of how to combine conventional truth with ultimate truth without the latter becoming a "thing" remains one of its hardest problems.
