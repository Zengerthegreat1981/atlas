# Neurobiology of Mental Illness

This field comprises research that explains psychiatric disorders through brain function, neurotransmitter chemistry, hormones, the immune system and genes, from the discovery of the roles of dopamine and serotonin in the 1960s to today's models of neural circuits and epigenetics.

## Roots in Arab-Islamic Medicine

The idea of tracing psychiatric disorder to the brain did not begin in the twentieth century. Physicians in the Islamic civilization placed the brain at the center of madness and melancholia, while also adding an explanation in terms of humors and temperaments. Abu Bakr al-Razi, in his al-Hawi, linked disorders of the mind to the condition and temperament of the brain. Ibn Sina, in al-Qanun fi al-Tibb, described melancholia, mania and epilepsy as conditions with physical causes, managed therapeutically through diet, medicine and calming. Abu Zayd al-Balkhi, in Masalih al-Abdan wa-al-Anfus, combined the treatment of the body with the treatment of the soul, distinguishing illnesses of bodily origin from those whose cause is anxiety and sorrow. Wards for the insane were established within the bimaristans in Baghdad, Cairo and Damascus, a subject treated by Michael Dols in his study of the madman in medieval Islamic society. This does not mean that the concept of humors and modern neurochemistry are identical, since the two theories differ in their tools and their proof. It shows, however, that the approach linking the soul to the organ has a history that predates modern neuroscience.

## Emergence of the Field in the 1960s

The modern field took shape once neurotransmitters could be measured in brain tissue. In the late 1950s Arvid Carlsson in Sweden showed that dopamine is an independent neurotransmitter and not merely a precursor of noradrenaline, and that its depletion by reserpine produces motor stillness that is reversed by L-dopa, which paved the way for the treatment of Parkinson's disease. Carlsson and Lindqvist then reported in 1963 that chlorpromazine and haloperidol raise the metabolites of dopamine and noradrenaline in the brain, leading to the inference that antipsychotics block dopamine receptors. In contrast, clinical observations showed that repeated doses of amphetamine produce a state resembling paranoid schizophrenia. In the mid-1970s Philip Seeman and Solomon Snyder and their colleagues (Creese, Burt and Snyder, 1976) independently showed that the clinical potency of antipsychotics corresponds to their ability to bind dopamine receptors (later identified as D2-type), which made the hypothesis measurable in the laboratory.

In depression, Joseph Schildkraut formulated the catecholamine hypothesis of mood disorders in 1965, and Alec Coppen proposed a role for serotonin in 1967. This was based on the observation that iproniazid and imipramine, among the first antidepressants, increase the availability of monoamines at the synapse. This line of work led to the development of fluoxetine in the Eli Lilly laboratories in the 1970s, the first selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor.

## What Changed Afterward

The following decades showed that the monoamine model is not sufficient. In 1991 Davis, Kahn, Ko and Davidson reformulated the dopamine hypothesis, arguing that hyperactivity in subcortical pathways is accompanied by reduced activity in the prefrontal cortex. In 2009 Howes and Kapur put forward version three, which makes excessive dopamine synthesis in the striatum the common link between genetic and environmental risk factors. Weinberger showed in 1987 that schizophrenia may be a disorder of brain development, meaning that its basis is laid years before symptoms appear.

In depression, research turned to the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. In the early 1980s Carroll and colleagues documented failure of the dexamethasone suppression test in a considerable proportion of people with major depression, and in 2008 Pariante and Lightman summarized the evidence for hyperactivity of the axis. In 2008 Dantzer and colleagues explained how inflammatory cytokines generate sickness behavior that overlaps with depression, and in 2016 Miller and Raison reviewed evidence that higher inflammation is associated with weaker responses to conventional antidepressants in some patients. The 2000 study by Berman and colleagues on the rapid antidepressant effect of ketamine drew attention to glutamate rather than serotonin alone.

At the genetic level, the working group of the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium identified 108 genomic loci associated with schizophrenia in 2014, and showed that each locus adds a small effect, so the illness is polygenic rather than the product of a single gene. From this arose the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) project at the US National Institute of Mental Health, presented by Insel and colleagues in 2010, which studies neural circuits and functional dimensions instead of traditional diagnostic categories.

## Limits and Criticism

The strength of evidence varies among the field's hypotheses. In 2022 Moncrieff and colleagues conducted a comprehensive umbrella review of reviews and found no consistent evidence that depression arises from low serotonin or from altered serotonergic activity. This sparked wide debate between those who saw it as a correction of a simplistic marketing narrative and those who held that its findings do not negate the efficacy of antidepressants. Diagnostically useful biomarkers remain scarce: there is no blood test or scan that confirms a psychiatric diagnosis in routine practice. The field is also criticized for a reductionism that neglects the roles of trauma, poverty and social and cultural meaning, a critique that Kirmayer and colleagues advance in cultural psychiatry. The umbrella that houses these lines is not a unified theory but a field of research in which multiple tools intersect and whose results vary.

This entry is educational and descriptive; it is not clinical advice. The hypotheses described here are partial explanations, not complete theories.
