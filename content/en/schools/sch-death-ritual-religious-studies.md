# The Academic Study of Death and Funerary Ritual: Douglas Davies and Tony Walter

A subfield of comparative religious studies that arose from the convergence of the sociology of religion with the study of funerary rituals and beliefs about death across cultures. It is distinguished from clinical thanatology by its focus on the social and religious structure of death, rather than on the psychological experience of the dying alone. The field took academic shape in Britain in the 1990s through two scholars working along complementary paths: a scholar of religion and a sociologist. Douglas Davies approached it from the comparative religious theory of ritual at Durham University, and Tony Walter from critical sociology at the University of Bath. Together they challenged the thesis of the "sequestration of death" that prevailed at the time.

## Classical Roots: Hertz and Van Gennep

Classical theories of death ritual preceded the field. In his essay "Death and the Right Hand" (1907), Robert Hertz showed that double burial (a temporary burial followed by the transfer of the bones) follows two social stages: the separation of the deceased from the world of the living and their transformation into an ancestor. He also showed that the end of mourning coincides with the completion of this transition, and that the body of the deceased and their relatives are treated as if in a state of transition. Arnold van Gennep, in *The Rites of Passage* (1909), linked the funeral with passage between social states. Later, Maurice Bloch and Jonathan Parry, in *Death and the Regeneration of Life* (1982), studied how the funeral is transformed into an affirmation of the renewal of life and of society. Parry, in *Death in Banaras* (1994), studied the cremation of the dead on the ghats. On this foundation Davies and Walter built their models.

## Death in the Islamic Tradition: Comparative Material

In the comparative literature, the Islamic funeral is treated as a codified ritual system: washing the body and shrouding it in white cloth; the collective funeral prayer, performed without bowing or prostration; burial as quickly as possible, with the grave oriented towards the qibla; disapproval of wailing and of building over graves; mourning for three days in general, and the waiting period of a widow of four months and ten days. Leor Halevi, in *Muhammad's Grave: Death Rites and the Making of Islamic Society* (2007), examined how death practices helped shape an Islamic identity distinct from Christianity and Judaism in the first centuries, so that the funeral became a field for producing juristic norms. Al-Ghazali, in the "Book on the Remembrance of Death and What Follows" within *Ihya' Ulum al-Din*, addressed preparation for death as the refinement of the soul (translated by T. J. Winter, 1989). Among the Shi'a, the fortieth-day commemoration, 'Ashura and mourning and condolence rituals are organised into a network of collective rites that make mourning a political-religious memory. These examples show that the comparative question about the relationship between ritual and the community is more pressing than the question about the individual.

## From "Sequestration of Death" to "Revival of Death"

Until the late 1980s, a thesis prevailed in British sociology. It was associated with Philippe Ariès and Geoffrey Gorer, and was later formulated in theoretical language by Philip Mellor and Chris Shilling (1993). It held that modernity concealed death from public life by moving it to the hospital and the morgue, and impoverished its public rituals. Tony Walter, in *The Revival of Death* (1994), argued that this thesis is partly correct in describing the mid-twentieth century, but that it overlooked a counter-revival that began in the 1970s. This included the hospice movement, the growing public discussion of loss and grief, and a growing media and cultural presence of death and bereavement. He proposed an alternative model for understanding the contemporary "good death", based on individual choice and social negotiation rather than on ready-made religious or medical prescriptions. His work belongs to the same current as the "continuing bonds" idea of Klass, Silverman and Nickman (1996), a critique of the Freudian model of grief, which requires severing the bond with the deceased.

## Davies's Theory of Funerary Ritual

In *Death, Ritual and Belief: The Rhetoric of Funerary Rites* (1997), Douglas Davies puts forward a comparative theory that explains why almost all known cultures produce funerary rituals. In his view, death is the site of "words against death": a symbolic-linguistic act produced by the community to reformulate the meaning of loss and to assert the continuity of collective identity in the face of an event that biologically threatens it. The book surveys several religious traditions, showing the diversity of ritual solutions (cremation, burial, reincarnation, collective immortality) to one human problem. In later works he extended this to the history of cremation (the *Encyclopedia of Cremation*, with Lewis Mates, 2005) and to *A Brief History of Death* (2005). Glennys Howarth, in *Last Rites* (1996), studied the work of the modern funeral director as a ritual intermediary between the family and the institution.

## Limits and Criticism

Critics argue that Davies tends toward a functional generalisation about "community and meaning" that hides the tension between family, church and state in actual funerals. They also argue that Walter's individual "choice" model reflects a British middle class and overlooks the poor and migrants, whom expenses and religious law constrain. Most studies have focused on British Christianity in a secular context. The comparison of Muslim and Hindu funerals in the contemporary West, and the legal problems they raise (separate Muslim burial sections, open-air cremation), has lagged behind. There is also a methodological debate about the relationship between the written ritual (jurisprudence and theology) and the practice, which may depart from it, and about the need for ethnography.
