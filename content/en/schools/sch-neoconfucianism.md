# Neo-Confucianism (Umbrella Term)

"Neo-Confucianism" is a modern Western name for a Chinese intellectual movement that extended from the late Tang period and the Song period (especially the 11th and 12th centuries) to the Ming period and the beginning of the Qing. It recast the Confucian heritage by adding a metaphysical, cosmic, and psychological dimension, in response to the intellectual challenge of Buddhism and Daoism. Chinese sources call it "Daoxue" (learning of the Way), "Lixue" (learning of principle), or "Xinxue" (learning of the mind/heart); no single Chinese name corresponds to the umbrella term. For centuries it became the official ideology in China and Korea and spread to Japan and Vietnam.

## Roots

Han Yu (768–824), in "On the Origin of the Way" (Yuan Dao), declared that the Confucian Way had been interrupted since Mencius, and attacked Buddhism as a foreign religion that dismantles the family and the state. Li Ao (772–841) followed him in "Book of Returning to the Nature" (Fu Xing Shu), which investigated human nature and the emotions. This current remained without an organized philosophy until the 11th century.

## Figures of the Northern Song

- **Zhou Dunyi** (1017–1073): presented the "Explanation of the Diagram of the Supreme Ultimate" (Taiji Tu Shuo), a short cosmology that links the "Supreme Ultimate" with yin and yang and the five elements.
- **Shao Yong** (1011–1077): author of a numerical theory of the cycles of the cosmos.
- **Zhang Zai** (1020–1077): made "qi" (material energy) the basis of existence, and wrote the "Western Inscription," which describes the universe as one family.
- **Cheng Hao** (1032–1085) and **Cheng Yi** (1033–1107): the two brothers who formulated the idea of "principle" (li) as the order of things. Later tradition traces the School of Mind to Cheng Hao and the School of Principle to Cheng Yi, though references differ on how sharp this division was.
- **Zhu Xi** (1130–1200): who gathered their heritage into a comprehensive synthesis. He made "li" the one supreme cosmic principle and "qi" the living matter in which it is embodied, held that the "Supreme Ultimate" is the sum of the principles, and stressed the "investigation of things" (ge wu), gradual study, and the "Four Books": the Analects, Mencius, the Great Learning, and the Doctrine of the Mean, for which he wrote commentaries. After 1313, under the Yuan, the state made them the official standard for the imperial examinations, and they remained so until 1905.

## The Opposing Current: The School of Mind

Lu Jiuyuan (1139–1193) denied that the truth is reached by studying external things and said that "the mind is principle"; a debate took place between him and Zhu Xi at Goose Lake (1175). Wang Yangming (1472–1529) then made "knowledge and action one" and "innate knowledge of the good" (liangzhi) the source of morality in the mind, and he influenced Japan and later reform movements.

## Later Developments

In the late Ming and early Qing, the "Kaozheng" current (evidential research) arose, which turned to philological and historical verification in place of metaphysical contemplation, and Dai Zhen (1724–1777) criticized the sanctification of "principle" as a means of restraining people. In the 20th century Feng Youlan's *History of Chinese Philosophy* (written in Chinese in the 1930s; the second volume of Derk Bodde's English translation appeared in 1953) presented this stage to English readers as Neo-Confucianism, and "Modern Neo-Confucianism" appeared as an independent movement.

## Regional Spread

In Korea it flourished under the Joseon dynasty with Yi Hwang (Toegye, 1501–1570) and Yi I (Yulgok, 1536–1584) and became state doctrine. In Japan it was spread by Fujiwara Seika and Hayashi Razan at the beginning of the Tokugawa period. In Vietnam it became a pillar of official education under the late Le dynasty.

## The Link with Islam in China

In the 17th and 18th centuries there appeared in China the "Han Kitab" movement of Chinese Muslim scholars who wrote on Islamic doctrine in Chinese using Neo-Confucian terminology. Wang Daiyu (c. 1584–1670) composed the "True Explanation of the True Teaching" (1642), and Liu Zhi (c. 1660–1730) wrote "Islamic Rites and Etiquette" (Tianfang Dianli, 1710), in which he explains doctrine and Sufism through the concepts of "li," "qi," and the "Supreme Ultimate." Sachiko Murata studied these texts in *Chinese Gleams of Sufi Light* (2000) and in *The Sage Learning of Liu Zhi* (2009). It is an example of a civilizational dialogue in which local terminology was employed to explain monotheism (tawhid), and it shows that Neo-Confucianism was open to interpretation in other religious contexts.

## Limits and Criticism

Some scholars hold that the name "Neo-Confucianism" imposes unity on very different currents, and that the "newness" itself is open to debate, since its proponents claimed to be restoring the original. Qing thinkers criticized the rigidity of "principle" in imposing harsh moral judgments on individuals, especially women. The adoption of Zhu Xi's commentaries in the examinations led to stagnation in education. Peter Bol, in *Neo-Confucianism in History* (2008), points out that the movement changed socially between the ruling elites and the local elites.
