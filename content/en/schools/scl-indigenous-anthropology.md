# Indigenous Anthropology

Indigenous anthropology is the practice and study of anthropology by Indigenous peoples themselves, and the body of theory and method that has grown out of that standpoint. It reverses the discipline's founding arrangement, in which Indigenous peoples were the objects of study and almost never its authors. Where classical anthropology sent outsiders to document other people's lives, Indigenous anthropology begins from the knowledge, languages, histories, and political claims of Indigenous communities, and it treats those communities as the authorities on their own worlds. It is at once a research tradition, a methodological movement, and a decolonial political project.

## History and Emergence

Anthropology was built on colonial encounter, and Indigenous criticism of that arrangement is as old as the discipline. From the late nineteenth century onward, Indigenous intellectuals challenged the way ethnographers represented them, but the academy rarely listened. The decisive break came in the late 1960s, when Vine Deloria Jr., a Standing Rock Sioux lawyer and theologian, published *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969), a furious indictment of anthropologists who, in his words, descended on reservations to collect data and careers while giving nothing back. Deloria's attack landed at the same moment as the wider Indigenous rights movements of the 1960s and 1970s, from the American Indian Movement in the United States to land rights campaigns in Australia and Canada.

Through the 1980s and 1990s, Indigenous scholars began to enter the academy in growing numbers and to build their own institutions, journals, and research protocols. Maori scholar Linda Tuhiwai Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies* (1999) became the movement's most influential manifesto, arguing that research itself had been one of the instruments of imperialism and that Indigenous peoples had the right to control how knowledge about them was produced. In the 2000s, a second wave of methodological writing — by Shawn Wilson, Margaret Kovach, Bagele Chilisa, and others — turned Smith's critique into a set of working methods grounded in Indigenous philosophies of knowledge.

## Key Figures

Vine Deloria Jr. (1933–2005) supplied the founding critique, insisting that anthropology served colonial power and that Indigenous peoples should define the terms of research about them. Linda Tuhiwai Smith (born 1950), of Ngapuhi and Ngati Awa descent in Aotearoa New Zealand, reframed research as a decolonial practice and gave the movement its central text. Shawn Wilson (born 1966), an Opaskwayak Cree scholar, developed the idea of relational accountability, the principle that research is answerable first to relationships rather than to academic institutions. Margaret Kovach, a Plains Cree and Saulteaux scholar, articulated Indigenous methodologies as a distinct paradigm rooted in tribal epistemologies. Kim TallBear (born 1968), a Sisseton Wahpeton Oyate scholar, extended the critique into genetics and science studies, showing how DNA research recycles colonial categories. Audra Simpson (born 1970), a Kahnawake Mohawk anthropologist, developed the concept of ethnographic refusal, the right of Indigenous peoples to withhold knowledge from the ethnographic record.

## Key Works

The canon of Indigenous anthropology is short but densely influential. Deloria's *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969) opened the critique. Smith's *Decolonizing Methodologies: Research and Indigenous Peoples* (1999) gave it a systematic methodology. Wilson's *Research Is Ceremony: Indigenous Research Methods* (2008) argued that research is a ceremonial act in which the researcher is accountable to the community, not merely to the university. Kovach's *Indigenous Methodologies: Characteristics, Conversations, and Contexts* (2009) set out a framework for research grounded in Indigenous knowledge systems. TallBear's *Native American DNA: Tribal Belonging and the False Promise of Genetic Science* (2013) showed how genetic testing undermines tribal sovereignty. Simpson's *Mohawk Interruptus: Political Life Across the Borders of Settler States* (2014) demonstrated ethnographic refusal in practice, writing about Kahnawake politics without revealing what the community chose to keep private.

## Method

Indigenous anthropology's methods follow from its epistemology: knowledge is relational, situated, and accountable. Research begins with community consent and community-defined questions, not with a researcher's hypothesis. Oral history, storytelling, and ceremony are treated as valid forms of evidence rather than as raw material to be translated into Western theory. Researchers are expected to give back — through co-authorship, community reports, language revitalization, or political support — and to accept that some knowledge is not theirs to publish. The principle of relational accountability means that the quality of research is judged by the quality of the relationships it builds and sustains, and by its usefulness to the community, rather than by citation counts alone. This is what distinguishes Indigenous anthropology from simply having Indigenous people do conventional anthropology: the methodology itself changes.

## Influence and Debates

Indigenous anthropology has reshaped the wider discipline. Ethics boards now routinely require community consent; collaborative and community-based participatory research have become standard in many subfields; and the return of museum collections and human remains — repatriation — has moved from a fringe demand to institutional policy. The movement has also influenced neighboring fields, from Indigenous studies and Native American and Indigenous studies programs to science and technology studies, where TallBear's work on DNA has become central.

Debates continue. Some critics ask whether Indigenous anthropology risks essentialism, treating "Indigenous" as a single identity rather than a diverse set of peoples with different histories. Others debate how far refusal can go before it limits scholarly understanding, and how Indigenous methodologies should relate to other critical traditions such as feminism and Marxism. Within the movement, there are tensions between scholars working inside universities and activists who see the academy itself as irredeemably colonial. What is no longer debated is the founding claim: Indigenous peoples are not anthropology's subject matter but its authors, and the discipline is stronger for the change.
