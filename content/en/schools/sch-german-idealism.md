# German Idealism (umbrella)

A broad philosophical umbrella extending from the "critical" Kant (1781) to the death of Hegel (1831), comprising a series of successive doctrines that share one central idea: **reason does not receive reality from outside but takes part in producing it**. German Idealism differs from Berkeley's subjective idealism in that it is a transcendental doctrine that starts from the structure of consciousness and reason, not from individual experience. This page deals with the general framework: the driving questions, the intermediate links, and the criticism, while the pages on Fichte, Hegel, and right and left Hegelianism treat each branch in detail.

## The Driving Questions

- **What remains of philosophy after Kant?** Kant left a dualism between appearances and the thing in itself, between theoretical and practical reason, and between nature and freedom, so the task of his successors became bridging these gaps.
- **The dispute over atheism and fatalism**: in 1785 Friedrich Heinrich Jacobi set off the "Pantheism Controversy" (the Spinoza dispute) when he claimed that Lessing had died a Spinozist and that methodical reason leads to determinism and atheism; the following generation was therefore committed to showing that philosophy can combine reason and freedom.
- **The single foundation**: Karl Leonhard Reinhold (1789) wanted to rebuild philosophy on the principle of "consciousness", and Salomon Maimon added sharp doubts about the thing in itself.

## The Internal Course

- **Kant** (1724–1804): in the *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781/1787) he defined the conditions of the possibility of knowledge, and in the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) and the *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790) he completed the structure.
- **Fichte** (1762–1814): made the active "I" the first principle of all knowledge.
- **Schelling** (1775–1854): extended idealism to include nature in the *Philosophy of Nature* (1797) and the *System of Transcendental Idealism* (1800), then turned to the philosophy of identity and then the philosophy of revelation.
- **Hölderlin, Hegel, and Schelling in Tübingen**: the three took part in the "Oldest Systematic Program of German Idealism" (c. 1796), a short text of disputed authorship that calls for an aesthetic philosophy and a religion of reason.
- **Hegel** (1770–1831): in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807) and the *Science of Logic* (1812–1816) he moves the project to an absolute doctrine that sees all reality as the expression of the Idea in necessary development.
- **Branchings after Hegel**: the current split into right, center, and left.

## Shared Features

- The methodological priority of the concept of "Spirit" (Geist), or the "I", or the "subject", over given matter.
- The concept of the "system" as the necessary form of a complete philosophy.
- The rejection of a categorical separation between knowledge, ethics, art, and religion.
- Interest in history as the domain in which reason is realized (Herder, Schiller, Hegel).
- The use of dialectic between opposites as a method and not merely as a disagreement.

## Influence and Reception

- It formed the ground for German Romanticism (Schlegel and Novalis), and for Marx, Kierkegaard, and Nietzsche as responses to it.
- In the twentieth century the Neo-Kantians (Marburg and Baden), Husserl, Heidegger, and Gadamer revived its study, and Frederick Beiser, Terry Pinkard, and Robert Pippin offered modern readings of it.
- In Arabic thought, scholars took an interest in German Idealism in the stages of translation and university study; Imam Abd al-Fattah Imam wrote on and translated Hegel, and Abd al-Rahman Badawi wrote on German philosophy and existentialism. Researchers traced Hegel's influence in modern Arabic thought, especially among Arab Marxists, while others compared Hegelian "Spirit" with Islamic Sufi philosophy, a comparison that remains debated.

## Limits and Criticism

- This is a didactic historical umbrella, not a unified doctrine, and it includes differing positions and sharp clashes (Fichte and Kant, Schelling and Hegel).
- Some historians exclude Kant from "German Idealism" because he rejected dogmatic idealism, and regard him as its founder without belonging to it.
- Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard, and Nietzsche criticized Hegel's systematicity as an abstraction that cancels the real individual.
- The Positivists and analytic philosophers (Russell and Moore) criticized its excess of metaphysics, although the analytic philosophers later reread it (McDowell and Brandom).
- Modern criticism (Habermas and Adorno) stresses its relation to state ideology and the national spirit.

## The Copernican Turn

Kant summed up his revolution by saying that he did in philosophy what Copernicus did in astronomy: instead of assuming that knowledge conforms to objects, we assume that objects conform to the conditions of our knowledge (time, space, and the categories). This "turn" is the common ground on which his successors built, though they differed: Fichte made the source of the conditions the act of the I, Schelling made nature and the subject two faces of one absolute principle, and Hegel held that the conditions themselves develop historically and dialectically. This explains why the whole period is called idealist: intelligibility is the structure of reality, not merely a reflection of it.
