# Anthropology of Aging

The anthropology of aging is the study of how growing old is understood, experienced, and organized across cultures. It treats aging not as a purely biological decline but as a social and cultural process: people learn what it means to be old, societies assign the elderly particular roles and obligations, and states build institutions — pensions, care homes, health systems — around the later years of life. Its central question is deceptively simple: what does it mean to grow old, and how does that meaning change from one society to another?

## History

Although anthropologists had long observed elders in the societies they studied, aging itself was rarely the subject of sustained inquiry until the late twentieth century. Early ethnography often treated old age as a background condition rather than a topic. Margaret Mead's *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928) concerned youth, and classic studies of kinship and politics mentioned elders mainly as holders of authority. The turning point came in the 1960s and 1970s, when anthropologists began to study aging as a life-course process in its own right. Margaret Clark and Barbara Anderson's *Culture and Aging* (1967) was one of the first works to treat the later years systematically, comparing how different societies organize retirement, widowhood, and dependence. In 1978 the Association for Anthropology and Gerontology (AAGE, now the Association for Anthropology, Gerontology, and the Life Course) was founded, giving the field an institutional home and a journal, *Anthropology & Aging*. The 1980s and 1990s brought a wave of ethnographies that made aging a central anthropological concern. Sharon Kaufman's *The Ageless Self* (1986) showed that older Americans often experience a continuing sense of self that resists the identity of "old age" imposed on them. Lawrence Cohen's *No Aging in India* (1998) examined how Alzheimer's disease and senility were understood in India, where aging carried moral and familial meanings very different from those in the United States. Sarah Lamb's *White Saris and Sweet Mangoes* (2000) explored aging among widows in West Bengal, showing how gender, religion, and kinship shape the experience of growing old. By the 2000s the field had expanded to address global aging, transnational care chains, and the politics of pensions and eldercare.

## Key Figures and Works

Several scholars define the field's core literature. Margaret Clark helped establish the anthropology of aging as a comparative project with *Culture and Aging* (1967). Jennie Keith, a leading figure in the field, edited influential volumes on aging across cultures and wrote *Old People, New Lives* (1977), a study of a retirement community in the United States. Sharon Kaufman's *The Ageless Self* (1986) remains a touchstone for its argument that identity in old age is actively maintained rather than passively lost. Lawrence Cohen's *No Aging in India* (1998) is widely cited for its analysis of how "bad families" and modernity were blamed for the suffering of the elderly. Sarah Lamb's *White Saris and Sweet Mangoes* (2000) and her later *Aging and the Indian Diaspora* (2009) connect aging to migration, gender, and transnational family life. Robert Rubinstein's edited volume *Anthropology and Aging* (1990) consolidated the field's methods and themes. More recently, Jason Danely's *Aging and Loss* (2014) examined end-of-life care in Japan, and Iza Kavedžija's work on hope and aging in Japan has extended the field toward questions of affect and the future. Simone de Beauvoir's *The Coming of Age* (1970), though a work of philosophy rather than ethnography, remains an important reference for the field's critique of how societies marginalize the old.

## Method

The anthropology of aging relies above all on long-term ethnography, often involving sustained relationships with elderly people in homes, care institutions, and communities. Its distinctive method is the life-history interview, which places an individual's aging within a whole biography rather than treating old age as a separate stage. Participant observation in nursing homes, senior centers, and households reveals the daily routines and small negotiations through which aging is lived. Comparative work is central: by placing, for example, American retirement communities alongside Indian joint families, anthropologists show that dependence, respect, and loneliness are not natural facts of aging but culturally organized experiences. The field also draws on demographic and medical knowledge while insisting that statistics about aging populations cannot explain what aging feels like. Increasingly, researchers study aging across borders, following migrants who care for aging parents at a distance and the global circulation of care workers.

## Influence and Debates

The anthropology of aging has reshaped gerontology by challenging the biomedical model of aging as decline. Its insistence that old age is culturally constructed influenced policy debates about eldercare, dementia, and "successful aging," a concept anthropologists have criticized for blaming individuals who age with illness or poverty. The field also contributed to debates about personhood, showing that the self persists and changes in old age rather than simply fading. Feminist anthropologists within the field have shown how aging is gendered: women's caregiving labor, widowhood, and bodily changes are experienced differently from men's. A continuing debate concerns universals: some scholars emphasize cross-cultural commonalities in the experience of bodily decline, while others stress that even frailty and memory loss are interpreted through local moral worlds. As populations age worldwide and care becomes a global industry, the anthropology of aging has become a key site for understanding inequality, kinship, and the politics of the life course.
