# Sociology of Work, Occupations & Industrial Relations

The contribution of Everett Hughes (1897–1983), one of the leading figures of the second generation of the Chicago School of sociology, who studied the "occupation" as a complex social and identity-forming experience that goes beyond the purely technical description of a job.

## The Central Idea

In *Men and Their Work* (1958), Hughes developed an interactionist perspective for studying occupations, holding that "occupational identity" is socially constructed through a complex interplay between the external expectations of society and the occupational group's own internal culture (its language, rituals, and occupation-specific humor). He formulated the concept of the "bundle of tasks": every occupation combines varied tasks that are socially sorted into "dirty work" (lower in status, concealed or delegated to others) and "clean work" (highlighted and presented as the essence of the occupation). This classification is itself determined socially rather than necessarily technically, and it often calls for an internal symbolic "legitimation" that those who perform such tasks devise themselves, to justify their work to themselves and to others and to preserve their occupational dignity despite external stigma. Hughes also studied in depth the "career" as a broad social concept that goes beyond technical job advancement to include the subjective shifts in identity and belonging that an individual undergoes across the successive stages of working life, from initial training and absorption of the occupation's internal culture to maturity and retirement, reinterpreting at each stage the meaning of what came before.

## The Career as a Social Concept

Hughes drew a clear distinction between the common objective notion of the "career" as a series of successive promotions and formal positions, and the "subjective" notion he emphasized: the sequence of changes in how individuals understand themselves and their relationship to their work and to others over time, regardless of how far this path matches external, formal standards of success. This means that two people may hold the same formal position yet live fundamentally different "careers" in terms of subjective meaning and the identity formed over time, and that the sociological study of an occupation requires understanding how individuals recount, to themselves and to others, the story of this path's development in retrospect, in a way that serves a coherent image of their occupational selves.

## Master Status and Dirty Work

Among Hughes's most enduring contributions is the concept of "master status": certain social or occupational attributes of an individual (being a doctor, or Black, or disabled) tend to override all their other attributes in determining how others perceive them and interact with them in almost every context, regardless of how relevant the attribute actually is to the specific situation. This partly explains how some occupations (such as the judiciary or medicine) become all-encompassing identities that wholly absorb the personality of their bearer, while other occupations remain merely a partial "job" that does not touch the core of the self-identity. As for the concept of "dirty work," Hughes, notably in "Good People and Dirty Work" (1962), extended it to include not only unpleasant physical tasks (such as collecting garbage or cleaning the morgue) but also "dirty" moral or symbolic tasks that society delegates to particular occupations to perform on its behalf (such as the executioner who carries out the death sentence or the guard who exercises justified institutional violence), so that those in these occupations bear a symbolic social stigma in exchange for relieving the rest of society of the burden of performing these tasks with their own hands.

## The Ethnographic Method and the Chicago School Legacy

As a link between the founding generation of the Chicago School (Park and Burgess) and the next generation of interactionist sociologists, Hughes embodied an ethnographic method based on direct field observation and systematic comparison between occupations that appear different (such as physicians, nurses, and janitors), in search of shared social patterns that go beyond the specificity of each occupation. At the University of Chicago, Hughes taught and mentored an entire generation of researchers, most notably Howard Becker (the sociology of deviance, occupations and art), who worked with him on the medical-student study *Boys in White*, and Erving Goffman (dramaturgical interactionism), who was also strongly influenced by him, so that his influence extended well beyond the sociology of occupations.

## Impact and Critique

This work provided a methodological foundation for the interactionist sociology of occupations, influencing Becker, Goffman and others, and the later sociology of work and professions in general. Critics, however, noted that Hughes's intense focus on the interactional-symbolic dimension of the occupation came at times at the expense of a deeper structural analysis of the relations of economic and class power that determine in the first place which tasks are classified as "dirty" and which as "clean"; more conflict-oriented approaches, such as Harry Braverman's labor process theory (*Labor and Monopoly Capital*, 1974), addressed that structural dimension.
