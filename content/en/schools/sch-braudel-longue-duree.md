# Braudel's Longue Durée and Structural History

**Fernand Braudel**, the second generation of the Annales School and its leading editor after Febvre, developed a methodological framework that divides historical time itself into multiple layers of different speeds, rather than treating it as a single homogeneous time unit.

## The Founding Work

*The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II* (La Méditerranée et le monde méditerranéen à l'époque de Philippe II, 1949) is a massive thesis, written in part from memory while Braudel was held in a German prisoner-of-war camp during the Second World War. The book treats the Mediterranean basin in the sixteenth century not only as a stage for the political events of Philip II's reign, but as an integrated geographical, environmental and economic unit moving at different temporal rhythms.

## The Three Temporal Layers

- **Geographical time (structural / longue durée)**: the slowest layer, almost fixed across centuries. It includes humanity's relationship with the environment, climate, major trade routes and natural geographic boundaries. This "long duration" is Braudel's most famous conceptual contribution: structures that shape historical possibility without changing at the speed of political events.
- **Cyclical time (conjoncture)**: a middle layer that moves more slowly than daily events but faster than geographic structure. It covers economic cycles, movements of prices and population, and the succession of social generations. It is measured in decades rather than centuries.
- **Event time (l'événement)**: the fastest and most superficial layer, comprising battles, political decisions and the deaths of kings. Braudel regarded it as "dust" (poussière) on the surface of history if read in isolation from the two deeper layers, despite its apparent importance to contemporaries.

## Method and Limitation

- **Priority of structure over event**: a radical shift away from "event history" and the centrality of the acting individual (the king, the commander), in favour of slow structural forces that exceed the will of individuals.
- **Later critique**: this framework was accused of excessive geographical and environmental determinism, and of marginalizing the agency of individuals and political events to a degree that makes it difficult to explain sudden change (revolutions, decisive wars). This critique pushed the third generation of the Annales School toward a partial return to topics closer to the individual and to mentalities (see the third-generation entry in this section).
