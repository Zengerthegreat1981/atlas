# Comparative Kinship Systems

## Definition

A central subfield of social anthropology that analyzes kinship, marriage, and descent systems across different cultures. It uses structural and functional approaches to understand how individuals organize themselves into kin groups, and how these systems define rights, obligations, inheritance, and permitted and prohibited marriage.

## Historical Context

The roots of the comparative study of kinship go back to the early American anthropologist Lewis Henry Morgan, who in his book *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity of the Human Family* (1871) produced the first systematic classification of kinship terminology systems across cultures. He distinguished between "classificatory" systems, which group multiple relatives under a single term (such as "father" applied to both the father and the father's brother), and "descriptive" systems, which give each kin relation its own term. In the 1920s and 1930s, Alfred Radcliffe-Brown developed a structural-functional approach to kinship as a mechanism for organizing social rights and obligations. Meyer Fortes and E. E. Evans-Pritchard then developed what became known as "descent theory," which focused on how group membership is transmitted through a single line of descent (patrilineal or matrilineal) in the African societies he studied. Claude Lévi-Strauss made a radical theoretical break in 1949 with *The Elementary Structures of Kinship*. He shifted the focus from descent to alliance, proposing that the basic function of marriage rules (such as the prohibition of incest) is not biological but social: to enforce the exchange of women between groups in order to build social alliances that bind the whole society together. This led to his distinction between "generalized" and "restricted" exchange marriage.

## Central Ideas and Methods

This school holds that kinship systems are not limited to biological ties. They are complex social constructions that govern marriage, inheritance, and social status, and each culture has its own rules of classification and interaction. The school distinguishes among the main principles of descent: patrilineal descent, in which descent and property are transmitted through the male line; matrilineal descent, in which they are transmitted through the female line, as in some societies of Central Africa and the Trobriand Islands; and bilateral or cognatic descent, which recognizes both the father's and the mother's relatives, as in most modern European societies. The school also studies exchange marriage between groups as a mechanism for building lasting social alliances, and analyzes kinship terminology systems as a reflection of the deeper social structure of a society rather than as mere vocabulary.

## Key Works and Figures

Lévi-Strauss's *The Elementary Structures of Kinship* (1949) remains the most influential theoretical work in the field. Alongside it stands Meyer Fortes's *Kinship and the Social Order* (1969), which represented the alternative British functionalist approach, and Robin Fox's introductory *Kinship and Marriage* (1967), which became a standard teaching reference. Later anthropologists studied rich specific cases, such as the complex marriage systems of Australian Aboriginal peoples (with their multiple section systems) and patrilineal systems in sub-Saharan Africa, comparing them with bilateral systems in modern Europe.

## Impact and Critique

For decades, the study of kinship formed the backbone of training in British and French social anthropology, and it influenced sociology, comparative law, and family studies. David Schneider, however, launched a radical critique of the whole field from the late 1960s onward, set out in full in *A Critique of the Study of Kinship* (1984). He argued that the concept of "kinship" itself, defined in terms of the "reality" of biological ties, is an ethnocentric Western assumption projected onto other cultures that may not organize their social bonds around biological connection at all. This critique led to a relative decline in the field's centrality, before it revived later within the new anthropology of kinship and studies of assisted reproduction.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The traditional Arab kinship system, based on strict patrilineal descent and the preference for marriage with the father's brother's daughter (parallel-cousin marriage), is a well-known comparative case in the literature. Anthropologists have long observed that this marriage pattern sits awkwardly with Lévi-Strauss's model of "exchange," which assumes the flow of women between distinct groups. Here, marriage to a close relative keeps the woman and her property within the same descent group rather than exchanging them outside it. Hilma Granqvist's *Marriage Conditions in a Palestinian Village* (1931) and Robert Murphy and Leonard Kasdan's "The Structure of Parallel Cousin Marriage" (1959) are early examples, and many later researchers have examined it in the contexts of the Gulf, the Levant, and the Maghreb. Islamic jurisprudence on inheritance and prohibited marriages also provides a detailed legal system for regulating kinship, which merits deeper comparative study within this field.
