# Moral Psychology

## Historical Context

Moral psychology investigates the actual psychological mechanisms that produce moral judgments, emotions and behavior, distinguishing it from normative moral philosophy, which asks what "ought" to be right rather than what actually happens in people's minds. The Islamic heritage anticipated this field in the form of "the science of refining character" (*tahdhib al-akhlaq*). Abu Ali Miskawayh (d. 1030 CE), in his book *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* (*The Refinement of Character*), drawing on Greek ethics, provided a psychological analysis of the three powers of the soul (the rational, the irascible and the appetitive) and how to train them to reach moderation. Al-Ghazali, in *The Revival of the Religious Sciences* (*Ihya' Ulum al-Din*), detailed the mechanisms of "diseases of the heart," such as arrogance and envy, and the methods of treating them, in what is regarded as an early model of what is today called "applied moral psychology." The modern academic field was founded in the West with Jean Piaget's studies of children's cognitive development in the 1930s. It then crystallized decisively with Lawrence Kohlberg in the 1960s, who developed the theory of stages of moral development. Since the early 2000s the field has witnessed an "intuitionist turn" associated with Jonathan Haidt ("The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail," 2001), which shifted the focus from inferential rationality to emotion and immediate intuition.

## Central Ideas: From Rationalism to Intuition

For decades the field was dominated by the "rationalist model" posited by Piaget and Kohlberg, according to which moral judgment is the product of conscious, logical reasoning that develops through stages, from self-centeredness to an understanding of universal principles of justice. Jonathan Haidt challenged this picture with his "Social Intuitionist Model," drawing on experiments showing that people make immediate moral judgments as an emotional reaction (such as disgust or anger), and then invent rational justifications "after the fact" to defend a judgment already reached intuitively. He called this phenomenon "moral dumbfounding" (Moral Dumbfounding), in which a person cannot justify their judgment but nonetheless holds to it. On this basis, Haidt built the "Moral Foundations Theory" (Moral Foundations Theory), originally comprising five foundations (care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion, and sanctity/degradation; a sixth, liberty/oppression, was later proposed), and argued that cultures and political currents differ in the weight they give each foundation. This intersects with the neuroimaging research of Joshua Greene and colleagues (2001), which found that "personal" dilemmas, such as pushing one person off a footbridge to save five, engaged emotion-related brain regions more than "impersonal" ones, such as flipping a switch. Greene interprets this within a dual-process account in which moral judgment results from the interaction of an intuitive emotional system and a more controlled deliberative one; both the findings and their interpretation are debated.

## Key Thinkers

In *The Moral Judgment of the Child* (1932), Jean Piaget presented the first empirical study of the development of children's understanding of rules, distinguishing between "the morality of constraint," based on strict obedience to authority, and "the morality of cooperation," based on mutual negotiation. Lawrence Kohlberg developed this framework in his well-known theory of six stages distributed across three levels (pre-conventional, conventional, and post-conventional), using hypothetical dilemmas such as the "Heinz dilemma" to measure the level of development. Carol Gilligan offered an influential feminist critique of Kohlberg in *In a Different Voice* (1982), arguing that his model favors a "justice" logic associated with men and neglects the "care" logic that often emerges in women's moral thinking, which contributed later to the emergence of "the ethics of care." Jonathan Haidt brought these ideas to a wide public in *The Righteous Mind* (2012). Developmental research by Paul Bloom, Kiley Hamlin and Karen Wynn (2007) and Bloom's *Just Babies* (2013) argues that infants show rudimentary preferences for helpful over harmful agents before language is acquired, though the interpretation of these findings is contested.

## Impact, Application and Objections

This field has changed our understanding of the nature of moral debate in the public sphere. Recognizing that much political and religious disagreement stems from intuitive differences in "moral foundations" rather than from logical error opens the door to more understanding dialogue, instead of simply assuming that the opposing side is "irrational." These theories have also been used in moral education and in designing programs to develop empathy in children. However, the intuitionist model faces a significant objection: if most of our moral judgments are intuitive and the product of evolution and culture, does critical moral reasoning retain any real role in correcting intuitions that may be biased (such as bias against strangers)? Haidt replies that reason, although it is not the original source of the judgment, remains capable of "social communication" that reshapes our intuitions over time through dialogue with others.

Example: in a well-known study by Haidt and colleagues (2000-2001), participants were told of two adult siblings who have sex once, with contraception, by mutual consent and without harm. Many condemned the act immediately and, when their stated reasons were rebutted, still maintained that it was wrong. Haidt took this "moral dumbfounding" as evidence for the primacy of intuition over reasoning in moral judgment; critics argue that participants may have been imagining harms or that the method invites such responses.
