# Feminist Anthropology

Feminist anthropology is the branch of anthropology that studies gender — how societies make men and women, and how they make masculinity and femininity — while also turning a feminist critique on anthropology itself. It begins from a simple observation with far-reaching consequences: for most of the discipline's history, ethnographies were written by men, about men, and for men, and women appeared in them mainly as wives, mothers, and background figures. You cannot understand a culture, feminist anthropologists argued, if you only listen to half of its members. Unlike feminist sociology, which studies gender through surveys and institutions, it works ethnographically and cross-culturally, and treats kinship as one site of gendered life among others.

## History

Feminist anthropology emerged in the 1970s alongside the second-wave feminist movement. The decisive moment came with two volumes: Woman, Culture, and Society (1974), edited by Michelle Zimbalist Rosaldo and Louise Lamphere, and Toward an Anthropology of Women (1975), edited by Rayna Reiter. Rosaldo's lead essay proposed that the near-universal subordination of women could be explained by a structural opposition between a domestic sphere associated with women and the home and a public sphere of politics and ritual dominated by men. Sherry Ortner's essay in the same volume, "Is Female to Male as Nature is to Culture?" (1974), asked whether women's association with reproduction explained their lower status.

In the 1980s the school turned from explaining universal subordination to questioning its own categories. Ethnographers showed that the public-domestic divide did not hold everywhere, that "woman" was not a unitary category, and that gender could not be separated from race, class, and colonial history. Lila Abu-Lughod's Veiled Sentiments (1986) studied Bedouin women's poetry as a language of feeling and resistance, and Marilyn Strathern's The Gender of the Gift (1988) argued that Melanesian ideas of personhood made Western feminist categories — including the very idea of "women" as a group — inapplicable. By the end of the decade the Association for Feminist Anthropology had been organized as a section of the American Anthropological Association, giving the school institutional form.

## Key Figures

Michelle Zimbalist Rosaldo (1944–1981) co-edited the founding volume and wrote Knowledge and Passion (1980), an ethnography of Ilongot emotion and social life. Rayna Reiter (later Rapp) edited Toward an Anthropology of Women (1975) and later became a leading anthropologist of reproduction and genetics. Sherry Ortner (born 1941) wrote the field's most debated early essay and later studied class and gender in the United States. Gayle Rubin (born 1949) reframed kinship as a political economy of sex. Lila Abu-Lughod (born 1952) wrote the classic ethnographies of Bedouin women and became a leading critic of the culture concept and of Western representations of Muslim women. Marilyn Strathern (born 1941), working in Papua New Guinea, challenged both anthropology and feminism to rethink gender, personhood, and comparison. Saba Mahmood (1961–2018) rethought agency, piety, and secularism.

## Key Works

Rosaldo and Lamphere's Woman, Culture, and Society (1974) is the founding collection. Reiter's Toward an Anthropology of Women (1975) is its companion volume. Rubin's "The Traffic in Women: Notes on the 'Political Economy' of Sex" (1975) remains a foundational theoretical text. Rosaldo's Knowledge and Passion: Ilongot Notions of Self and Social Life (1980) is the classic feminist ethnography of emotion. Abu-Lughod's Veiled Sentiments: Honor and Poetry in a Bedouin Society (1986) showed how poetry carried women's unspoken feelings. Strathern's "An Awkward Relationship: The Case of Feminism and Anthropology" (1987) argued that feminist anthropology had been accommodated as a niche rather than transforming the discipline. Her The Gender of the Gift: Problems with Women and Problems with Society in Melanesia (1988) is the school's most sustained critique of cross-cultural comparison.

## Method

Feminist anthropology's method begins with who is listened to: it centres women's voices, experiences, and interpretations, and asks what changes in an account when women are the informants rather than the background. Fieldwork is reflexive and attentive to the ethnographer's own gender, position, and relationships: a woman ethnographer in a patriarchal society, or a man studying women's lives, faces different access, different risks, and different obligations, and those differences are part of the data. The method is intersectional before the term was common: it asks how gender is lived together with class, race, age, religion, and colonial history, rather than in isolation. It studies the intimate and the everyday — childbirth, housework, courtship, gossip, dress — as seriously as politics and ritual, on the grounds that power is made in ordinary life.

## Influence and Contemporary Debates

Feminist anthropology transformed the discipline: no ethnography today can ignore gender, and its questions about voice, position, and power fed directly into the postmodern critique of representation. It reshaped the study of kinship, showing that marriage and descent are political arrangements rather than natural facts; the study of religion, showing how piety and devotion are gendered practices; and the study of the state, showing how law, welfare, and medicine govern women's bodies. Its internal debates have been fierce: whether gender subordination is universal or historically specific; whether Western feminist categories can be applied cross-culturally at all; and whether the school's early focus on women left out men, masculinity, and the full range of gendered experience.

Two further developments widened the field. First, non-Western and postcolonial feminisms challenged the assumption that Western feminist categories travel unchanged: Oyèrónkẹ́ Oyěwùmí's *The Invention of Women* (1997) argued that gender was not an organizing principle of pre-colonial Yoruba society, while Abu-Lughod and Mahmood questioned how Western feminism represents Muslim women and their agency. Second, studies of gender diversity beyond a male–female binary, such as Serena Nanda's *Neither Man nor Woman: The Hijras of India* (1990) and Will Roscoe's *Changing Ones: Third and Fourth Genders in Native North America* (1998), showed that gender categories vary across societies and fed directly into queer anthropology.
