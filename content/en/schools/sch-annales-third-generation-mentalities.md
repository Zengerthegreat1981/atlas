# Annales School, Third Generation: History of Mentalities

The third phase of the French Annales School, extending roughly from the late 1960s through the 1980s, shifted the movement's primary focus from the broad socioeconomic structures and geographical determinants favored by Fernand Braudel to the historical study of **collective mentalities** (*mentalités collectives*). Historians investigated the shared cognitive systems, emotional sensibilities, and imaginative worldviews that shaped how human groups experienced life, death, time, and society, often operating below the level of conscious philosophical articulation. Led by figures such as Jacques Le Goff, Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, Georges Duby, Philippe Ariès, and Michel Vovelle, this movement became widely known under the banner of the "New History" (*la nouvelle histoire*), a phrase popularized by Le Goff and Pierre Nora in the 1970s.

## Intellectual Foundations and Methodology

- **Mentalities versus Intellectual History:** Unlike traditional history of ideas, which traced elite philosophical and literary canons, the history of mentalities sought to uncover the unconscious, unexpressed, or everyday assumptions of ordinary people. Historians explored attitudes toward childhood, the body, sexuality, illness, dreams, and the afterlife through indirect, non-canonical documentation: diocesan visitation records, wills, church sermons, folklore, iconography, and judicial proceedings.
- **Historical Anthropology:** Influenced by structural anthropology (Claude Lévi-Strauss) and social psychology, third-generation historians treated culture as an interconnected system of symbols and practices. Jacques Le Goff integrated anthropology with medieval history, examining how concepts of time transformed from ecclesiastical "church time" to secular "merchant's time."
- **Key Methodological Works:** 
  - Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie's *Montaillou* (1975) utilized the detailed fourteenth-century inquisition registers of Bishop Jacques Fournier to reconstruct the emotional, sexual, and religious world of a Pyrenean village.
  - Philippe Ariès's *L'Homme devant la mort* (1977) charted the long-term evolution of Western attitudes toward death over a millennium, from collective acceptance ("tamed death") to modern denial.
  - Georges Duby's *Les Trois Ordres ou l'imaginaire du féodalisme* (1978) analyzed how the tripartite ideological division of feudal society (those who pray, those who fight, those who work) functioned as a powerful social imaginary.
  - Jacques Le Goff's *La Naissance du purgatoire* (1981) argued that the twelfth-century spatialization of Purgatory reflected shifting social, economic, and penitential mentalities.
- **Quantitative History of Mentalities:** Michel Vovelle pioneered serial, statistical methodologies to analyze religious mentalities, examining thousands of Provençal wills across the eighteenth century to quantify changing bequest patterns, invocations of saints, and requests for masses, thereby tracing gradual processes of secularization and de-Christianization.

## Critiques and Limitations

Despite its immense international popularity, the history of mentalities faced significant methodological challenges:
- **Conceptual Ambiguity:** Critics, including British social historians and Italian microhistorians, argued that "mentality" remained ill-defined, often treating complex and divided societies as possessing a single, homogeneous collective mind.
- **Neglect of Agency and Conflict:** The approach risked downplaying social tensions, political conflict, and individual agency by attributing human behavior to overarching, slow-moving mental structures.
- **The Shift toward Cultural History:** By the late 1980s, the term "mentalities" was largely superseded by "cultural history" (*histoire culturelle*) and the "history of representations," led by scholars such as Roger Chartier, who emphasized the differentiated reception of texts, active appropriation, and social differentiation rather than passive collective mentalities.
