# Annales School, Third Generation: History of Mentalities

The third phase of the French Annales School, which extended roughly from the late 1960s to the 1980s. The school's interest shifted from the structural economic and social history that characterised the generation of Fernand Braudel to the study of **collective mentalities** (*mentalités collectives*): the shared intellectual, emotional and imaginative systems that govern how a human group perceives its world, without necessarily being formulated as an explicit consciousness among its members. This phase was led primarily by Jacques Le Goff, Emmanuel Le Roy Ladurie, Georges Duby, Philippe Ariès and Michel Vovelle. In the 1970s, Le Goff and Pierre Nora coined the phrase "new history" (*nouvelle histoire*) as the banner of this transformation.

## Intellectual Foundations

- **Mentalities, not ideas:** Unlike the history of ideas, which traces the texts of elites, the history of mentalities seeks the shared, often unconscious, conceptions of ordinary people — of death, time, the body, dreams and sin — through indirect sources: court records, wills, icons and church preaching.
- **Historical anthropology:** Le Goff borrowed the tools of structural anthropology (Claude Lévi-Strauss) to read the Middle Ages, regarding popular culture as a historical subject as much as politics and the economy.
- **Foundational examples:** Le Roy Ladurie's *Montaillou, village occitan* (1975) reconstructed the intellectual and emotional life of a fourteenth-century French village through Inquisition records. Philippe Ariès's *L'Homme devant la mort* (1977) traced the changing attitudes of Westerners towards death over a thousand years. Michel Vovelle studied baroque piety and the wave of "de-Christianisation" in eighteenth-century Provence through the analysis of wills.
- **Quantitative history of mentalities:** Vovelle in particular used statistical methods (quantifying wills and burial formulas) to measure slow religious transformations that are difficult to detect in narrative sources.

## Limitations

- The field has been accused of conceptual vagueness: "mentality" is a broad term, difficult to define operationally or verify empirically, which exposed it to criticism from social historians who wanted more precise criteria.
- Since the 1980s the term has declined in favour of narrower concepts such as "cultural history" (*histoire culturelle*) and "history of representations" (*histoire des représentations*) in the work of Roger Chartier, who explicitly criticised the breadth of the notion of mentality and the absence of individual agency within it.
- Its reliance on exceptional sources (such as the Inquisition records of Montaillou) raises questions about the generalisability of its findings.
