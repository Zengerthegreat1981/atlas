# Formal Semantics and Montague Grammar

A current in linguistic semantics developed by the American philosopher and logician **Richard Montague** in the late 1960s and early 1970s. It uses model-theoretic semantics to assign interpretations to fragments of natural language. In *Universal Grammar* (1970) he wrote that there is, in his opinion, "no important theoretical difference between natural languages and the artificial languages of logicians." A closely related statement opens his essay "English as a Formal Language" (1970).

## The Principle of Compositionality

Montague grammar rests on the **principle of compositionality**, which Montague formalised by treating syntax and semantics as algebras related by a homomorphism. Under this principle, the meaning of a complex expression is a function of the meanings of its parts and of the syntactic rule that combines them, and each syntactic rule is paired with a semantic rule. The claim is about the design of the grammar, not a description of psychological processing. The most famous treatment of this approach was published in his article "The Proper Treatment of Quantification in Ordinary English," known by the abbreviation PTQ (1973).

## Intensional Logic and Possible Worlds

Montague used intensional logic to treat sentence meaning through **possible worlds semantics**. He distinguished between **extension**, the referent of an expression in a given world and at a given time, and **intension**, the function that links every possible world and time to the extension of the expression in them. This distinction made it possible to explain complex phenomena that resisted simple extensional semantics, such as intensional verbs (like "believe" or "must") and conditional sentences, by linking them to sets of possible worlds rather than to a single fixed referent.

## Generalized Quantifiers and Categorial Grammar

Montague relied on **categorial grammar**, which Kazimierz Ajdukiewicz and Joachim Lambek had developed earlier, to assign each syntactic category a corresponding semantic type. In a simple extensional fragment, for example, names denote individuals and an intransitive verb is a function from individuals to truth values; in PTQ itself, noun phrases are treated uniformly as sets of properties. This mechanism enabled the treatment of quantificational expressions ("every," "some," "most") as **generalized quantifiers**. Jon Barwise and Robin Cooper later formulated this framework in a more general form, and it became a standard tool for the logical analysis of the structure of noun phrases.

## Later Development: Heim, Kratzer and Kamp

Barbara Partee developed Montague's work and made it systematically accessible to linguists who were not specialists in logic, connecting it with the tools of generative grammar. Irene Heim then extended the framework through "File Change Semantics," to treat pronominal reference across sentences, especially in the logically problematic "donkey sentences." This ran parallel to "Discourse Representation Theory" (DRT), which Hans Kamp developed for a similar purpose. Angelika Kratzer added a precise semantic analysis of modal expressions through the concepts of the "modal base" and the "ordering source."

## Impact and Relation to Pragmatics

Although formal semantics is historically independent of the Chomskyan generative lineage, having arisen from the intersection of mathematical logic and analytic philosophy rather than from structural linguistics, it became a standard framework for sentence meaning in theoretical linguistics. It has interacted with Gricean pragmatics in treating phenomena such as presupposition, focus particles and information structure, forming an interdisciplinary field between formal semantics and formal pragmatics.
