# Psychological Anthropology

Psychological anthropology studies relations between social life and mental life: how people develop selves, feel and name emotions, reason, remember, dream, and experience distress. It combines ethnography with concepts drawn from psychoanalysis, psychology, linguistics, and cognitive science, and examines whether those concepts travel across societies.

## Culture and personality

In the United States the field grew from the culture and personality research of the 1920s to 1940s. Ruth Benedict's *Patterns of Culture* (1934) compared cultural configurations, and Margaret Mead studied childhood and adolescence in the Pacific. Edward Sapir questioned treating culture as separate from the individuals who carry it. Abram Kardiner and Ralph Linton proposed a basic personality structure shaped by shared child-rearing institutions, and Cora Du Bois tested related ideas on the island of Alor. Wartime studies of national character extended these methods to large states and drew criticism for sweeping generalisation.

## Renaming and diversification

Francis L. K. Hsu promoted "psychological anthropology" as a broader name in a volume of 1961. John and Beatrice Whiting organised comparative research on child socialisation, including the Six Cultures project. Melford Spiro and Gananath Obeyesekere developed psychoanalytically informed accounts of religion and symbolism, in different directions. Cognitive anthropologists studied classification and, later, cultural models shared unevenly within groups; Roy D'Andrade traced this history. The Society for Psychological Anthropology and the journal *Ethos* provided institutions from the 1970s.

## Self, emotion, and experience

Jean Briggs's *Never in Anger* (1970) described emotional training in an Inuit family through her own difficult place within it. Michelle Rosaldo's work on the Ilongot treated emotion as bound up with social relations and judgment. Catherine Lutz argued that Western assumptions separating emotion from reason shape research, using material from Ifaluk. Robert Levy examined the ways Tahitians elaborate some feelings and leave others little named. These studies caution that a translated emotion word does not guarantee an identical experience, and that vocabulary alone does not reveal what people feel.

## Mind, distress, and recent work

Research on mental illness, trauma, addiction, and healing connects the field with medical anthropology and cultural psychiatry. Thomas Csordas used embodiment to study charismatic healing. Tanya Luhrmann examined American psychiatric training and, later, practices through which some Christians learn to experience God as responsive. Other researchers study child development, moral reasoning, and attention in settings poorly represented in laboratory samples.

## Debates

Scholars disagree about psychic universals, the explanatory value of psychoanalysis, and the relation between experiments and fieldwork. Early work often treated a society as one personality type. Later research gives greater weight to variation, history, and inequality, while the problem remains of how to compare minds without either assuming sameness or making difference absolute.
