# Pyrrhonian Skepticism

A philosophical current that took its name from **Pyrrho of Elis** (c. 360–c. 270 BCE), who is said to have studied with Anaxarchus and to have accompanied Alexander the Great on his Asian campaign. Pyrrhonian skepticism is the best documented of the ancient skeptical traditions. It differs from Academic skepticism in that it does not rest in the claim that things are probable or improbable, but aims at the **suspension of judgment** (ἐποχή) as a way of reaching "freedom from disturbance" (ἀταραξία).

## Context

Pyrrho accompanied Alexander to Asia (from 334 BCE) and, according to Diogenes Laertius, met the Indian "naked philosophers" (gymnosophists) and the Persian magi, an encounter that may have influenced his outlook. He then returned to Elis, where he lived simply and was honoured by his fellow citizens. He wrote nothing; his thought is known through his follower Timon of Phlius and through much later reports.

## Phases

- **Pyrrho himself (c. 360–c. 270 BCE)**: the founding figure; he left no writings.
- **Timon of Phlius** (c. 320–c. 230 BCE): Pyrrho's follower and publicist, a poet and satirist. His *Silloi* were satirical verses on the philosophers, and his *Pytho* recounted his encounter with Pyrrho. Only fragments of his work survive.
- **The revival**: after the school lapsed, Aenesidemus of Knossos (1st century BCE) re-founded Pyrrhonism in opposition to the Academy of his day, and Agrippa (date uncertain) is credited with the "five modes".
- **Sextus Empiricus** (late 2nd to early 3rd century CE): a physician-philosopher whose *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* and *Against the Mathematicians* are our fullest sources.

## Central Concepts

- **Freedom from disturbance (ἀταραξία)**: the aim of the Pyrrhonist, not insensibility but the absence of the distress caused by holding firm opinions on disputed matters. Sextus says it followed the suspension of judgment "as a shadow follows a body".
- **Suspension of judgment (ἐποχή)**: withholding assent from claims about how things are in their own nature.
- **Equipollence (ἰσοσθένεια)**: for any argument one can set against it an opposing argument of equal force, which leads to suspension.
- **Non-assertion (ἀφασία)**: the Pyrrhonist does not affirm even that nothing can be known; his utterances report how things appear to him.
- **Living by appearances**: Sextus describes a fourfold guidance for life without dogma: the guidance of nature, the compulsion of the feelings, the tradition of laws and customs, and the teaching of crafts.
- **The Ten Modes (τρόποι)**: ten lines of argument, attributed to Aenesidemus and reported by Sextus, designed to induce suspension. They draw on differences:
 1. among animals;
 2. among human beings;
 3. among the sense organs;
 4. among circumstances or conditions of the perceiver;
 5. in positions, distances and places;
 6. in mixtures (what appears depends on what the object is mixed with);
 7. in the quantities and constitutions of things;
 8. in relativity (everything appears in relation to something);
 9. in frequency or rarity of occurrence;
 10. in customs, laws, myths and dogmatic assumptions.
- **The Five Modes of Agrippa**: disagreement, infinite regress, relativity, hypothesis and circularity, aimed at any attempt to justify a claim.

## Difference from Academic Skepticism

| Dimension | Pyrrhonian Skepticism | Academic Skepticism |
|-------|--------------|---------------|
| **Position** | Suspension of judgment on non-evident matters | Arcesilaus urged suspension; Carneades defended the persuasive (πιθανόν) as a practical guide |
| **Inquiry** | Continues to inquire (the "skeptic" is literally an inquirer) | Holds, in Sextus's account, that nothing can be firmly apprehended |
| **Aim** | Freedom from disturbance (ἀταραξία) | Not explicitly tranquillity |
| **Founder** | Pyrrho (c. 360 BCE); revived by Aenesidemus | Arcesilaus (head of Plato's Academy from c. 268 BCE) |
| **Institution** | No continuous school until the revival | Plato's Academy (Middle and New Academy) |

## Legacy

- **Renaissance and early modern thought**: the rediscovery of Sextus in the sixteenth century (Latin editions of Henri Estienne, 1562, and Gentian Hervet, 1569) fed the skepticism of Montaigne and the doubts that Descartes sought to overcome.
- **Hume**: his skeptical arguments about induction and the external world resemble Pyrrhonian modes, although he favoured a "mitigated" skepticism over Pyrrhonism.
- **Contemporary epistemology**: Agrippa's trilemma of regress, circularity and dogmatic assumption remains a standard formulation of the problem of justification.

## Difficulties

- **Self-reference**: if the skeptic says that nothing can be known, he seems to claim knowledge. Sextus replies that Pyrrhonian expressions are like a purgative that expels itself along with the humours, and that they merely report what appears.
- **The problem of action**: can one live without belief? Sextus answers with the fourfold observance of nature, feelings, custom and craft.

## Limits

- Our principal sources are late (Sextus, Diogenes Laertius, Aristocles), and we do not know the historical Pyrrho precisely.
- Timon's works are lost except for fragments; a standard collection is H. Diels, *Poetarum Philosophorum Fragmenta* (1901).
- The report preserved by Aristocles, citing Timon, says that things are equally indifferent, unmeasurable and undecidable, so that neither sense-perceptions nor opinions are true or false. Scholars disagree about whether this makes Pyrrho a skeptic about knowledge, a metaphysician of indeterminacy, or an ethical thinker (see Bett 2000; Burnyeat and Frede 1997).
- The portrayal of skeptics in sources hostile to them (for example Cicero) may be biased.
