# Chicago School Urban Ecology

## A Laboratory Called Chicago

In the 1920s the University of Chicago became the centre of American sociology, and the city of Chicago became its laboratory. Between 1890 and 1930 Chicago grew from a city of about a million people to a metropolis of three million, swollen by immigration from Europe and migration from the American South. Its stockyards, railways, slums and ethnic neighbourhoods offered sociologists a living field site on their doorstep. The department, founded in 1892 and often described as the first sociology department in the United States, was shaped after 1914 by Robert E. Park, a former journalist, and by Ernest W. Burgess. Their approach, known as human or urban ecology, borrowed the language of plant and animal ecology and applied it to the city: human communities, like biological ones, compete for territory, differentiate into specialised areas, and settle into a natural equilibrium — until growth disturbs it again.

## Park and the Human Ecology of the City

Park's programme was set out in the textbook he wrote with Burgess, *Introduction to the Science of Sociology* (1921), and in the collection *The City* (1925), which Park edited with Burgess and Roderick D. McKenzie. Park argued that the city is not merely a collection of buildings and people but a state of mind, a body of customs and traditions, and an organised pattern of attitudes. Beneath the city's institutional order lies a biotic order, driven by competition. Individuals and groups compete for the most desirable locations, and this competition produces a spatial pattern: business concentrates at the centre, residences sort themselves by income and ethnicity, and distinctive "natural areas" emerge — the slum, the immigrant colony, the bohemian quarter, the suburb.

## The Concentric Zone Model

The most famous product of the school is Burgess's concentric zone model, published in *The City* (1925). Burgess proposed that the city grows outward from its centre in five concentric rings. At the core is the central business district of offices, shops and theatres. Around it lies the zone in transition, a deteriorating area of factories, warehouses and rooming houses, where the poorest newcomers settle and where vice and crime concentrate. Beyond that are the zone of working-class homes, the zone of better residences, and finally the commuter zone of suburbs. Growth proceeds by invasion and succession: as the centre expands, each zone pushes outward into the next. The model was a bold simplification — rivers, railways and industry distort the rings — but it gave urban research a testable map, and Chicago researchers found delinquency and other social-problem rates highest in the zone in transition and generally falling toward the suburbs.

## Field Studies: Hoboes, Gangs and Delinquency

The school's reputation rests less on its theory than on its ethnographies. For *The Hobo* (1923), Nels Anderson drew on life among homeless migrant workers around Chicago's West Madison Street, documenting their world of casual labour, missions and jungle camps. Frederic M. Thrasher's *The Gang* (1927) studied more than a thousand Chicago youth gangs and argued that gangs arise in the interstitial areas between established neighbourhoods, where social control is weakest. Clifford R. Shaw and Henry D. McKay's *Juvenile Delinquency and Urban Areas* (1942) mapped delinquency rates across Chicago over three decades and showed that high rates persisted in the same inner-city areas even as the ethnic groups living there changed completely — evidence that place and social disorganisation, not group character, explained crime. William I. Thomas and Florian Znaniecki's *The Polish Peasant in Europe and America* (1918–1920) was an important source of the school's idea of the definition of the situation, later summarised in the "Thomas theorem" (formulated by W. I. Thomas and Dorothy S. Thomas in *The Child in America*, 1928): if people define situations as real, they are real in their consequences.

## Wirth and Urbanism as a Way of Life

Louis Wirth, a student of Park who became the school's leading theorist of the city, carried urban ecology toward a more sociological theory of urban life. In his essay "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938), published in the *American Journal of Sociology*, Wirth defined the city by three features: large population, high density and social heterogeneity. Together they produce a distinctive way of life: relationships become impersonal and segmental, kinship and neighbourhood weaken, and formal organisations take their place. The city liberates the individual from the close control of the village, Wirth argued, but it also produces anonymity and a blasé indifference — an idea he owed to Georg Simmel. Wirth's theory has been criticised for exaggerating urban anomie, but it remains the classic statement of how size and density reshape social life.

## Later Neighbourhood Research

The ecological tradition was revived in the 1990s and after. Robert Sampson and colleagues, drawing on the Project on Human Development in Chicago Neighborhoods, argued that neighbourhood differences in violence are associated with "collective efficacy", the combination of social cohesion and willingness to intervene for the common good (Sampson, Raudenbush and Earls, *Science*, 1997). Sampson's *Great American City* (2012) extends this to a long-run study of Chicago neighbourhoods.

## Related Concepts and Schools

Several concepts and schools connect directly with urban ecology. Social disorganisation, the weakening of shared rules and of the institutions that enforce them, links Thomas and Znaniecki to Shaw and McKay, and later to the idea of collective efficacy and the sociology of crime. Georg Simmel's formal sociology, and especially his essay on the metropolis and mental life (1903), stands behind Park, who had attended Simmel's lectures in Berlin, and behind Wirth's account of urban impersonality. Symbolic interactionism shares the Chicago habit of first-hand observation and the idea of the definition of the situation, but concentrates on how meaning arises in interaction rather than how populations arrange themselves in space. Segregation, assimilation, and urbanisation are further core concepts: Park's account of a race relations cycle of contact, competition, accommodation, and assimilation was later widely criticised for presenting assimilation as a natural end point. Marxist and political-economy approaches to the city, associated with writers such as David Harvey and Manuel Castells, form the main contrast, explaining urban space through investment, class conflict, and state policy instead of ecological competition.

## Criticism and Legacy

By the 1940s the Chicago School's dominance was fading. Critics charged that its ecological language was a misleading metaphor: cities are not forests, and competition for land is structured by property markets, racial discrimination and political power, not by natural law. The concentric zone model fitted Chicago better than it fitted older European cities or the sprawling automobile suburbs of the post-war era. Later urban sociologists argued that the school ignored capitalism and the state, treating the spatial order of the city as natural when it was in fact produced by landlords, developers and planners. Yet the school's legacy is lasting. It helped make fieldwork and the case study central to sociology, shaped urban sociology as a field, and left concepts — natural area, invasion and succession, social disorganisation, the definition of the situation — that are still in use. Its insistence that social life must be studied where it happens, in the streets and institutions of a real city, remains a model for ethnographic research, and its maps of Chicago are still taught as a systematic attempt to connect the organisation of space with the organisation of society.
