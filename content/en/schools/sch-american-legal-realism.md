# American Legal Realism

An intellectual movement in American law schools that flourished mainly between the 1920s and 1930s (especially at Columbia and Yale), sharing a radical skepticism about the capacity of **abstract legal rules** to explain or predict what courts actually do.

## A Comparison with Islamic Jurisprudence: The Judge Between Text and Context

Islamic jurisprudence offers a partial parallel to the Realist insight that applying a legal rule to a particular case is not a purely mechanical deductive process. The principle that **fatwas differ with changes in time, place and circumstances**, which jurists of the schools discussed and which Ibn al-Qayyim al-Jawziyya theorized in his book *I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in 'an Rabb al-'Alamin* (Informing the Signatories on behalf of the Lord of the Worlds), holds that a judge or mufti does not apply the text in a vacuum but weighs it against the situation of the questioner, the prevailing custom, and the consequences of the ruling. Likewise, the usulis opened a broad path to understanding that the context of a legal or judicial decision exceeds the wording of the abstract rule itself: al-Shatibi in *al-Muwafaqat* with his theory of **maqasid** (objectives), and al-Qarafi in his distinction between the imam's action as a jurist issuing fatwas, his action as a judge, and his action as head of state. This awareness, however, remained governed among them by the constraints of the Sharia and its objectives, and was not an empirical doubt about whether binding rules existed at all, as it was for the American Realists, whose skepticism aimed at stripping the legal rule itself of its sanctity.

## The Shared Thesis: "What Courts Actually Do"

Unlike legal formalism, which assumes that judges derive their decisions logically from prior rules (the model the Realists attacked), the Realists hold that the **actual judicial decision** is often made first on grounds that are openly non-legal (intuition, social context, the character of the judge), and then **justified afterwards** in the language of rules and precedents. Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. summarizes this spirit in his foundational essay "The Path of the Law" (1897): "The prophecies of what the courts will do in fact, and nothing more pretentious, are what I mean by the law." See the **prediction theory of law**.

## Leading Figures

- **Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.**: A justice of the U.S. Supreme Court, regarded as the movement's intellectual father despite preceding it chronologically.
- **Karl Llewellyn**: The movement's most prominent academic theorist, author of *The Bramble Bush* (1930).
- **Jerome N. Frank**: Developed a psychological dimension of realism (skepticism about facts, not only rules) in *Law and the Modern Mind* (1930). He argued that legal uncertainty stems mainly from the difficulty of proving facts before a court (witness testimony, assessment of evidence) more than from the ambiguity of the rules themselves. He later called this "fact-skepticism," distinguishing it from the "rule-skepticism" represented by Llewellyn.
- **Felix Cohen**: In his article "Transcendental Nonsense and the Functional Approach" (1935), he offered a similar critique of abstract legal concepts, calling for a functional analysis that connects the rule to its actual social consequences rather than preoccupying itself with its internal logical coherence alone.

## Rule-Skepticism in Llewellyn

Karl Llewellyn distinguishes between "paper rules," which are written in books and codes, and "real rules," which actually govern the behavior of courts in practice, proposing that serious study of law should focus on the latter rather than the former. This connects to his broader project of drafting the **Uniform Commercial Code**, in which he tried to embody his realist vision by formulating flexible standards (such as the standard of "good faith" and "commercial practice in the trade") that give judges wide latitude to adapt to the actual facts of a dispute rather than being bound by rigid prior rules.

## Criticism of Realism

The movement was subjected to fundamental methodological criticisms. Its opponents (foremost among them Lon Fuller) argued that the Realists went too far in stripping law of its normative character, reducing it to pure behavioral prediction and ignoring that judges themselves feel a real obligation toward rules rather than merely anticipating their own behavior. H. L. A. Hart later pressed a related point in *The Concept of Law* (1961), arguing that rule-skepticism overlooks the internal point of view of those who accept rules as standards. Other critics charged that focusing on "the judge's personality" and "intuition" leads to judicial relativism that weakens the legal certainty and predictability that law is supposed to provide to those it governs. Nevertheless, the movement remained a fertile subject of debate, because it opened the way to studying law as a real social practice rather than merely a closed logical system.

## Later Impact

American Realism later paved the way for more radical critical movements, such as Critical Legal Studies (CLS) in the 1970s, and influenced the development of economic analysis of law and Empirical Legal Studies, which take seriously the question of "what actually drives judicial decision-making" with more modern methodological tools. **Scandinavian Legal Realism** (Hägerström and Ross), a parallel current that arose independently of its American counterpart in a different geographical setting, shared the same concern with dismantling metaphysical legal concepts, although it rested on a Scandinavian analytic philosophy with different roots.

## Scandinavian Legal Realism

The Scandinavian movement began with the Uppsala philosopher Axel Hägerström (1868-1939), who rejected metaphysics and held that value judgments express attitudes and are neither true nor false. He argued that notions such as right and duty correspond to no observable entity and traced features of Roman legal thought to quasi-magical beliefs about invisible powers.

His followers applied this critique in different ways. Anders Vilhelm Lundstedt attacked the idea of a pre-existing sense of justice and urged that law be understood through social welfare. Karl Olivecrona, in *Law as Fact* (1939), described legal rules as independent imperatives that influence conduct through their psychological effect, without anyone's command in a literal sense. The Danish jurist Alf Ross, in *On Law and Justice* (English translation 1958), analysed a statement that a rule is valid law chiefly as a prediction that judges will apply it because they experience it as binding. In his essay *Tu-Tu* he argued that words such as ownership name no thing but usefully link legal conditions to legal consequences.

Compared with the Americans, the Scandinavians were more concerned with the meaning of legal concepts and less with litigation practice. H. L. A. Hart criticized Ross for neglecting how participants themselves use statements of validity.
