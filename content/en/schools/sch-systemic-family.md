# Systemic and Family Therapy

Systemic and family therapy is a **therapeutic school** that shifts the unit of treatment from the individual to the **system** (the family, the couple, the group, the organization). Instead of treating an isolated "patient," the therapist treats the **relationships and reciprocal patterns** among individuals, assuming that the "problem" is not inside a person but **inside a pattern** that the system repeats. The question is no longer "What is wrong with this individual?" but "What keeps this pattern in circulation?" The basic cognitive shift is from **systems (an analytic category)** to **systems of interaction (reciprocal action)**, and from **linear** causation (cause → effect) to **circular** causation (each element changes the rest and is changed by the rest).

The terms "family therapy," "couple therapy," and "systemic therapy" overlap: "family therapy" names the setting, while "systemic" names a way of thinking that can also be applied to individuals, couples, and organizations.

## Intellectual Context

- **General systems theory** (Bertalanffy, 1968): a living organism is an open system that maintains itself through exchange with its environment.
- **Cybernetics** (Wiener, 1948): communication, feedback, self-regulation.
- **Communication theory** (Bateson; Ruesch & Bateson, 1951; *Steps to an Ecology of Mind*, 1972): information understood as "a difference that makes a difference."
- **The Palo Alto project** (Bateson, Jackson, Haley, Weakland, 1956): the paper "Toward a Theory of Schizophrenia" (1956) proposed the **double bind**, a repeated pattern of contradictory communication, as a way of understanding family interaction around schizophrenia. The hypothesis was influential for family communication research, though it was not confirmed as a cause of schizophrenia.
- **The American humanistic movement** (1960s): it coincided with the countercultural wave.

## Historical Phases

- **Pre-founding** (1930s-1940s): child guidance and marriage counseling movements, and early family-oriented psychiatry such as Nathan Ackerman's in New York.
- **Founding** (1950s): Bateson's Palo Alto schizophrenia research project (from 1952); Murray Bowen's family research at the NIMH and Georgetown; Lyman Wynne and Theodore Lidz's family studies; Ackerman's clinical work. Minuchin develops "structural family therapy" in the 1960s (*Families and Family Therapy*, 1974).
- **Flourishing** (1970s-1980s): Minuchin developed structuralism. The Mental Research Institute in Palo Alto and Haley and Madanes developed "strategic therapy." The Milan group (Mara Selvini Palazzoli, Luigi Boscolo, Gianfranco Cecchin, and Giuliana Prata) developed systemic family therapy built on hypothesizing, circular questioning, and positive connotation (*Paradox and Counterparadox*, 1978).
- **Diversification** (1970s-2000s): Satir's growth model (*Peoplemaking*, 1972), Internal Family Systems therapy (Schwartz, developed in the 1980s), emotionally focused couple therapy (Johnson and Greenberg, from the 1980s), reflecting teams (Tom Andersen, Norway, mid-1980s), and Open Dialogue (Seikkula and colleagues, Finland, from the 1980s).
- **Contemporary integration** (2000s-present): cognitive-behavioral couple therapy (CBCT), multidimensional family therapy (MDFT), and family-based approaches to specific problems.

## Founding Concepts

- **System**: a set of interconnected elements, in which any element changes when any other element changes. The family is a system.
- **Feedback loop**: the information a system receives about its outputs, with which it adjusts its behavior. Positive feedback amplifies; negative feedback calms.
- **Recurrent patterns**: not individual behavior but repeated interaction among individuals.
- **Implicit rules**: "Don't talk about money," "Don't cry in front of Father," and so on. The system protects itself through unconscious rules.
- **Double bind**: a message on two conflicting levels. Example: a mother says "I love you" in a tone of contempt. The recipient is "wrong whatever he does."
- **Triangulation**: a third person is drawn in to calm a dyadic conflict.
- **Differentiation of self from the family of origin** (Bowen): the individual's capacity to be "himself" or "herself" within family belonging, neither dissolved nor cut off.
- **Hypothesizing, neutrality, circularity**: the Milan principles.
- **Internal Family Systems (IFS, Schwartz)**: each person carries "parts" in interaction: the Manager, the Firefighter, the Exile, and the core Self.

## Major Figures

- **Gregory Bateson** (1904-1980): founder of Palo Alto, the double-bind schizophrenia project.
- **Salvador Minuchin** (1921-2017): structuralism.
- **Murray Bowen** (1913-1990): family systems theory, differentiation.
- **Carl Whitaker** (1912-1995): the experiential model (Existential-Experiential).
- **Virginia Satir** (1916-1988): the humanistic model, with its focus on self-esteem.
- **Paul Watzlawick** (1921-2007): communication theory, strategic therapy (MRI).
- **Jay Haley** (1923-2007): strategic therapy, paradoxical interventions.
- **Sue Johnson** (1947–2024): EFT (emotionally focused couple therapy).
- **Richard Schwartz** (1949–): IFS (Internal Family Systems).
- **Jaakko Seikkula** (1953–): Open Dialogue (Finland).
- **Mara Selvini Palazzoli** (1916–1999, alongside Luigi Boscolo, Gianfranco Cecchin, and Giuliana Prata): the Milan school.
- **Tom Andersen** (1936–2007): reflecting teams.

## The Major Sub-Currents

- **Structural family therapy** (Structural, Minuchin): boundaries, subsystems, coalitions.
- **Strategic family therapy** (Strategic, MRI Haley, Madanes): problems as solutions, prescriptions, paradoxes.
- **Family systems therapy** (Bowen, Multigenerational): differentiation, emotional transmissions.
- **The experiential model** (Whitaker): the body, emotions, the therapeutic relationship.
- **The Milan school** (Selvini Palazzoli, Boscolo, Cecchin, Prata): early work in first-order cybernetics, later development toward second-order approaches, circular questions, neutrality.
- **Family-centered therapy** (Satir, Experiential): self-esteem, growth.
- **Emotionally focused couple therapy (EFT, Johnson)**: attachment, emotional bonding.
- **Imago therapy** (Hendrix): conscious partnership.
- **Internal Family Systems therapy (IFS, Schwartz)**: parts, the core Self.
- **Open Dialogue (Seikkula, Finland)**: schizophrenia, the network of therapeutic inclusion.
- **Reflecting teams (Andersen)**: the team discusses its ideas openly in front of the family, who then comment on them.

## Clinical Applications

- **Disorders in children and adolescents** (behavioral, academic, emotional).
- **Eating disorders** (for example family-based treatment, the Maudsley approach, for adolescents).
- **Depression and anxiety** in the couple/family context.
- **Addiction** (an individual's addiction within a family system).
- **Couple relationships** (EFT, Gottman, Imago, CBCT).
- **Schizophrenia** (Open Dialogue, Seikkula).
- **Institutional applications** (organizations and schools).

## Relation to Neighboring Schools

- **Psychoanalysis**: Bowen trained as a psychiatrist in a psychoanalytic milieu, then moved from the individual to the family as the unit of study.
- **Humanistic therapy**: Satir's growth model and Whitaker's experiential approach have humanistic and experiential roots.
- **CBT**: cognitive-behavioral couple therapy (CBCT, Christensen).
- **Postmodernism / narrative** (White, Epston, Anderson, Goolishian): later developments.

## Criticism and Limits

- **Underestimating the individual**: it may cancel out a person's suffering in favor of the "pattern."
- **Complexity of treatment**: it requires several individuals, and scheduling is difficult.
- **Uneven empirical evidence**: some family-based treatments (for example for adolescent eating disorders and conduct problems) have good support, while evidence for others is thinner.
- **The risk of imposing a Western view of the family**: in other cultural contexts (the extended family, collective motherhood), the very concept of "boundaries" may be unsuitable.
