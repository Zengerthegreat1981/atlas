# International Political Economy

**International political economy** emerged as a distinct academic field in the 1970s at the intersection of international relations and economics. It was driven by the collapse of the Bretton Woods system of fixed exchange rates in 1971 and the oil price shock of 1973. These two events revealed that international economic relations cannot be understood in isolation from the distribution of political power among states, nor the reverse.

## Susan Strange and Structural Power

The British researcher Susan Strange made one of the field's most important contributions with the concept of **"structural power"**: the capacity to shape the frameworks that define other actors' economic choices in the first place (the systems of finance, security, knowledge and production), rather than merely the direct "behavioural" power to compel another party to take a particular action. This distinction helped explain how major states maintain deep economic influence even when their visible military or diplomatic power declines.

## The Three Traditional Schools of the Field

The field is traditionally divided into three competing currents. The realist current sees the nation-state as the central actor, which harnesses the economy in the service of its security and strategic interests. The liberal current sees trade and economic integration as a mutual interest that reduces the likelihood of conflict between states. The structuralist current, influenced by Marxism and dependency theory, sees the international economic system as a hierarchical structure that reproduces inequality between a dominant industrial core and peripheral producers of raw materials.

## Bretton Woods and Its Collapse: A Founding Moment

The collapse of the Bretton Woods fixed-exchange-rate system in 1971, when the United States unilaterally cancelled the link between the dollar and gold, was a founding moment for the field. It showed clearly that the rules of the international monetary system, which seemed to be neutral technical arrangements, were in fact a sovereign political decision serving the interests of the state dominating the system. This lesson became a cornerstone of later analysis in international political economy.

## Complex Interdependence and Global Governance

Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye developed the concept of "complex interdependence" in *Power and Interdependence* (1977) to describe a world in which the economic interests of states are intertwined through multiple channels (trade, investment and international institutions), such that economic gains are difficult to separate from traditional security calculations. This framework paved the way for the study of international institutions (the IMF and the World Trade Organization) as arenas for negotiating the rules of the global economic game, rather than as merely neutral technical instruments.

## Criticism of the Washington Consensus from Within the Field

Economists such as Joseph Stiglitz, whose arguments were widely taken up in the field, offered an influential critique of the "Washington Consensus" policies that the IMF imposed on developing and financially distressed countries. They argued that the recipe of rapid financial liberalisation without building adequate regulatory institutions had aggravated financial crises rather than solving them. This critique strengthened the presence of the structuralist current within the field by showing that the rules of international economic institutions are not technically neutral but reflect balances of political power.

## The Impact of Financial Globalisation on State Sovereignty

The field is also concerned with how the cross-border movement of capital, accelerating since the 1980s, has reduced the capacity of national governments to pursue economic policies independent of the preferences of international financial markets. A sudden outflow of capital can punish any government that adopts policies that do not please international investors. This raises fundamental questions about the future of national economic sovereignty in a financially integrated world.

## The Field's Continuing Methodological Debate

International political economy remains a multi-method field without a single theoretical consensus. It is contested between economists who favour formal modelling tools and political scientists who favour historical and institutional descriptive analysis. This methodological diversity is sometimes regarded as a weakness, reflecting the field's lack of a unified theory, and at other times as an advantage that allows a more comprehensive understanding of a complex phenomenon that is hard to reduce to a single methodological framework.

## International Economic Institutions as a Central Research Subject

The field devotes wide research attention to how the major international economic institutions (the International Monetary Fund, the World Bank and the World Trade Organization) operate, as arenas in which the rules of the global economic game are shaped through genuine political bargaining among states of unequal power, rather than as technical bodies applying purely scientific economic principles. This perspective explains why voting weights and capital shares within these institutions reflect the existing balance of global economic power, instead of being neutral with respect to it.

## A Distinctive Breadth of Analytical Levels

The field is distinguished by its ability to combine several levels of analysis at once: the decisions of individual states, the behaviour of multinational corporations, the structure of international institutions and even the social movements opposing globalisation. This breadth of method distinguishes it from the narrower traditional international economics, which focuses mainly on formal models of trade and capital flows without directly addressing the questions of political power distribution that determine the conditions of those flows in the first place.
