# Mohism (Mojia)

Mohism is a Chinese philosophical school founded by Mozi (Mo Di, c. 470–391 BC). It appeared in the Warring States period and was a principal adversary of Confucianism. It rests on an impartial, unbiased universal love, and on the criterion of the general benefit of the people in judging politics, ritual and war, and it is among the oldest consequentialist philosophies in history. It also had followers organized in a quasi-military fashion, and its later adherents developed inquiries in logic, optics and mechanics.

## Mozi and his life

His life is known only in broad outline. He came from the state of Lu or Song, and it is likely that he originated in the class of craftsmen or skilled laborers, as shown by the many examples drawn from industry and construction in his book. He began by studying Confucian traditions and then broke with them because he considered their rituals costly and their preference for relatives unjust. The "Book of Mozi", in the chapter "Gongshu", relates that he walked for ten days to dissuade the king of Chu from attacking Song, and that in a debate with the engineer Gongshu Ban he showed that Song's defenses defeated every ruse of attack. The book attributed to him, which has seventy-one chapters of which fifty-three remain, presents the views of the school in multiple forms written by his followers in different generations.

## The ten theses

The school organizes its teachings into ten theses that group into pairs:

- **Universal love (jian ai)**: every person should care for others as he cares for himself and his family, because all disorder arises from partiality toward one and not another. This does not mean sentiment but a practice from which everyone benefits.
- **Condemnation of offensive war (fei gong)**: killing in war is worse than theft and individual murder, and it accuses rulers of inconsistency when they count the killing of one person a crime and the killing of thousands a feat of heroism. It accepts legitimate defense.
- **Exalting the worthy (shang xian)** and **identifying upward (shang tong)**: offices are granted by competence, not lineage, and standards of right are unified in society under a just authority.
- **Economy in expenditure and in burial, and condemnation of music** (jie yong, jie zang, fei yue): spending on lavish ritual is not beneficial to the people.
- **The will of Heaven, the affirmation of spirits, and the denial of fate**: Heaven loves people and rewards the beneficent, in the spirits there is punishment for wrongdoers, and the claim that fate is predetermined paralyzes effort (fei ming).

The school offers three standards for testing statements (san biao): the historical foundation, the sensory experience of the people, and the usefulness of the act for the benefit of the state and the people.

## Later Mohism

During the fourth and third centuries BC the followers of the school composed the "Canon" (Mojing) and its explanations (Jingshuo) and chapters on logic and disputation, including the "Greater Selection" and the "Lesser Selection". They contain definitions in geometry, time and space, a treatment of mirrors, shadows and the reflection of light through a small hole (one of the earliest descriptions of the camera obscura), and rules for disputation and for distinguishing names. These works were in contention with the School of Names, and its followers replied to the paradoxes of Hui Shi and Gongsun Long.

## Decline and revival

Mencius criticized Mohism, saying that a love that does not distinguish between the father and the stranger is "without a father", Xunzi rejected it for its austerity, and Zhuangzi said that its doctrine is harsh on people. After the Qin unification (221 BC) and the rise of official Confucianism under the Han, the school died out as an organized movement, and of its book only a disordered text survived within the "Daoist Canon". In the late Qing, Bi Yuan, Wang Niansun and Sun Yirang (who issued an "annotation" of the book in 1894) re-edited the texts, and then Liang Qichao and Hu Shih took interest in it at the beginning of the twentieth century because the Later Mohist writings contain an early Chinese body of work on logic and science.

## Comparison with the Islamic heritage

Mozi's criterion of the general benefit resembles, to some extent, the principle of "maslaha" (public interest) in Islamic jurisprudential thought of al-Ghazali and al-Shatibi; al-Ghazali made maslaha the preservation of religion, life, intellect, lineage and property, and this is oriented to the objectives of the law and is tied to revelation. The Mohists differ in that their criterion is humanistic and utilitarian and rests on the will of Heaven by way of love. The comparison remains analytical and does not imply historical influence.

## Limits

The doctrine is criticized for making universal love contingent on utilitarian motives, for grounding ethics in fear of divine punishment, and for the fact that its extremely unified organization tends toward authoritarian centralism. Scholars doubt the dating of the book's chapters and the connection of the later logic to the founder. Fraser says that describing Mozi as a "utilitarian" in the modern sense is a simplification, since he holds a religious ethical theory that depends on Heaven's care.
