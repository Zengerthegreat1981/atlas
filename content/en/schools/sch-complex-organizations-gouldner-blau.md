# Sociology of Complex Organizations: Gouldner & Blau

Careful field ethnographies of actual bureaucratic organizations, which revealed gaps between the "ideal" Weberian model of bureaucracy and its actual day-to-day practice.

## The Central Idea

In *Patterns of Industrial Bureaucracy* (1954), Alvin Gouldner studied an American gypsum plant in the field. He showed how different patterns of compliance with bureaucratic rules arise according to their source and the meaning they have for the workers. In "mock bureaucracy," rules are imposed from outside (for example a no-smoking rule demanded by insurers) and everyone in practice ignores them by tacit agreement. In "representative bureaucracy," rules spring from real internal consensus and are applied seriously. In *The Dynamics of Bureaucracy* (1955), Peter Blau studied an American government office. He documented how employees in practice violate rigid official rules in order to achieve the organization's real goals more efficiently (for example, officials consulting colleagues informally about difficult cases although the rules directed them to consult a supervisor). This reveals a structural contradiction between actual efficiency and literal compliance with the formal rules.

## Historical Context

The two studies came within the wider context of American organizational sociology in the 1950s, which sought to test empirically, on the ground, the ideal model of bureaucracy formulated by Max Weber decades earlier, rather than being content to analyze it theoretically as an abstract model. Alvin Gouldner (1920–1980) was a student of Robert Merton at Columbia University. He drew from his teacher an interest in the "unintended consequences" of bureaucratic social action. He applied participant observation to a gypsum plant in the United States after the death of its old "paternalistic" manager and the appointment of a new manager who was more strictly bureaucratic, a change that allowed him to compare two successive management styles within the same plant. Peter Blau (1918–2002), a Jewish Austrian immigrant who fled the Nazis to the United States, applied a similar methodology in two government agencies, a federal agency enforcing labor-related law and a state employment agency, in order to compare two bureaucratic administrations with different tasks.

## Key Concepts

Gouldner distinguished three distinct bureaucratic patterns according to the source of the rule and its social meaning for the workers. "Mock bureaucracy" is where rules are imposed from an outside party (such as an insurance company) and both sides (management and workers) ignore them by an unannounced tacit agreement. "Representative bureaucracy" is where rules spring from real internal agreement on shared values (such as safety rules that workers and management both endorse). "Punishment-centered bureaucracy" is where new rules are imposed unilaterally by one party (the new management) and met with tacit resistance from the other. Blau, for his part, documented the phenomenon of "informal organization": networks of relationships, exchanges and unwritten norms that arise spontaneously among employees to facilitate actual work and to avoid obstructive formal rules. Blau saw such informal arrangements as often serving the organization's goals, and not merely as deviations from the formal rules.

## Impact

These studies offered an important empirical correction to the abstract Weberian model of bureaucracy. They showed that "informal organization" is an integral part of the functioning of any actual bureaucratic organization, and they founded an ethnographic tradition in the sociology of organizations that continued later in the work of Michael Burawoy and other students of workplaces. Gouldner later became a radical critic of functionalist sociology itself in his influential book *The Coming Crisis of Western Sociology* (1970), while Blau developed his later path toward structural social exchange theory.

## Critique

Some later sociologists faulted the two studies for being limited to two narrow field cases (one plant, two agencies), which restricts the possibility of generalizing their three-part or two-part typologies to radically different kinds of organizations such as large global corporations, contemporary non-profit organizations, or the decentralized digital organizations that emerged later. Critics also noted that the focus on "bureaucratic dysfunction" as a deviation from an ideal Weberian model implicitly assumes that this ideal model was ever fully achievable in practice. Weber himself cast doubt on that assumption, since he described his model as an abstract analytical tool (an Ideal Type) and not a literal realistic description of any existing bureaucracy. Robert Merton, Gouldner's teacher, for his part pointed out that strict adherence to formal rules can itself turn into an obstacle to efficiency when it shifts from a means to an end in itself, a process often described as "goal displacement" (Merton, 1940). Merton's interest in the unintended consequences of bureaucratic rules informed both studies.
