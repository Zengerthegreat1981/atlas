# Food and Religion Studies

Food and religion studies is a subfield within comparative religious studies. It examines how dietary rules (kashrut in Judaism, halal and haram in Islam, Christian fasting, Hindu vegetarianism, and Buddhist rules of food purity) are used as mechanisms for drawing symbolic boundaries between the religious community and the outsider, and for regulating the body and spiritual discipline within the community itself. The field is not a theology of food (as in Norman Wirzba's *Food and Faith*, a reflective theological work that falls outside this scope). It is a descriptive, historical and anthropological study of the social and symbolic function of food in religious traditions.

## Founding Works: Freidenreich and Griffith

David Freidenreich (professor of religious studies at Colby College), in *Foreigners and Their Food* (University of California Press, 2011), offers a comparative study of three monotheistic legal traditions (Judaism, Christianity and Islam). It shows how each tradition shaped food laws concerning "foreigners" (non-believers) as a tool for building a distinct collective identity, rather than as mere health or ritual rules. R. Marie Griffith (a historian of American religion), in *Born Again Bodies* (University of California Press, 2004), traces the history of diet and bodily discipline movements within evangelical American Protestantism from the nineteenth to the late twentieth century. She shows how fasting and dieting became entangled with the theology of individual salvation and moral discipline.

## The Analytical Axis

The two works meet in a shared thesis: food is not marginal to religiosity but central to the making of boundaries. For Freidenreich, these are the boundaries of the religious community toward the outside. For Griffith, they are the boundaries of the disciplined body within the community. The Jewish branch of Rosenblum and the Islamic branch of Fischer join this axis, followed by the Hindu/Buddhist branch of Khare, who examines the rules of food purity and monastic discipline in Indian traditions. Taken together, this axis parallels the broader anthropological thesis of Mary Douglas on impurity and danger as mechanisms of social classification (*Purity and Danger*, 1966), a theoretical root that predates this entire subfield.

## Relevance to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Fischer, in his study of Islamic consumption among the Malays of modern Malaysia, offers a model of how the concept of *halal* has shifted from a simple jurisprudential category (permitted versus forbidden) into a complex consumer system that includes commercial certification and oversight of global food supply chains. This reflects an ongoing negotiation between classical Sharia and the modern consumer economy. Ramadan fasting, as organized collective abstention with a daily social iftar, is another central case for understanding how temporary physical deprivation becomes a tool of collective spiritual solidarity. This parallels Griffith's analysis of the dietary discipline of American evangelicals as a bodily and spiritual discipline, not merely a secular diet.

## Impact and Criticism

The field has broadened the understanding of the religious function of food beyond the older reductive reading, which explained dietary prohibitions purely by health reasons (as in Maimonides' health-based explanation of the pig prohibition). Instead it emphasizes their symbolic role in building identity and social boundaries. However, the field still requires more comparative work that brings the four branches (Jewish, Islamic, Hindu-Buddhist and Christian) together in one synthetic framework, rather than treating them separately, as has traditionally been done.

## Additional Case Studies

Caroline Walker Bynum, in her study of medieval women, offers an influential analysis of how nuns and Christian female saints used severe fasting (in some cases to the point of self-mortification) as a means of controlling their bodies and of spiritual expression in a male-dominated world that denied them other institutional powers. The fasting body thus became a religious language in its own right. R. S. Khare, by contrast, treats food in Indian traditions as a mediator for classifying caste purity and pollution. Strict rules determine who may serve food to whom and who may share a meal with whom, so that the table becomes a site for reproducing religious social hierarchy.

## Food and Religious Hospitality

The field also studies hospitality as a central religious virtue connected to food. The Qur'an and the Sunnah urge honoring the guest ("whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him honor his guest"). Traditional Arab cultures preserve detailed hospitality rituals (serving coffee and dates, slaughtering for a guest) that carry religious and moral meanings beyond ordinary social generosity. Similarly, Judaism preserves the symbolic custom of *ushpizin* at the Feast of Booths, in which symbolic spiritual guests from the biblical patriarchs are hosted. Some Christian churches preserve the custom of foot washing (following Christ at the Last Supper, John 13) as an act of hospitality and mutual service that carries deep theological meaning about humility.
