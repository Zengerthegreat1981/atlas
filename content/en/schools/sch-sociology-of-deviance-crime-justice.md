# Sociology of Deviance, Crime & Justice

A field that brings together multiple theories explaining deviance and crime, from "social control" theory, which asks "why do most people not deviate?" rather than "why do some deviate?", to the analysis of media "moral panic" about crime.

## The Central Idea

In *Causes of Delinquency* (1969), Travis Hirschi reversed the dominant question in theories of deviance (why do some people deviate?) into the opposite one: since deviance is inherently tempting to every human being (immediate gain, excitement), the more important question is "why do most people not deviate?" He answered with "Social Control Theory": the stronger an individual's bonds to conventional society (emotional attachment to family and school, commitment to conventional goals, involvement in time-consuming conventional activities, belief in the legitimacy of social rules), the less likely he or she is to deviate. Deviance results from the weakening of these bonds, not from a special motive to deviate. In *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (1972), Stanley Cohen developed a parallel analysis of how the media and other agencies inflate limited instances of deviance (British youth gangs in his study) into a collective "Moral Panic" out of proportion to their actual scale, manufacturing a "Folk Devil" who is blamed for broader social anxiety that has no direct connection to the phenomenon itself.

## Context: A Diversity of Traditions Explaining Deviance

This field continues, and interacts with, several earlier traditions for explaining crime: Robert Merton's "structural strain" theory, which linked deviance to a gap between generalized cultural goals (material success) and the unequally distributed legitimate means of achieving them; Edwin Sutherland's "differential association" theory, which explained delinquency through the social learning of techniques and justifications for deviance through interaction with deviant groups; and "Labeling Theory" in Howard Becker and Edwin Lemert, which held that deviance is not an objective property of the act itself but the product of a social classification that society (especially the formal agencies of control) attaches to a particular act or actor. Hirschi and Cohen came in the late 1960s and early 1970s to add two complementary angles to this rich debate.

## Hirschi's Empirical Method

In his study Hirschi used a self-report survey, a method that had developed since the 1950s. He questioned several thousand secondary-school boys in California directly about their actual deviant behavior (rather than relying only on official police records, which reflect law-enforcement biases), and measured with statistical precision four elements of the "social bond": Attachment, Commitment, Involvement, and Belief. He found a strong statistical association between weakness in these four elements and self-reported rates of delinquency, which gave his theory empirical support, although self-report measures and the cross-sectional design have been criticised.

## Moral Panic: The British Case Study

Cohen based his analysis on a concrete case study: limited clashes between two British youth subcultures (the "Mods" and the "Rockers") at English seaside resorts in 1964, which the popular press inflated into an alleged existential threat to the entire British social order. Cohen's analysis of the initial media coverage distinguished exaggeration and distortion, prediction of further trouble, and symbolisation, through which names, styles and places came to stand for the offenders as "folk devils". He argued that this reaction could amplify deviance, as publicity and tighter control drew more young people into later events and hardened group identities.

## Impact

These two frameworks provided basic analytical tools that are still used in the contemporary sociology of crime and the media, especially in analyzing media coverage of waves of "panic" over recurring social issues (immigrant crime, juvenile delinquency, terrorism). Hirschi's social bond theory has informed discussion of delinquency prevention through family and school ties.

## Additional Note

Cohen's concept of "moral panic" later moved far beyond its limited original British context to become a standard analytical tool worldwide in the study of how the media construct successive social crises (drug crime, youth violence, immigration), although critics note that its application requires attention to the different social and media contexts involved.

Hirschi later continued, in collaboration with Michael Gottfredson, to develop a more comprehensive alternative theory in *A General Theory of Crime* (1990), which focuses on "Low Self-Control," formed early in childhood, as the main explanatory factor for criminal behavior across the different stages of life, a notable development of his original theoretical position on the social bond.

## Concluding Note

Despite the evident difference between them in analytical level (individual in Hirschi, collective and media-based in Cohen), these two frameworks remain complementary in portraying the sociology of deviance as a field that balances explaining the behavior of deviant individuals themselves with explaining societal and institutional reactions to that behavior. They are two inseparable, interrelated levels in understanding deviance socially.
