# Philosophical Daoism

An ancient Chinese philosophical current that makes the "Dao" (道), the path or cosmic principle that cannot be grasped by description, the origin of things and the standard of the good life. It is known in the Chinese tradition as "Daojia" (the school of the Dao), in contrast to "Daojiao", the institutional religious teaching. The philosophical study of Daoism concentrates on texts, concepts, arguments and the history of interpretation, not on priesthood and ritual.

## Foundational texts

- **Daodejing (the Book of the Dao and Virtue)**: attributed to Laozi ("the Old Master"), it consists of about five thousand characters in eighty-one poetic chapters. The traditional account, as in Sima Qian's *Records of the Grand Historian*, says that Laozi is Li Er or Lao Dan, who was an archivist in Zhou and a contemporary of Confucius, but contemporary scholars hold that the text is a collective composition that took shape in the fourth and third centuries BC. Archaeological discoveries have redrawn the picture: the Guodian manuscripts (1993, about 300 BC) contain portions of the text arranged in a different order, and the two Mawangdui manuscripts (1973, about 168 BC) present a complete version in which the chapters on "de" (virtue) come before the chapters on the "Dao", the reverse of the current order.
- **Zhuangzi**: attributed to Zhuang Zhou (about 369–286 BC), it contains thirty-three chapters, of which the first seven "Inner Chapters" are closest to the author himself, while the rest contain multiple voices. It is famous for its symbolic tales such as the butterfly dream, Cook Ding and the great fish (Kun), and it uses paradox and irony to overturn the reader's assumptions.
- **Other texts**: the *Liezi* and the *Huainanzi* (139 BC), which in the court of Liu An combined Daoism, cosmology and politics.

## Concepts and arguments

- **The Dao**: it cannot be named without distortion: "the Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao". It is that by which things are as they are; it is neither a personal god nor a first matter, and in some passages it is a nothingness that gives birth to being.
- **Wuwei (無為)**: "non-coercive action", that is, acting in harmony with the course of things, not idleness. In politics it means that the best ruler is the one who does not impose excessive intervention, so that things take care of themselves.
- **Ziran (自然)**: "what is so of itself", the origin of the concept of nature in modern Chinese.
- **The opposition of opposites**: easy and difficult, long and short, each is defined by its opposite, with the result that values are relative to a perspective and must not be treated as absolute.
- **The relativity of knowledge**: in the chapter "Discussion on Making All Things Equal" in the Zhuangzi, an argument is given that perspectives cannot be ranked from outside themselves, so the butterfly dream asks: am I Zhuang Zhou dreaming that he is a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming that it is Zhuang Zhou? Zhuangzi takes this as a call to dissolve "this" and "that", not as a call to pessimistic doubt.
- **Death and life**: at the death of his wife, Zhuangzi is shown singing and drumming on a basin, regarding death as part of the cosmic transformation, and so he calls for receiving life and death without attachment.

## History of interpretation

- **Huang-Lao**: a school in the third and second centuries BC that combined the Yellow Emperor with Laozi and influenced early Han politics.
- **Xuanxue (Mysterious Learning)**: in the third and fourth centuries CE, Wang Bi (226–249) commented on the book in existential language that made "non-being" (wu) the origin of being, and Guo Xiang developed a commentary on the Zhuangzi. Scholars connect it with the transformations that preceded the entry of Buddhism into Chinese thought and influenced it.
- **Mutual influence with Buddhism**: Daoist terms were used in translating the earliest Buddhist concepts, and then the Chan school was influenced by the Zhuangzi.
- **The modern era**: Angus Graham in *Disputers of the Tao* (1989) offered a history of the diversity of the schools, Chad Hansen in *A Daoist Theory of Chinese Thought* (1992) argued that ancient Chinese philosophy views language as a practical discrimination of names, not a representation of meanings, and Hans-Georg Moeller presented *Daoism Explained* (2004).

## Comparative reading with Islamic mysticism

The most prominent comparative work on the subject is the Japanese scholar Toshihiko Izutsu's study *Sufism and Taoism* (1983), which compared the concepts of Ibn Arabi with those of Laozi and Zhuangzi: the unity of being, the perfect man, the Dao, and the ideal man, without claiming a historical transmission but through an analysis of the structure of thought. This comparison is suitable for understanding how a thought that speaks of what lies beyond language can offer a practical life that transcends dualities.

## Limits and critique

- Nothing is historically certain about Laozi, and the two texts are collections of multiple materials, so it is difficult to attribute a consistent position to each of them.
- Some consider "Daoist ethics" vague, since it gives no clear standard of conduct, while others hold that this is intentional.
- The expressions of "non-action" may be exploited to justify political passivity, and some contemporary Western libertarians have read them as founding their doctrine, a reading far from their original context.
- Translations are so numerous that the Western image of the Dao becomes tied more to the translator than to the text.
