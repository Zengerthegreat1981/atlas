# Humeanism (Empirical Skepticism)

A philosophical tradition deriving from **David Hume** (1711-1776), the Scottish philosopher and historian, often regarded as the greatest philosopher to write in English. Humeanism carries British empiricism to its skeptical conclusions and stands in tension with traditional metaphysics. Hume analysed causation as constant conjunction plus a habit of expectation rather than a discernible power, denied that the self is an observable substance, argued that testimony can hardly establish miracles, and criticised natural theology. He influenced Kant, utilitarianism, logical positivism, and much twentieth-century philosophy.

## Founding Context

Hume was born in Edinburgh into a Scottish Presbyterian family and attended the University of Edinburgh from about 1723, leaving without a degree. He read Locke, Berkeley, Malebranche, Bayle, Newton, and Hutcheson. After a nervous breakdown around 1729 he moved to La Flèche in France (1734-1737), where he drafted *A Treatise of Human Nature*. Books 1 and 2 appeared in 1739 and Book 3 in 1740; the work, as he later wrote, "fell dead-born from the press." He recast its argument in *An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (1748) and *An Enquiry concerning the Principles of Morals* (1751), and became better known in his lifetime as the author of the *History of England*.

## Leading Figures

- **David Hume** (1711-1776): the founder.
- **Adam Smith** (1723-1790): his close friend, whose *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759) develops a sympathy-based moral psychology.
- **Adam Ferguson** (1723-1816): friend and fellow figure of the Scottish Enlightenment.
- **Henry Home, Lord Kames** (1696-1782): judge and philosopher, an early correspondent and patron.
- **Francis Hutcheson** (1694-1746): moral-sense philosopher with whom Hume corresponded while completing Book 3 of the *Treatise*; Hume never studied under him.
- **Thomas Reid** (1710-1796): the leading critic of Hume, replying with common-sense philosophy.
- **Immanuel Kant** (1724-1804): said Hume interrupted his "dogmatic slumber."
- **Jeremy Bentham** (1748-1832), **James Mill** (1773-1836), and **John Stuart Mill** (1806-1873): utilitarians who inherited Hume's empiricism and his emphasis on utility.

## Central Concepts

### 1. Impressions and Ideas

All perceptions of the mind divide into **impressions** (forceful, lively perceptions of sensation and feeling) and **ideas** (their fainter copies in thought). The "copy principle" holds that every simple idea derives from a corresponding impression, which Hume uses as a test of whether a term has meaning.

### 2. Association

Complex ideas are built from simple ones, and the mind connects them by resemblance, contiguity, and cause and effect.

### 3. Causation

We never perceive a necessary connection between cause and effect, only that events of one kind regularly follow events of another. The idea of necessity arises from a felt determination of the mind, produced by custom, to expect the effect on seeing the cause.

### 4. The Self as a Bundle

Searching introspectively, Hume finds no impression of a simple, continuing self, only a "bundle or collection" of perceptions in perpetual flux. He admitted in the Appendix to the *Treatise* (1740) that he could not satisfactorily explain their unity.

### 5. Reason and the Passions

"Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions" (*Treatise*, 2.3.3). Reason alone cannot motivate action, so morality, which does move us to act, cannot rest on reason alone.

### 6. Miracles

In section 10 of the first *Enquiry*, Hume argues that no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the event it reports. Since a miracle is a violation of laws of nature confirmed by uniform experience, the balance of evidence is always against it.

### 7. Religion

In the *Natural History of Religion* (1757) Hume explains religious belief by human fears and hopes rather than by reason, and in the *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion* (published 1779) he subjects the design argument to sustained criticism. How far the *Dialogues* express his own view, and whether he was an atheist or a skeptic about religious knowledge, is debated.

### 8. The Problem of Induction

Our expectation that the future will resemble the past cannot be justified by demonstrative reasoning, since the contrary is conceivable, nor by experience, since that would assume the very principle in question. Hume concludes that inference from experience rests on custom rather than reason.

### 9. Sentimentalist Ethics

Moral distinctions arise from feelings of approval and disapproval. In the second *Enquiry* Hume stresses utility and agreeableness as sources of virtue; in the *Treatise* sympathy plays the central role in explaining moral sentiment.

## Hume and "Humeanism"

Hume the historical philosopher, whose positions were often more nuanced and sometimes naturalistic rather than purely skeptical, should be distinguished from "Humeanism," a later label for views associated with him. Interpreters disagree, for example, about whether Hume denied that causation involves real powers or only that we can know them. Contemporary Humeanism usually refers to specific doctrines: in metaphysics, the "Humean mosaic" of David Lewis, on which there are no necessary connections between distinct existences; in ethics, sentimentalism and expressivism (for example Simon Blackburn); and in the theory of motivation, the claim that beliefs alone cannot motivate.

## Relation to Other Schools

- **Lockeanism**: Hume took over Locke's empiricist starting point but rejected the notion of substance and pressed Berkeley's criticism of the primary/secondary quality distinction.
- **Berkeleianism**: Hume remarked that Berkeley's arguments admit of no answer and produce no conviction, and used them for skeptical ends.
- **Scottish Common Sense**: a direct reply, developed by Reid and his followers against Hume's skepticism.
- **Continental rationalism and Kant**: Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) answers Hume's challenge about causation with the categories as conditions of experience.
- **Utilitarianism**: Bentham acknowledged Hume's influence on his thinking about utility.
- **Logical positivism**: Ayer and Carnap drew on Hume's distinction between relations of ideas and matters of fact ("Hume's fork") in criticising metaphysics.
- **The Church of Scotland**: Hume was passed over for chairs at Edinburgh (1744-45) and Glasgow (1752) amid accusations of atheism, and an attempt to have him censured by the General Assembly in 1755-56 failed.

## Later Influence

- **In philosophy**: Kant's response to Hume shaped German Idealism, and Hume's problem of induction is a central problem for twentieth-century philosophy of science.
- **In psychology**: William James and the associationist tradition drew on Hume's analysis of the mind.
- **In ethics and political thought**: his account of justice as a convention serving common utility, and his essays on commerce and government, were influential in the Scottish Enlightenment and beyond.
- **In contemporary philosophy**: Humean views remain prominent in metaphysics (laws of nature, causation) and metaethics.

## The Philosophical Predicament

- **Tension with rationalism**: the critique of causation shows that the causal inference on which natural science relies lacks demonstrative justification. Kant replied with the categories as conditions of experience.
- **Tension with ethics**: if reason alone cannot motivate, morality needs another basis. Kant replied with the categorical imperative.
- **Tension over the self**: if the self is a bundle, personal identity becomes a problem.
- **Tension with theology**: the critique of miracles and of the design argument fed Enlightenment criticism of religion.
- **Tension over induction**: responses include Popper's rejection of inductive justification and later reliabilist and probabilistic proposals; the problem remains open.
