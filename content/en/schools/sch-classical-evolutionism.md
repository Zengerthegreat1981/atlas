# Classical Evolutionary Anthropology

An early systematic school of anthropology. It prevailed in Britain and the United States in the final decades of the nineteenth century, shaped by nineteenth-century evolutionary thought, including the debates around Darwin's theory of biological evolution (1859), and by the wave of colonial exploration that made it possible to gather ethnographic reports on non-European peoples.

## The Central Idea

The school rested on a central assumption, "unilinear evolution": that all human societies follow the same evolutionary path from "primitive" to "civilized" stages, and that contemporary cultural differences among peoples do not reflect variation in paths but differences in position on one and the same evolutionary ladder. Its pioneers, led by Edward Tylor and Lewis Henry Morgan, used the "armchair comparative method": compiling reports by missionaries, traders and colonial administrators about scattered peoples and arranging them in a hypothetical evolutionary sequence, mostly without direct field observation (a partial exception was Morgan, who worked closely with Seneca and other Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) people and made field trips among Native American peoples further west).

## Figures and Works

- **Edward Tylor**: defined "culture" in an anthropologically foundational way in *Primitive Culture* (1871), and developed the concepts of "animism" and "survivals."
- **Lewis Henry Morgan**: established the comparative study of kinship terminology in *Systems of Consanguinity and Affinity* (1871), and in *Ancient Society* (1877) presented a three-stage evolutionary model (savagery, barbarism, civilization) that later influenced Engels.

## Critique

The school was subjected to decisive criticism by the generation of Franz Boas and his school at the start of the twentieth century. It was accused of ethnocentrism (ranking non-European peoples in the lowest tiers), of using an armchair method rather than fieldwork that separated theory from specific ethnographic facts, and of ignoring "cultural diffusion" as an alternative explanation for similarities between cultures in place of independent parallel invention. Nevertheless, the school remains the historical starting point against which academic anthropology that followed (Boasian, British functionalist) defined and criticized itself.

## Broader Intellectual Context

The emergence of this school coincided with the peak of European colonial expansion in Africa and Asia, and its theoretical framework provided an apparently scientific justification for a colonial hierarchy that already rested on political and economic foundations. If colonized peoples stood at a "lower" stage of the evolutionary ladder, then their rule by "more advanced" peoples became a "natural" and temporary condition, until they too progressed along the same path. This link between scientific theory and the colonial project made the later critique of this school a core part of a wider critique of the relationship between anthropology and colonialism in general.

## Morgan's Influence on Marxism

Lewis Henry Morgan's influence extended beyond anthropology into political philosophy. Friedrich Engels drew directly on Morgan's three-stage model in *The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State* (1884), linking the evolution of kinship and property to historical class development. This Marxist adoption of Morgan's theory meant that a modified version of unilinear evolutionism remained present in Marxist thought even after academic anthropology had fully abandoned it.

## Tylor's Concept of "Survivals"

Edward Tylor developed an influential concept: "survivals," practices or beliefs that continue in a "civilized" society despite having lost their original function, understood as petrified traces of an earlier evolutionary stage. Examples include popular belief in certain rituals or superstitions that lost their original functional meaning but persisted as social habits. Tylor used this concept as indirect evidence for the evolutionary path he assumed, treating these "survivals" as social fossils that reveal stages of development the "civilized" society itself had passed through in its distant past.

## Tylor's Foundational Definition of Culture

One of Tylor's most lasting contributions is his definition of the concept of "culture" itself, in the opening of *Primitive Culture* (1871): "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom, and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society." This definition became a standard point of reference for later discussions of culture, although Tylor himself used it in the service of a unilinear evolutionary project that later generations of anthropologists rejected sharply.

## Diffusionism as a Rival Alternative

Alongside Boas's critique, the German-Austrian school of Diffusionism, led by Fritz Graebner and Wilhelm Schmidt, offered a rival explanatory alternative to unilinear evolutionism. Similarities between geographically distant cultures do not necessarily reflect parallel, independent evolutionary paths; they may result from the actual historical diffusion of cultural elements through trade, migration and conquest from a limited number of original "culture circles" (*Kulturkreise*). This proposal, though it sometimes exaggerated the tendency to trace every cultural similarity to a single source, provided an important correction to the classical evolutionist assumption that every society invents its cultural solutions in complete isolation from its neighbors.

Although its central assumptions collapsed, this school remains the ground on which academic anthropology grew into an independent discipline, and the later development of the field (Boasian, functionalist, structuralist) cannot be understood without first understanding what these schools were reacting to and rejecting.
