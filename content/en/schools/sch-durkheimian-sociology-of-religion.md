# Durkheimian Sociology of Religion and Collective Ritual

The last of Émile Durkheim's major works: his study of the simplest forms of religious life, drawing on the ethnography of Australian Aboriginal tribes, in order to derive from it a general theory of religion's social function rather than its theological content.

## Historical Context

Durkheim wrote this work in the last years of his life, after devoting decades to developing his positivist sociological method in *The Rules of Sociological Method* (1895) and *Suicide* (1897). He chose to study the "simplest" and structurally most primitive religion rather than the most complex and historically developed: totemism among the Australian Aboriginal tribes. He relied on field ethnographic reports gathered by others (such as Baldwin Spencer and Francis Gillen) rather than on direct observation of his own. His starting point was a methodological assumption that studying the most primitive and simple form of a social phenomenon reveals its general essence more clearly than studying the complex forms accumulated historically in the great monotheistic religions.

## The Central Idea

In *The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* (1912), Durkheim argued that the essence of religion is not belief in a supernatural being or a particular god, but the fundamental social distinction between the "sacred" and the "profane" (Sacré/Profane): a rigid binary classification of things, actions and times into two opposed categories separated by ritual, not a difference in the intrinsic nature of the things themselves. He argued that what is actually worshipped in religious ritual through the totemic symbol is society itself, manifested in a sacred form that participants can hardly perceive with direct awareness: when the members of a tribe gather around their common totem, they are in reality worshipping the collective force of society itself, which transcends each individual taken separately. He developed the concept of "collective effervescence" (Effervescence collective) to describe the intense emotional state that seizes a group during massed ritual gatherings: a collective enthusiasm exceeding what each individual feels alone, producing a sense of a superhuman power that goes beyond the individual. Such effervescence renews social solidarity and reproduces the collective consciousness periodically and regularly across the social history of any group. He identified the "totemic principle", an impersonal sacred force akin to what Melanesian peoples call "mana", as the form in which this collective power is experienced, and he argued that the classification of things under the totems of the Australian clans mirrors the structure of the clans themselves, an argument developed further with Marcel Mauss in *Primitive Classification* (1903).

## Major Works

*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life* is the last of the four major works that shaped Durkheim's career (after *The Division of Labour in Society* 1893, *The Rules of Sociological Method* 1895 and *Suicide* 1897). It was published only five years before his death (he died in 1917), and many later scholars regarded it as the mature theoretical culmination of his entire intellectual project on the origin of social bonds, the collective consciousness, and the means of their historical continuity across successive generations.

## Influence and Criticism

The work had a deep influence on British functional anthropology (Radcliffe-Brown in particular, who applied a related approach to the Andaman Islanders and to Australian societies), and later the sociology of ritual and everyday interaction in Erving Goffman, who borrowed the idea of "interaction ritual" (Interaction Ritual) and transferred it from the massed collective religious context to fine-grained individual everyday interaction (such as rituals of greeting and politeness). The work was later criticized by historians of religion, who held that Durkheim's reliance on indirect secondary ethnographic reports (rather than direct field research that he conducted personally) leaves some of his empirical details about Australian totemism open to revision. Some also questioned generalizing his conclusions from a single isolated Australian case to the "essence" of human religion as a whole across all cultures and civilizations. Nevertheless, the book remains one of the most cited classic works in the sociology of religion to this day, an indispensable core reference in any university course on sociological theories of religion, and a near-obligatory starting point for any contemporary academic discussion of the relationship between collective ritual and social solidarity.

## Relevance to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Durkheim's framework of the "sacred and the profane" and his concept of "collective effervescence" can be applied to Islamic collective rituals. Examples include the Hajj season to Mecca, where millions of pilgrims from widely differing national, ethnic and linguistic backgrounds gather in a single intense collective experience unified in time and place, and the great Shi'i ritual gatherings such as the Arbaeen pilgrimage in Karbala. Read through the idea of "collective effervescence," such gatherings can be seen as renewing the collective consciousness and social solidarity among the participants through a shared, massed emotional experience that transcends the isolated individual and ties him to a community wider than the nation and the sect.
