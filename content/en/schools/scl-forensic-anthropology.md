# Forensic Anthropology

Forensic anthropology is the application of physical anthropology and human osteology to legal questions, above all the identification of the dead. When a body is found burned, decomposed, dismembered, or reduced to bone, the forensic anthropologist reads the skeleton for the information a court or a family needs: the person's sex, age at death, stature, ancestry, and any sign of disease, injury, or the cause of death. The field also reconstructs the events around death — how long a body has lain in the ground, whether remains were moved, and whether a fracture happened before death, at the moment of death, or after. Behind the technical work lies a human purpose: to return a name to the unknown dead, to give evidence that can convict the guilty or free the innocent, and to give families certainty after loss.

## Origins and History

Forensic anthropology grew out of physical anthropology in the twentieth century, but its professional birth came in the 1970s. During the Korean War and the Second World War, physical anthropologists had been called on to identify war dead, and the anatomist Thomas Dwight had laid foundations with his work on the skeleton as a record of identity in the nineteenth century. The turning point was institutional: in the early 1970s the American Academy of Forensic Sciences created a Physical Anthropology section, giving the field a professional home. In 1977 the American Board of Forensic Anthropology was founded to certify practitioners, separating trained experts from self-styled ones and establishing the field as a forensic science in its own right. The first generation of specialists — among them J. Lawrence Angel at the Smithsonian, a pioneer of skeletal identification in forensic casework, and William Maples at the University of Florida — built the methods on which the field still rests.

The field expanded dramatically in the 1980s. William Bass founded the Anthropological Research Facility at the University of Tennessee in 1981, a pioneering research centre where donated bodies could be studied as they decomposed under controlled conditions. The "Body Farm," as it became known, produced the data on decomposition rates that underpin modern estimates of time since death. In 1984 Clyde Snow, working in Argentina after the fall of the military junta, showed that forensic anthropology could serve human rights: his team exhumed the victims of the "disappeared," identified them, and provided the evidence used in the trials of the generals. That work was formative for forensic anthropology as a tool of transitional justice, and teams modelled on Snow's Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team now work in conflict zones and mass graves around the world.

## Key Figures and Works

Douglas Ubelaker's Human Skeletal Remains (1978) became the field's standard manual, setting out how to excavate, describe, and interpret skeletal material and how to estimate sex, age, and stature from bone. William Bass's Human Osteology (first published 1971; third edition 1987) systematized the measurement of the skeleton for identification. Clyde Snow's work in Argentina and later in the former Yugoslavia and Iraq made forensic anthropology a human-rights discipline. T. Dale Stewart's Essentials of Forensic Anthropology (1979) codified the field's early methods, and Karen Ramey Burns's Forensic Anthropology Training Manual (1999) became the standard teaching text. In Britain, Sue Black's work on identification and her popular writing brought the field to a wider public, while the development of forensic facial reconstruction, advanced by practitioners such as Richard Neave, extended identification to cases where only a skull survives.

## Method

The forensic anthropologist's method begins in the field, where the recovery of remains is itself a scientific act: the position of every bone is mapped, the soil is sieved, and the context is recorded so that the evidence survives in court. In the laboratory, the skeleton is cleaned, measured, and examined. Sex is estimated from the pelvis and skull, age from the wear of teeth and the fusion of bones, stature from the length of the long bones, and ancestry from the shape of the skull. Trauma is analyzed in three categories: antemortem injuries that healed during life, perimortem injuries that occurred around death and may indicate the cause, and postmortem damage from animals, weather, or the recovery itself. The field works alongside forensic pathology, odontology, and DNA analysis, and its findings are presented in court, where the anthropologist must explain both conclusions and their limits. The Daubert standard, adopted by American courts in 1993, forced the field to demonstrate that its methods are tested, peer-reviewed, and have known error rates, a demand that has made modern forensic anthropology more rigorous and more cautious.

## Influence and Legacies

Forensic anthropology has changed criminal justice, disaster response, and human rights work. Its methods identify victims of air crashes, tsunamis, and terrorist attacks, and its practitioners have worked at the World Trade Center, in Rwanda, in Bosnia, and in the mass graves of Iraq and Guatemala. The field's human-rights branch has given families the bodies of the disappeared and courts the evidence to convict perpetrators of atrocity. It has also influenced archaeology and physical anthropology, whose techniques it borrows and refines, and popular culture, where television and fiction have made the forensic anthropologist a familiar figure — a fame the field itself regards with ambivalence, since real casework is slower, more uncertain, and more careful than its screen version. Its future lies in new technologies: isotope analysis that can say where a person lived, three-dimensional imaging, and the integration of DNA and osteological evidence.
