# Analytic Philosophy of Mind

Analytic philosophy of mind is the tendency that addressed the mind–body problem in the twentieth century with the tools of linguistic and logical analysis, and later in cooperation with neuroscience and artificial intelligence. It is treated here as a series of successive and disputing positions between 1949 and the beginning of the twenty-first century; the roots older than Descartes are presented on the general philosophy-of-mind page. The discussions revolve around two questions: how are mental states related to brain states? And how can mental states be "about" something or have a qualitative character?

## Logical Behaviourism

Gilbert Ryle in "The Concept of Mind" (1949) attacked what he called the "ghost in the machine," that is, Descartes's conception of the mind as an independent inner theatre, and considered it a "category mistake": to speak of intelligence or belief is to speak of behavioural dispositions, not of hidden events. Wittgenstein in "Philosophical Investigations" (1953) approached the subject through the private language argument, arguing that the meanings of sensation words are not determined by a private inner indication. Logical behaviourism was then weakened by objections, among them that a single mental state manifests in different behaviours depending on the other states.

## Identity Theory

U. T. Place presented "Is Consciousness a Brain Process?" (1956) and J. J. C. Smart developed the idea in "Sensations and Brain Processes" (1959): mental states are brain states in the sense in which lightning is an electrical discharge. Putnam objected with the argument of "multiple realizability": pain can be felt by beings with different brains, and perhaps by beings with no brains. Kripke added in "Naming and Necessity" (lectures of 1970, published 1972 and as a book in 1980) the argument that an identity between pain and a brain state would have to be necessary if true, but intuition rejects that.

## Functionalism

Hilary Putnam in "Minds and Machines" (1960) and in "Psychological Predicates" (1967) formulated the idea that a mental state is defined by its causal role and not by its material composition, illustrating it with the Turing machine. Jerry Fodor followed him in "Psychological Explanation" (1968), and David Lewis and David Armstrong in a causal interpretation that links the mental state to its inputs, outputs and other states. Unlike logical behaviourism, which analyses mental states as dispositions to behave, functionalism allows internal states that interact causally with one another and with inputs and outputs. Cognitive science consolidated this tendency. However, John Searle answered it with the "Chinese Room" thought experiment (1980): a computer that follows rules for processing symbols does not understand meaning. Ned Block presented the "China brain" argument (1978) against functionalism on account of missing qualia.

## Other Positions

- **Anomalous monism**: Donald Davidson presented "Mental Events" (1970): every mental event is a physical event, but there are no strict laws linking the two descriptions.
- **Eliminative materialism**: Paul Churchland in the article "Eliminative Materialism and the Propositional Attitudes" (1981) called for folk psychology to be recognized as a false theory that a neural theory will replace. He was preceded by Paul Feyerabend and Richard Rorty in the 1960s, and his wife Patricia Churchland developed the programme further as "neurophilosophy" (*Neurophilosophy*, 1986).
- **Biological naturalism**: Searle holds that consciousness is a biological phenomenon caused by the brain, a position that rejects both dualism and functionalism.
- **Representationalism**: Dretske and Tye hold that phenomenal consciousness reduces to representational content.

## The Hard Problem and the Explanatory Gap

Thomas Nagel wrote "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974): experience has a subjective perspective that is not exhausted by objective description. Frank Jackson formulated the knowledge argument (1982): Mary, the scientist who knows all the physical facts about colours while in a black-and-white room, learns something new when she sees the colour red. Joseph Levine named the gap between a neural description and a description of what it is like the "explanatory gap" (1983). David Chalmers coined the phrase "the hard problem" (1995) in "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness," then presented a position close to property dualism in "The Conscious Mind" (1996). Daniel Dennett replied in "Consciousness Explained" (1991) with the "multiple drafts" model, which doubts the existence of a central stage of consciousness.

## Its Impact

This philosophy became part of the cognitive sciences: the functionalists contributed to the emergence of cognitive psychology, and the discussions of consciousness opened a field for studying the relation between neural correlates and conscious experience. It then entered the ethics of artificial intelligence: can a machine have consciousness or interests?

## Limits and Criticisms

- Analytic philosophy of mind is subject to a phenomenological critique: it presupposes an abstract mind and then searches for its place in nature, whereas the phenomenological tradition holds that the body and the world come first.
- The conceivability arguments (zombies and Mary) are still disputed: do they prove something about reality or about the limits of our conceptions?
- Some scholars doubt the unity of the "mind" itself, and prefer to study separate capacities.
- The discussion of consciousness still suffers from differing definitions among phenomenality, access consciousness and self-consciousness.

## Philosophy of Mind and Philosophical Psychology

Although contemporary discussions frequently treat philosophy of mind and philosophical psychology as interchangeable, they have distinct historical scopes and methodological emphases. Philosophical psychology historically developed from classical and scholastic analyses of the soul (*de anima*), faculties, perception, volition, and emotion. In the twentieth century, spurred by the work of G. E. M. Anscombe (*Intention*, 1957) and the later Wittgenstein, philosophical psychology investigated the conceptual grammar of agency, reasons versus causes, voluntary action, belief-desire explanations, and the passions. Analytic philosophy of mind, by contrast, developed primarily as a metaphysical and epistemological branch of post-positivist philosophy, centering upon the mind–body problem, mental ontology (physicalism, functionalism, computationalism), and the nature of mental representation and qualia. While contemporary philosophy of mind interfaces directly with cognitive neuroscience and artificial intelligence, philosophical psychology maintains strong continuity with action theory, moral psychology, and philosophical ethics.
