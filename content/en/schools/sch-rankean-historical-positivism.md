# The Rankean School and Historical Positivism ("As It Actually Happened" and Source Criticism)

Developed primarily by the German historian Leopold von Ranke (1795–1886) during his long tenure at the University of Berlin, the Rankean approach helped transform the writing of history from a branch of rhetoric or moral philosophy into an autonomous academic discipline grounded in systematic archival research and source criticism. Ranke also helped develop the university research seminar, creating an institutional framework for the professional training of historians that spread across Europe and North America.

## The Problem and the Motto

In the preface to his first major work, *Geschichten der romanischen und germanischen Völker von 1494 bis 1514* (*Histories of the Latin and Germanic Peoples*, 1824), Ranke famously stated that history had often been assigned the task of judging the past and instructing the present for the benefit of future ages, but that his own work did not aspire to such high offices: it sought merely to show "how it actually happened" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*). 

While later positivist readers interpreted this motto as a call for passive, mechanical fact-gathering, Ranke intended it as a critical boundary against two dominant tendencies of his time: moralistic didacticism and speculative philosophical teleology (such as the sweeping systems of G. W. F. Hegel). History was not to be bent to speculative schemas; it had to be established through contemporary documents (*Urkunden*).

## Archival Method and Source Criticism

Ranke established several methodological principles that became foundational for modern historical inquiry:

- **Primacy of the Primary Source**: Prioritizing documents contemporary with the events under study (diplomatic dispatches, council minutes, treaties, personal letters) over later secondary compilations. Ranke made extensive use of unprinted state records, notably the famous Venetian ambassadorial reports (*relazioni*), showing how state archives could uncover the motives and negotiations of historical actors.
- **External and Internal Criticism**: Subjecting documents to philological and contextual scrutiny. External criticism establishes authenticity, authorship, provenance, and integrity (distinguishing original records from later forgeries or corruptions). Internal criticism interrogates the author's motives, biases, competence, and proximity to the event. This dual critical procedure paralleled earlier traditions of textual verification, such as classical philology and the Islamic science of transmitter criticism (*isnad* and *jarh wa-ta'dil*), although Ranke developed his method within modern European archival contexts.
- **The University Seminar**: Ranke institutionalized historical training at Berlin through the advanced research seminar (*historisches Seminar*), beginning in the 1830s. Rather than listening passively to lectures, students learned to decipher paleography, interrogate primary documents, and defend critical monographs. His students, including Georg Waitz, Heinrich von Sybel, and Wilhelm von Giesebrecht, carried this pedagogical model throughout the academic world.
- **Historicism and Divine Immediacy**: Ranke rejected the Enlightenment belief in linear moral progress, asserting instead that "every epoch is immediate to God" (*jede Epoche ist unmittelbar zu Gott*). Each era possessed its own unique spirit and intrinsic value, and had to be understood on its own historical terms rather than judged retroactively by the standards of the present.

## Historiographical Limitations and Critiques

Despite its enormous influence, the Rankean school encountered substantial criticism:

- **State-Centered and Elite Bias**: Because Ranke relied heavily on official diplomatic and state archives, his narratives concentrated overwhelmingly on high politics, warfare, diplomacy, and great statesmen. This political orientation tended to neglect social, economic, cultural, and everyday life, as well as the experiences of women and non-elite classes—a gap that subsequent movements, notably the French *Annales* school and 20th-century social history, sought to correct.
- **The Positivist Myth of Objectivity**: In *That Noble Dream* (1988), Peter Novick argued that the American historical profession largely misunderstood Ranke's philosophical historicism, turning *wie es eigentlich gewesen* into an emblem of naive positivism and detached neutrality. Ranke himself believed that the historian, through sympathetic immersion in the documents, discerned an underlying spiritual and divine order in history.
