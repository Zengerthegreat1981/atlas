# The Rankean School and Historical Positivism ("As It Actually Happened" and Source Criticism)

A method founded by the German historian **Leopold von Ranke** at the University of Berlin, which transformed the writing of history from a narrative literary art into an **academic discipline** with strict rules for verifying sources. He also founded the first university research seminar to train historians in these rules.

## The Problem and the Motto

In the preface to his *Histories of the Latin and Germanic Peoples* (1824), Ranke declared that the aim of his book was not "to judge the past" nor "to teach the present," but simply to present the event "as it actually happened" (*wie es eigentlich gewesen*). This motto became shorthand for a broader methodological project: separating history from moral preaching or teleological philosophy (as in Bossuet or Hegel), and grounding it in **a primary investigation of documents contemporary with the event** (diplomatic correspondence, minutes of assemblies, official documents), rather than in later or secondary narratives.

## The Method

- **Priority of the primary source**: preference for the document contemporary with the event (*Urkunde*) over later narratives, and tracing the chain of transmission back to the closest possible witness. This principle structurally parallels the requirement of an unbroken chain of transmission (*isnad*) in the hadith tradition, although it arose in an independent European context.
- **Internal and external criticism of the document**: examining the authenticity of the document itself (is it forged? Where did it come from?) before examining its content and the credibility of its author. This is the foundation of what was later organized under the name "internal and external source criticism" (see section 8 of this chapter).
- **The university seminar**: Ranke's institutional innovation. It trained graduate students collectively to analyze a particular primary document, instead of relying on narrative lectures. This model spread worldwide and remains the professional training template for historians to this day.
- **Rejection of philosophical teleology**: unlike Hegel, who was his contemporary in Berlin, Ranke refused to subject history to a comprehensive philosophical plan (whether a religious teleology or a Hegelian dialectic), insisting that "every epoch is immediately close to God" (*jede Epoche ist unmittelbar zu Gott*). In other words, each era has its own intrinsic value, independent of its position in an alleged progressive course.

## The Limitation

- A later charge that the Rankean "objectivity" concealed an implicit bias toward the history of states, diplomacy and political elites (because the official documents available were mostly documents of states and governments). This led the French Annales school later to broaden the concept of "source" to include social, economic and cultural material.
- Peter Novick's critique, in *That Noble Dream* (1988), of how Ranke's motto was transformed in American reception into a naive positivist doctrine claiming total neutrality, unlike the complex position of Ranke himself.
