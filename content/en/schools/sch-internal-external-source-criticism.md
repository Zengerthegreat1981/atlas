# Internal and External Source Criticism (General Method)

The primary methodological instrument shared by all rigorous academic historiography, regardless of theoretical orientation: before any source is used as evidence for a historical fact, it is subjected to two distinct examinations.

## External Criticism (Lower Criticism)

Asks: **Is this document genuinely authentic?** — an examination of the material authenticity of the source itself: its actual date of production (not the date claimed within it), the true identity of its author (not the attributed author), and the integrity of its transmission through copying and translation without alteration, forgery, or interpolation. Its tools include paleography, diplomatics (analyzing script, paper, and ink for ancient documents), and textual criticism (comparing multiple manuscript copies to trace scribal errors) — a method structurally parallel to what Hadith scholars developed in isnad criticism from a different angle.

## Internal Criticism (Higher Criticism)

Presupposes the document's authenticity (once it has passed external criticism) and then asks: **Is what it states true or reliable?** — examining the author's credibility (Was the author an eyewitness or reporting from others? Did they have an interest or bias distorting their account?), corroborating their testimony against other independent sources for the same event, and assessing the internal consistency of the account with itself and with the broader known historical context.

## Practical Method

Historians are practically trained to classify sources into **primary** (contemporary with the event, produced by a participant or observer) and **secondary** (later narrative or analysis relying on primary sources), prioritizing reliance on primary sources whenever possible, while remaining conscious that every source — even a primary one — is produced from a specific social and political vantage point warranting critical interrogation rather than unquestioning, literal acceptance.

## Methodological Codification: Bernheim, Langlois, and Seignobos

An influential systematic codification of this methodology was provided by German historian **Ernst Bernheim** in his *Lehrbuch der historischen Methode* (1889), where he set out an influential, comprehensive scheme of steps for examining historical sources from discovery to final interpretation. This was followed, with even wider influence across the Francophone and international academic worlds, by French historians **Charles-Victor Langlois** and **Charles Seignobos** in *Introduction aux études historiques* (1898). They transformed the principles of internal and external criticism from dispersed intuitive practices into a structured pedagogical curriculum taught in university history programs, establishing what later became known as the "French Methodical School."

## Practical Implementation Steps

External criticism practically involves three sequential steps: **authentication/dating** (determining the document's actual date of production using material evidence such as paper, ink, or script), **attribution of authorship** (identifying the real author by comparing style and language with other securely attributed works), and **tracing provenance** (tracking the document's path from its original production until it reached the researcher, uncovering any distortion introduced during copying, translation, or preservation). Internal criticism, on the other hand, requires evaluating the **plausibility of the content** (is it consistent with the broader known historical context?), analyzing potential **authorial bias** (the author's political, religious, or personal interests in depicting the event in a specific light), and comparing the account with independent testimonies to establish agreement or contradiction.

## An Independent Islamic Precedent: Isnad and Matn Criticism

The Islamic scholarly tradition independently developed a sophisticated methodology for narrative evaluation through the twin disciplines of *ʿilm al-jarḥ wa-l-taʿdīl* (the science of biographical evaluation and transmitter criticism) and *naqd al-ḥadīth* (the critical analysis of traditions). Developed between the second and fourth centuries AH (eighth to tenth centuries CE) by scholars such as Shuʿba ibn al-Ḥajjāj (d. 160 AH / 776 CE), Yaḥyā ibn Maʿīn (d. 233 AH / 848 CE), Aḥmad ibn Ḥanbal (d. 241 AH / 855 CE), and Muḥammad ibn Ismāʿīl al-Bukhārī (194–256 AH / 810–870 CE), and later codified by Ibn al-Ṣalāḥ (d. 643 AH / 1245 CE) in his *Muqaddima*, this system bifurcated narrative assessment into chain criticism (*naqd al-isnād*) and content criticism (*naqd al-matn*). 

Chain criticism operated as a form of external verification: it scrutinized the unbroken chronological sequence of narrators (*ittiṣāl*), their moral integrity (*ʿadāla*), and their mnemonic accuracy (*ḍabṭ*), supported by extensive biographical dictionaries (*kutub al-rijāl*). Concurrently, content criticism examined the substantive text for hidden defects (*ʿilal*), anachronisms, or irreconcilable contradictions with established historical facts, textual canons, or rational plausibility—a dimension later theorized extensively by the North African historian Ibn Khaldūn (732–808 AH / 1332–1406 CE) in his *Muqaddima* as the essential criterion for historical reporting (*taḥqīq al-akhbār*). While Western source criticism emerged primarily around the material authenticity of physical archival documents, the Islamic tradition addressed the verification of oral reports and scribal transmissions, converging on the principle that no historical statement may be accepted as fact without rigorous authentication of its witnesses and logical appraisal of its content.

## Modern Extension: Forensic Document Examination

This methodology finds contemporary continuity in **forensic document examination**, which employs advanced technologies such as spectroscopic ink analysis, radiocarbon dating of paper, and computer-assisted handwriting analysis to determine the authenticity of contested historical documents (such as the forged Hitler Diaries exposed in 1983 by German federal laboratory analysis). This extension shows that the core principle of source criticism — physical verification of authenticity before trusting content — has not lost its relevance, but has gained more precise technical tools with the evolution of modern forensic and analytical sciences.
