# Phenomenology (Phänomenologie)

Phenomenology is a philosophical movement founded by Edmund Husserl (1859–1938) at the beginning of the twentieth century. Its aim is to describe the structures of experience as they are given to consciousness, without prior metaphysical or scientific assumptions about the world. Its slogan is "to the things themselves". This entry concerns the Husserlian origin and its method; the branches that grew out of it (existential, hermeneutic and somatic) each have their own entry.

## Husserl: biography and foundation

Husserl was born in Prossnitz (Prostějov) in Moravia and studied mathematics in Leipzig, Berlin and Vienna, receiving his doctorate in Vienna (1883) on the calculus of variations. He then studied under Franz Brentano (1884–1886) and turned to philosophy. He published *Philosophy of Arithmetic* (1891), which Gottlob Frege criticized for confusing logic with psychology. Husserl replied with his critique of "psychologism" in the "Prolegomena" to the first volume of the *Logical Investigations* (1900–1901), and then dealt in the second volume with the structures of meaning and intention, so the book was the actual birth of the school. He taught in Göttingen (1901–1916), where students gathered around him, among them Adolf Reinach, Edith Stein and Roman Ingarden, and then in Freiburg (1916–1928), where Heidegger succeeded him in his chair.

## Method

- **Epoché (bracketing)**: suspending the natural attitude in which we take the existence of the external world for granted, not to deny it but to examine how it is presented to us.
- **Phenomenological and transcendental reduction**: the return from things to the acts of consciousness that give things their meaning, as set out in *Ideas* Book I (1913) and in the *Cartesian Meditations* (French translation 1931, German text 1950).
- **Imaginative variation**: varying a thing in imagination in order to discover what cannot be removed from it, that is, its essence.
- **Intentionality**: all consciousness is consciousness of something. He took the idea from Brentano (1874) and made it the axis of his project, distinguishing in it between the act of intending (noesis), its intended content (noema) and the sensory material (hyle).

## Stages of development

In his first stage Husserl turned to describing the essences of meanings and their relation to objects, which attracted the realist supporters in Munich and Göttingen (Pfänder, Scheler and Reinach). Then in *Ideas* he turned the helm toward "transcendental idealism", and many of his early students parted from him. In the *Lectures on the Phenomenology of the Internal Consciousness of Time* (published by Heidegger in 1928) he analyzed the structure of lived time in terms of retention and anticipation. In his last phase he introduced the concept of the "lifeworld" in the *Crisis of the European Sciences* (1936, with part of it published in Belgrade), in which he held that modern natural science forgets the lived ground from which it arose.

## Relation to Logical Positivism

The relation was mostly one of opposition. The Vienna Circle rejected Husserl's claim to intuit essences and his transcendental idealism, and Carnap criticized Heidegger's metaphysics in 1932. Yet Carnap had attended Husserl's seminars in Freiburg in 1924-1925, and the constructional project of his *Logical Structure of the World* (1928) shows traces of Husserl's influence.

## Phenomenology and the Islamic heritage

Contemporary scholars have compared phenomenology with some topics in Islamic philosophy. Ibn Sina's example of the "flying man" presents a self-awareness prior to the senses, and Jari Kaukua studied this tradition in *Self-Awareness in Islamic Philosophy*. Some contrast al-Suhrawardi's "knowledge by presence" with the method of direct description. These are structural comparisons that do not imply historical influence. In the modern Arab sphere Hassan Hanafi employed the phenomenological method in his study of the principles of jurisprudence in his French thesis *Les méthodes d'exégèse* (1965).

## Key figures

- **Edmund Husserl**: the founder, author of the *Logical Investigations*, *Ideas* and the *Crisis of the European Sciences*.
- **Max Scheler** (1874–1928): the phenomenology of values and sympathy, and a material ethics of values.
- **Edith Stein** (1891–1942): philosopher and assistant to Husserl, who wrote on empathy (1917) and was murdered in Auschwitz.
- **Alfred Schutz** (1899–1959): carried the method into sociology in *The Phenomenology of the Social World* (1932).
- **Eugen Fink**, Ingarden and Aron Gurwitsch: heirs and developers of the method.

## The close of his career

After the Nazis took power Husserl, who was of Jewish origin, was excluded from official university life, so the first part of the *Crisis* appeared outside Germany. He died in Freiburg in 1938. Father Herman Leo Van Breda rescued his enormous manuscripts (about forty thousand pages in shorthand) and moved them to Leuven in Belgium, where the Husserl Archives was founded.

## Limits and criticisms

- **The methodological problem of solipsism**: the "transcendental ego" remains the starting point, which makes it difficult to establish the other. Husserl tried in the Fifth Meditation to deal with this through empathy and analogy, but Levinas, Sartre and others considered the solution insufficient.
- **Introspection**: analytic critics and then cognitive scientists doubt the possibility of a neutral description of experience from the first-person perspective, and naturalized phenomenology (Varela, Gallagher and Zahavi) has attempted to respond by linking it to neuroscience experiments.
- **The paradox of origin**: Heidegger and Derrida hold that the reduction presupposes a pure presence of meaning that is never realized, because time and the sign precede consciousness.
- **Difficult terminology** made its influence mostly academic, although its existential extensions reached the general public.
