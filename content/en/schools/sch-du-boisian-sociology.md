# Critical Sociology of Race and Double Consciousness

The project of W. E. B. Du Bois (1868–1963), the first Black American to earn a doctorate from Harvard University and a pioneer of the systematic empirical sociological study of Black American communities.

## The Central Idea

In *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899), Du Bois carried out one of the first comprehensive field social surveys in the history of American sociology, preceding the Chicago School in time. He rejected the biological racist explanations then prevalent for poverty and crime among Black people in favor of a structural-historical explanation (the institutional legacy of slavery and discrimination). In *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), he formulated his most famous concept: "Double Consciousness," the feeling of always seeing yourself through the eyes of the other (the white), measuring your self by the yardstick of a world that looks on you with a mixture of contempt and pity, which produces a divided sense of "two-ness".

## Historical Context

Du Bois was born in Massachusetts in 1868 and studied at Fisk University and then Harvard, where he earned his doctorate in 1895 with a dissertation on the suppression of the Atlantic slave trade. In 1896 he was engaged by the University of Pennsylvania to conduct a comprehensive social survey of Philadelphia's predominantly Black Seventh Ward, a project that yielded *The Philadelphia Negro* (1899). In this survey Du Bois gathered precise field data from some 2,500 households through direct home interviews that he conducted largely by himself, documenting housing, work, education, crime and family life with a statistical detail unusual for its time. From 1897 he taught at Atlanta University, where he directed the annual Atlanta Conferences on the study of Black life, and took part in founding the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) in 1909, editing its magazine *The Crisis* from 1910 to 1934. He thus combined rigorous academic research with direct political activism against racial segregation, a rare combination of the roles of researcher and activist that marked his whole long career.

## Key Works and Concepts

In *The Souls of Black Folk* Du Bois developed the concepts of "Double Consciousness" and the "Color Line." He famously predicted that "the problem of the twentieth century is the problem of the color line," that is, the dividing line between races globally and not within America alone. Du Bois also engaged in a famous public intellectual debate with Booker T. Washington over the strategy for the advancement of Black Americans. Washington called for a focus on vocational education and gradual accommodation to the existing situation, while Du Bois called for full academic education for the "Talented Tenth" of future Black leaders and for a direct political demand for full civil rights without delay. In practice this debate crystallized in the founding by Du Bois and his colleagues of the "Niagara Movement" in 1905, an early Black American organization to demand openly an end to racial segregation and full equality before the law. Four years later it paved the way for the founding of the NAACP itself. Du Bois was a leading intellectual figure in the NAACP until he resigned in 1934 (he returned from 1944 to 1948), despite repeated disagreements with other leaders.

## Impact

Du Bois's work remained marginalized in the traditional historiography of American sociology, which celebrated the Chicago School as its starting point, despite his methodological study preceding it in time. His founding status has been restored in recent decades through a critical review of the history of the field, which acknowledged that his academic marginalization was a direct reflection of racial discrimination in American university circles at the time and not of any scientific weakness in his method. Today he is taught as one of the founders of American empirical sociology alongside the Chicago School, and his concepts have become fundamental in race studies and contemporary postcolonial critique.

## Critique

Some historians have faulted the early Du Bois for relying partly on a classificatory language of moral "classes" within Black society (categories running from the "respectable" to the "deviant"), which reflected some of the moral-reformist assumptions of his era, before he abandoned it in his later, more structural works of explanation. Du Bois himself also shifted late in life toward more radical leftist and internationalist positions, moving to Ghana, where he died in 1963 on the eve of the famous March on Washington. This political shift provoked an academic debate about the continuity or rupture of his intellectual project between its early and late phases. Most contemporary scholars see the thread linking the two phases as his steady commitment to tying rigorous empirical sociological analysis to a clear political project of liberation, refusing to separate "neutral" academic research from open moral commitment to the causes of racial justice.
