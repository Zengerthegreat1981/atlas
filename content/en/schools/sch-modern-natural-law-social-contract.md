# Modern Natural Law and Social Contract Theory

A trunk in legal theory that brings together seventeenth-century philosophers who reformulated natural law apart from the direct Thomist theological foundation (in contrast to the tradition of **Aquinas**), relying instead on **independent human reason** and the social contract as the two foundations of legal obligation. This shift represents a pivotal moment in the history of Western legal philosophy. While ancient and medieval natural law (from Aristotle to Aquinas) remained organically tied to a cosmic or divine teleology, modern natural law in Grotius and his successors came closer to a deductive system starting from human rational nature itself. This paved the way for a later, broader separation between morality and law in positivist philosophy.

## A Jurisprudential Precedent: The Overarching Objectives and Ibn Khaldun's Concept of 'Umran

It is worth recalling that the idea of grounding political and legal association on a systematic rational basis was not foreign to Islamic thought, centuries before Grotius and Hobbes. Al-Shatibi developed in *al-Muwafaqat* the theory of the **overarching objectives of Sharia** (the preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny and property) as a systematic rational basis in light of which partial legislation is understood. In the *Muqaddimah* (1377), Ibn Khaldun offered a socio-political analysis of 'asabiyya, the state and human civilization ('umran), approached from a quite different angle to the question Hobbes would later pose about how human society passes from chaos to order. Ibn Khaldun, however, describes the laws of 'umran with a near-empirical method rather than building a normative contractual theory as Hobbes and Locke did. The decisive difference is that the Islamic tradition continued to frame reason and political society within a transcendent legal teleology, unlike the course of Grotius, Hobbes and Locke toward a humanist foundation of law that gradually dispenses with direct theological conditioning.

## Grotius: Natural Law Apart from Theology

**Hugo Grotius** is often regarded as a foundational figure of this shift. In *On the Law of War and Peace* (1625), he proposes that the basic principles of natural law (such as the duty to keep promises) hold **even if we assume, for the sake of argument, that God does not exist** (*etiamsi daremus*). This separates natural law from its direct divine condition and paves the way for establishing **international law** as an independent field.

## Hobbes: Civil Law from the Womb of the Contract, Not Nature

**Thomas Hobbes**, in *Leviathan* (1651), develops a more pessimistic version. Natural law in the "state of nature" practically fails to guarantee peace, because of the war of all against all. Individuals therefore agree, through a **social contract**, to surrender their natural rights to an absolute sovereign (the Leviathan) who enacts binding **civil law**. This is a shift from natural law as the direct source of obligation to the contract and sovereignty as the actual basis of positive law. Hobbes is often read as a forerunner of later positivist conceptions of law as the command of the sovereign, as in Austin.

## The Essential Difference Between Hobbes and Locke: The Basis and Limits of Sovereignty

The deeper difference between Hobbes and Locke lies in how each conceives of **what individuals actually surrender** in the social contract. For Hobbes, individuals surrender almost all of their natural rights to the absolute sovereign in exchange for security, so subjects have no right to resist sovereignty however unjust it becomes, except within the limits of defending immediate life. For Locke, by contrast, the surrender is limited and conditional. Individuals delegate only the power to enact laws and to execute them fairly, and they retain the right of **resisting government** and revoking the contract if authority violates the condition of protecting basic natural rights. This is the theoretical basis that later thinkers of the American Revolution explicitly drew on, such as Thomas Jefferson in the Declaration of Independence (1776).

## Locke: Property and Law as Protection of Prior Natural Rights

In the *Second Treatise of Government* (published 1689, dated 1690), **John Locke** offers a more optimistic alternative. The state of nature is not necessarily a state of war, and individuals possess natural rights (life, liberty, property) **before** they form political society. The function of civil law and government is to **protect** these prior rights, not to create them from nothing. This framework directly influenced the drafting of later liberal constitutions and the idea of inalienable natural rights.

## Pufendorf and Wolff: A Systematic Organization of the Tradition

**Samuel Pufendorf**, in *On the Law of Nature and of Nations* (1672), continued Grotius's project by linking natural law to the idea of "sociability" (*sociabilitas*) as the original impulse behind the need for binding rules. He went beyond Hobbes's pessimism without reaching Locke's full optimism. **Christian Wolff** then reorganized this entire tradition into a rigorous deductive system influenced by Leibniz's rational method, in *Jus Gentium Methodo Scientifica Pertractatum* (1749), giving natural law an almost geometric form of sequential logical reasoning. This later paved the way for Kant's critical reception of the whole tradition at the end of the eighteenth century.
