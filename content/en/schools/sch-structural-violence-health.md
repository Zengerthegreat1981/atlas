# Structural Violence & Health

A critical branch of medical anthropology, developed by Paul Farmer and Nancy Scheper-Hughes, which links the global distribution of disease and suffering to unequal economic and political structures, rather than to local culture or biology alone.

## Central Idea

This current advances the concept of "structural violence": harm inflicted on people by unjust social, economic and political structures (poverty, colonialism, economic exploitation, the exclusion from health care), with no direct individual agent who can be held directly responsible, in contrast to direct personal violence. It holds that interpretive medical anthropology alone (such as Kleinman's model of explanatory models) may be content to understand how a patient culturally interprets his illness, without questioning the unequal global structures (international debt, neoliberal health policies) that made him vulnerable to the disease and deprived him of treatment in the first place.

## Historical Context

The Norwegian peace researcher Johan Galtung originally coined the term "structural violence" in 1969 in the context of peace studies. Paul Farmer, a physician and medical anthropologist, is the one who carried it deeply into medical anthropology, beginning with his work in Haiti from the early 1980s. There he founded the organisation Partners in Health in 1987 to provide direct medical care alongside anthropological analysis. In parallel, Nancy Scheper-Hughes, working from her research in a shantytown in north-east Brazil, where she first lived in the 1960s, developed a parallel analysis of the relationship between chronic poverty, infant death and the body.

## Major Works and Representatives

In *Pathologies of Power* (2003), Farmer shows that the global distribution of infectious diseases (tuberculosis, AIDS) follows the lines of structural inequality (poverty, international debt, health austerity policies), not pure biological randomness, and that the neglect of treating the poor in Haiti and Peru is not a natural fate but the result of political and economic choices that can be held to account. In *Death Without Weeping* (1992), Scheper-Hughes shows how extreme poverty shapes mothers' responses to repeated infant deaths in a Brazilian shantytown (she calls the town "Bom Jesus da Mata"), including postponed emotional attachment to infants judged unlikely to survive. She argues that maternal attachment is not simply an instinct but is shaped by an economic structure in which the death of infants is a daily recurrence amid hunger and inadequate health care.

## Impact and Criticism

This current had a profound influence in linking medical anthropology to human rights and global health justice. It established the model of "engaged or militant anthropology", which goes beyond neutral academic description to direct work aimed at changing the conditions being studied, as Farmer did in practice through his organisation. The approach has been criticised from within the field on the grounds that an exclusive focus on macro-economic structures may weaken attention to the local cultural meanings of illness, which were the focus of interpretive medical anthropology (Arthur Kleinman), and that the discourse of the "structural victim" may sometimes overlook the agency and resistance of patients themselves.

## Relation to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The framework of structural violence was later applied to health studies in Arab contexts, including studies of siege, war and the restriction of access to health care in Gaza and Iraq, where the distinction between direct violence (bombardment) and structural violence (economic blockade, prevention of the import of medicines) was borrowed to analyse patterns of unequal illness and death.

## Extension of the Concept to Prisons and Migration

The concept of structural violence went beyond the narrow field of health and was later used to analyse mass incarceration in the United States and the experiences of undocumented migrants at the border. Later studies showed that the deprivation of health care inside prisons and detention centres repeats the pattern that Farmer documented in Haiti: systematic harm distributed unequally among the poor, Black and immigrant populations, without a single individual agent who could be directly prosecuted, but the product of policies, laws and institutions accumulated over decades.

## The Concept in a Time of Epidemics

The framework of structural violence proved its usefulness anew during the COVID-19 pandemic, when anthropological and medical studies documented how mortality and infection rates were distributed with sharp inequality between ethnic and economic groups within a single state (as in the United States and Brazil), and between countries of the North and South in the global distribution of vaccines. This confirmed Farmer's original thesis that epidemics do not "discriminate at random", but follow the lines of structural inequality that already exist.
