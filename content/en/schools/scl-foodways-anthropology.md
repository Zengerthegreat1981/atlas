# Foodways Anthropology

Foodways anthropology — the anthropology of food — studies what people eat, how they produce and prepare it, and what food means. Its subject is everything around eating: who grows the food and who cooks it, who eats first and who eats last, which foods are sacred and which are disgusting, and how a meal creates a family, a community, or a nation. The term "foodways" comes from the study of folklore, where it named the whole complex of food habits, and anthropologists adopted it because it captures what the field insists on: food is never only nutrition. Eating is the most intimate way in which the outside world enters the body, and every society surrounds it with rules, meanings, and power. To study food is to study economy, gender, religion, identity, and history at once.

## Origins and History

Anthropologists have always studied food, but the field took shape as a distinct branch in the 1960s and 1970s. Claude Lévi-Strauss made food a subject of theory in The Raw and the Cooked (1964), arguing that cooking is the human act par excellence: the transformation of nature into culture, and that the oppositions of raw and cooked, fresh and rotten, map onto the fundamental categories of a society's thought. Mary Douglas, in her essays on food and purity, showed that food rules — what may be eaten, with whom, and in what order — are statements about social order: the meal is a social system in miniature. Marvin Harris, in Cows, Pigs, Wars, and Witches (1974), offered the materialist counter-argument: food taboos such as the Hindu reverence for the cow or the Jewish and Muslim prohibition of pork have practical, ecological explanations, and the anthropologist's job is to find them.

The field came into its own in the 1980s. Sidney Mintz's Sweetness and Power (1985) became its classic text: a history of sugar that traced the crop from Caribbean slave plantations to the English working-class tea table, showing how a taste for sweetness was created by empire and industry, and how sugar fuelled the Industrial Revolution. Mintz's book made food anthropology a historical and political science, not only a study of the exotic. At the same time, ethnographers turned to the everyday politics of the kitchen: who cooks, who serves, and how the division of food work reflects the division of power between men and women. Carole Counihan's The Anthropology of Food and Body (1999) brought gender and the body to the centre of the field, showing how women's control of food is a source of power and how eating disorders express cultural ideals of the body.

## Key Figures and Works

Lévi-Strauss's The Raw and the Cooked (1964) gave the field its structuralist foundation. Mary Douglas's work on purity and on the structure of the meal gave it a sociology of food rules. Marvin Harris's Cows, Pigs, Wars, and Witches (1974) is the classic statement of the materialist position. Sidney Mintz's Sweetness and Power (1985) remains the field's most influential book, and his later work on the Caribbean and on food and modernity shaped the study of globalization. Jack Goody's Cooking, Cuisine and Class (1982) compared the cuisines of Africa and Eurasia and argued that differences in cooking reflect differences in social hierarchy and the organization of production. Carole Counihan's The Anthropology of Food and Body (1999) made gender central. More recent landmarks include the work of Arjun Appadurai on cookbooks and national cuisine in India, and the ethnographies of the Slow Food movement and of industrial agriculture that study how food systems are being remade by globalization.

## Method

Foodways anthropology uses the full range of ethnographic methods, but it has its own signature techniques. The anthropologist eats with people, learns to cook their food, and records the work of the kitchen — shopping, preparation, serving, and cleaning — as carefully as any ritual. Food diaries and dietary recalls measure what people actually eat, while the ethnography of the meal records who sits where, who is served first, and what is said at the table. The field is also historical: Mintz's method of following a single commodity — sugar, coffee, the potato — through the world system has become a model for studying globalization. More recently, the field has adopted multi-sited ethnography, following food from farms and factories to markets, restaurants, and homes, and sensory ethnography, attending to taste, smell, and the embodied experience of eating. The method is deliberately embodied: the anthropologist's own palate is an instrument of research.

## Influence and Legacies

The anthropology of food has reshaped how the social sciences think about consumption, globalization, and the body. Mintz's commodity history became a model for the study of global capitalism, and the field's work on cuisine and identity — how immigrants keep a cuisine, how nations invent national dishes, how the Slow Food movement resists industrial food — has shaped the study of nationalism and migration. The field's influence extends into public health, where anthropological studies of diet inform nutrition policy, and into the study of agriculture, where research on indigenous farming and seed systems challenges industrial models. It has also given anthropology one of its most successful public faces: food writing, cookbooks, and food television owe much to the field's questions, even when they do not cite them. The field's current frontier is the food system itself: climate change, industrial meat, the politics of hunger, and the question of how eight billion people will be fed without destroying the planet that feeds them.
