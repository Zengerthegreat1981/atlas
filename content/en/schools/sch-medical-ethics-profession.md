# Medical Professional Ethics

Medical professional ethics is concerned with the standards that govern the relationship of the physician to the patient, to the profession, and to society. It treats these standards as a system of specialized professional obligations that goes beyond technical competence to a deep ethical responsibility toward human life and dignity.

## Medicine and Ethics in Islamic Civilization

Classical Islamic medicine saw the early establishment of a complete professional ethical code. Abu Bakr al-Razi (d. c. 925 CE) is credited with a treatise on the physician's conduct, *Akhlaq al-Tabib* (The Ethics of the Physician), which urges kindness toward patients, restraint in exploiting their need, and humility before the limits of medical knowledge, treating the purpose of medicine as service to the human being rather than material gain. In *al-Qanun fi al-Tibb* (The Canon of Medicine), Ibn Sina (d. 1037 CE) systematized medical knowledge and methods of diagnosis and treatment, and his work reflects the expectation of careful, responsible clinical judgment. Ishaq ibn ʿAli al-Ruhawi (9th century) wrote *Adab al-Tabib* (The Etiquette of the Physician), one of the earliest Arabic works devoted to the ethics of the profession. It is known for addressing the physician's conduct, the duty to keep learning, and the physician's dealings with patients and colleagues. This tradition established the principle that medicine is a religious and ethical trust before it is a craft. Modern oaths and codes for Muslim physicians, such as the Islamic Code of Medical Ethics adopted in the 1980s, likewise draw on the Hippocratic tradition while grounding the duties in Islamic belief.

## The Western Historical Context

The oldest foundational text of Western medicine is the Hippocratic tradition (5th-4th century BCE), whose Oath enjoins the physician to benefit patients, avoid harm, keep confidences and refrain from exploiting patients. The oath remained a symbolic point of reference for centuries. The field, however, did not crystallize as a systematic academic discipline until the twentieth century, following shocking research scandals. These included the Tuskegee syphilis study, which continued for decades without the informed consent of African American participants, and the Nuremberg Doctors' Trial, which exposed Nazi medical abuses. The Nuremberg Code (1947), which emerged from that trial, and the later Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 1964) established informed consent as a core requirement of medical research.

## Central Ideas and Core Principles

In their authoritative work *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, Tom Beauchamp and James Childress formulated a framework that became a global standard, known as "principlism" or the "four principles": respect for patient autonomy in making treatment decisions, non-maleficence (avoiding harm), beneficence (acting to benefit the patient), and justice in the distribution of limited health resources. Branching from this framework are sharp debates over informed consent; medical confidentiality and its limits when it conflicts with the safety of others; end-of-life ethics, such as mercy killing and the withdrawal of treatment; and the fairness of organ donation allocation.

## Leading Thinkers

Alongside al-Razi, Ibn Sina, and al-Ruhawi in the Islamic tradition, the leading contemporary figures include Beauchamp and Childress with their principlist approach; Edmund Pellegrino, who (with David Thomasma) defended a virtue-based philosophy of medicine centered on the healing relationship; and Robert Veatch, who advanced a contract model of the physician-patient relationship in contrast to the traditional paternalistic model.

## Impact and Contemporary Application

These debates contributed to the establishment of hospital ethics committees, binding professional codes issued by physicians' associations and organizations such as the World Medical Association, and national legislation regulating informed consent and the protection of patient privacy. A prominent applied example is the debate over allocating scarce mechanical ventilators during the COVID-19 pandemic, which revived the controversy over justice in health allocation in times of scarcity.

## The Tension Between Medical Paternalism and Patient Autonomy

One of the most sensitive tensions in this field is that between the traditional paternalistic model, which gives the physician authority to determine what is in the patient's interest on the basis of technical expertise, and the modern autonomy model, which makes the conscious will of the patient a decisive reference even when it contradicts the medical recommendation. This tension has widened in recent decades with the rise of "evidence-based medicine" on one side and patient rights movements on the other, to the point that obtaining documented informed consent has become a mandatory legal and ethical procedure before any surgical intervention or research participation. The problem becomes more complex with patients who lack decision-making capacity, such as children or people with severe cognitive impairment, where the concept of "substituted judgment" is used, through which guardians try to infer what the patient would have chosen had he been able to express himself. The ethics of allocating rare health resources, such as intensive care beds or very expensive drugs, also raises the question of what criteria of fair priority should apply: should resources go to those in greatest need, to those most likely to recover, or on a "first come, first served" basis? These questions remain alive in every major health crisis that exposes the fragility of the balance between medical competence and ethical justice.
