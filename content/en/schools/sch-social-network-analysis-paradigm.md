# Sociology of Social Networks and Structural Analysis

A research programme that shifted the unit of sociological analysis from the "individual" or the "group" to the "tie" itself, offering a mathematical-structural method for studying how social networks form and shape individual outcomes.

## Early Origins

This research programme was preceded by early roots in "sociometry", developed by the social psychologist Jacob Moreno in the 1930s, when he drew visual diagrams of the relations of attraction and repulsion among members of small groups (school classes, correctional institutions). The British anthropologists John Barnes (who studied a Norwegian island parish in 1954) and Clyde Mitchell (who studied urban networks in Central Africa) and others of the Manchester school later developed the idea in the 1950s and 1960s, before it crystallized fully as an integrated quantitative sociological method in the work of Granovetter and Wellman in the 1970s.

## The Central Idea

In his pivotal article "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973), Mark Granovetter presented a counterintuitive finding: "weak" ties (passing acquaintances, not close friends) are more useful than "strong" ties (close friends, family) for obtaining new information (such as job opportunities), because strong ties tend to be closed within similar social circles that repeat the same information, whereas weak ties reach distant social circles carrying entirely different information. Barry Wellman later developed "Structural Analysis" as a general sociological method that rejects explaining social action by the attributes of isolated individuals (income, education, personality) in favour of explaining it by the structure of their actual relations within a wider social network: who knows whom, who is connected to whom, and how some mediate between others.

## The Empirical Study of Getting a Job

Granovetter tested his thesis empirically in his field study *Getting a Job* (1974), in which he interviewed professional, technical and managerial workers in Newton, Massachusetts. Among those who found their jobs through personal contacts, most reported seeing the contact only occasionally or rarely, and few saw the contact often. Granovetter read this as support for the argument that useful new information flows through weak "bridges" linking distant social circles, though the study was small and did not directly measure tie strength beyond contact frequency.

## Economic Embeddedness

Granovetter later developed an influential parallel concept, "embeddedness". He argued that economic action (commercial transactions, hiring decisions, commercial trust) is not abstract rational action isolated from social context, as abstract neoclassical economic theory assumes, but is always "embedded" within concrete networks of actual social relations that determine who trusts whom and who deals with whom, so that economic behaviour cannot be fully understood in isolation from the surrounding social network structure.

## Wellman's Structural Method

Barry Wellman championed structural analysis as a methodological stance: he rejected explaining any social behaviour by reference to the "attributes" of isolated individuals (such as income, level of education or even "personality") as sufficient independent variables, insisting that the individual's position within the structure of his or her actual social network (centrality, density of ties, diversity of circles) is the deeper explanatory variable. He applied this method in his well-known study *The Community Question* (1979), which showed that the residents of the urban area of Toronto had not lost their intimate networks of social support, as pessimistic modernization theories had predicted, but had reorganized them geographically across wider distances (the "Liberated Community") instead of relying on the narrow traditional spatial neighbourhood alone.

## Mathematical Tools

Social network analysis borrowed tools from mathematical graph theory: a network is represented as a set of "nodes" (representing individuals or organizations) linked by "edges" (representing relations), and precise quantitative properties are measured, such as "centrality" (the importance of a node in the network), "density" (the ratio of actual ties to those theoretically possible), and "betweenness" (the extent to which a node lies on the shortest path between two other nodes, a formal measure of brokerage that is related to, though not identical with, Granovetter's idea of bridges). These precise quantitative tools made it possible to turn network analysis from a general figurative metaphor into a rigorous empirical measurement method that can be compared across different cases.

## Impact

Social network analysis became an independent quantitative method in its own right, extending across sociology, economics and computer science, and it later formed the direct theoretical foundation for understanding the structure of digital networks and social media. Its concepts (weak ties, embeddedness, network centrality) became standard analytical tools in the study of contemporary digital networks, recommendation algorithms and the viral spread of information across the internet, so that these classical concepts became part of the basic intellectual structure of modern data science itself and of the analysis of today's large digital networks such as the major social media platforms around the world.
