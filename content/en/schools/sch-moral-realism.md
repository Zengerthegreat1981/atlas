# Moral Realism

Moral realism is a metaethical position holding that there are objective moral facts that are independent of the opinions and feelings of individuals and of the customs of societies. On this view a moral proposition such as "torture is wrong" is true or false regardless of who believes it, just as mathematical or physical propositions are.

## Roots in Islamic Theology (Kalam)

Islamic theology (*ʿilm al-kalam*) raised the question of moral realism at an early stage and with precision, through the issue of rational good and evil (*al-tahsin wa-l-taqbih al-ʿaqliyyan*). The Mu'tazila, whose leading later theologian was Qadi ʿAbd al-Jabbar (d. 1025 CE), held that good and evil are intrinsic properties of actions themselves, perceived by reason independently of revelation, and that God commands what is good because it is good, not the reverse. This is an explicitly realist and rationalist moral position. The Ash'ari school, named for Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (d. 935 CE), held in contrast that good and evil are established only through revelation: whatever God commands is good because He commands it. This position is closer to divine voluntarism than to natural realism. Muslim philosophers in the Aristotelian tradition also discussed the relation between reason and revelation in ethics. The Maturidi school, for its part, took an intermediate position, granting reason the ability to perceive the goodness or badness of some actions while the full binding force of religious law depends on revelation.

## The Western Historical Context

In Western philosophy, the roots of moral realism go back to Plato's theory of the Forms, which made "the Good" an objective ideal existing in its own right, and continue through Aristotle's naturalism, which linked the good to the flourishing of human nature. The realist position receded in modernity before the rise of Humean empiricism, which doubted the possibility of deriving an "ought" from an "is." It then receded before twentieth-century logical positivist non-realism, which regarded moral propositions as mere expressions of emotion. Realism revived in analytic philosophy from the 1970s and 1980s onward, notably with the "Cornell realists" (Richard Boyd, Nicholas Sturgeon, David Brink).

## Core Ideas and Subpositions

Contemporary realism divides into two main currents. Naturalist realism holds that moral properties are natural or social properties (or are constituted by them), such as the flourishing of a living being or the satisfaction of human needs; some naturalists are reductive and others, such as the Cornell realists, are non-reductive. Non-naturalist realism holds that moral properties are real and objective but cannot be reduced to natural properties; they are more like "primitive," independent properties. Among the most important challenges facing the realist position are the argument from widespread moral disagreement across cultures and the argument from queerness, put forward by J. L. Mackie, which holds that positing objective moral facts commits one to strange entities that do not fit our scientific picture of the world.

## Leading Thinkers

Among the most prominent contemporary realists are Derek Parfit, who in *On What Matters* defended a non-naturalist realism in which the moral conclusions of the major theories converge; David Brink, who developed a naturalist realism in *Moral Realism and the Foundations of Ethics* (1989); and Russ Shafer-Landau, who defended non-naturalist realism in his book *Moral Realism: A Defence* (2003). On the opposing side, J. L. Mackie (an error theorist) and Simon Blackburn (a quasi-realist expressivist) are prominent critics.

## Impact and Contemporary Application

Moral realism remains a central question in justifying international human rights discourse, which presupposes that acts such as genocide and torture are objectively wrong, regardless of whether some political regimes acknowledge this. As an illustration, the moral realist holds that a society's denial of the wrongness of slavery does not make slavery morally acceptable, just as a group's denial that the earth is round does not change its astronomical reality.

## The Problem of Moral Knowledge and Its Solutions

The moral realist faces a pressing epistemological question: how can the human mind perceive objective moral facts if these facts are immaterial and not observable through the senses? Non-naturalist realists have proposed several solutions, the most prominent being the idea of "rational intuition," by which one grasps the truth of some basic moral propositions directly, without inference, in the way we grasp logical axioms such as the principle of non-contradiction. Naturalist realists avoid this problem by linking moral facts to natural properties that can be accessed through ordinary cognitive methods, such as the scientific observation of the effects of actions on the flourishing of living beings. A notable approach in this direction is the Cornell realism of Richard Boyd, Nicholas Sturgeon and David Brink. It holds that moral properties are natural properties that figure in good causal and explanatory theories, in something like the way "health" is constituted by sound physiological functioning, while denying that moral terms can be defined in non-moral vocabulary. This epistemological question remains a major obstacle, and contemporary philosophers continue to debate it without a final resolution.

## Realism in Practical Life

Adopting moral realism has a concrete practical effect on how we understand moral disagreement. If there is a single objective truth, then a disagreement between two opposing parties necessarily means that at least one of them is mistaken, just as in scientific disagreement. This opens the way to the possibility of genuine moral progress in history, rather than mere change in tastes and customs. Opponents of realism, by contrast, argue that this view carries the risk of intellectual dogmatism, when the holder of an opinion believes that he alone possesses the absolute truth. Some contemporary realists have sought to mitigate this risk by distinguishing between metaphysical confidence in the existence of an objective truth and epistemic humility about our limited capacity to reach it with complete certainty. This allows one to combine belief in the objectivity of ethics with openness to criticism and continual revision.
