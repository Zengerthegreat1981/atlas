# Decolonial Philosophy (Global Umbrella)

A philosophical umbrella broader than the Latin American "Modernity/Coloniality" program, comprising voices from Africa, Asia, the Arab world, the Black diaspora, and the indigenous peoples of the Americas and Oceania. They share an assumption that Western modernity and the supposedly "universal" knowledge arose entangled with colonialism, slavery, and empire, and that real liberation requires questioning the very structures of knowledge, from curricula to the language of instruction and publication standards, and not political regimes alone. Its influence extends from African liberation writings to the Arab critique of Orientalism and of institutional practices in universities.

## Voices from Africa

- **Frantz Fanon** (1925–1961): in *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) he analyzed the psychological and existential effects of colonial violence alongside its political ones, and called for a liberation that does not replicate the European model.
- **Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o** (born 1938): in *Decolonising the Mind* (1986) he held that imposing the colonizer's language in education is a colonization of the mind, and he decided to write in Gikuyu.
- **Kwasi Wiredu** (1931–2022) and **Paulin Hountondji** (1942–2024): called for the "conceptual decolonization" of African philosophy and for "endogenous" knowledge based on the questions of local societies.
- **Mogobe Ramose**: presented "African philosophy through ubuntu" (1999) as a basis for understanding the person in relation to the other: "a human being is a human being through other human beings", or "I am because we are".
- **Achille Mbembe**: in *On the Postcolony* (2001) he analyzes the persistence of colonial structures of rule and violence in independent states.
- **Sabelo Ndlovu-Gatsheni**: in *Coloniality of Power in Postcolonial Africa* (2013) he describes coloniality as a continuing condition.

## Voices from the Arab and Islamic World

Modern Arab thought did not wait for this term to criticize epistemic colonialism. Malek Bennabi (1905–1973), in his books *The Conditions of Renaissance* (1948) and *Vocation de l'Islam* (1954), described what he called "colonizability", that is, the internal readiness that enables the colonizer to dominate, and he called for freeing the Muslim mind from alienation as from occupation. Edward Said wrote *Orientalism* (1978), showing how Western knowledge produced a subordinate "Orient", and before him Anouar Abdel-Malek analyzed the same matter in "Orientalism in Crisis" (1963). Hassan Hanafi called for a "science of Occidentalism" so that the West becomes the object of study, Abdallah Laroui contributed to the critique of contemporary Arab ideology, Taha Abderrahmane was concerned with criticizing dependence on transplanted concepts, and Abdelkebir Khatibi reflected on the "double critique" of both the West and the heritage. These joined a common path with decolonial thought, though they did not adopt the name.

## Voices from Asia and the Diaspora

- **Gandhi**: in *Hind Swaraj* (1909) he saw modern civilization itself as a disease, and called for self-sufficiency.
- **Ashis Nandy**: in *The Intimate Enemy* (1983) he studied how the colonized internalize the logic of the colonizer.
- **Kuan-Hsing Chen**: in *Asia as Method* (2010) he called for using Asian experiences as a reference for interpreting one another, not by comparison with the West.
- **Sylvia Wynter** (1928–2024): in "Unsettling the Coloniality of Being/Power/Truth/Freedom" (2003) she tied modernity to the definition of the "human" in the image of the white Western man.
- **Linda Tuhiwai Smith**: in *Decolonizing Methodologies* (1999) she criticized Western research on the Maori and other peoples as an extension of epistemic plunder.
- **Boaventura de Sousa Santos**: in *Epistemologies of the South* (2014) he spoke of "epistemicide" and the need for "cognitive plurality".

## Central Concepts

Coloniality in its three dimensions, namely power, knowledge, and being; Mignolo's colonial difference; border thinking; epistemicide; the pluriverse; and then "decolonizing methodologies". All agree that ending direct colonial rule did not end relations of domination. "Ubuntu" for Africans, "Pachamama" for Andeans, and the "Dao" in Chinese thought represent resources for thinking beyond the Cartesian dualism of subject and object and of human and nature.

## Institutional Practice

The debate moved to universities: in March 2015 the "Rhodes Must Fall" movement at the University of Cape Town began with the removal of the statue of Cecil Rhodes, then branched out to other universities and raised the problem of "decolonizing the curriculum" in philosophy, history, and the sciences. In London and elsewhere, calls arose to broaden syllabi with non-European texts and to return looted museum holdings.

## Limits and Criticism

- Critics warn of an "inverted essentialism" that sets a non-European essentialism against a European one, thereby establishing the same sharp "West/non-West" dualism.
- Eve Tuck and K. Wayne Yang wrote in "Decolonization Is Not a Metaphor" (2012) that transferring the language of "decolonization" to the sphere of culture and curricula without the recovery of land empties the concept of its material content for indigenous peoples.
- It is sometimes hard in practice to separate decoloniality from postcolonialism and Marxism.
- Some hold that the use of highly academic terminology isolates the field from actual liberation movements.
- The question of language remains present, since most of the debate is written in English.
