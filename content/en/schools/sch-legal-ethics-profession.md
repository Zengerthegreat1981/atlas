# Legal Professional Ethics

## Historical Context: The Judicial Conduct Literature in Islamic Jurisprudence

Judicial ethics is among the earliest fields to be recorded in Islamic legal thought. From the first centuries, jurists devoted independent works to it under the title *adab al-qadi* ("the conduct of the judge"). Among the best known is the *Adab al-Qadi* of the Shafi'i jurist Abu al-Hasan al-Mawardi (d. 450 AH / 1058 CE), which sets out the conditions for holding judicial office (knowledge, integrity, soundness of the senses) and the etiquette of sitting to judge, and insists on equality between litigants even in the judge's glance and smile, so as to avoid any apparent preference for one of the parties. These norms drew on the letter traditionally attributed to the caliph Umar ibn al-Khattab to his judge Abu Musa al-Ash'ari (its authenticity is debated), often regarded as an early charter of judicial ethics in Islamic history. It is reported to contain principles of "understanding" the claim, "equality" between the litigants, not being swayed by anger, impatience, or desire while judging, and the rule that a judge should review his own judgment if he later realizes that an earlier error was made. Jurists also addressed the ethics of the *wakil* (agent) and the advocate (the representative of a litigant) under the chapter on agency, affirming the impermissibility of concealing a right or defending someone known to be wronged. In the West, the roots of modern legal ethics go back to the organization of the barristers' associations (Inns of Court) in England in the Middle Ages. In the twentieth century they culminated in the issuance of formal codes such as the American Bar Association's *Canons of Professional Ethics* (1908) and, later, its *Model Rules of Professional Conduct* (1983).

## Central Ideas: The Tension between Loyalty to the Client and Loyalty to Justice

The core of legal professional ethics revolves around a structural tension between two duties: the lawyer's duty to advocate zealously for the interests of the client (Zealous Advocacy), whatever they may be, and the lawyer's duty as an "officer of the court" (Officer of the Court) to serve justice and the integrity of the judicial system. On the one hand, the lawyer is expected to exert maximum legal effort on the client's behalf even if personally believing the client to be guilty, on the principle that every accused person deserves a full defense in order to guarantee due process (Due Process), not to secure their actual acquittal. On the other hand, this zeal is bounded by strict limits: the prohibition on presenting testimony the lawyer knows to be false (Suborning Perjury); the duty of confidentiality and the evidentiary attorney-client privilege (Attorney-Client Privilege), which protect the client's communications with their lawyer but are subject to exceptions, such as the crime-fraud exception for communications used to further future crimes or frauds; rules on conflicts of interest that prevent representing two opposing parties; and the duty of candor toward the tribunal (Candor Toward the Tribunal), which takes precedence over the client's interest. In addition, there is the responsibility of "access to justice" (Access to Justice): many bar associations require their members to perform hours of voluntary work (Pro Bono) for those who cannot afford litigation fees.

## Key Thinkers and Reference Points

Al-Mawardi is a foundational reference in Islamic jurisprudence for formulating the conditions and method of the just judge, while Ibn al-Qayyim al-Jawziyya, in *I'lam al-Muwaqqi'in*, offered a profound critique of some jurists' and muftis' circumvention of the objectives of the Shari'a, establishing the concept of "forbidden stratagems" (*al-hiyal al-muharrama*), which resembles contemporary debates about lawyers exploiting procedural loopholes. In modern Western thought, Monroe Freedman, in his book *Lawyers' Ethics in an Adversary System* (1975), advanced the boldest defense of "absolute zeal" on behalf of the client, even in difficult criminal cases, a position that provoked wide debate lasting for decades. By contrast, Richard Wasserstrom (in "Lawyers as Professionals: Some Moral Issues," 1975) and William Simon (in *The Practice of Justice*, 1998) criticized this role-differentiated, morally neutral model (the "amoral advocate") and argued that lawyers remain responsible for the justice of the ends they serve, including by declining tactics that they regard as morally unjust even if they are formally legal.

## Practical Test Cases

Typical cases are studied to illustrate these dilemmas. In one, a defense lawyer learns from the client, in a communication protected by privilege, the location of the body of a missing victim, and faces a conflict between the obligation of absolute confidentiality and the suffering of the victim's family, who do not know what has become of their daughter. Closely related facts arose in *People v. Belge* (New York, 1975), where the court sided with protecting the lawyers' confidentiality, emphasizing the trust between lawyer and client on which the adversary system depends; the case remains controversial and is widely taught. In another case, a lawyer discovers that a witness gave false testimony in favor of the lawyer's client without the lawyer's prior knowledge. Under the model rules, the lawyer is required to take remedial steps that may reach the point of informing the tribunal if the witness cannot be persuaded to correct the testimony, even if this harms the client.

## Ethics of the Public Prosecution and State Responsibility

Public prosecutors face a different dilemma from defense lawyers. They do not represent a private party but represent "justice" itself as agents of the state, which imposes a higher ethical standard on them. Their duty is not to secure the largest possible number of convictions, but to ensure that justice proceeds correctly, which includes the mandatory disclosure of any evidence that exonerates the accused (the *Brady* duty, from *Brady v. Maryland*, 1963), even if it weakens the prosecution's case. Numerous studies have documented wrongful convictions caused by prosecutors concealing exculpatory evidence in pursuit of a record of convictions. This is among the gravest ethical violations in the legal profession, because it strikes at the core of trust in the entire judicial system.

## Regulation of the Profession and Mechanisms of Accountability

In most countries, the legal profession is subject to self-regulatory bodies (bar associations and disciplinary councils) that have the power to impose sanctions ranging from reprimand to suspension of the license or striking the name from the register of licensed lawyers. This distinguishes the profession from many others that are subject to direct government supervision. This self-regulatory structure generates continuing debate about its effectiveness compared with independent external oversight, since it is feared that bar associations may tend to protect the collective reputation of the profession at the expense of holding individual violators accountable with sufficient rigor.
