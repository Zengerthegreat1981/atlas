# Marginal Revolution

**The marginal revolution** (anticipated in part by Hermann Heinrich Gossen in 1854) is the label historians of economic thought give to a radical methodological shift that took place between 1871 and 1874, when three economists — the Englishman **William Stanley Jevons**, the Austrian **Carl Menger**, and the French-born **Léon Walras**, who worked in Switzerland — published works, developed largely independently of one another, that share one central idea: the value of a good is not determined by the quantity of labour needed to produce it, as the classical economists held, but by the **marginal utility** that the last additional unit yields to the consumer.

## From the value of labour to marginal utility

The classical school — Smith, Ricardo and, after them, Marx — had built its theory of value around **labour** as the objective source of exchange value. The marginal revolution shifted this axis entirely toward subjectivity: the value of a thing lies not in the thing itself but in the marginal satisfaction it gives a particular individual at a particular moment, and this satisfaction diminishes with each additional unit the individual obtains. This is what became known as the law of **diminishing marginal utility**. The shift explained a puzzle that had puzzled the classical economists: why is the price of water, which is essential for life, so low, while the price of diamonds, a luxury, is so high? The marginal answer is that water's abundance keeps the utility of an additional unit of it low, while the scarcity of diamonds keeps the marginal utility of a unit of them high.

## Three independent paths to one idea

In *The Theory of Political Economy* (1871), Jevons set out an early mathematical formulation of the principle of marginal utility using the calculus, influenced by an English utilitarian tradition that goes back to Bentham. Menger, in *Principles of Economics* (1871), put forward the same principle in qualitative, non-mathematical language, founding what would later become the **Austrian School**, and linked marginal utility to the individual's ranking of needs according to subjective priorities. Walras, in *Elements of Pure Economics* (1874), used marginal utility to build a broader mathematical model describing how all markets in a whole economy reach equilibrium at the same time — a line of work that would later branch into an independent school in general equilibrium theory.

## The Austrian extension: Böhm-Bawerk and capital theory

Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk, a leading follower of Menger, developed the principle of marginal utility into a theory of capital and interest. He held that interest reflects individuals' time preference — their preference for present consumption over deferred consumption — rather than the exploitation of workers, as Marx had argued. This line of thought would later fuel sharp debates between Austrian and Marxist economics over the nature of profit and interest.

## The American extension: Clark and marginal productivity

In the United States, John Bates Clark applied marginal logic to the market for factors of production. He proposed that in a competitive market a worker's wage tends to equal the **marginal product** of the last unit of labour employed. This theory would become a normative pillar for explaining the distribution of income among wages, profits and rent in later neoclassical economics.

## The break with classical political economy

The marginal revolution was a systematic break deeper than merely replacing one theory of value with another. It shifted the unit of analysis from the great social classes (landowners, capitalists and workers) that dominated the thinking of Smith, Ricardo and Marx, to the **individual consumer maximizing his utility**, drawing on mathematical tools (calculus in particular) that had not been central to classical writing. This shift in the unit of analysis paved the way for the gradual change in the name of the field from "political economy" to "Economics", stripped of an explicit political qualifier.

## The double legacy: Marshall and Walras

The legacy of the marginal revolution split into two main lines that developed out of it: a British Marshallian line that focused on **partial equilibrium** in a single market, relatively isolated from the rest of the economy, and a Walrasian line that focused on the **general equilibrium** of all markets together in a single, integrated mathematical system. These two lines would evolve into two relatively distinct schools within the broader neoclassical economics.

## The effect of mathematics on the field's language

The marginal revolution required mathematical tools that had not been central to the descriptive prose of Smith and Ricardo, above all the differential calculus. The definition of "marginal utility" is itself the first derivative of the total utility function with respect to the quantity consumed, and the definition of "equilibrium" is the point at which these derivatives are equalized across the consumption alternatives available to the individual. This symbolic shift at the time drew objections from historical and institutional economists, who saw the mathematical formulation as excessive abstraction that strips economic analysis of its concrete institutional and historical context. The methodological dispute continued to echo in later debates between mathematical economics and alternative institutional and historical approaches.
