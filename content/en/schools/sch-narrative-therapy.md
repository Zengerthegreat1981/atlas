# Narrative Therapy

A constructivist and postmodern therapeutic approach developed from the 1980s by Michael White in Adelaide, Australia, and David Epston in Auckland, New Zealand. It starts from the idea that **identity has no fixed essence but is built through the narratives** that individuals tell about themselves, and that **"problems" are not attributes of the person but dominant stories** that can be re-authored. It is classed among the systemic family therapies, with applied extensions in work with children, families, trauma, and community settings.

## Historical Context

Narrative therapy emerged in an intellectual setting contemporary with the brief family-therapy techniques of the Mental Research Institute (MRI) in Palo Alto, California (Paul Watzlawick and Richard Fisch), and with the social constructionist currents embraced in the United States by Kenneth Gergen. White and Epston added two essential elements: (1) philosophical and anthropological roots in Foucault (power and discourse), Gregory Bateson, Jerome Bruner (narrative), and, later in White's work, Jacques Derrida; (2) an orientation toward **"collaborative work"** with the client as author and editor of his or her own story. The foundational book *Narrative Means to Therapeutic Ends* (1990) gathered their developments of the preceding decade. White died in 2008; his work continues through the Dulwich Centre in Adelaide, which he co-founded, and through David Epston and others in New Zealand and internationally.

## Core Theoretical Concepts

### Dominant Stories and Alternative Stories
Narrative therapy holds that every individual carries **dominant narratives** about himself or herself, shaped by cultural, social, and relational discourses. These stories restrict what the individual sees as possible. For example, a person who struggles with failure in romantic relationships may carry a dominant story of "I am unworthy of love." The problem is not a "truth" but **"a frame for reading events"**, and this is where the therapeutic work lies.

### Externalization: A Pivotal Technique
The most important and most innovative technique of narrative therapy: **externalizing conversations**, that is, separating the "problem" from the person's identity. Instead of saying "you are depressed," one says "depression is visiting you" or "it is a heavy guest in your life." The result: the individual becomes **an agent against the problem** rather than **identified with it**.

### Unique Outcomes
Every dominant story (however negative) **does not explain all the events of life**. There always remain "unique outcomes": moments when the individual departed from the dominant story. The therapist's task: **to search for these moments** and amplify them in order to rewrite the story.

### The Absent but Implicit
A concept developed by White, drawing on Derrida: when individuals tell their story, there are always **absent implicit values** (concepts the individual honors that have not been stated explicitly). The therapist's task: to draw out these values to enrich the new story.

### The Therapist's Position
Influenced by Foucault's analysis of power, narrative therapists question the idea of the therapist as neutral expert. White described a "de-centered but influential" stance: the client is the primary authority on his or her life, while the therapist takes responsibility for the questions asked.

## Core Clinical Techniques

### Externalizing Conversations
A pivotal technique: questions that help the client name the problem, map its behavior, assess its effects, and assess the individual's effect on it. Example: "When is depression at the height of its power?" "What works against it?"

### Re-authoring and Amplifying Unique Outcomes
The client recounts particular moments that broke the dominant story. The therapist asks **scaffolding questions** about these moments: "How were you able to do that?" "What does that say about you?" "If I asked a friend about you at that moment, what would they say?"

### Therapy Documents
A unique technique: writing **letters, certificates, and documents** that summarize the therapeutic transformation. The therapist sends them to the client between sessions, and sometimes outside witnesses sign them. These documents **embody the new story** and allow it to be shared.

### Rituals and Audience (Outsider Witness Practice)
Drawing on Barbara Myerhoff's work on definitional ceremonies, White developed practices in which a client's story is told in front of an audience of invited outsider witnesses (for example other people or family members). The witnesses respond in a structured way, saying what caught their attention, what images it evoked, what it echoed in their own lives, and how they were moved, rather than evaluating or advising.

### Certificates (Certificate/Celebration of Achievement)
Symbolic documents given to the client on passing therapeutic stages, to serve as a witness to the "stories of transformation."

## Applications

- **Work with children**: especially externalization (the child says "the worry monster sits on my shoulder," and together they design ways of dealing with it).
- **Trauma and abuse**: working with the stories of shame and self-blame that can follow abuse, with attention to the person's own responses and resistance.
- **Eating disorders**: externalizing "anorexia" as a cultural force and exploring how the individual resists it (the Anti-Anorexia/Anti-Bulimia League, developed by Epston and colleagues).
- **Communities**: collective narrative practices, developed through the Dulwich Centre with communities affected by trauma and injustice, including Aboriginal communities in Australia.

## Evidence Base

Narrative therapy is harder to study with standard trial designs because of its emphasis on individual meaning and its suspicion of standardized measurement. The research base is therefore smaller than for manualized therapies:

- A study of narrative therapy for adults with major depressive disorder (Vromans & Schweitzer, 2011) reported improvements in depressive symptoms in a small uncontrolled study.
- Much of the literature consists of qualitative studies and case reports, and there are few randomized trials comparing it with CBT. The atlas therefore rates the evidence at the "probably efficacious" level at most.
- There is an ongoing debate between supporters, who regard standardization as at odds with the approach, and critics, who regard the limited evidence as a weakness.

## Relation to Neighboring Schools

- **Systemic family therapy**: one of its deepest branches. It shares the interest in relationships and social context, but differs through its postmodern reading and its focus on stories and discourse.
- **Post-structuralism and constructivism**: the philosophical root. Foucault, Derrida, Bruner, Gergen.
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)**: it differs in philosophical foundations (narrative rather than cognitive) but converges on a practical focus on stories and interpretations.
- **Solution-focused therapy (SFT)**: both emphasize clients' strengths and preferred futures (compare unique outcomes with exceptions), but narrative therapy gives more weight to cultural discourse and the history of the problem story.
- **Multicultural therapy**: integration with the narratives of indigenous and marginalized peoples.

## Criticism and Limits

- **The problem of power**: despite the rhetoric of "empowerment," narrative therapy has been criticized for possibly **monopolizing its interpretation of stories** through techniques that appear neutral but carry the authority of the expert therapist.
- **Limited documentation**: few standardized randomized controlled trials.
- **Application with severe cases**: its effectiveness in psychosis or severe addiction is less well documented.
- **Practical concession**: it may be harder to deliver in settings with very short contacts.

## Legacy and Influence

The narrative approach has influenced: (1) **community work** through the Dulwich Centre's collective narrative practices; (2) **work with marginalized communities**, including refugees and indigenous peoples; (3) **the framing of trauma** in terms of the person's responses and values rather than only symptoms; (4) wider **narrative approaches in counseling, education, and the helping professions**.
