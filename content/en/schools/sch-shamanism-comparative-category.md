# Shamanism as a Comparative Category and Its Critique

An analytic term that gathers the debate in comparative religious studies over the concept of "shamanism." Is it an analytic category valid for classifying religious-healing practices that are structurally similar across distant cultures (Siberia, the Americas, Central Asia and Africa), or a modern European construct that imposes an artificial similarity on diverse local practices with no real structural link between them? The subject is treated here as the history of an academic category, not as a presentation of the practice or an endorsement of it.

## The Origin of the Term

The word is borrowed from the Tungusic languages of Siberia (šaman). It passed into European languages through Russian and Dutch travelers in the seventeenth century, and was then generalized to practices that are geographically distant. This is the linguistic basis on which the debate about the comparative category rests.

## The Foundational Position: Eliade

Mircea Eliade, in *Le Chamanisme et les techniques archaïques de l'extase* (1951), offered the most influential academic formulation of the concept. He defined the shaman as an "Ecstasy Specialist" who undertakes spiritual journeys to other worlds, celestial or subterranean, for healing or for mediation between his community and spiritual powers. He distinguished between the shaman who "ascends" by his own will and spirit, and the possessed person in whom spirits dwell. He held that the first structure recurs across distant cultures, which justifies treating it as a single comparative category.

## Possessed Persons and Priests in the Arab Heritage: The Limits of Comparison

The Arab-Islamic heritage offers a useful test of the category. In pre-Islamic Arabia, the Arabs knew the *kahin* (priest or soothsayer) and the *arrafa* (diviner), the former speaking in inspired rhymed prose (*saj'*) and traditionally said to be aided by a familiar spirit (jinn). Toufic Fahd, in *La Divination arabe* (Brill, 1966), collected their images and rituals and investigated the ecstasy and mediation they involved. After Islam, spirit rituals continued in Muslim lands: the *zar* in Egypt, Sudan and Ethiopia, in which spirits are summoned through music and dance to treat patients. Janice Boddy studied these in *Wombs and Alien Spirits* (University of Wisconsin Press, 1989). Some argue that Eliade excludes these cases because they are "possession" rather than "ascent." I. M. Lewis, in *Ecstatic Religion* (Penguin, 1971), treated shamanism and spirit possession as related phenomena on a single continuum. It thus becomes clear that the breadth or narrowness of the category depends on the boundary the researcher draws, not on a decisive field datum.

## Critique of the Category

Later scholars, most prominently Alice Kehoe in *Shamans and Religion: An Anthropological Exploration in Critical Thinking* (Waveland Press, 2000), criticized this generalization. They argued that it: first, projects a specific Siberian model onto practices that do not resemble it in their details; second, neglects the historical and political differences between contexts, since European colonization of the Americas and Siberia shaped how these practices were documented and described from outside; third, reproduces an "evolutionary-primitive" view that sees shamanism as an earlier stage of religion preceding "developed" religions. This last view parallels criticisms directed at Eliade's comparative method in general. Jane Monnig Atkinson, in her review (*Annual Review of Anthropology*, 1992), added that the "shamanisms" in the literature are multiple and diverse. Andrei Znamenski, in *The Beauty of the Primitive: Shamanism and the Western Imagination* (Oxford University Press, 2007), showed how Western imagination, from Romanticism to the New Age movement, shaped the "shaman" into a model of authentic spirituality.

## New Shamanism and the Limits of Defending the Category

In the 1980s, "Core Shamanism" emerged through Michael Harner in *The Way of the Shaman* (Harper & Row, 1980). It stripped the techniques of the journey from their cultural contexts and presented them as general training. Critics of the category see this as evidence that "shamanism" is the product of academic classification followed by marketing, rather than an ancient unified tradition. On the other hand, scholars such as Michael Winkelman defended the category on the basis of shared neurological and psychological foundations in altered states of consciousness. A middle argument remains: the category is useful as a temporary comparative tool if it is presented as a set of "family resemblances" rather than as a single essence.

## Where the Debate Sits in the Field

This debate is a miniature model of a broader tension in comparative religious studies, between the "comprehensive" approach that seeks common patterns across cultures and the "contextual" approach that insists on the historical particularity of each local practice. It is the same tension found in Jonathan Z. Smith's critique of Eliade. It branches into regional studies such as Siberian shamanism, Korean shamanism (*musok*) and the religions of Native Americans.
