# French Methodical School (Seignobos and Langlois)

An explicit French methodological codification of the rules of historical research, crystallized in the book *Introduction to the Study of History* (*Introduction aux études historiques*, 1898) by **Charles-Victor Langlois** and **Charles Seignobos**. It was an influential and widely adopted "methodological manual" for training professional historians in France, and it shaped the formation of university historians there for decades.

## Method

- **Strict hierarchy of the research process**: the book divides the historian's work into clear successive steps: (1) the search for documents (Heuristique), (2) external criticism (verifying the document's authenticity, date, and author), (3) internal criticism (analyzing its content and credibility), and (4) synthesis and the final historical presentation.
- **The essentiality of the written document**: the book holds that "there is no history without documents" (*Pas de documents, pas d'histoire*), a definition of the legitimate historical source that almost confines itself to the official written document (administrative, legal, diplomatic), implicitly excluding other sources such as material culture, economic statistics, or oral testimony.
- **Craft, not philosophy**: like the Rankean school that inspired it, the book avoids any comprehensive philosophical theory about the meaning of history, and presents itself as a "manual of the craft" (a technical discipline), not a philosophical doctrine.

## Influence and Decline

This model dominated French academic training until the 1930s, when it faced sharp criticism from the founders of the journal *Annales* (Lucien Febvre and Marc Bloch, 1929), who accused it of narrowness: confining itself to "event history" (*histoire événementielle*) of politics and diplomacy, and neglecting the long-term social, economic, and mental structures. This critique did not cancel the usefulness of the documentary criticism tools themselves, but it radically broadened the definition of the legitimate historical source and subject.

## Background of the School's Emergence: Establishing the Republican University

The codification by Langlois and Seignobos came in a specific political context: the French Third Republic, which saw in training professional secular historians according to a rigorous method a tool for building national citizenship after the defeat of 1870 against Prussia, as an alternative to traditional ecclesiastical education. The methodical school was therefore not merely a neutral technical choice, but a self-consciously national project: training a generation of history teachers for the secondary schools and the new regional universities (*Université*) according to unified rules that could be taught and reproduced, away from the individual literary style of earlier Romantic historians such as Jules Michelet.

## Seignobos and Langlois: The Division of Labour

The book combined two complementary contributions. **Langlois**, a medievalist and specialist in archives and bibliography, was chiefly responsible for the sections on locating and collecting documents (heuristics), while **Seignobos**, a historian of modern and contemporary political history, wrote much of the sections on critical analysis and synthesis. This collaboration made the book a teaching text of a dual nature: a theory of document criticism combined with direct practical application, which explains its wide diffusion in French historians' training programmes for decades afterward.

## International Reception and Influence

The book's influence extended far beyond France. Translated into English in the year of its publication (1898) by G. G. Berry, it rapidly became a standard textbook across British and American universities. It was widely used in the training of graduate students in Anglophone universities. American historians of the generation of **James Harvey Robinson** engaged directly with its tenets, even as they subsequently developed the "New History" movement to expand inquiry beyond formal political and administrative documents.

## Enduring Value Despite Criticism

Despite the success of the Annales critique in broadening the horizon of legitimate history, Langlois and Seignobos's four steps (search, external criticism, internal criticism, synthesis) remain a core part of historical method training, including the Annales historians themselves, who broadened the subject of research without abandoning the necessity of carefully verifying the authenticity and credibility of a document before using it. The breadth of a subject (economy, mentalities, climate) does not by itself exempt one from the rigour of documentary criticism when a document requiring verification is available.

## Comparison with the German Tradition

Langlois and Seignobos drew their inspiration directly from the German model of **Ranke** and the school of philological criticism (*Quellenkritik*) developed by **Barthold Georg Niebuhr** and Ranke himself at the Prussian university at the beginning of the nineteenth century. However, they offered a French version that was simpler and more systematically codified: a working manual that could be taught directly to university students, unlike the German approach, which remained closer to an oral tradition acquired through apprenticeship with a particular professor in a research seminar (*Seminar*), rather than through a single printed unified methodological book.
