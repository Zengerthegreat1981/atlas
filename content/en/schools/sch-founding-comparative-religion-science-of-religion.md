# The Founding of the 'Science of Religion': Max Müller and the Birth of Comparative Religion

The foundational moment in which the study of the world's religions in Europe was transformed from a theological or missionary dispute into an independent academic field aspiring to the status of a "science" is attributed primarily to the German-British philologist Friedrich Max Müller (1823–1900), Professor of Comparative Philology at Oxford from 1868.

## The Arab Precedent and Its Transmission to Europe

Historians of the field (Sharpe, Chidester) observe that Müller and his contemporaries were not the first to write a systematic description of comparative religion. Roughly eight centuries earlier, an Arab-Islamic tradition (al-Biruni in *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind*, al-Shahrastani in *al-Milal wa-l-Nihal*, and Ibn Hazm in *al-Fisal*) had written about multiple religions in a relatively neutral descriptive manner. This heritage was among the sources that nourished the emerging European field itself: William Cureton published al-Shahrastani's *al-Milal wa-l-Nihal* in London (1842–1846), Theodor Haarbrücker translated it into German (1850–1851), and Eduard Sachau edited al-Biruni's *al-Athar al-Baqiya* (1878) and *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind* (1887), then translated them into English (1879 and 1888). The *Sacred Books of the East* edited by Müller included Edward Palmer's translation of the Qur'an (volumes VI and IX, 1880). By contrast, the Arab tradition did not become a continuous, institutionally established academic field, unlike Müller's project, which established platforms and chairs that produced a connected line of scholars extending through the German History of Religions School and then the twentieth-century phenomenology of religion (Otto, Wach, Eliade). This Arab heritage remained for Europeans a source of information more than a methodological model, and it was not read as a precedent for the project.

## From Philology to the "Science of Religion"

This orientation arose from the accumulation of vast textual material made available in the nineteenth century: critical editions of the Sanskrit Veda (Müller himself published the *Rig Veda* between 1849 and 1874), translations of the Zoroastrian Avesta, and Chinese, Islamic and Buddhist texts, which could now be studied with comparative philological tools, the same method that comparative linguists had developed to classify the Indo-European languages. In his *Lectures on the Science of Religion* (delivered in 1870 and published in 1873), Müller set out the programme of a new science founded on comparison and history, and expressed it in his famous phrase that he who knows only one religion knows none. Together with his colleagues he produced the series *Sacred Books of the East* (50 volumes between 1879 and 1910, from the Oxford University Press), which made the texts of the Asian religions available in English. On the European continent, Cornelis Tiele at Leiden held the first chair in the history of religions (1877), wrote *Outlines of the History of Religion* (1876), and Chantepie de la Saussaye presented *Textbook of the History of Religion* (1887); chairs and institutions followed in Geneva, Brussels and Paris (the religious sciences section of the École pratique des hautes études, 1886), which institutionalised the field.

## Early Schism: Philology Versus Anthropology

The founding project soon divided against itself. Müller remained committed to a philological explanation of the origin of myths ("the disease of language": myths arise from a slip in describing natural phenomena, most of them solar), while a later generation of British anthropologists, led by Edward Tylor in *Primitive Culture* (1871), moved to a radically different explanation: the origin of religion is a belief in spiritual beings ("animism") that arises from the attempt of "primitive" man to explain dreams and death. Andrew Lang, in *Custom and Myth* (1884), criticised Müller's solar theory by drawing on ethnographic comparison. James Frazer inherited the dispute and expanded the evolutionary framework in *The Golden Bough*. By the beginning of the twentieth century the anthropological current had in practice prevailed over Müller's linguistic explanation, but Müller's institutional framework, namely the idea that religions are a subject of scientific and methodical comparison rather than theological dispute, is what remained and shaped the academic field to this day.

## Critique: Orientalism and Race

The founding project has been criticised from several angles. Tomoko Masuzawa, in *The Invention of World Religions* (2005), showed that the "world religions" were classified by Müller and his colleagues on linguistic-racial bases ("Aryan" and "Semitic") carrying a hierarchical ordering that placed Christianity at the top, and that the field inherited these frameworks without awareness. David Chidester, in *Empire of Religion* (2014), noted that the material of religions was gathered through colonial officials and missionaries, and that Müller never visited India, relying on what was sent to him. The *Sacred Books of the East* project is itself contested: it strips texts of living use, presents the East as an ancient scriptural heritage rather than contemporary traditions, and selects texts according to the priorities of its translators. Historians hold that the field Müller founded, which promised scientific neutrality, did not always achieve it, and that the very idea of "religion" carried Protestant assumptions about belief and scripture.
