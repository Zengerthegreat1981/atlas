# Feminist Jurisprudence

A critical current that developed partly in dialogue with **critical legal studies**, prompted by an observation similar to the one that produced **critical race theory**: that CLS, despite its radical critique of law's neutrality, neglected **gender** as an independent axis requiring special analysis that cannot be reduced to a general class analysis. Earlier feminist legal waves (the late 1960s and early 1970s) had focused mainly on formal equality before the law (the right to vote, the right to work, the prohibition of direct discrimination). From the late 1970s, feminist jurisprudence developed toward a deeper critique, asking whether legal "neutrality" is possible at all in a system whose basic rules were historically formulated in the absence of women from the positions where law is made.

## Anticipation from Islamic jurisprudence: the specificity of women's rulings and the ijtihad of jurists

Islamic jurisprudence knew a distinct field sometimes called "fiqh al-nisa'" (women's jurisprudence), in which jurists addressed rulings that take account of women's particular situation (such as rulings on maintenance, custody, dower, and guardianship in marriage). The schools varied in their interpretations on matters such as a woman's authority over herself in marriage, where the Hanafis held that a sensible adult woman may contract her own marriage, contrary to the majority of Shafi'is, Malikis, and Hanbalis. Modern reformist thinkers such as Muhammad Abduh, Qasim Amin, and later Nasr Hamid Abu Zayd offered readings that reconsider some historical jurisprudential rulings from a historical and purposive perspective. They argued that many of the prevailing jurisprudential interpretations were historically a reflection of the patriarchal social customs of the period of codification, not of a definitive and fixed text. This approach partly shares ground with the Western feminist critique of law's purported "neutrality", though it differs fundamentally in its reference, method, and outcomes.

## MacKinnon: law makes masculinity the standard

In *Toward a Feminist Theory of the State* (1989), **Catharine MacKinnon** developed a radical critique: liberal law, which claims neutrality and objectivity, is in fact built on a **male perspective presented as a general, neutral standard**. Her most notable practical contribution was formulating (in *Sexual Harassment of Working Women*, 1979) the **theory of sexual harassment** as a form of sex discrimination in the workplace, a legal framework that American courts later formally adopted.

## West: critique of the assumption of "separation" in legal theory

In her influential article "Jurisprudence and Gender" (1988), **Robin West** advanced a different critique. The major Western theories of law, both liberal and critical, rest on an implicit assumption about the **separate, independent self**. West argues that this model reflects a stereotypically male experience more than a universal human reality, neglecting the experiences of **connection and relation** characteristic of many women's lives (such as pregnancy and motherhood).

## Concrete legal applications

Feminist jurisprudence went beyond theoretical critique to bring about actual changes in American law. Besides MacKinnon's theory of sexual harassment, the movement influenced the reformulation of marital rape laws (marital rape having historically been excluded from the definition of rape in English and American common law), and the development of legal frameworks for treating domestic violence as a public matter rather than a private affair between spouses, as it had traditionally been classified. It also contributed to the debate on **indirect discrimination**, in which employment criteria that appear neutral on their face (such as physical strength requirements) show an actual discriminatory effect against women when examined statistically.

## The psychological strand: Gilligan and "a different voice"

West draws partly on the work of the psychologist **Carol Gilligan** in *In a Different Voice* (1982), which argued that women tend more toward an **ethics of care and relationship** than toward the **ethics of abstract justice and rights** prevailing in traditional theories of moral development.

## Multiple currents and internal disagreement

Feminist jurisprudence was not a unified current. While MacKinnon represents the "radical feminist" line, which focuses on power and sexual domination, Gilligan's and West's approach belongs to what is known as **difference feminism**, which celebrates the specificity of women's experience rather than demanding a strict equality that ignores differences. **Equality feminism**, an older current, objected to this, arguing that emphasizing a "different female voice" may reproduce the stereotypes historically used to justify excluding women from public life. **Martha Fineman** later added a broader dimension in her *vulnerability theory*. Rather than focusing on gender alone, she proposes that **universal human vulnerability** (not our being specifically women or men) is the foundation on which the response of the state and of law should be built. This generalizes the critique of feminist jurisprudence into a general theory of the relationship between the individual and the state.
