# Arabic Linguistic and Literary Thought

An umbrella covering the fields that studied the structure, grammar, meaning, rhetoric and literary criticism of the Arabic language, from the codification of grammar in Basra and Kufa in the 8th century CE to the classical theories of rhetoric and literary criticism set out at length in the 4th and 5th centuries AH.

## Major Trajectories

- **The grammar of Sibawayh**: Sibawayh (Abu Bishr 'Amr ibn 'Uthman ibn Qanbar, d. c. 180 AH/796 CE) set out the first surviving integrated grammar of Arabic in the work known as *al-Kitab* ("The Book"), which remained a principal reference for centuries. The book describes the Arabic sentence through case inflection (*i'rab*), the governing element (*'amil*) and composition, drawing on pre-Islamic poetry, the Quran and the speech of Arabs whose usage the grammarians took as eloquent. Earlier teaching is reported, including the name of Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali, but those beginnings are known only from later accounts.
- **Philosophy of grammar**: A theoretical debate later developed about the nature of grammatical rules themselves: are they laws derived by induction from the speech of the Arabs, or general logical categories imposed on language? This debate echoed in the rival grammatical schools of Basra and Kufa.
- **Rhetoric and the theory of nazm**: A major theoretical statement came with 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani (d. 471 AH/1078 CE; some sources give 474 AH/1081 CE) in his two books *Asrar al-Balagha* (The Secrets of Rhetoric) and *Dala'il al-I'jaz* (The Proofs of Inimitability). Al-Jurjani linked grammar and literary beauty through the theory of *nazm* (syntactic composition), which holds that the eloquence of speech lies not in individual words taken alone but in the way they are composed and arranged, both grammatically and semantically. This theoretical framework was later used to explain the rhetorical inimitability (*i'jaz*) of the Quran.
- **Classical literary criticism**: Developing alongside grammar and rhetoric, literary criticism evaluated poetry and prose by standards of eloquence (*fasaha*), expressiveness (*bayan*) and good composition (*husn al-sabk*), from the first critics of poetry to the schools of literary criticism of the Abbasid period.

## The Pre-Islamic Poetic Root

This linguistic tradition drew its first citational material from pre-Islamic poetry, which was preserved and transmitted orally by reciters (*ruwat*) before it was written down. This explains the close links between this school and the school of pre-Islamic Arab thought.

## Basra, Kufa and Baghdad

Grammar took shape in two rival centres. Basra gathered al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi (d. c. 170 AH/786 CE), credited with establishing the science of prosody (*'arud*), in which the metres of poetry are arranged in five circles comprising fifteen metres. A sixteenth metre, *al-mutadarik*, was added later. He is credited with *Kitab al-'Ayn*, the earliest Arabic dictionary arranged by the points of articulation, although completion is often assigned to his student al-Layth ibn al-Muzaffar. On this foundation his student Sibawayh built *al-Kitab*. Kufa is associated with al-Kisa'i (d. 189 AH/805 CE) and al-Farra' (d. 207 AH/822 CE), author of *Ma'ani al-Quran*. In the later portraits, the Kufans were more willing to accept rare readings and anomalous poetic usage and to analogize from a few examples, while the Basrans preferred analogy from regular, widely attested patterns and rejected rules built on isolated anomalies. The dispute was carried to Baghdad. Abu al-Barakat ibn al-Anbari (d. 577 AH/1181 CE) collected it in *al-Insaf fi Masa'il al-Khilaf bayn al-Nahwiyyin al-Basriyyin wa-l-Kufiyyin*, which in the usual count comprises 121 questions (some copies list slightly fewer). Modern scholars, from Gotthold Weil onward, argue that this duality is sharper in the biographical tradition than in the early texts, and that the real difference is a method of handling evidence.

## Literature and Criticism before al-Jurjani

An earlier body of poetry criticism preceded al-Jurjani. Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi (d. 231 or 232 AH/845–847 CE) ranked poets in *Tabaqat Fuhul al-Shu'ara'* (The Classes of Master Poets) and held that poetry is a craft which those trained in it recognize, as a money-changer recognizes a coin. Al-Jahiz (d. 255 AH/868–869 CE) wrote *al-Bayan wa-l-Tabyin* (Eloquence and Exposition), making *bayan* central to understanding and tying eloquence to the situation of speech. Ibn Qutayba (d. 276 AH/889 CE) wrote *al-Shi'r wa-l-Shu'ara'* (Poetry and Poets). Qudama ibn Ja'far (d. c. 337 AH/948 CE), in *Naqd al-Shi'r* (The Criticism of Poetry), tried to state poetic quality in logical criteria drawn in part from Greek divisions. Al-Amidi (d. 370 or 371 AH/981–982 CE) compared Abu Tammam and al-Buhturi in *al-Muwazana*. Al-Qadi al-Jurjani (d. 392 AH/1001–1002 CE) examined al-Mutanabbi and his critics in *al-Wasata bayn al-Mutanabbi wa-Khusumihi* (The Mediation between al-Mutanabbi and His Opponents). Al-Jurjani inherited questions about wording and meaning, and about craft and natural talent.

## The Quran and the Question of Inimitability

Interest in language was not a literary luxury; it was driven by the need to understand the Quranic text and to fix its readings. Hence arose books on metaphor (*majaz*), on the meanings of the Quran (*ma'ani*) and on its rare words (*gharib al-Quran*), and then treatises on the inimitability of the Quran (*i'jaz*) by al-Rummani (d. 384 AH), al-Khattabi (d. 388 AH) and al-Baqillani (d. 403 AH), which prepared the ground for al-Jurjani's theory of *nazm*.

## Caveats

- This umbrella groups several subfields and is not a single temporally homogeneous school. The gap between the founding of grammar (the 2nd century AH) and the peak of theoretical rhetoric (the 5th century AH) spans nearly three centuries.
- Many early grammatical works (before Sibawayh) are lost, and knowledge of them relies on later quotations, which makes the question of when the "beginning" of grammar occurred a matter of debate.
