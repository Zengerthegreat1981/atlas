# Arabic Linguistic and Literary Thought

An umbrella covering the fields that studied the structure, grammar, meaning, rhetoric and literary criticism of the Arabic language, from the codification of grammar in Basra and Kufa in the 8th century CE to the classical theories of rhetoric and literary criticism that reached their peak in the 4th and 5th centuries AH.

## Major Trajectories

- **Founding the science of grammar**: Sibawayh (Abu Bishr 'Amr ibn 'Uthman ibn Qanbar, d. c. 180 AH/796 CE) produced the first integrated grammatical system of Arabic in his work known briefly as *al-Kitab* ("The Book"), which remained the supreme reference in Arabic grammatical studies for centuries. The book established an inductive method describing the structure of the Arabic sentence (*i'rab* or case inflection, the governing element *'amil*, and composition), drawing on citations from pre-Islamic poetry, the Quran and the speech of eloquent Arabs.
- **Philosophy of grammar**: A theoretical debate later developed about the nature of grammatical rules themselves: are they laws derived by induction from the speech of the Arabs, or general logical categories imposed on language? This debate echoed in the rival grammatical schools of Basra and Kufa.
- **Founding the science of rhetoric**: Its theoretical peak came with 'Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani (d. 471 AH/1078 CE) in his two books *Asrar al-Balagha* (The Secrets of Rhetoric) and *Dala'il al-I'jaz* (The Proofs of Inimitability). Al-Jurjani linked grammar and literary beauty through the theory of *nazm* (syntactic composition), which holds that the eloquence of speech lies not in individual words taken alone but in the way they are composed and arranged, both grammatically and semantically. This theoretical framework was later used to explain the rhetorical inimitability (*i'jaz*) of the Quran.
- **Classical literary criticism**: Developing alongside grammar and rhetoric, literary criticism evaluated poetry and prose by standards of eloquence (*fasaha*), expressiveness (*bayan*) and good composition (*husn al-sabk*), from the first critics of poetry to the schools of literary criticism of the Abbasid period.

## The Pre-Islamic Poetic Root

This linguistic tradition drew its first citational material from pre-Islamic poetry, which was preserved and transmitted orally by reciters (*ruwat*) before it was written down. This explains the close links between this school and the school of pre-Islamic Arab thought.

## Basra, Kufa and Baghdad

Grammar took shape in two rival centres. Basra gathered al-Khalil ibn Ahmad al-Farahidi (d. c. 170 AH/786 CE), founder of the science of prosody ('*arud*), by which he systematised the metres of poetry into five circles comprising fifteen metres. He is credited with the *Kitab al-'Ayn*, the first Arabic dictionary, whose entries are arranged by the points of articulation of the letters, although some scholars think its completion was by his student al-Layth ibn al-Muzaffar and others. On this foundation his student Sibawayh built his book. Kufa was known for al-Kisa'i (d. 189 AH/805 CE) and al-Farra' (d. 207 AH/822 CE), author of *Ma'ani al-Quran*. Their approach was more tolerant of rare readings, poetic usages and analogy (*qiyas*) based on a few examples, whereas the Basrans were generally stricter in relying on attested usage (*sama'*) and rejected extensive analogy. The dispute moved to Baghdad, into the councils of caliphs and viziers, until Ibn al-Anbari (d. 577 AH/1181 CE) collected its questions in *al-Insaf fi Masa'il al-Khilaf bayn al-Nahwiyyin al-Basriyyin wa-l-Kufiyyin* (Justice in the Points of Disagreement between the Grammarians of Basra and Kufa), which comprises 121 issues. Modern scholars argue that the duality of the two schools is less rigid than biographical dictionaries suggest, and that the question concerns a method of handling evidence more than two entirely distinct doctrines.

## Literature and Criticism before al-Jurjani

An earlier body of work in poetry criticism preceded al-Jurjani. Ibn Sallam al-Jumahi (d. 231 AH/845 CE) ranked poets into classes in *Tabaqat Fuhul al-Shu'ara'* (The Ranks of the Champion Poets), and he showed that poetry has a craft which those knowledgeable in it recognize, as a money-changer recognizes a dirham. al-Jahiz (d. 255 AH/868-869 CE) wrote *al-Bayan wa-l-Tabyin* (Eloquence and Exposition), making *bayan* the axis of understanding and communication and linking eloquence to the situation of speech. Then Ibn Qutayba (d. 276 AH/889 CE) wrote *al-Shi'r wa-l-Shu'ara'* (Poetry and Poets), and Qudama ibn Ja'far (d. c. 337 AH/948 CE) wrote *Naqd al-Shi'r* (Criticism of Poetry), attempting to codify poetic quality by logical criteria under the influence of Greek thought. al-Amidi (d. 370 AH/981 CE) pursued a comparison in *al-Muwazana* between Abu Tammam and al-Buhturi, and al-Qadi al-Jurjani (d. 392 AH/1002 CE) examined the poetry of al-Mutanabbi and his critics in *al-Wasata bayn al-Mutanabbi wa-Khusumihi* (The Mediation between al-Mutanabbi and His Adversaries). Thus al-Jurjani inherited a body of questions about wording and meaning, craft and natural talent.

## The Quran and the Question of Inimitability

Interest in language was not a literary luxury; it was driven by the need to understand the Quranic text and to fix its readings. Hence arose books on metaphor (*majaz*), on the meanings of the Quran (*ma'ani*) and on its rare words (*gharib al-Quran*), and then treatises on the inimitability of the Quran (*i'jaz*) by al-Rummani (d. 384 AH), al-Khattabi (d. 388 AH) and al-Baqillani (d. 403 AH), which prepared the ground for al-Jurjani's theory of *nazm*.

## Caveats

- This umbrella groups several subfields and is not a single temporally homogeneous school. The gap between the founding of grammar (the 2nd century AH) and the peak of theoretical rhetoric (the 5th century AH) spans nearly three centuries.
- Many early grammatical works (before Sibawayh) are lost, and knowledge of them relies on later quotations, which makes the question of when the "beginning" of grammar occurred a matter of debate.
