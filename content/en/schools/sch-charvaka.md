# Chārvāka / Lokāyata (Indian Materialism)

An ancient Indian materialist tradition, regarded as the most radical of the unorthodox (nāstika) Indian schools. It holds that the perceptible world is the only reality, that sense perception is the only source of knowledge, and that the soul, God, the afterlife, and otherworldly recompense are illusions. It is known by the two names Chārvāka and Lokāyata (that is, "that which prevails among the people" or "the worldly world"), and its views have been preserved in the expositions of its opponents rather than in texts of its own, so our knowledge of it is indirect.

## Origins and Sources

Tradition attributes the school to the sage Bṛhaspati, and it is thought that the *Bṛhaspati Sūtra* was an ancient foundational text of the school that was subsequently lost, leaving only transmitted fragments. As for "Chārvāka," it appears as the name of a person in some sources; in one account he is a disciple of Bṛhaspati, while other traditions derive the name from *cāru-vāk* ("one of pleasing speech"). Ancient texts attest to materialist views at an early date: the *Sāmaññaphala Sutta* in the Pali Buddhist canon (Dīgha Nikāya) presents the position of the teacher Ajita Kesakambalī, who taught that human beings are composed of the four physical elements and dissolve back into them upon death, denying any karmic fruit of good or bad deeds. The term *Lokāyata* appears in the *Arthaśāstra* as well as early Buddhist and Jain texts with the meaning of worldly philosophy or disputation. The most systematic later exposition of the school is found in the fourteenth-century compendium *Sarva-darśana-saṃgraha* by Mādhava (Vidyāraṇya); another key related text is the eighth-century *Tattvopaplavasiṃha* by Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa, which pursued a radical scepticism toward all epistemic instruments (*pramāṇas*).

## Central Ideas

- **Sense perception as the foundation of knowledge**: the school rejected inference (anumāna) because the universal relation between cause and effect (vyāpti) cannot be established by perception, and rejected testimony and sacred texts as proofs.
- **The four elements**: earth, water, fire, and air, which alone are reality; the school does not recognize ether as a fifth element because it is not perceived.
- **Consciousness as a material product**: it arises from the composition of the elements as intoxication arises from fermenting substances; among their famous examples is their statement that liquor arises from ingredients none of which is intoxicating on its own. Consequently consciousness ends with the dissolution of the body: "when the body becomes ashes, how could it return?"
- **Denial of karma and reincarnation**: there is no other life, and no reward or punishment.
- **Pleasure and worldly good**: pleasure is the highest good and pain is evil, and giving up pleasures for fear of pain is folly, like one who gives up grain because it has husks. The famous saying is attributed to the school: "As long as you live, live happily; borrow and drink ghee."
- **Conduct**: the sources differ in describing their ethics. Their opponents portray it as a call to sensuality, while modern studies hold that some of them espoused a moderate practical ethics based on worldly interest ("artha" and "kāma") and on a realistic politics.

## Criticism by Opponents

All the schools replied to them. The Nyāya criticized their argument against inference on the ground that rejecting it itself uses inference, and that a general scepticism about inference cannot be expressed without an argument. The Buddhists and Jains replied by analysing consciousness as a series that is not reducible to a composition of elements. And Vedāntins such as Śaṅkara argued for a conscious self irreducible to the body. These replies had great merit, since they pushed Indian philosophers to formulate the theory of inference and causality with precision.

## Comparison with Arab-Islamic Thought

There is no evidence of a relation of direct influence, but comparison reveals echoes. Islam knew the "Dahriyyun" (the materialists), whose saying the Qur'an reports: "There is nothing but our life in this world; we die and we live, and nothing destroys us except time" (al-Jathiya 24), and the "Sumaniyya," from whom the theologians reported a rejection of inference and a restriction to the senses; among the heretics (zanadiqa) of the Abbasid era were Ibn al-Rawandi and Abu Bakr al-Razi. Abdurrahman Badawi studied the "History of Atheism in Islam" (first published 1945; reprinted 1993) and Sarah Stroumsa "Freethinkers of Medieval Islam" (1999). The similarity between the Sumaniyya and the Chārvāka in rejecting inference may go back to the structure of the sensualist position and not to a historical transmission.

## Influence

- It pushed all the schools of India to clarify their positions on consciousness, the soul, and inference.
- Twentieth-century thinkers revived interest in it: Debiprasad Chattopadhyaya wrote "Lokāyata: A Study in Ancient Indian Materialism" (1959) and established a link between Marxism and the Indian materialist heritage.
- It furnished evidence of the plurality and independence of Indian thought, and of a materialist tradition that arose without derivation from the Greeks.

## Limitations and Criticism

- The original texts were lost, so everything we know reached us from opponents who present the position in a way that may be distorted or satirical.
- Ramkrishna Bhattacharya, in "Studies on the Cārvāka/Lokāyata" (2011), holds that generalizing "materialism" to it may exceed the evidence, and that the disagreement among its currents has not become clear.
- Reliance on the image of "sensualism" is exaggerated and comes in large part from the satire of opponents.
- An epistemology limited to perception suffers from a self-referential problem: establishing that perception is the only source is not done by perception.
