# History of Non-Western Ethics

This field examines the moral traditions that arose outside the Greco-Latin philosophical circle and its modern European extensions. Among the most widely studied are the Islamic, Chinese and Indian traditions; African, indigenous and other traditions also deserve separate treatment. They are traditions with independent conceptual systems, which cannot be reduced to Western moral categories such as utilitarianism, deontology and virtue ethics, although they intersect with those categories on some questions.

## The Islamic Moral Tradition

Islamic moral thought rests on two interrelated sources: revelation, represented by the Qur'an and the Sunna, and rational reflection. The latter produced three major streams.

The first is theological. The Mu'tazila and the Ash'aris debated the question of goodness and badness (*husn* and *qubh*). Are these two rational qualities inherent in actions, which reason perceives independently, as the Mu'tazila held? Or are they established only through the revealed law (*shar'*), as the Ash'aris, led by Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari, maintained?

The second is philosophical. It drew on Greek ethics and reformulated it within a monotheistic framework. Its most prominent representatives were Abu Nasr al-Farabi, with his theory of the virtuous city; Ibn Miskawayh, whose book *Tahdhib al-Akhlaq* developed a theory of virtue as a mean between excess and deficiency; and Abu Hamid al-Ghazali, who drew on jurisprudence, theology and Sufism in *Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din*, presenting a method for purifying the soul and refining inner ethics.

The third is jurisprudential. It formulated the objectives of the Shari'a (*maqasid al-shari'a*) as the preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny and property. Al-Ghazali gave an early systematic statement of this classification, and al-Shatibi later developed the theory in *al-Muwafaqat*. Also noteworthy is the contribution of al-Raghib al-Isfahani, who analyzed Qur'anic concepts linguistically and ethically in *al-Dhari'a ila Makarim al-Shari'a*, and Ibn Hazm of Cordoba, who in *al-Akhlaq wa-l-Siyar* presented a practical ethics aimed at relieving distress of the soul.

## The Chinese Moral Tradition

Classical Chinese ethics centers on two rival schools.

Confucianism, associated with Confucius (Kongzi) and the sayings collected in the *Analects*, rests on *ren* (humaneness or benevolence) as a comprehensive virtue, and on *li* (ritual propriety, rites and social customs). These regulate the five great social relationships: between ruler and subject, father and son, husband and wife, elder and younger brother, and friend and friend. Mencius developed these ethics with his theory of the innate goodness of human nature. Xunzi, by contrast, held that human nature is disordered (often rendered "evil") and requires education and ritual.

Daoism, attributed to Laozi and his book *Dao De Jing*, proposes a different ethics. It rests on *wu wei* (non-action, meaning non-coercive non-interference in the course of nature), and on liberation from the social artificiality that Confucianism institutionalized. It calls for humility, simplicity and harmony with the *Dao*, the original cosmic principle.

## The Indian Moral Tradition

The Indian moral tradition brings together Hindu, Buddhist and Jain thought within a broadly shared framework of concepts: karma, the transmigration of souls, and ultimate liberation (*moksha* or *nirvana*).

In Hinduism, moral conduct is regulated through the concept of *dharma*, meaning the duty appropriate to one's social position and stage of life. The *Bhagavad Gita* elaborates this in the dialogue between Krishna and Arjuna about the duty to fight without attachment to the fruits of action. This teaching is often summarized as *nishkama karma*, action free from selfish aims.

Buddhism, traditionally traced to Siddhartha Gautama, bases its ethics on the "Eightfold Path" that leads to the end of suffering, and on the virtue of *ahimsa* (non-violence) toward all living beings. It also rests on universal compassion and loving-kindness, which the Mahayana tradition later developed in the figure of the bodhisattva, who vows to work for the liberation of all beings. The principle of *ahimsa* is also central to Jain ethics.

## Comparison and Contemporary Impact

This comparative survey shows that non-Western ethics was no less complex or systematic than Greco-Latin ethics. It offered original conceptual alternatives, such as the rational grounding of good and bad, the cosmic harmony of the Dao, and Buddhist non-violence. Academic interest in these traditions has been renewed within comparative philosophy since the late twentieth century, in an effort to move beyond European centrism in the teaching of the history of ethics.

## Methodological Notes

The researcher in this field faces a persistent methodological problem: the risk of projecting Western categories such as utilitarianism and deontology onto traditions that were never formulated in those terms. This calls for interpretive caution that respects the internal particularity of each tradition. The Confucian concept of *ren*, for example, cannot be reduced to an Aristotelian virtue or a Kantian duty. It is relational in nature, taking shape within a network of reciprocal social roles. This led some contemporary scholars, notably Roger Ames and Henry Rosemont, to propose "Confucian role ethics" as an interpretive framework, although others defend virtue-ethical readings. Likewise, *husn* and *qubh* in Mu'tazili thought are not an Islamic version of Western moral realism. They rest on a particular theological conception of divine justice and wisdom.

This methodological care contributed to the development of a subfield known as comparative philosophy, which seeks dialogue among traditions rather than imposing a single framework on all of them. It also notes that many strands of these traditions link ethics to a higher existential purpose, whether that is God's pleasure, cosmic harmony or liberation from suffering. Some modern Western ethical systems, by contrast, treat the moral question as separable from questions about ultimate meaning.
