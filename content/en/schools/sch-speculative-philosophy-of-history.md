# Speculative Philosophy of History (Vico, Hegel, Marx, Toynbee, Spengler)

A way of thinking about history that seeks to discover a **comprehensive meaning, pattern or law** governing the course of all human history. It is not a method for scrutinizing individual facts (as in source criticism), but a comprehensive explanatory system that gives history a direction and a purpose.

## Giambattista Vico and Civilizational Cycles

In *The New Science* (Scienza Nuova, 1725), **Giambattista Vico** is the first to propose a systematic modern speculative system. Every civilization passes through three successive, cyclical stages: the age of the gods (myth and religion), the age of heroes (military aristocracy), and the age of men (democracy and reason), before it dissolves and a new cycle begins (ricorso). He is regarded as a precursor of Hegel and Marx in the attempt to find a "science" of the logic of human history itself.

## Hegel: The Cunning of Reason and the March of Freedom

In his *Lectures on the Philosophy of History* (published after his death, from his lectures of 1822–1830), Hegel argues that world history is a dialectical march toward the realization of "Spirit" (Geist) becoming conscious of itself as freedom. It proceeds through successive nations, each of which carries the "principle" of Spirit in a particular age and is then exhausted, handing the role on to a later nation. Individuals of history (such as Napoleon) are instruments of a "cunning of reason" (List der Vernunft), which uses their passions to achieve broader rational ends that they do not understand.

## Marx: From Idealism to Materialism

Marx is a critical heir to Hegel. He retains the dialectical and teleological structure of history (a defined course toward an end), but "turns it on its head": the driving force of history is not the absolute Spirit but the material contradictions in the relations of production (see `sch-historical-materialism-as-method`).

## Toynbee and Spengler: Cycles of Independent Civilizations

In the twentieth century, **Oswald Spengler** (*The Decline of the West*, Der Untergang des Abendlandes, 1918–1922) and **Arnold Toynbee** (*A Study of History*, 12 volumes, 1934–1961) proposed two morphological systems. The great "civilizations" (not nations or classes) are treated as semi-independent organic units, each passing through a life cycle from birth to growth to decline, like a living being. Spengler, with strict organic determinism, predicted the decline of Western civilization. Toynbee, with a more flexible framework, linked the rise of civilizations to their creative responses to environmental and social "challenges" (the theory of challenge and response).

## Common Limitation

This entire pattern faced sharp criticism in the twentieth century (especially from critical philosophy of history, the next item in this section) for **imposing a predetermined overall pattern** on diverse historical material that does not necessarily conform to it, and for falling into generalizations that are difficult to refute or to verify empirically in detail.
