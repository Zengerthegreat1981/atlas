# Bilingualism & Cognitive Control

A field of research at the intersection of psycholinguistics and cognitive psychology. It studies how managing two active languages at the same time affects the brain's general executive functions (such as inhibition, task switching and working memory), not language alone. This research direction took systematic shape in Canada and the United States from the late 1980s onward, and is associated with names such as Ellen Bialystok and David Green. It has been one of the most active areas of psycholinguistic research since the 1990s, building on earlier Canadian work such as Peal and Lambert's 1962 study of bilingual children in Montreal.

## The central hypothesis: dual activation

The school starts from a well-established experimental observation: the two languages of a bilingual remain active together in the brain even when only one of them is being used. The vocabulary of the non-target language competes for activation and interferes with the target language. This imposes on the brain a continuous mechanism of selection and inhibition to avoid interference. According to the hypothesis, this constant need for selection and control trains the brain's general executive system and strengthens its performance even outside the linguistic context.

## Green's inhibitory control model

**David Green** formulated the "Inhibitory Control Model" in 1998. It assumes that a general supervisory system organizes the "task schemas" associated with each language, and inhibits the schema of the unused language during production or comprehension in the other language. Green and Jubin Abutalebi later extended this framework into the "Adaptive Control Hypothesis," which links different patterns of bilingual communicative contexts (such as sudden or planned switching) to different patterns of demand for executive control.

## The "bilingual advantage" and the replication crisis

Early research by Bialystok promoted the idea of a "bilingual cognitive advantage," holding that the continuous practice of managing two languages gives bilinguals a measurable superiority in executive control tasks, and even a delay in the onset of dementia symptoms in older adults (interpreted in terms of "cognitive reserve"; Bialystok, Craik and Freedman, 2007). From the 2010s onward, however, these findings have been strongly contested. Many studies failed to reproduce the effect, and de Bruin, Treccani and Della Sala (2015) argued that publication bias had favoured positive results. Other studies failed to find the effect with the same sample sizes or with more precise methodological controls (such as controlling for socioeconomic status). This opened a broad methodological debate about the true size of the effect and the conditions under which it appears.

## Associated lexical representation models

The field is linked to models of how the two languages are represented and accessed. The Revised Hierarchical Model of Judith Kroll and Erika Stewart (1994) describes how links between second-language words and concepts change with proficiency. The Bilingual Interactive Activation Plus model (BIA+) of Ton Dijkstra and Walter van Heuven (2002) describes competition between the two languages during word recognition. Both are models of processing, not direct evidence for an executive-control advantage.

## Moderating factors

Researchers emphasize that the effect of bilingualism on executive control is not uniform. It varies according to the age at which the second language was acquired, the degree of proficiency in it, the frequency of switching between the two languages, and its social context. This links to François Grosjean's work on "language modes," which describes how bilinguals move along a continuum between a largely monolingual mode and a bilingual mode depending on the situation and interlocutor.
