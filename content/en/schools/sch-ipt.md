# Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT)

Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT) is a short-term, protocol-defined psychotherapy developed by Gerald Klerman and Myrna Weissman in the 1970s, building on the New Haven-Boston Collaborative Depression Research Project; their foundational manual, *Interpersonal Psychotherapy of Depression*, was published in 1984. IPT focuses on the relationship between mood disorder (especially depression) and interpersonal relationships, and works within a limited time frame (usually 12–16 weeks) across three phases. It is today among the brief therapies best supported by empirical evidence, and several treatment guidelines list it among the recommended psychotherapies for major depression.

## Historical Context and Founding

IPT arose at the intersection of two currents: the tradition of brief psychoanalytic therapy (Brief Dynamic Therapy), and the effort to test psychotherapies empirically alongside antidepressant medication in the New Haven-Boston Collaborative Depression Research Project. Its theoretical roots lie in the interpersonal psychiatry of Adolf Meyer and Harry Stack Sullivan and in attachment theory (Bowlby), which supports the focus on the link between close relationships and mood. Klerman and Weissman, with colleagues including Eugene Paykel, Bruce Rounsaville, and Edith Chevron, developed a structured protocol for depression, which was later adapted for other conditions and age groups.

Weissman, with John Markowitz and others, and clinicians such as Scott Stuart carried the approach forward; today training and accreditation are overseen by professional bodies such as the International Society of Interpersonal Psychotherapy.

## Core Theoretical Principles

IPT rests on a central idea: depression is a disorder linked to relationships, and the reverse is also true. IPT connects mood to the social context in which the individual lives, on the assumption that improving relationships will improve mood, and that improved mood will improve relationships. The idea is not "linear causality" but a **loop of mutual interaction** between the two.

IPT rejects both extreme positions: it is neither a cognitive therapy that focuses on thoughts nor a dynamic therapy that explores the unconscious. It is a "focused" therapy concentrating on four areas of interpersonal problems:

1. **Grief**: complicated loss, especially after the death of a loved one.
2. **Interpersonal conflicts (Role Disputes)**: recurrent disagreements with an important person.
3. **Role Transitions**: a change in life status (marriage, divorce, childbirth, retirement).
4. **Interpersonal Deficits**: chronic isolation or difficulties in forming relationships.

These areas are not diagnostic categories but **drivers** of the therapeutic work. The therapist begins by helping the client identify the most relevant area, and then the therapy is directed toward it.

## Therapeutic Structure: Three Phases

- **Initial phase (roughly sessions 1–3)**: assessment, establishing the relationship, explaining the diagnosis, and the "treatment contract," an agreement on the area of focus. It includes a clear explanation of depression as an illness and not a moral failing, with normalization of symptoms.
- **Middle phase (roughly sessions 4–13)**: direct work on the identified area, through strategies specific to each area (techniques for grief, communication analysis, rebuilding relationships, and so on).
- **Final phase (roughly sessions 14–16)**: termination of therapy, summarizing the gains, and discussing early warning signs of relapse.

## Core Therapeutic Strategies

- **Clarification**: helping the client name feelings accurately.
- **Affective Expression**: encouraging the expression of emotions in the session.
- **Assertiveness Exploration**: helping the client learn to express needs.
- **Communication Analysis**: breaking down actual interaction patterns and linking them to recurrent patterns.
- **Use of the Therapeutic Relationship**: exploring how the relationship pattern recurs in the therapist's presence.

## Its Position in the Therapeutic Field

IPT occupies a unique position among the schools. It is a **short-term, directive therapy** like CBT, but it **focuses on relationships** and not on thoughts. It is **protocol-defined** like DBT and CBT, but it **avoids the unconscious interpretation** that distinguishes psychoanalysis. It is **supportive and exploratory at once**, with an organized stance toward depression as a medical condition.

Among psychotherapies it is usually grouped with the short-term, problem-focused treatments alongside CBT and brief psychodynamic therapy, and meta-analyses (for example Cuijpers et al.) find its effects on depression broadly comparable to those of CBT.

## Clinical Applications and Evidence

IPT has been applied across a wide range of disorders: major depression (the primary application), bipolar disorder (interpersonal and social rhythm therapy, developed by Ellen Frank and colleagues), bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, antenatal and postpartum depression, and adolescent depression (IPT-A). The empirical evidence is particularly strong in major depression: a meta-analysis by Cuijpers and colleagues (2011) found IPT effective for depression, with no clear difference from other active psychotherapies. Practice guidelines, including those of the American Psychiatric Association, list it among the recommended psychotherapies for major depression. As with any treatment, outcomes vary between individuals, and decisions about care belong with a qualified clinician.

## The Target Disorder and the Competing Technique

IPT's primary indication is **major depressive disorder**, and the APA Division 12 (Society of Clinical Psychology) listing rates it as having strong research support for depression under its 1998 criteria (with re-evaluation under the 2015 criteria still outstanding), drawing on the original trials by Klerman, Weissman, and colleagues in the 1970s and the comparative trials that followed. IPT can be contrasted with **Behavioral Activation (BA)**, a behavioral alternative for treating depression: while IPT focuses on rebuilding relationships and addressing loss and role transitions, BA aims at directly increasing reinforcing behaviors regardless of the relational content.

## Limitations and Criticism

- **The "interpersonal" determinism**: explaining all depression through relationships may seem mechanistic, especially in depression with a clear biological basis.
- **A rigid protocol**: adherence to the protocol may limit the therapist's flexibility in complex cases (comorbid personality disorders).
- **Cultural adaptation**: it was developed in the American context, and transferring it to non-Western cultures requires adaptations that have not all been carried out.

## Legacy and Influence

IPT is one of the few therapies that arose in an experimental research environment and crossed safely into clinical practice. It influenced the development of other time-limited, manualized psychotherapies. It is trained and applied internationally, with adaptations for group formats and for low-resource settings, for example group IPT for depression in Uganda.
