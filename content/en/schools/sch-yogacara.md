# Yogācāra / Vijñānavāda

Yogācāra ("practitioners of yoga"), also called Vijñānavāda ("the doctrine of consciousness") and Vijñaptimātra ("representation-only"), is a Mahayana Buddhist school that appeared in India around the 4th century CE. It focuses on the analysis of mental experience and on the idea that what we take to be independent external things are in reality representations produced by the process of consciousness. Tradition linked it with Maitreya, Asaṅga and Vasubandhu. It influenced Buddhist logic and epistemology and Chinese, Japanese and Tibetan Buddhism, and is distinguished by a careful study of the mechanisms of the unconscious mind.

## Origins and texts

Tradition ascribes the school to "Maitreya" (Maitreyanatha), whom the followers regard as a celestial teacher, though some scholars think he was a historical teacher. Asaṅga (about the 4th century) of Gandhara in northwest India stood out, and his brother Vasubandhu (about the 4th or 5th century), who earlier composed the "Abhidharmakosha", well known in the schools of the early doctrines. Texts of the school include the "Samdhinirmocana Sutra" (Sutra Unraveling the Knots), the "Yogācārabhūmi", and the "Mahāyānasaṃgraha" by Asaṅga, and among Vasubandhu's works the "Twenty Verses" (Viṃśatikā), the "Thirty Verses" (Triṃśikā) and the "Exposition of the Three Natures" (Trisvabhāvanirdeśa). They were then commented on by Sthiramati (about 510–570) and Dharmapala (530–561), and left a mark on Dignāga (about 480–540) and Dharmakīrti (about 600–660) in epistemology.

## Central ideas

- Representation-only: what is perceived as an independent external thing is nothing but a mental representation, so there is no real duality between the perceiving subject and the perceived object. Vasubandhu uses the examples of dreams and seeing illusions, and answers the objection that dreams are not regular by saying that consciousness changes under the influence of seeds stored in it.
- The eightfold consciousness: five sensory consciousnesses, the mental consciousness (manovijñāna), the defiled consciousness (manas) attached to the image of an "I", and the storehouse consciousness (ālayavijñāna), which stores the seeds of actions (bīja) and the traces of habits, and continues across births.
- The three natures: the imagined (parikalpita), that is, attributing subject and object to what is not so; the dependent (paratantra), that is, the process of causes and conditions; and the perfected (pariniṣpanna), that is, the reality of things free of imagination.
- Transformation of the basis (āśraya-parāvṛtti): enlightenment is the transformation of the storehouse consciousness into wisdom, and the eight consciousnesses are transformed into four wisdoms.
- The "three turnings of the wheel of the Dharma" in the Samdhinirmocana Sutra: it interprets the Buddha's teaching as three stages, treating the teaching of emptiness (the second turning) as incomplete and the third turning as definitive.

## Disagreement in interpretation

Scholars debate whether it is an existential idealism that denies the external world, or a phenomenological epistemological theory that studies the structure of experience without deciding on external existence. Stefan Anacker and others incline toward an idealist interpretation; Thomas Kochumuttom argues that Vasubandhu's position is a realist pluralism about experience; and Dan Lusthaus in "Buddhist Phenomenology" (2002) holds that it is an analysis of the formation of experience. It was opposed by the Madhyamaka school, such as Candrakīrti, which held that fixing a consciousness as the reality reproduces the substance that Buddhism had denied. The "Yogācāra-Madhyamaka" school, represented by Śāntarakṣita in the 8th century in Tibet, tried to combine the two.

## Impact

Xuanzang (602–664) transmitted the texts into Chinese and composed the "Cheng weishi lun" (659), so that the "Faxiang" (Weishi) school was founded with his pupil Kuiji (632–682), and in Japan "Hossō". Its ideas influenced East Asian Buddhism, including the concept of the storehouse consciousness in Chinese and Korean thought. The theory of Dignāga and Dharmakīrti became a foundation of Buddhist debate logic in Tibet. Some scholars have compared Yogācāra with Husserl's phenomenology and with Berkeley's idealism.

## Comparison with Islamic thought

The Yogācāra discussion of dream and reality resembles what al-Ghazali reports in "al-Munqidh min al-Dalal" when he describes the stage of doubt in sense and reason and asks whether waking may be to what comes after it as dreaming is to waking; but he emerged from doubt through a light that God cast into his breast, not through a proof. The mutakallimun counted a group of people who denied the realities of things as "sophists", and the link between them and Yogācāra is not historically established, so the comparison remains purely philosophical.

## Limits

The interpretation of the storehouse consciousness is still a matter of debate: is it a fixed consciousness resembling the self that Buddhism denied, or a continuing process? Critics faulted the school for reintroducing a continuing mental entity, and said that the denial of matter is hard to explain when people agree in perception. Historians also point to the difficulty of determining what Asaṅga wrote and what Vasubandhu wrote, since there is a dispute over the unity of Vasubandhu, author of the Abhidharma and author of the Yogācāra.
