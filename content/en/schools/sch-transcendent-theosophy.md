# Transcendent Theosophy (Mullā Ṣadrā)

Transcendent Theosophy is a philosophical doctrine formulated by Sadr al-Din Muhammad ibn Ibrahim al-Shirazi, known as Mulla Sadra (979 or 980–1050 AH / 1571 or 1572–1640 CE), in Safavid-era Iran. It is an attempt to combine four paths into a single system: Avicennian Peripatetic philosophy, Suhrawardian illuminationist wisdom, the theoretical gnosis (irfan) associated with Ibn Arabi, and the theological and religious knowledge drawn from the Qur'an and the hadith. Sadra calls his comprehensive book *The Transcendent Theosophy in the Four Intellectual Journeys*. It is among the most influential systems in late Islamic philosophy, and it is still taught in the seminaries and universities of Iran and elsewhere.

## Biography and Context

Mulla Sadra was born in Shiraz to a well-off family and studied in Isfahan, the capital of Shah Abbas I, under Mir Damad (d. 1041 AH), Shaykh Baha'i (d. 1030 AH) and Mir Abu al-Qasim Findiriski. He then withdrew under pressure from conservative jurists who were displeased with his philosophy, and spent many years in the village of Kahak near Qom, in contemplation and revision. Later he returned to Shiraz to teach at the school built by the governor of Fars, and he wrote many of his major works. He died in Basra on his way to the pilgrimage for the seventh time. His work was not a break with what came before but the product of dialogue with the commentators of Suhrawardi and Ibn Sina in Shiraz and Isfahan.

## The Major Principles

- The primacy of existence: existence is the reality that is realized in itself, while quiddity is a mental consideration that the intellect abstracts from the limits of existence. This reverses the position of Suhrawardi, who is usually read as holding quiddity to be primary, and it is close to the metaphysics of existence in the tradition of Ibn Arabi, whose direct influence on Sadra is still debated by scholars.
- The gradation (tashkik) of existence: the reality of existence is one, and its levels differ in intensity and weakness, in priority and posteriority; the difference between God and the contingent is one of degree, not of kind.
- The simplicity of reality: the simple reality is all things and is none of them; the highest existence encompasses the perfections of what lies beneath it.
- Substantial motion: corporeal nature is not fixed in its substance and moving only in its accidents, as Aristotle and Ibn Sina held; rather its very substance is in perpetual flux toward a higher perfection. With this he explained the temporal origination of the corporeal world and the ascent of the soul.
- The unity of the intellect and the intelligible: knowledge is a present existence of the knower, so the soul unites with the forms it intellects.
- Corporeal in origination, spiritual in subsistence: the soul arises from the body and then becomes independent through ascent, and the resurrection takes place in an imaginal body, in reply to those who limited it to the spiritual resurrection alone or the bodily one alone.
- The proof of the truthful (burhan al-siddiqin): establishing the Necessary Being from existence itself, not from contingent things.

## The Four Journeys

Sadra organizes his book on the journey of the gnostic: the journey from creation to the Real (on existence, quiddity and causes); then the journey in the Real by the Real (the divine names and attributes); then the journey from the Real to creation by the Real (acts and the worlds of origination); then the journey in creation by the Real (the soul and the resurrection). In his other books, *al-Masha'ir*, *al-Mabda' wa al-Ma'ad*, *al-Shawahid al-Rububiyya*, the *Commentary on Usul al-Kafi* and the *Qur'an Commentary*, he applies his method to theology, prophecy and exegesis.

## The School and Its Students

Among his students were Muhammad Muhsin Fayd Kashani (d. 1091 AH), who combined wisdom with hadith; Abd al-Razzaq Lahiji (d. 1072 AH), author of *Shawariq al-Ilham*; and Qadi Sa'id Qummi. It was then revived in the 13th century AH by Hadi Sabzawari (d. 1289 AH) with his *Sharh al-Manzuma*, and in the 14th century AH by Allama Muhammad Husayn Tabataba'i (1904–1981), who wrote *Bidayat al-Hikma* and *Nihayat al-Hikma* in Arabic as two textbooks, and who annotated the *Asfar* in the Beirut edition. Murtada Mutahhari, Abdullah Javadi Amoli and others studied under him. In the West, scholars came to know the school through Henry Corbin (1903–1978), then Seyyed Hossein Nasr and Fazlur Rahman.

## Limits and Criticism

Many jurists and usulis opposed Sadraism, among them the Akhbaris, who rejected philosophical reasoning, and some theologians who rejected the doctrine of gradation. Critics of the primacy of existence point to the difficulty of conceiving an existence stripped of quiddity. Some critics hold that the unity of graded existence comes close to the doctrine of the unity of being that many jurists reject, although Sadra himself defended God's transcendence. In the modern era, scholars hold that a number of Sadra's theses, such as the unity of the intellect and the intelligible, need careful interpretation in order to withstand the critique of contemporary epistemology.
