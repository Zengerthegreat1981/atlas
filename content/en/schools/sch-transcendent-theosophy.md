# Transcendent Theosophy (Mullā Ṣadrā)

Transcendent Theosophy is a philosophical doctrine formulated by Sadr al-Din Muhammad ibn Ibrahim al-Shirazi, known as Mulla Sadra (979 or 980–1050 AH / 1571 or 1572–1640 CE), in Safavid-era Iran. It is an attempt to combine four paths into a single system: Avicennian Peripatetic philosophy, Suhrawardian illuminationist wisdom, the theoretical gnosis (irfan) associated with Ibn Arabi, and the theological and religious knowledge drawn from the Qur'an and the hadith. Sadra calls his comprehensive book *The Transcendent Theosophy in the Four Intellectual Journeys*. It is one of the most influential systems in late Islamic philosophy, and it is still taught in the seminaries and universities of Iran and elsewhere.

## Biography and Context

Mulla Sadra was born in Shiraz to a well-off family and studied in Isfahan, the capital of Shah Abbas I, under Mir Damad (d. 1041 AH), Shaykh Baha'i (d. 1030 AH) and Mir Abu al-Qasim Findiriski. He then withdrew under pressure from conservative jurists who were displeased with his philosophy, and spent many years in the village of Kahak near Qom, in contemplation and revision. Later he returned to Shiraz to teach at the school built by the governor of Fars, and he wrote many of his major works. He died in Basra on his way to the pilgrimage for the seventh time. His work was not a break with what came before but the product of dialogue with the commentators of Suhrawardi and Ibn Sina in Shiraz and Isfahan.

## The Major Principles

- The primacy of existence (*aṣālat al-wujūd*): existence is the objective reality that is realized in itself, while quiddity (*māhiyya*) is a mental consideration that the intellect abstracts from the limitations of determinate existence. This inverts the position of Suhrawardī, who regarded quiddity as primary and existence as a mental construct (*iʿtibārī*).
- The gradation (*tashkīk*) of existence: the reality of existence is one, yet its degrees vary in intensity, priority, and perfection; the ontological difference between the Necessary Being and contingent realities is one of degree rather than categorical genus and species.
- The simplicity of reality (*basīṭ al-ḥaqīqa*): a simple reality is all things in an eminent manner while being none of them in their particular deficiencies; the highest existence encompasses the perfections of all lower orders.
- Substantial motion (*al-ḥaraka al-jawhariyya*): physical reality is not static in its substance and moving only in its accidents, as Aristotle and Avicenna maintained; rather, material substances undergo continuous ontological transformation toward higher spiritual realization.
- The unity of the intellect and the intelligible (*ittiḥād al-ʿāqil wa-l-maʿqūl*): knowledge is not a mere passive impression of external forms, but an existential presence in which the intellective soul unites with the intelligible forms it conceives.
- Corporeal in origination, spiritual in subsistence (*jismāniyyat al-ḥudūth rūḥāniyyat al-baqāʾ*): the human soul originates through the development of the physical organism and ascends toward immaterial independence, resurrecting in an imaginal realm (*ʿālam al-mithāl*).
- The proof of the truthful (*burhān al-ṣiddīqīn*): establishing the existence of God directly from the contemplation of existence itself, rather than inferring it from contingent creatures or physical motion.

## The Influence of Ibn ʿArabī and Waḥdat al-Wujūd

The relationship between Mullā Ṣadrā and the mystical tradition of Ibn ʿArabī and his commentators (notably Ṣadr al-Dīn al-Qūnawī, Dāwūd al-Qayṣarī, and ʿAbd al-Razzāq al-Kāshānī) is central to Transcendent Theosophy. Ṣadrā integrated the theoretical gnosis (*ʿirfān naẓarī*) of Akbarian metaphysics into a rigorous philosophical discourse:
- **From Gradation to Unity**: Some modern commentators have observed a progression in the *Asfār*. Ṣadrā begins by defending the systematic gradation (*tashkīk*) of multiple existents to satisfy discursive philosophical inquiry, but advances in later volumes to a unified metaphysical monism akin to Ibn ʿArabī's *waḥdat al-wujūd* (the unity of being).
- **Relational Existence (*wujūd rābiṭ*)**: In Ṣadrā's culminating ontology, contingent entities do not possess independent, self-standing existence that merely receives divine light; rather, they are sheer relations, dependencies, and connective manifestations (*rawābiṭ*) of the single, absolute Reality. Multiple phenomena are the epiphanies (*tajalliyāt*) and determinations (*shuʾūn*) of the divine essence, reconciling rational demonstration with unitive mystical perception.

## The Four Journeys

Sadra organizes his book on the journey of the gnostic: the journey from creation to the Real (on existence, quiddity and causes); then the journey in the Real by the Real (the divine names and attributes); then the journey from the Real to creation by the Real (acts and the worlds of origination); then the journey in creation by the Real (the soul and the resurrection). In his other books, *al-Masha'ir*, *al-Mabda' wa al-Ma'ad*, *al-Shawahid al-Rububiyya*, the *Commentary on Usul al-Kafi* and the *Qur'an Commentary*, he applies his method to theology, prophecy and exegesis.

## The School and Its Students

Among his students were Muhammad Muhsin Fayd Kashani (d. 1091 AH), who combined wisdom with hadith; Abd al-Razzaq Lahiji (d. 1072 AH), author of *Shawariq al-Ilham*; and later figures such as Qadi Sa'id Qummi belong to the same current. It was then revived in the 13th century AH by Hadi Sabzawari (d. 1289 AH) with his *Sharh al-Manzuma*, and in the 14th century AH by Allama Muhammad Husayn Tabataba'i (1904–1981), who wrote *Bidayat al-Hikma* and *Nihayat al-Hikma* in Arabic as two textbooks, and who annotated the *Asfar* in the Beirut edition. Murtada Mutahhari, Abdullah Javadi Amoli and others studied under him. In the West, scholars came to know the school through Henry Corbin (1903–1978), then Seyyed Hossein Nasr and Fazlur Rahman.

## Limits and Criticism

Many jurists and usulis opposed Sadraism, among them the Akhbaris, who rejected philosophical reasoning, and some theologians who rejected the doctrine of gradation. Critics of the primacy of existence point to the difficulty of conceiving an existence stripped of quiddity. Some critics hold that the unity of graded existence comes close to the doctrine of the unity of being that many jurists reject, although Sadra himself defended God's transcendence. In the modern era, scholars hold that a number of Sadra's theses, such as the unity of the intellect and the intelligible, need careful interpretation in order to withstand the critique of contemporary epistemology.
