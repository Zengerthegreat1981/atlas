# Psychological Anthropology

Psychological anthropology is the study of how culture and mind shape each other. It asks how people in different societies experience emotion, develop a sense of self, understand illness and healing, raise children, and make sense of dreams, spirits, and the sacred. Where psychology often seeks universal laws of mental life, psychological anthropology insists that mental life is always lived inside a culture — and that culture, in turn, is carried and remade inside individual minds.

## History and Origins

The field descends from the "culture and personality" school of early twentieth-century American anthropology. Franz Boas argued that culture shapes the mind, and his students took the argument further: Ruth Benedict's Patterns of Culture (1934) described whole societies as having distinctive psychological configurations, while Margaret Mead's Coming of Age in Samoa (1928) showed that adolescence, emotion, and temperament varied with culture. In the 1930s and 1940s, psychoanalytically trained anthropologists such as Abram Kardiner, author of The Individual and His Society (1939), and Cora Du Bois, author of The People of Alor (1944), tried to connect childrearing practices to adult personality, developing the idea of the "basic personality structure" of a society. Ralph Linton worked with Kardiner on that formulation, supplying ethnographic material on shared institutions. Edward Sapir argued that a culture cannot be set apart from the individuals who carry it. Wartime studies of national character, including Ruth Benedict's account of Japan, stretched the same methods to whole states and drew criticism for sweeping generalisation.

After the Second World War, the approach came under criticism for overgeneralising about whole cultures and for relying on untested psychoanalytic theory. John Whiting and Irvin Child's Child Training and Personality: A Cross-Cultural Study (1953) offered a more rigorous alternative, using systematic cross-cultural comparison to link childrearing practices to personality traits. Francis L. K. Hsu's edited volume Psychological Anthropology (1961; revised edition 1972) helped popularise the new name for the field, signalling a break from the older culture-and-personality tradition, and the Society for Psychological Anthropology was founded in 1977 as a section of the American Anthropological Association, with its journal Ethos established in 1973.

From the 1970s onward, the field diversified. Cognitive anthropologists such as Ward Goodenough and Roy D'Andrade studied how people categorise the world and how cultural knowledge is organised in the mind. Melford Spiro examined religion and the unconscious, and Gananath Obeyesekere brought psychoanalytic insight to Sri Lankan spirit possession and Buddhist practice. The interpretive turn associated with Clifford Geertz's The Interpretation of Cultures (1973) shifted attention from personality types to meaning — to how people interpret their experience in culturally specific ways.

## Key Figures and Works

Richard Shweder's Thinking Through Cultures (1991) is a landmark of the field, arguing for a cultural psychology in which the mind and the cultural world are mutually constitutive. Shweder's studies of moral reasoning in India showed that ideas about purity, duty, and the self differed fundamentally from American ones, challenging the assumption of universal moral psychology. Robert LeVine's work on child development in Africa linked childrearing to social and economic conditions.

Robert I. Levy's Tahitians: Mind and Experience in the Society Islands (1973) became a model ethnography of emotion, showing how Tahitians experience and talk about feelings in ways shaped by their cultural emphasis on self-control and social harmony. Unni Wikan's Managing Turbulent Hearts: A Balinese Formula for Living (1990) explored how Balinese people manage emotion through cultural practices of concealment and composure. Vincent Crapanzano's The Hamadsha: A Study in Moroccan Ethnopsychiatry (1973) examined healing rituals and spirit possession as forms of psychological experience. Jean Briggs's Never in Anger (1970) described emotional training in an Inuit family from the uneasy place she occupied inside it. Michelle Rosaldo treated Ilongot emotion as bound up with social relations and judgment. Catherine Lutz argued, from fieldwork on Ifaluk, that a Western split between emotion and reason shapes what researchers notice. Levy also showed that Tahitians elaborate some feelings and leave others little named. A translated emotion word does not by itself establish an identical experience, and a vocabulary list does not show what people feel. Thomas Csordas used embodiment to interpret charismatic healing.

More recent work includes Tanya Luhrmann's Of Two Minds: An Anthropologist Looks at American Psychiatry (2000), an ethnography of American psychiatrists that treats biomedicine itself as a culture, and her later work on prayer, voice-hearing, and religious experience. The field has also been shaped by the study of the self and personhood, notably in the volume Culture Theory: Essays on Mind, Self, and Emotion (1984), edited by Richard Shweder and Robert LeVine, and by the anthropology of the senses and embodiment.

## Method

Psychological anthropology combines ethnography with methods borrowed from psychology. Researchers conduct long-term participant observation, life-history interviews, and the systematic study of everyday interaction, alongside psychological tests, dream collection, and the recording of narratives and conversations. A distinctive method is the person-centred interview, developed by Robert Levy and others, which explores how individuals experience their lives, relationships, and emotions in their own terms.

The field also uses cross-cultural comparison to test claims about universality. The Human Relations Area Files and the work of the Whitings' Six Cultures project — notably Children of Six Cultures (1975), by Beatrice Whiting and John Whiting — used standardised observation of children in six societies to compare socialisation and social behaviour. More recently, researchers combine ethnography with experimental and cognitive methods, studying, for example, how people in different cultures reason about minds, spirits, and moral responsibility.

## Influence and Contemporary Directions

Psychological anthropology has influenced cultural psychology, developmental psychology, and psychiatry. Its critique of universalist models helped produce the field of cultural psychiatry, and its ethnographies of emotion challenged the assumption that basic emotions are the same everywhere. Its work on the self and personhood shaped debates in philosophy and cognitive science about the nature of consciousness and agency.

Contemporary directions include the anthropology of mental health and the global circulation of psychiatric categories; the study of trauma, memory, and post-conflict healing; research on religious experience and the psychology of belief; and the study of ageing, dementia, and care. The field also engages with neuroscience, asking how cultural experience shapes the brain and how biological and cultural accounts of mind can be integrated. Its enduring contribution is a double insistence: that the mind is always cultural, and that culture is always psychological — lived, felt, and remade by persons.

## Debates

Scholars disagree about psychic universals, the explanatory reach of psychoanalysis, and how experiments should sit alongside fieldwork. Early studies often treated a society as one personality type. Later work gives more weight to variation, history, and inequality. Comparison still has to avoid both assuming that minds are the same and treating difference as absolute.
