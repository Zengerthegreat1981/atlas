# First Language Acquisition and Its Stages

A branch of psycholinguistics that studies how children acquire their native language rapidly and systematically, despite the limited linguistic input they receive. It spans the period from the first sounds (babbling) to the mastery of complex grammar in early childhood.

## The Central Question: Innateness or Usage?

This branch intersects directly with the open debate on **linguistic innateness**. The generative tradition (influenced by Chomsky) holds that the speed with which a child acquires their language is evidence for a prior innate grammatical structure (universal grammar). The usage-based tradition (Tomasello and others) holds that acquisition is a gradual learning process driven by repeated exposure to actual language use, requiring no specialized innate grammatical structure. **This debate remains open.**

## Biological Foundations: The Critical Period Hypothesis

Eric Lenneberg (1967) proposed a biological framework linking the capacity for language acquisition to a neural plasticity that is limited in time (a critical period extending from childhood to around puberty). The framework is supported by documented exceptional cases of severe early linguistic deprivation, most notably the case of "Genie," discovered in 1970 after near-total deprivation of linguistic communication until the age of thirteen. Despite later intensive training, she never fully mastered complex grammar. Her case has been cited as evidence for a critical age threshold, although the severe abuse and general deprivation she suffered make it impossible to isolate language as the only factor, so it is treated with caution.

## The Successive Stages of Acquisition

Acquisition passes through successive stages that have been documented in detail across many languages. **Babbling** begins around the sixth month, when the infant produces repeated syllables (such as "baba" or "dada") regardless of the surrounding language. Next is the **one-word stage** (holophrastic stage) near the first birthday, when a single word carries the meaning of a full sentence in context. Then comes the **two-word stage**, around eighteen months, in which the first rudimentary grammatical combinations appear ("mama water"). This is followed by a rapid expansion in sentence complexity and vocabulary that continues until roughly the age of five or six, by which time the child has mastered most of the basic grammatical rules of their language.

## Berko's Wug Test and Overregularization

Jean Berko Gleason provided a pivotal experimental demonstration (the Wug Test, 1958) that children do not memorize words mechanically but instead derive productive morphological rules. When children were shown a picture of a single imaginary creature called a "wug" and then asked to complete "these are two...", most answered "wugs," although they had never heard this word before. A related phenomenon is **overregularization**, in which children apply the regular rule to irregular verbs after learning the rule (saying "goed" instead of "went"), even though they had pronounced these verbs correctly at an earlier stage as stored words. This pattern became a central battleground between rule-based symbolic models and statistical connectionist models (the past-tense network of Rumelhart and McClelland, 1986, and the critique by Pinker and Prince, 1988).

## Child-Directed Speech

The nature of the speech that adults direct to children (child-directed speech, or motherese) plays an important role in facilitating acquisition: a higher and more varied pitch, shorter and grammatically simpler sentences, and deliberate repetition of new words in varied contexts. These features help the infant segment a continuous stream of speech into distinct phonetic and lexical units.

## Impact

This branch established an active research field that brings together linguistics, developmental psychology, and neuroscience. It remains a central arena for the innateness-versus-usage debate in explaining the unique linguistic capacity of humans. Exceptional testimonies, such as Helen Keller's experience at the water pump (her sudden grasp of linguistic symbolism in 1887, shortly before her seventh birthday, despite the loss of hearing and sight in infancy), have also enriched the discussion of the decisive moment in acquiring the principle of linguistic symbolism itself.

## The Development of Sound and Morphology

Phonological acquisition begins before birth in practice: research by Anthony DeCasper, Jacques Mehler and others indicates that newborns prefer their mother's voice and the rhythm of the language heard in the womb over other sounds. Morphologically, children follow a regular path in acquiring grammatical affixes (such as the plural and past tense in English), in a nearly fixed order. Roger Brown documented this order in his landmark longitudinal study of three children (Adam, Eve, and Sarah), proposing that this order reflects an underlying shared semantic and grammatical complexity regardless of individual differences between children.

## Catherine Snow and the Social Interaction in Acquisition

In contrast to the generative focus on innate structure, Catherine Snow emphasized the social interactive role in acquisition. She showed how adults gradually adjust their child-directed speech according to the child's actual level of linguistic development, in what resembles the "scaffolding" described by Jerome Bruner, which supports the child's progress step by step, rather than a passive, fixed-difficulty linguistic input.
