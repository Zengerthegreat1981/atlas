# Legal Pluralism

A field of legal theory that starts from a simple but deeply consequential empirical observation: **in most societies, there is no single exclusive legal system governing the behavior of individuals**. Rather, several normative systems coexist: the official law of the state, local customs, religious laws, and the rules of ethnic or commercial communities. These may conflict or complement one another.

## Historical Precedent: Pluralism of Schools in Islamic Jurisprudence

Long before this question became an academic field in the West under its contemporary name, Islamic jurisprudence had, for centuries, an internal structural experience of legal pluralism: the plurality of the **jurisprudential schools** (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafii, Hanbali and others), which operated simultaneously on the same revealed texts without any one of them monopolizing exclusive authority of interpretation. This was theorized by the science of **Usul al-Fiqh** through the concept of *ijtihad* and the legitimacy of the diversity of its rulings (the distinction between a difference of variety, *ikhtilaf tanawwu'*, and a difference of contradiction, *ikhtilaf tadadd*, found in classical sources). The Ottoman **millet** system, and earlier arrangements for the *dhimmi* communities (Jews and Christians) in Islamic states, added another layer of pluralism: the application of personal status laws within the scope of their own religious courts, in parallel with the Islamic law governing Muslims and public affairs. This is a historical model of what Griffiths would later call "weak" or juristic legal pluralism, since the official system itself explicitly recognized the plurality of sources of legal authority according to religious affiliation.

## The Definitional Question: What Counts as "Law" at All?

Legal pluralists challenge a common implicit assumption in legal positivism (especially in **Kelsen** and **Hart**): that "law" is defined by a single source of reference (the state, or a single rule of recognition). In his foundational article "What is Legal Pluralism?" (1986), **John Griffiths** argues that this is **legal centralism**, which describes reality in an idealized rather than a realistic way. Even within modern states, multiple normative systems actually operate side by side: a commercial dispute may be formally subject to state law while actually being resolved through customary arbitration within a particular commercial community.

## Types: "Strong" versus "Weak" Pluralism

Griffiths distinguishes between **weak (juristic) legal pluralism** (in which the official legal system itself explicitly recognizes the plurality of sources of legal authority, as in the multiple religious personal status systems in some states, including the personal status systems derived from Islamic law and applied to this day in many countries alongside a general civil code) and **strong (deep, or social-scientific) legal pluralism** (the existence of multiple actual normative systems regardless of the official recognition of the state, such as commercial or community customs that operate in practice in isolation from, or even in conflict with, official law).

## The Anthropological Contribution: Sally Engle Merry

In her reference article "Legal Pluralism" (1988), **Sally Engle Merry** developed this framework from an empirical anthropological angle, proposing a distinction between **classical legal pluralism** (studied by colonial sociologists, in which local customs confronted European law in the colonies) and **new legal pluralism** (which appears within modern Western societies themselves, where norms of neighborhood, work and family, and the rules of private institutions compete with the official law of the state, without necessarily bearing a colonial or religious character). Anthropologists have also described how individuals in everyday practice may choose among the available normative systems (*forum shopping*, a term associated with Keebet and Franz von Benda-Beckmann), selecting the forum that serves their interest in a particular dispute, regardless of the formal hierarchy assumed between the systems.

## Critique and Theoretical Limitations

The field faces a recurring methodological critique: if every effective social norm is counted as "law" in the weak pluralist sense (club rules, family traditions, university regulations), the concept of "law" may lose its analytical boundaries and become a broad synonym for any "normative system" whatsoever. This objection has been raised by some legal positivists (including critical readings of Hart's legacy) against the definitional expansion found in Griffiths and his followers. Nevertheless, the field has remained highly influential in colonial and postcolonial studies, in understanding the multiple personal status systems of states with mixed religious majorities, and in analyzing international commercial arbitration as a normative system that is quasi-independent of the laws of states.

## Contemporary Applications: Postcolonial States and International Law

The tools of legal pluralism are today used intensively in the study of postcolonial states, many of which inherited a hybrid legal structure directly from the colonial past: a civil or public law imported from the colonizer, coexisting with local tribal customs and with religious rulings (Islamic, Hindu or other) in matters of family and inheritance in particular. Researchers analyze this coexistence as an ongoing arena of political negotiation, not merely a technical gap presumed to be resolved by complete legal unification, since each of these systems carries deeply rooted social legitimacy that is difficult to abolish by legislative decree. At the international level, the concept of legal pluralism is also invoked to describe the tense relationship between general international law, national state laws, the legal systems specific to international organizations, and multinational corporations, where no single decisive hierarchy determines which prevails in every case.

## Impact on General Legal Theory

Legal pluralism changed the place of the question "what is law?" in the academic field. Instead of assuming that the answer must be a single, comprehensive and exclusive definition (as Kelsen sought through the *Grundnorm*, or Hart through the "rule of recognition"), pluralism proposes that the definition itself should remain empirically open to the diversity of actual practices of social normativity across cultures and times. This position brought it closer to some currents of legal realism and to critical social studies of law, and made it an interdisciplinary field bringing together comparative legal scholars, anthropologists and legal sociologists, rather than remaining a marginal sub-field as it was at its origins in the 1970s and 1980s.
