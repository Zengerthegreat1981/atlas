# Urban Sociology and City Planning

Jane Jacobs's influential critique of destructive modern urban planning practices, and her defense of the organic social vitality of traditional, mixed-use urban neighborhoods.

## The Central Idea

In *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961), Jane Jacobs (1916–2006), an activist and writer rather than an academic by formal training, criticized the dominant modernist urban planning model (strict separation between residential and commercial zones, isolated residential towers, expressways cutting through neighborhoods). She held that this model destroys the unplanned "organic order" that arises naturally in dense, traditional, mixed-use urban neighborhoods. She argued that lively, safe streets depend on "Eyes on the Street": the constant presence of residents and shopkeepers who spontaneously watch the public space throughout the day, thanks to the diversity of uses within the same building (housing, shops and offices side by side) and the diversity of building ages and densities. They do not depend on a clean architectural design imposed from above, which separates functions and empties the street of daily life outside certain hours.

## Historical Context

Jane Jacobs wrote *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (1961) after years of journalistic work following urban planning projects in New York City. There she directly observed the damage done by the "Urban Renewal" programs then dominated by the powerful planner Robert Moses. These programs demolished traditional, densely populated urban neighborhoods in favor of isolated residential towers and wide expressways. (One example is Greenwich Village, where she lived and where she took part in successful campaigns against road projects, including a proposed extension of Fifth Avenue through Washington Square Park and, later, the Lower Manhattan Expressway.) The programs drew on the ideas of the Swiss-French planner Le Corbusier and Ebenezer Howard's "Garden City" model, which dominated modernist urban planning thought in the mid-twentieth century.

## Main Ideas

Jacobs, an activist and writer rather than an academic by formal training, criticized the dominant modernist urban planning model (strict functional separation between residential, commercial and industrial zones, isolated residential towers surrounded by green spaces empty of daily activity, and expressways cutting through traditional neighborhoods and tearing their fabric). She held that this model destroys the unplanned "organic order" that arises naturally in dense, traditional, mixed-use urban neighborhoods. She argued that lively, safe streets depend on what she called "Eyes on the Street": the constant, spontaneous presence of residents and shopkeepers who watch the public space throughout the different hours of the day. This presence rests on the diversity of uses within a single building (housing above shops, offices next to restaurants); on the diversity of building ages and densities (old, low-rent buildings alongside new ones, allowing economic and social diversity among residents and merchants); and on short residential blocks (Short Blocks), which encourage varied movement on foot instead of exclusive reliance on the car. She regarded this organic diversity, and not a clean architectural design imposed from above that separates functions and empties the street of daily life outside certain hours, as the real guarantee of urban safety and resistance to crime, rather than the police or architectural barriers alone.

## Major Works and Extensions

Jacobs developed her thesis further in later works such as *The Economy of Cities* (1969), in which she argued that cities are the main engines of economic growth and, controversially, that agriculture itself first developed out of urban economies. Herbert Gans's field study *The Urban Villagers* (1962), on Boston's Italian-American neighborhood (the West End), complemented Jacobs's argument from a different angle. It showed how the neighborhood, apparently "poor" in the eyes of planners, actually sheltered a rich and cohesive social network that its demolition for "urban renewal" destroyed. This actual destruction of neighborhoods was later summed up in the slogan "urban renewal is Negro removal" (associated with James Baldwin in 1963), reflecting its disproportionate effect on poor and Black neighborhoods.

## Impact and Critique

This critique deeply influenced the reorientation of global urban planning away from the modernist "functional separation" model toward designs more respectful of the existing organic social fabric. It became a foundational reference in urban studies and influenced the later "New Urbanism" movement, which promotes walkable, mixed-use neighborhoods. Some critics observed that the success of Jacobs's model in particular neighborhoods (such as Greenwich Village itself) later coincided with sharply rising property values and rents there. Neighborhoods "saved" in this way thus became vulnerable to gentrification, which pushed out less wealthy residents.
