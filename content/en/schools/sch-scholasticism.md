# Scholasticism (umbrella)

A tradition of philosophical-theological teaching and method that dominated European schools and universities from roughly the eleventh century to the seventeenth. The name comes from the Latin *scholasticus*, "of the school", because its principal activity took place in cathedral schools, monasteries and later universities (in Arabic, sometimes rendered *al-Madrasiyya*, from *madrasa*). It sought to reason systematically about Christian doctrine and to reconcile faith with philosophical reason, above all with the logic and natural philosophy of Aristotle, drawing on the Church Fathers (Augustine) and Boethius, and, from the twelfth century, on Arabic philosophy (Avicenna, Averroes) through Latin translations made in Toledo and elsewhere. It is a method and an institutional setting rather than a single doctrine, and it includes opposed positions (Thomist, Scotist, Ockhamist, Augustinian).

## Founding context

The Gregorian reform of the Church (the pontificate of Gregory VII, 1073–1085) and the growth of towns encouraged cathedral schools such as Laon, Chartres and Paris. In the twelfth century, translations of Aristotle's logical, physical and metaphysical works from Greek and Arabic reached the Latin West, producing a major intellectual shock. Universities took shape around 1200 (Paris and Oxford in the late twelfth century and early thirteenth, Bologna as a law school from the late eleventh century, Toulouse founded 1229) and became the main centres of scholastic work. The distinction between scholastic theology and "medieval philosophy" is itself disputed: many scholastics were professional theologians, and philosophy was taught in the arts faculties as preparation for theology.

## Leading figures

### The formative period (eleventh and twelfth centuries)

- **Anselm of Canterbury** (1033–1109): the motto "faith seeking understanding" (*fides quaerens intellectum*); the *Proslogion* and its ontological argument; *Cur Deus Homo*.
- **Peter Abelard** (1079–1142): logician and theologian, author of *Sic et Non* (a collection of conflicting authorities) and *Ethics*.
- **Peter Lombard** (c. 1096–1160): author of the *Sentences*, the standard theological textbook for centuries.
- **Bernard of Clairvaux** (1090–1153): a monastic critic of Abelard.

### The thirteenth century

- **Albertus Magnus** (c. 1200–1280): teacher of Aquinas and a great commentator on Aristotle.
- **Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274): see Thomism.
- **Bonaventure** (1221–1274): Franciscan theologian in an Augustinian tradition, critical of some Aristotelian positions.
- **Roger Bacon** (c. 1214–1292): Oxford Franciscan interested in languages, optics and experimental science.
- **John Duns Scotus** (c. 1266–1308): the "subtle doctor", known for the univocity of being and for his account of the will.

### The fourteenth century and later

- **William of Ockham** (c. 1287–1347): see Ockhamism.
- **Nicole Oresme** (c. 1320–1382): natural philosopher and economic writer.
- **Pierre d'Ailly** (1351–1420).
- **Francisco de Vitoria** (c. 1483–1546) and **Francisco Suárez** (1548–1617): leading figures of the Spanish "Second Scholasticism".

## Central concepts

- **Faith and reason**: scholastic theology starts from revealed doctrine and asks what reason can show about it. Most scholastics held that truth is one, so that faith and philosophy cannot truly contradict each other. The "double truth" doctrine is not Aquinas's; it was a charge laid against certain Parisian Aristotelians in the condemnations of 1270 and 1277.
- **Anselm's argument**: God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived"; such a being cannot exist in the mind alone, since then something greater could be conceived; therefore it exists in reality. Aquinas and many others rejected it; Kant later criticized it.
- **Aquinas's Five Ways**: arguments for God's existence from motion, efficient causation, contingency and necessity, degrees of perfection, and the order of nature.
- **Creation**: scholastics combined Aristotelian metaphysics (four causes, act and potency) with creation from nothing (*creatio ex nihilo*).
- **The problem of universals**: whether general terms such as "humanity" refer to real common natures. The main positions were realism, in various forms (Anselm, Scotus); nominalism (Roscelin, Ockham), which denies real universals outside the mind; and moderate or "conceptualist" views (Abelard, Aquinas), which locate universals in things and in the mind abstracting from them.
- **Natural law**: universal moral principles accessible to reason, given its classic scholastic formulation by Aquinas, and developed later by Spanish scholastics such as Vitoria and Suárez in discussions of rights and international law.
- **Ontology**: the term "ontology" itself arose in early seventeenth-century Protestant scholasticism (Rudolf Goclenius, 1613; Johannes Clauberg).

## Methods

- **Lectio**: reading and exposition of an authoritative text.
- **Quaestio**: posing a question and weighing arguments on both sides.
- **Disputatio**: formal debate, including the "quodlibetal" disputations on any topic.
- **Determinatio**: the master's resolution of the question.

## Schools within Scholasticism

- **Chartres and the Victorines**: twelfth-century schools with Platonist and mystical tendencies.
- **Franciscan and Dominican traditions**: Bonaventure and Scotus on one side, Albert and Aquinas on the other.
- **Latin Averroism** (Siger of Brabant and others): a radical Aristotelianism that raised the question of the autonomy of philosophy.
- **Nominalism** (Ockham and followers) and the *via moderna*.
- **Second Scholasticism**: sixteenth- and seventeenth-century Iberian renewal, in part a response to the Reformation.

## Later influence

- **Reformation and humanism**: Luther, Calvin and Renaissance humanists attacked scholastic method and the use of Aristotle in theology, though Protestant scholasticism developed in the seventeenth century.
- **Early modern philosophy**: Descartes, Leibniz and others were trained in scholastic traditions and often reacted against them, while Kant examined the traditional proofs of God's existence.
- **Law and political thought**: scholastic natural-law theory contributed to later thinking about rights and the law of nations.
- **Science**: scholastic natural philosophy (for example on motion, in Buridan and Oresme) formed part of the background to early modern science.

## Limits and criticisms

- **Authority and method**: critics from the Renaissance onward charged scholasticism with arid logic-chopping and excessive deference to Aristotle.
- **Faith and reason**: the relation of philosophy to theology was contested within the schools (for instance in the Parisian condemnations of 1277, which also touched some positions of Aquinas; Aquinas was canonized in 1323 and the Paris condemnation of his propositions was revoked in 1325).
- **Universals**: the dispute was never settled and continued into modern philosophy.
- **Definition**: historians disagree about how far "scholasticism" names a doctrine, a method or a historical period.
