# Constitutional Law Theory

A field of legal and political philosophy that examines the foundations and structure of constitutional authority: how public power is distributed among the organs of the state, what makes the supreme law (the constitution) binding on all other laws, and who holds the right to interpret it and to review whether legislation conforms to it.

## Intellectual Foundations

- **Separation of powers**: In *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), the French philosopher Montesquieu formulated the founding principle on which liberal constitutionalists have relied ever since: distributing public power among three distinct branches (legislative, executive, judicial) so that each checks the others and no single body can monopolize absolute power.
- **Judicial review of constitutionality (Judicial Review)**: *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) before the U.S. Supreme Court established the principle that legislation contrary to the constitution is void, and that the judiciary is the authority that determines the constitutionality of a law. It was the first precedent in which the U.S. Supreme Court nullified a law enacted by Congress, and it became the reference model that other constitutional systems around the world adopted or modified (the specialized constitutional courts of continental Europe follow a different pattern from the decentralized American model).
- **Federalism and subsidiarity**: Theories of distributing power vertically between a central government and sub-national units (states, provinces), and the principle of subsidiarity, which holds that functions should be assigned to the lowest level of government capable of performing them effectively.
- **Checks and balances**: Concrete institutional mechanisms that give practical form to the separation of powers, such as the executive veto, legislative confirmation power, and judicial review.
- **Bill of rights and constitutional rights**: The foundational part that defines the basic rights and freedoms that individuals may invoke against the state, and which usually forms the most rigid part in terms of amendment procedure.

## Competing Models of Separation of Powers

Scholars commonly contrast a **formalist** approach to the separation of powers, which insists on strict boundaries between the legislative, executive and judicial functions, with a **functionalist** approach, which accepts a degree of overlap so long as no branch is able to undermine the core role of another. Political scientists add that in practice the discipline among branches also depends on factors outside the constitutional text, such as party competition.

## Limits

- The debate over the limits of judicial review (do unelected judges have the right to nullify a law issued by elected representatives?), a problem Alexander Bickel named the "counter-majoritarian difficulty" in *The Least Dangerous Branch* (1962), has not been resolved theoretically and remains one of the most vital questions in contemporary constitutional theory.
- Comparative constitutional models (American, continental European, German federal) differ structurally to a large degree, so generalizing a single "theory" across all of them is a matter of interpretive judgment.
