# Cognitive Science of Religion

A research current that emerged in the early 1990s seeks to explain the widespread occurrence of religious beliefs and practices across cultures by tracing them to general human cognitive structures and evolutionary mechanisms, rather than to the doctrinal content specific to each tradition. Its leading figures, Pascal Boyer, Justin Barrett and Scott Atran, all drew on tools from cognitive psychology and evolutionary anthropology. Stewart Guthrie had preceded them with *Faces in the Clouds* (1993), a theory holding that religion is a systematic form of anthropomorphism arising from a perceptual strategy that assumes agency in ambiguous situations. E. Thomas Lawson and Robert McCauley, in *Rethinking Religion* (1990), set out a cognitive programme for explaining ritual.

## Central Thesis

According to this current, religion is not an arbitrary cultural structure but a by-product of cognitive mechanisms that evolved for purposes other than religion: detecting agents (predator and prey), inferring the mental states of others (Theory of Mind), and the selective memory of concepts that are slightly counter-intuitive. This explains why religious beliefs resemble one another across distant cultures that had no contact with each other: they exploit the shared cognitive "software" of the human species. Boyer set out the thesis in *The Naturalness of Religious Ideas* (1994) and then in *Religion Explained* (2001), emphasising that religious ideas spread because they fit the systems of intuitive inference, not merely because they are emotionally appealing.

## The Two Core Mechanisms

- Minimally counter-intuitive concepts: Religious beings (gods, spirits, ancestors) are mentioned and transmitted easily because they violate only one intuitive expectation of their category (a being that has a mind but no body), while the rest of their properties remain as expected. This makes them memorable without being chaotic and hard to recall. Experiments by Boyer and Ramble (2001) on the memory of concepts across several cultures supported this.
- Hyperactive agency detection: An evolved cognitive tendency to attribute an obscure movement or sound to an intentional agent even when no agent is present, because the cost of a false positive (imagining an agent that does not exist) is lower than the cost of a false negative (missing a real predator). According to Barrett, this tendency produces a natural cognitive readiness to believe in invisible agents. His work with Keil (1996) on the conception of God in the mind, between the natural and the abstract, supports this claim.

## The Social Dimension: Costly Signalling

Alongside the individual cognitive explanations of Boyer and Barrett, Scott Atran added a functional social dimension, drawing on the work of William Irons and Richard Sosis: costly signalling theory. This holds that costly religious practices (fasting, arduous rituals, taboos) are honest signals of an individual's commitment to the group, which solve the "free-rider" problem and raise cooperative trust within the group. In *In Gods We Trust* (2002), Atran connects the individual cognitive explanation with a group-level explanation of religion's survival and its cultural-evolutionary success. Ara Norenzayan extended this line in *Big Gods* (2013), linking belief in a watchful god to the expansion of large societies.

## Whitehouse's Modes of Religiosity

Harvey Whitehouse adds another structural dimension, based on fieldwork in Papua New Guinea rather than on desk-based reflection alone. He distinguishes between the "doctrinal mode" (rituals that are repeated at high frequency and transmitted through semantic memory and organised teaching) and the "imagistic mode" (rare rituals of high emotional intensity stored in episodic memory), as set out in *Inside the Cult* (1995) and *Modes of Religiosity* (2004). This distinction explains a structural, institutional variation between religions (centralised versus decentralised leadership) that the individual-cognitive theories of Boyer and Barrett do not explain on their own.

## Position within the Field's Methodological Map

This current occupies the reductive pole, opposite the non-reductive phenomenology of religion (Otto and Eliade). The latter holds that religion has a specific essence (*sui generis*) that cannot be reduced, whereas cognitive scientists hold that its scientific explanation requires reducing it to general cognitive mechanisms that are not religious in nature. It also shares with Durkheim's sociology the character of an "external" explanation of religion, but it replaces social function with an individual cognitive structure. It differs from attachment theory, which locates the origin of religiosity in an emotional relationship rather than in inferential mechanisms.

## Criticism

This current is criticised from several directions:

1. By phenomenologists and theologians, for reducing religious experience to a neural mechanism without explaining its lived meaning for the believer.
2. From within cognitive science itself, on the grounds that "by-product" explanations are difficult to refute empirically and may over-generalise across highly diverse religious systems.
3. From the evolutionary selectionist school (David Sloan Wilson, *Darwin's Cathedral*, 2002), which holds that religion is a collective adaptation rather than a by-product.
4. From anthropology, on the grounds that most experiments were conducted on Western university samples and that the notion of "counter-intuitiveness" is partly cultural.
5. From researchers working on what is called "theological incorrectness" (Jason Slone, 2004), who argue that believers state an abstract doctrine but act on an anthropomorphic intuition, which complicates the measurement of belief.
