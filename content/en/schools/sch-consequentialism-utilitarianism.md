# Consequentialism and Utilitarianism

A broader umbrella that brings together consequentialist ethical theories, which judge the rightness or wrongness of an act by its outcome rather than by its intrinsic properties. The most famous and historically most elaborated of these is utilitarianism, which determines the value of an act by the extent to which it maximizes the total welfare or happiness of those it affects.

## Intellectual Foundations

- **Consequentialism as a general framework**: any ethical theory holding that acts are evaluated solely by their results, not by the agent's intention or the nature of the act itself. Utilitarianism is the most widespread and historically influential consequentialist formulation, but there are other consequentialist formulations (such as rule consequentialism, virtue consequentialism or satisficing views). Consequentialism is usually described as agent-neutral: the value of outcomes does not depend on whose outcomes they are.
- **Classical utilitarianism**: systematically formulated by Jeremy Bentham in his *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (1789), which set out the "principle of utility" as a computational standard for measuring the pleasure and pain produced by each act. John Stuart Mill later developed it in his short work *Utilitarianism* (serialized 1861, book form 1863), adding a qualitative distinction between "higher" (intellectual) and "lower" (sensual) pleasures, in response to the objection that Bentham's calculus treats all forms of pleasure as equal.
- **Sidgwick**: Henry Sidgwick's *The Methods of Ethics* (1874) gave the classical doctrine a rigorous and influential statement, defending universal benevolence while acknowledging the unresolved tension between it and rational self-interest.
- **Contemporary utilitarianism**: since the mid-twentieth century, philosophers such as R. M. Hare (two-level utilitarianism) and Peter Singer (preference utilitarianism, later hedonistic views) have reformulated the theory and applied it to issues such as global poverty, animal welfare and bioethics. Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) is a major source of work on population ethics and personal identity, which bear on how outcomes are aggregated, although Parfit was not a straightforward utilitarian.

## Varieties of Consequentialism

Act consequentialism assesses each act by its outcome; rule consequentialism, developed for example by Brad Hooker in *Ideal Code, Real World* (2000), assesses acts by whether they conform to rules whose general acceptance would produce the best outcomes. Hedonistic views identify the good with pleasure and the absence of pain, preference views with the satisfaction of preferences, and pluralist views with several goods. Scalar and satisficing versions (for example Michael Slote's) relax the demand to maximize. Samuel Scheffler's *The Rejection of Consequentialism* (1982) examined agent-centered prerogatives as a way of limiting its demands.

## The Central Debate with Deontological Ethics

The central objection to utilitarianism is that, in principle, it may justify sacrificing the rights of an individual or a minority if this produces greater utility for the majority (as in thought experiments such as "the doctor who kills an innocent patient to save five"). Deontological ethics regards this as a violation of individual dignity that no consequentialist calculation can justify. A second line of objection, pressed by Bernard Williams in *Utilitarianism: For and Against* (1973), is that consequentialism undermines personal integrity by requiring agents to set aside their own projects. Consequentialists reply that the theory is a standard of right action, not a decision procedure, that rules and dispositions that protect rights usually promote good outcomes, and that intuitions about extreme cases may be unreliable.

## Limitations

- The dispute between act utilitarianism and rule utilitarianism (whether an individual act is assessed by its direct outcome or by the outcome of following a general rule) has not been philosophically resolved, and it is one of the most technical debates in the field.
