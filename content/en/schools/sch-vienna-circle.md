# Logical Positivism (Vienna Circle)

The Vienna Circle was a group of philosophers and scientists who met in Vienna between the 1920s and the mid-1930s around Moritz Schlick (1882–1936), united by a desire for a scientific philosophy that uses modern logic and rids itself of metaphysics. Philosophers and mathematicians, physicists and sociologists belonged to it; among its most prominent members were Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath, Hans Hahn, Friedrich Waismann, Herbert Feigl, Viktor Kraft and Karl Menger, and Kurt Gödel attended some of its meetings. Its outlook was called "logical positivism" (a name popularized in the United States by Albert Blumberg and Herbert Feigl in 1931) or, preferred by Neurath and others, "logical empiricism", the latter stressing a more liberal attitude toward verification and the empirical basis of science. It became an influential current in analytic philosophy and the philosophy of science in the twentieth century.

## Origins and Context

The Circle goes back to informal seminars that began around 1907 and brought together Hahn, Neurath and Philipp Frank, and it developed when Schlick was called in 1922 to the chair in the philosophy of the inductive sciences previously held by Mach and Boltzmann. Its background was Machian empiricism and the modern logic formulated by Frege, Russell and Wittgenstein, above all the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus* (1921), which fascinated the members and was read sentence by sentence in the sessions. Wittgenstein was not a member, but he met Schlick, Waismann and some of the members. In 1928 the "Ernst Mach Society" was founded to disseminate the ideas, and in 1929 the manifesto "The Scientific Conception of the World: The Vienna Circle" appeared, written by Neurath, Carnap and Hahn, followed by the journal *Erkenntnis* (1930–1938).

## Central Theses

- The verification principle: a proposition with cognitive meaning is either analytic (true by virtue of logic and definition) or empirical and verifiable by experience. Its formulations vary among the members; Schlick presented it in "Meaning and Verification" (1936) and Ayer popularized it in *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936).
- Critique of metaphysics: in his essay "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932) Carnap held that metaphysical statements, in his example Heidegger's "The Nothing nothings", are pseudo-propositions that admit neither truth nor falsity, but express feelings or an attitude toward life.
- The unity of science: Carnap and Neurath sought a common scientific language linking the sciences to one another, and launched the *International Encyclopedia of Unified Science* (1938).
- Logical analysis: in *The Logical Structure of the World* (1928) Carnap tried to construct scientific concepts from the primary data of experience by means of logic, and in *The Logical Syntax of Language* (1934) he formulated the "principle of tolerance", that everyone is free to choose the linguistic framework that suits them.
- Ethics: Ayer and others regarded ethical statements as expressions of attitude rather than descriptions of facts, whereas Schlick and Neurath took an interest in ethics from a social perspective.

## Internal Disagreements

The Circle was not a single bloc. Neurath differed with Schlick and Carnap over "protocol sentences", holding that there is no final foundation on which knowledge is built, and that science is a ship repaired on the open sea. Neurath was also more bound up with socialist politics and social planning, whereas Schlick preferred theoretical philosophy. Waismann later criticized the verificationist approach, under the influence of Wittgenstein.

## Dissolution

The Mach Society was closed in 1934 after Austria came under an authoritarian right-wing government, Hahn died the same year, Carnap emigrated to the United States in 1935, and then Schlick was killed in 1936 on the steps of the university, shot by a former student, Johann Nelböck. After the annexation of Austria (1938) those who remained left: Neurath went to the Netherlands and then England (died 1945), Feigl, Gödel and Menger moved to America, and Kraft stayed in Vienna. This dispersal led to the spread of the ideas in Anglo-American philosophy.

## Influence in the Arab World

Logical positivism reached Arab intellectuals through Zaki Naguib Mahmoud (1905–1993), who studied in Britain, and who published *Positivist Logic* (1951) and then *The Myth of Metaphysics* (1953), calling for the analysis of language and for what he called "scientific philosophy", and criticizing a number of legacies of Arab thought as talk without verifiable meaning. He later revised his position and drew closer to the heritage, and most supporters of positivism in the Arab world did not continue after the 1960s. His opponents, who saw in his position an imported positivism that does not take account of the civilizational context, debated him.

## Limits and Critique

Quine, in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), criticized the sharp distinction between analytic and synthetic and the idea of reducing all meaning to experience. Karl Popper, in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934), put falsifiability in place of verification as the criterion for demarcating science; he was at odds with members of the Circle although not one of them. Thomas Kuhn (1962) showed that the history of science does not follow the picture of logical accumulation. The irony is that the verification principle is itself neither an analytic proposition nor empirically verifiable. Hempel and others afterwards accepted that a sharp criterion is hard to formulate.
