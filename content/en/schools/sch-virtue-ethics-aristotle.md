# Virtue Ethics

## Historical and Intellectual Context

Virtue ethics is an ethical current that places the character and virtues of the agent at the center of ethical thinking, rather than focusing on individual acts (as in deontological ethics) or their consequences (as in utilitarianism). Its classic systematic formulation, building on Socrates and Plato, is due to **Aristotle** (384–322 BCE) in his *Nicomachean Ethics* (Ἠθικὰ Νικομάχεια), where he made the supreme aim of human life **happiness/flourishing** (εὐδαιμονία, *eudaimonia*), achieved through the practice of the **virtues** (ἀρετή) in accordance with the "mean" (μεσότης) between excess and deficiency, such as courage between rashness and cowardice. This current declined in modern Western philosophy in favor of Kantian deontological ethics and utilitarianism, before being revived vigorously in the twentieth century.

## Central Ideas

- **Happiness (Eudaimonia)**: not fleeting pleasure but the flourishing of the whole of life through the exercise of reason and virtue. It is the end that is pursued for its own sake, not for the sake of something else.
- **Virtue as a stable disposition (Hexis)**: virtue is a firm disposition acquired through habit and training, not a passing feeling or theoretical knowledge alone.
- **The doctrine of the mean**: every moral virtue lies at a midpoint between two vices (excess and deficiency), determined by the judgment of the practically wise person (*phronimos*) rather than by a fixed rule.
- **Practical wisdom (Phronesis)**: the capacity to discern the right action in a particular situation. It is the condition for applying the moral virtues to changing circumstances.
- **Friendship and shared life**: Aristotle devoted two full books of the *Nicomachean Ethics* to friendship (*philia*), regarding it as necessary for human flourishing, not merely as a utilitarian means.

## Key Thinkers

- **Aristotle**: the classic source, in the *Nicomachean Ethics* and the *Eudemian Ethics*.
- **Elizabeth Anscombe**: in her well-known essay "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958), she criticized modern deontological and utilitarian ethics and called for a return to an Aristotelian concept of moral psychology. It is often regarded as a starting point of the contemporary revival.
- **Alasdair MacIntyre**: in his central book *After Virtue* (1981), he offered a comprehensive critique of the fragmentation of modern moral discourse, and called for the restoration of an Aristotelian-traditional framework of virtue linked to social practices and narratives.
- **Philippa Foot**: in her work (for example *Natural Goodness*, 2001), she developed a "naturalist" version of virtue ethics that links virtue to the biological and practical flourishing of the human being.
- **Rosalind Hursthouse**: in *On Virtue Ethics* (1999), she offered a systematic contemporary formulation of a theory of right action grounded in virtue (roughly, that an action is right if it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances).

## Impact and Application

Virtue ethics has influenced contemporary moral debate, moving it away from reduction to rules or utilitarian calculations. Its influence extends to **healthcare ethics**, **professional ethics** (such as medicine and education), and environmental and educational virtue ethics, where the moral agent is viewed as a person who develops his or her character through practice, not merely as an executor of rules. It has also influenced Islamic moral philosophy through contemporary readings that connect Aristotelian virtue ethics with concepts such as *makarim al-akhlaq* (noble character) among Muslim philosophers, such as al-Farabi and Miskawayh, who drew on the Aristotelian and Platonic traditions.
