# Applied Anthropology

Applied anthropology is the use of anthropological knowledge and methods to solve practical human problems — in public health, development, education, business, environmental management, and government. Where academic anthropology asks what a society is and how it works, applied anthropology asks what can be done, and how anthropological understanding can make an intervention work better, or prevent it from doing harm. It is the discipline's oldest ambition and, for much of its history, its most contested one: the same knowledge that helps a health program succeed can also help a government control a population, and applied anthropologists have spent a century arguing about where help ends and complicity begins. The same practice extends to disaster response, heritage, law, and design. Across those settings the recurring questions are who defines the problem, who receives the findings, and who lives with the consequences.

## History and Emergence

Applied work is as old as anthropology itself. In the nineteenth century, anthropologists advised colonial governments, and in the early twentieth, figures such as Bronislaw Malinowski argued that anthropology should be useful — Malinowski's "practical anthropology" promised colonial administrators a science of how to govern subject peoples, a proposal that now reads as a cautionary tale. The field took institutional form in the United States in the 1930s and 1940s. The Society for Applied Anthropology was founded in 1941, and its journal began publication the same year (as *Applied Anthropology*, renamed *Human Organization* in 1949), giving practitioners a forum of their own. During the Second World War, anthropologists worked on wartime relocation, on morale, and on the administration of occupied territories; after the war, many moved into development, public health, and community studies.

In British colonial Africa, supporters of indirect rule treated knowledge of local law, land tenure, and political authority as an aid to administration, and Malinowski set out that case in a 1929 essay on practical anthropology. In the United States, anthropologists advised the Office of Indian Affairs during the policy changes of the 1930s. Wartime research also covered food habits and societies the United States was fighting. Ruth Benedict's *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946) grew out of the study of Japan at a distance. Anthropologists were employed inside the camps where Japanese Americans were incarcerated, work later treated as a test of professional responsibility.

The postwar decades saw the field professionalize. Sol Tax's "action anthropology" — developed from the late 1940s and in the 1950s in the Fox Project with the Meskwaki of Iowa — tried to square the circle: the anthropologist would help a community achieve goals the community itself defined, rather than goals set by an outside agency. In the 1960s and 1970s, applied anthropologists worked in international development, in nutrition and health programs, and in the War on Poverty's community action programs. A separate experiment in directed change was the Vicos project in highland Peru. From 1952, Allan Holmberg of Cornell University had the university lease a hacienda and sought to move control of the estate toward the people who worked it. Supporters presented the project as participatory reform; critics asked how far outsiders had still chosen its goals. Later collaborative, participatory, and advocacy anthropology share the demand that the people studied help set the questions and the use of the results. The field's textbooks consolidated in the 1980s: Erve Chambers's *Applied Anthropology: A Practical Guide* (1985) and John van Willigen's *Applied Anthropology: An Introduction* (1986) defined the curriculum, and van Willigen's later editions and the edited volume *Applied Anthropology: Domains of Application* (2005, with Satish Kedia) mapped the field's expanding range of settings.

## Key Figures

Sol Tax (1907–1995) founded action anthropology and the model of the anthropologist as facilitator of community-defined change. Margaret Mead (1901–1978) was the most prominent public applied anthropologist, advising governments and writing for general audiences on child-rearing, food habits, and national character. George Foster (1913–2006) shaped the anthropology of development and public health, and his *Applied Anthropology* (1969) was a standard text. John van Willigen systematized the field's methods and its domains of practice, from agriculture to aging. Erve Chambers set out the practical craft of applied work. In recent decades, anthropologists such as Susan Crate have carried applied work into climate change and environmental research.

## Key Works

The field's landmarks are its textbooks and program statements. Foster's *Applied Anthropology* (1969) set out the postwar consensus. Van Willigen's *Applied Anthropology: An Introduction* (1986) is a widely used survey of methods — applied research, social impact assessment, evaluation research, cultural brokerage, and needs assessment. Chambers's *Applied Anthropology: A Practical Guide* (1985) is the complementary handbook. Ervin's *Applied Anthropology: Tools and Perspectives for Contemporary Practice* (2000) updated the field for the era of globalization. Kedia and van Willigen's *Applied Anthropology: Domains of Application* (2005) mapped the field's settings, from agriculture and environment to health, nutrition, education, and business. Rylko-Bauer, Singer, and van Willigen's "Reclaiming Applied Anthropology: Its Past, Present, and Future" (2006) argued that applied work should be reclaimed as central to the discipline rather than treated as a second-class career.

## Method

Applied anthropology's methods are the methods of ethnography, adapted to the constraints of real-world problems: rapid assessment procedures, focus groups, participatory appraisal, needs assessment, and evaluation research. The work is typically collaborative and time-bound — a health program has a deadline, a resettlement plan has a budget — and the anthropologist must produce findings that non-anthropologists can use. A distinctive method is cultural brokerage: translating between the world of a community and the world of an agency, so that each can understand the other's categories and constraints. Another is social impact assessment: predicting, before a dam is built or a mine is opened, how the project will affect the people who live there. The method's defining feature is accountability to two audiences at once: the community affected and the institution paying for the work — and the tension between them is the field's permanent condition.

## Influence and Debates

Applied anthropology has changed how institutions work. Health programs now routinely include ethnographic research on local understandings of illness; development agencies employ anthropologists to assess the social consequences of their projects; and the idea that an intervention must be culturally appropriate is now a truism in public health, education, and environmental management. The field has also changed anthropology, forcing it to confront the question of what its knowledge is for.

The debates are ethical and structural. The ethics of intervention: when an anthropologist helps a government resettle a community or a corporation mine a landscape, whose interests are being served? The postcolonial critique: does applied anthropology, however well-intentioned, reproduce the colonial relationship between expert and subject? And the question of employment: as academic jobs have contracted, more anthropologists work outside the university, and the discipline has had to decide whether that is a loss or a liberation. The field's answer, increasingly, is that applied anthropology is one of the discipline's most demanding forms — because it must be right, not only interesting, and because the people it studies are also its clients and its judges.

Named programmes made those questions public. Project Camelot, a United States Army-sponsored study of the conditions of insurgency, was cancelled in 1965 after it became known in Chile. Research linked to counterinsurgency in Thailand drew protest during the Vietnam War. From the mid-2000s the Human Terrain System placed social scientists with military units; in 2007 the executive board of the American Anthropological Association voiced its disapproval of the programme, concluding that it created conditions likely to place anthropologists in violation of the association's code of ethics. Ethics codes ask for informed consent, openness about who pays for the work, and the avoidance of harm. Those rules are hard to meet when a client owns the data, when people in a community want different things, or when refusing the assignment leaves the decision to others with less local knowledge.

Talal Asad's edited volume *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973) pressed anthropologists to examine how the discipline had served imperial power. Vine Deloria Jr.'s *Custer Died for Your Sins* (1969) attacked research that took knowledge from Native communities without serving them. Some practitioners distinguish applied anthropology, which works for a defined client, from engaged anthropology, which takes an open side in a conflict. The boundary between the two is not fixed.
