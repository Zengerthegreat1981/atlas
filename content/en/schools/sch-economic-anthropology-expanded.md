# Economic Anthropology

Economic anthropology is the study of how people produce, distribute, exchange, and consume the things they need and value — and of what "the economy" means in societies where markets are absent, marginal, or embedded in kinship and ritual. Its founding insight is that economic life is never purely economic: gifts create obligations, feasts create rank, and the decision to work, share, or hoard is shaped by moral ideas about what a good person and a good society look like. Where economics begins with the model of the self-interested individual maximizing utility, economic anthropology begins with ethnography and asks what people actually do with things, and why.

## History and Emergence

Economic anthropology emerged as a distinct subfield in the 1940s, though its roots run to the beginnings of the discipline. Bronislaw Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) described the kula ring, a system of ceremonial exchange of shell valuables across the islands of Melanesia, in which objects circulated for prestige rather than profit — a direct challenge to the idea that economic behavior is everywhere the same. Marcel Mauss's *The Gift* (1925) generalized the point: in societies without markets, gifts are never free; they carry obligations to give, to receive, and to reciprocate, and those obligations are the fabric of social life. Melville Herskovits's *The Economic Life of Primitive Peoples* (1940) gave the field an early textbook and a broad comparative framework. A revised edition appeared in 1952 under the title *Economic Anthropology*.

The subfield's defining debate erupted in the 1950s and 1960s between formalists and substantivists. The formalists, among them Raymond Firth, Robbins Burling and Edward LeClair, argued that economic theory — choice under scarcity — applies everywhere, and that anthropology's job was to show how it operated in different institutional settings. Harold Schneider and Scott Cook also pressed the case that models of decision-making could be used widely. The substantivists, led by Karl Polanyi, argued that the economy is "embedded" in social relations in non-market societies, and that market exchange is only one of three forms of integration, alongside reciprocity and redistribution. Polanyi separated a formal sense of "economic," meaning choice among scarce means, from a substantive sense, meaning the institutions through which people make a livelihood. George Dalton and Paul Bohannan developed that comparison in studies of African markets and exchange. The essays collected in *Trade and Market in the Early Empires* (1957) set out much of this programme. Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* (1944) argued that the self-regulating market was a historical invention of nineteenth-century Europe, not a universal human condition. The debate shaped a generation of research and still frames the field.

## Key Figures

Marcel Mauss (1872–1950), Durkheim's nephew and collaborator, laid foundations for the anthropology of exchange with his theory of the gift. Karl Polanyi (1886–1964), an Austro-Hungarian economic historian, supplied the substantivist framework of embeddedness, reciprocity, and redistribution. Melville Herskovits (1895–1963) helped establish economic anthropology as a teaching field in the United States. Raymond Firth (1901–2002) was a leading figure associated with the formalist position and produced detailed ethnographies of Maori and Tikopia economics. Marshall Sahlins (1930–2021) reframed the debate with *Stone Age Economics* (1972), arguing that hunter-gatherers were the "original affluent society," working few hours and wanting little. Later writers have questioned the time-use evidence behind that picture. David Graeber (1961–2020) revived Mauss for the twenty-first century, arguing in *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) that debt, not barter, is the historical foundation of money.

## Key Works

The canon runs from Mauss's *The Gift* (1925) through Polanyi's *The Great Transformation* (1944) to Sahlins's *Stone Age Economics* (1972), the three books most students still read first. Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) remains the founding ethnography of ceremonial exchange. Herskovits's *The Economic Life of Primitive Peoples* (1940) is an early synthesis of the field. Graeber's *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011) brought economic anthropology to its widest audience, arguing that five thousand years of history show money emerging from credit and obligation rather than from barter between strangers. Alongside these, monographs such as Stephen Gudeman's studies of peasant economies and Jane Guyer's work on African currencies showed how ethnography could revise economic theory from below.

## Method

Economic anthropologists combine the ethnographer's long-term fieldwork with the systematic measurement of economic life: household budgets, time-allocation studies, the tracking of goods through markets and gift networks, and the analysis of prices, wages, and debts. The method is comparative by design, since its central question is whether categories such as "price," "profit," "work," and "money" mean the same thing everywhere. Recent work has extended the method to new domains: the anthropology of finance, of global supply chains, of cryptocurrency, and of the moral economies of debt and welfare. The field's distinctive contribution is to show that economic behavior is always also social behavior — that a market transaction, a remittance, or a refusal to sell can be a statement about kinship, status, and belonging.

## Influence and Debates

Economic anthropology has changed both anthropology and economics. Within anthropology, it established the principle that no domain of life is separate from the rest: production and exchange are tangled up with ritual, politics, and gender. Its substantivist critique forced economists to confront the historical specificity of the market, and its ethnographies of gift and debt have become standard references in economic sociology and heterodox economics. The formalist-substantivist debate itself has been declared dead more than once, yet its terms keep returning, because the question it posed — whether human economic life is everywhere the same or everywhere different — has no final answer. The field's contemporary frontier is the anthropology of capitalism itself: how finance, logistics, and digital platforms reorganize work and value, and how people resist, adapt to, or remake those systems in everyday life.

## Later lines of research

French anthropologists including Claude Meillassoux and Maurice Godelier analysed modes of production and control over labour and reproduction. Feminist research showed that a household is not always one decision-making unit, and that unpaid work supports wage economies. Studies of peasants asked how family farming, subsistence, credit, and demands from the state interact.

Later work also moved past a simple contrast between gift and commodity. Chris Gregory elaborated that contrast, while Arjun Appadurai's *The Social Life of Things* (1986) followed objects as they entered and left the status of a commodity. Jonathan Parry and Maurice Bloch argued that money takes on different moral meanings in different cycles of exchange. Annette Weiner drew attention to possessions people try to keep out of circulation while giving other things away. Ethnographies of markets as organised practice include Caitlin Zaloom's study of futures trading and Karen Ho's study of Wall Street. Graeber's history of debt reached a wide readership and also drew criticism. Calling an economy embedded does not by itself show who benefits or who carries risk. Work on platforms, informal labour, microcredit, and cash transfers returns to older questions about obligation and power.
