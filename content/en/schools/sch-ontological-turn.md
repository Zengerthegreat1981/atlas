# The Ontological Turn in Anthropology (Viveiros de Castro, Holbraad)

## Definition

A recent theoretical current in anthropology that crystallized from the work of the Brazilian anthropologist Eduardo Viveiros de Castro on Amazonian cosmologies since the late 1990s. It proposes taking cultural differences with ontological seriousness, meaning as concerning the nature of existence itself, rather than treating them only as symbolic or interpretive matters, as interpretive and structuralist anthropology traditionally did.

## Historical Context

In 1998 Viveiros de Castro published his pivotal article "Cosmological Deixis and Amerindian Perspectivism," which laid the theoretical foundation for this current. Drawing on his fieldwork among Brazilian Amazonian peoples, he observed that their cosmologies do not assume, as modern Western thought does, a single shared objective "nature" that humans interpret through multiple cultures. The reverse holds: a multiplicity of "natures" (multinaturalism) under a relatively shared "culture" among beings. A jaguar, for example, sees itself as a person and the blood it drinks as manioc beer, while humans see blood; the point of view changes, not the shared culture. This proposal met the work of the Danish anthropologist Martin Holbraad, who studied divination practices among Ifá priests in Cuba. Holbraad proposed that basic concepts such as "truth" itself may differ ontologically from one context to another, and not merely in their symbolic interpretation.

## Main Ideas and Method

This current challenges an implicit assumption that dominated classical anthropology for a long time: that there is a single, objective, universal "nature" that humans interpret through multiple cultural-symbolic systems (multiple cultures over one nature). The task of the anthropologist was to explain other people's beliefs within this single shared natural framework. Instead, the ontological turn proposes that different cultural systems may presuppose radically different "ontologies," meaning different conceptions of what exists in the world in the first place and how it is connected. Ethnographic honesty, on this view, requires taking these divergent ontologies seriously as philosophical positions, rather than reducing them to symbolic "beliefs" within a prior Western natural framework that is presumed to be the only correct standard for understanding reality.

Methodologically, this calls for what Holbraad terms "ontological experimentation": the researcher allows their own analytical concepts to change radically in response to what they encounter in the field, instead of imposing ready-made Western concepts (such as "belief" or "symbol") on materials to which they do not necessarily apply.

## Key Works and Representatives

Viveiros de Castro's foundational article (1998) and his later book *Cannibal Metaphysics* (2014) remain the most important texts of the current. Alongside them is the book *The Ontological Turn: An Anthropological Exposition* (2017) by Martin Holbraad and Morten Axel Pedersen, which provided the first comprehensive and systematic methodological framing of the whole current. The current also intersected with the work of the French thinker Philippe Descola in his book *Beyond Nature and Culture* (2005), which developed a comparative classification of four universal "ontologies" across world cultures (totemism, animism, analogism, and naturalism).

## Impact and Criticism

This current opened a deep philosophical debate about the limits of cultural relativism and the possibility of comparing radically different systems. It influenced the reformulation of basic questions in anthropological theory about what translation between cultures actually means. It has been criticized from two directions. Some traditional anthropologists accused it of philosophical overreach and of the risk of falling into absolute relativism that makes any critical or ethical dialogue between cultural systems difficult. (How can a harmful practice be criticized if it is "real" within its own ontology?) Other researchers in "Indigenous anthropology," such as Zoe Todd, accused it of borrowing the knowledge of Indigenous American peoples and turning it into an abstract academic philosophical theory, without giving adequate credit to its original holders or involving them in the theoretical conversation itself.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

Classical Islamic cosmological concepts of the unseen world, including jinn and angels, present beings with a real existence parallel to the perceptible world rather than mere figurative symbols. These are a rich subject for comparison with the propositions of the ontological turn about the multiplicity of ontologies, although they have not yet been studied with this current's specific tools. Most classical Islamic thought assumes a single comprehensive ontology that encompasses the world of the witnessed and the unseen together within one coherent system, rather than a multiplicity of "natures" in the sense specifically described by Amazonian perspectivism. This structural difference means that any direct comparison between the two systems requires great methodological caution before assuming a superficial equivalence. The same caution applies to any hasty comparison between religious cosmological systems and non-scriptural Indigenous cosmological systems, since rushing the comparison risks obscuring the fine distinctions that make each cosmological system internally coherent in the first place.
