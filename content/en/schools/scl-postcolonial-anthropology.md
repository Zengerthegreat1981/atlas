# Postcolonial Anthropology

Postcolonial anthropology is the reworking of anthropology in the aftermath of empire. It asks what the discipline becomes when its former subjects are independent citizens, when its field sites are sovereign states, and when the categories it inherited — "tribe," "tradition," "development" — are exposed as products of colonial rule. Where colonial anthropology studied societies under empire, and where the critique of colonial anthropology exposed that complicity, postcolonial anthropology tries to build a different practice: one that takes seriously the knowledge, politics, and agency of formerly colonized peoples, and that treats colonialism not as a closed chapter but as a living structure that continues to shape the present.

## History and Emergence

Postcolonial anthropology emerged in the 1980s from three converging currents. The first was the political fact of decolonization itself: by the 1970s, most of the societies anthropologists studied were independent, and the old arrangement — a European scholar studying a colonial subject — was no longer tenable, practically or morally. The second was the critique of colonial anthropology launched by Talal Asad's *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973), which argued that colonial power had shaped the discipline's theories as well as its fieldwork. The third was postcolonial theory in literary and cultural studies, above all Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), which showed how Western scholarship had constructed the "Orient" as an object of domination, and Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988), which asked whether the most marginalized — above all colonized women — could ever be heard within Western frameworks of representation.

Through the 1990s, anthropologists such as Ann Laura Stoler, James Ferguson, Akhil Gupta, and Lila Abu-Lughod turned these arguments into ethnographic research programs. Stoler's studies of Dutch colonialism in Indonesia showed how empire worked through intimacy — through sexuality, domesticity, and the raising of children — as much as through armies and administrations. Ferguson's *The Anti-Politics Machine* (1990) showed how development projects in Lesotho depoliticized poverty, expanding bureaucratic power while failing to deliver their stated goals. Abu-Lughod's "Writing Against Culture" (1991) challenged anthropology's core concept itself, arguing that the idea of bounded "cultures" froze difference and served the interests of power.

## Key Figures

Edward Said (1935–2003), a Palestinian-American literary scholar, supplied the field's founding text, though he was not an anthropologist himself. Talal Asad (born 1932), a Saudi-born anthropologist trained at Oxford, gave the critique its anthropological form. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak (born 1942) posed the problem of representation and the subaltern that haunts the field. Homi Bhabha (born 1949) developed the concepts of hybridity, mimicry, and the "third space" in *The Location of Culture* (1994), arguing that colonial encounter produces new, mixed cultural forms rather than simple domination or resistance. Ann Laura Stoler (born 1949) made the intimate life of empire an ethnographic object. Lila Abu-Lughod (born 1952) challenged the culture concept and wrote influential ethnography on resistance and of the politics of representation. James Ferguson connected postcolonial critique to development, globalization, and the politics of aid in Africa.

## Key Works

The canon begins with Said's *Orientalism* (1978) and Asad's *Anthropology and the Colonial Encounter* (1973), the two books that made the field possible. Spivak's "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) remains one of the most cited essays in the tradition, and Bhabha's *The Location of Culture* (1994) its most influential theoretical statement. Stoler's *Race and the Education of Desire* (1995) showed how colonial rule was organized through sexuality and the family. Ferguson's *The Anti-Politics Machine* (1990) became the classic anthropology of development. Abu-Lughod's "Writing Against Culture" (1991) is the field's most direct challenge to anthropology's own foundations. Together these works moved postcolonial anthropology from critique to a positive program: ethnography that attends to power, history, and the agency of the colonized.

## Method

Postcolonial anthropology's method is ethnography with its premises revised. Fieldwork remains central, but the ethnographer is expected to attend to history — to read the colonial archive alongside the village — and to power — to ask who speaks, who is heard, and who benefits from the research. Representation is treated as a political act: how people are written about matters, and the old convention of speaking for others is replaced, wherever possible, by collaboration, co-authorship, and the inclusion of local voices and categories. The method also turns the ethnographic gaze back on the former colonizers, studying development agencies, museums, and bureaucracies as ethnographic subjects in their own right. This is what distinguishes postcolonial anthropology from the study of colonialism as a historical topic: it is a revision of the discipline's practice, not merely a new subject matter.

## Influence and Debates

Postcolonial anthropology has reshaped the discipline. It made the history of anthropology itself a legitimate research topic; it helped normalize multi-sited ethnography, which follows people, goods, and ideas across borders rather than studying a single bounded village; and it pushed anthropology toward engagement with the politics of development, migration, and global inequality. Its influence extends to museum studies, where the decolonization of collections is now a central concern, and to the anthropology of the state, where Ferguson's analysis of development has become a model.

Debates continue over the field's scope and its blind spots. Critics note that postcolonial theory was built largely from the experience of South Asia and the Middle East, and that it fits Latin America and Africa unevenly. Others argue that the emphasis on discourse and representation has neglected material questions of land, labor, and extraction. And a recurring question is whether postcolonial anthropology has changed the discipline's institutions — its hiring, its publishing, its fieldwork — or only its language. What is settled is the field's central achievement: anthropology can no longer pretend that colonialism was a backdrop to its work. It was one of its conditions, and its aftermath is one of its subjects.
