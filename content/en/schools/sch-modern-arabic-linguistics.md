# Modern Arabic Linguistics

An academic current in twentieth-century Arab scholarship that sought to apply the tools of modern linguistics (phonetics, descriptivism, structuralism) to the study of the Arabic language, with positions ranging from renewing the grammatical heritage to moving beyond it.

## From Modern Phonetic Description to Rereading Grammar

This current began by introducing modern phonetics into Arabic studies (Ibrahim Anis), and then expanded to revisit the classical Arabic grammatical tradition itself with modern linguistic tools (Tammam Hassan). Its aim was neither a complete break with that tradition nor a blind importation of Western methods, but a critical dialogue that draws out of classical Arabic grammar descriptive insights that were latent within it.

## Studying Dialects Instead of Prescriptivism

Some pioneers of this current (such as Ibrahim Anis) turned to the neutral descriptive study of contemporary Arabic dialects, moving away from the traditional prescriptive stance that treats dialects as deviations from Fusha (Standard Arabic). They treated the dialects as independent linguistic systems that deserve scientific description in their own right.

## Tammam Hassan's Qarā'in Theory

Tammam Hassan's most prominent theoretical contribution is the "theory of qarā'in" (contextual indicators), presented in *The Arabic Language: Its Meaning and Structure* (1973). It holds that understanding the grammatical meaning of an Arabic sentence cannot be achieved through inflection (i'rab) alone, as classical grammar emphasized, but through the convergence of several "indicators" acting together. These are verbal indicators (such as inflection, word order, morphological form, agreement and intonation) and semantic indicators (grammatical relations such as predication and specification), considered together with the situational context of the utterance. In this way Hassan sought to move beyond reducing grammatical meaning to abstract inflectional movement, and to recover a comprehensive functional view that resembles in spirit the approaches of modern functional linguistics. He pursued it, however, largely by rereading the classical Arabic grammatical tradition itself rather than by directly importing ready-made Western frameworks.

## Ahmad Mukhtar Omar and Kamal Bishr

Ahmad Mukhtar Omar extended this current into Arabic semantics in his reference work *Semantics* (1982). The book offers Arab readers a systematic survey of modern linguistic theories of meaning, from historical semantics to structural and generative semantics, applied to examples from Arabic and from the Arabic grammatical and linguistic heritage. Kamal Bishr contributed to establishing Arab sociolinguistics in *Sociolinguistics: An Introduction*, studying phenomena of linguistic plurality and the diglossic relationship between Fusha and colloquial Arabic in contemporary Arab societies, using a modern sociolinguistic methodology.

## Abdessalam al-Messaddi and Mazen al-Waer: Critical Reception of Western Linguistics

The Tunisian Abdessalam al-Messaddi made a notable effort, in *Linguistics and Its Epistemological Foundations*, to present Western linguistic schools (structuralism and generative grammar) to Arab readers, alongside a critical examination of their philosophical foundations and of how well they suit the study of Arabic in particular. The Syrian linguist Mazen al-Waer likewise wrote works introducing modern linguistic methods and applying them to Arabic.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

This current differs from many attempts to modernize other disciplines in that it did not break with the classical Arabic grammatical heritage (the grammar of Sibawayh, Ibn Jinni and al-Khalil ibn Ahmad). It treated that heritage as a store of knowledge carrying the seeds of modern descriptive and linguistic insights. Examples include al-Khalil's early recognition of the morphological root system, and Ibn Jinni's view in *al-Khasa'is* on the relationship between word and meaning. Tammam Hassan and his peers therefore undertook a critical revival of this heritage with modern tools, rather than a blind importation cut off from the Arabic context.

## The Question of Diglossia and Contemporary Codification

The phenomenon of diglossia between Fusha (the language of writing, education and official media) and the many local colloquial varieties (the language of actual daily life) became a central research theme for this current, dating from Charles Ferguson's classic description of the phenomenon in 1959, which cited Arabic as a principal example. Arab linguists (such as Ibrahim Anis in *On Arabic Dialects*) studied these colloquial varieties descriptively, with precise phonetic and morphological methods. Meanwhile, the normative relationship between Fusha and the colloquials, and the limits of appropriate modern linguistic codification for contemporary Arabic (such as questions of Arabization and the coining of new scientific terminology), have remained ongoing topics of debate within this current to the present day.

## Impact

This current established a modern Arab academic tradition in linguistics that continues to this day. It influenced the curricula for teaching Arabic language and its sciences in Arab universities throughout the second half of the twentieth century and beyond, and it prepared later generations of Arab linguists to apply more recent computational and generative tools to the study of Arabic without breaking with the descriptive and critical foundation laid by these founding figures.

## Relationship with Arab Language Academies

This academic current also interacted with official institutions such as the Academy of the Arabic Language in Cairo and other Arab academies. These bodies carried out practical codification tasks, such as approving new scientific terms or setting spelling rules. Sometimes they acted in agreement with the recommendations of modern linguists, and sometimes they took a more conservative stance, inclined to regulate the language normatively according to the old grammatical tradition. Some modern linguists have criticized the slowness of these academies in keeping pace with modern descriptive views that depart from traditional grammatical analogy.
