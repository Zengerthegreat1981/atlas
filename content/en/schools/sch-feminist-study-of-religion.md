# Feminist Study of Religion

An academic field that emerged within American religious studies in the late 1960s and early 1970s. It subjects texts, beliefs and religious institutions (especially Western Christianity) to critical analysis from a gender perspective, examining how theological conceptions of divinity, priestly authority and sin were historically formulated within androcentric frameworks. It is distinguished from confessional feminist theology (a religious practice from within) by being a branch of critical academic study of religion when it treats gender in religious discourse as an object of research rather than necessarily a confessional stance. Its early pioneers, however, often wrote from a dual position between academic criticism and reformist advocacy.

## The Foundational Division: Daly's Radicalism and Ruether's Reformism

Mary Daly (`thk-mary-daly`), professor of theology at Boston College, represented the radical wing of the field. She began from a reformist Catholic position in her first book (*The Church and the Second Sex*, 1968), then in *Beyond God the Father* (`wrk-beyond-god-the-father-daly`, 1973) moved to a more radical thesis: that depicting divinity in the image of a male Father ("if God is male, then the male is God") is not an incidental linguistic detail but a symbolic structure that legitimates male domination at the metaphysical level itself. She called for abandoning traditional Christian theology altogether rather than reforming it from within.

By contrast, Rosemary Radford Ruether (`thk-rosemary-radford-ruether`) represented the reformist wing. In *Sexism and God-Talk* (`wrk-sexism-and-god-talk-ruether`, 1983) she developed a systematic feminist theological method that re-reads Christian sources (the Bible, tradition and experience) from a critical feminist perspective without leaving the Christian framework. She held that the prophetic-liberative message within the tradition itself, rather than outside it, is the most appropriate instrument for criticising its later male biases.

## Methodological Impact

This foundational division (reform from within versus radical departure) established a model that was later repeated in parallel branches within other religious traditions. By the 1980s the field had become a standard part of American academic religious studies curricula, intersecting methodologically with the critique of Orientalism and discourse analysis without merging into them.

## The Jewish and Islamic Branches: Reform from Within the Foundational Text

The reformist model (rather than the separatist one) recurred in two other non-Christian traditions. In Judaism, Judith Plaskow (`thk-judith-plaskow`) argued in *Standing Again at Sinai* (`wrk-standing-again-at-sinai-plaskow`, 1990) that the absence of women's voices from the revelation narrative at Sinai, and from later rabbinic interpretive authority, is a structural absence that calls for reformulating the concepts of Torah, God and Israel from within, not for leaving the tradition. In Islam, Amina Wadud (`thk-amina-wadud`) offered in *Qur'an and Woman* (`wrk-quran-and-woman-wadud`, 1992; Oxford University Press edition 1999) an influential systematic interpretive reading of the Qur'an from a woman's perspective. She argued that the Qur'anic text itself, once separated from the layers of male-centred historical interpretation, supports a broader equality between the sexes than has been realised in practice. This places her methodologically closer to the reformist wing (Ruether) than to the separatist wing (Daly). Alongside her, Fatima Mernissi (`thk-fatima-mernissi`) worked in *The Veil and the Male Elite* (`wrk-veil-and-male-elite-mernissi`, 1991) on a different gateway within the Islamic tradition itself: the critique of the chains of transmission of the Prophetic hadith (rather than the direct Qur'anic text), using the traditional science of hadith criticism (*ilm al-rijal*). She showed that transmitters of hadith that were historically used to exclude women from authority had been criticised within the very Sunni sources relied upon. The Islamic branch thus comprises two complementary paths, interpretation of the text (Wadud) and critique of its transmission (Mernissi). Both intersect methodologically with Islamic feminism as a broader intellectual current (`sch-islamic-feminism`) without merging into it.

## The Buddhist Branch: Reconstruction, Not Departure

Outside the three monotheistic traditions, Rita M. Gross (`thk-rita-gross`) represented the Buddhist branch of the field. In *Buddhism After Patriarchy* (`wrk-buddhism-after-patriarchy-gross`, 1993) she provided a critical history of the exclusion of nuns from positions of teaching authority across several Buddhist traditions, and then proposed a reconstruction of practice and institution consistent with the central Buddhist principle that gender is not essential to the possibility of liberation. This reformist position from within the tradition places her methodologically closer to Ruether, Plaskow and Wadud than to Daly.

## The Hindu Branch: Critique of Orientalism, Not Reform from Within

The Hindu branch differs structurally from the others. It was represented by Sharada Sugirtharajah (`thk-sharada-sugirtharajah`) in her book *Imagining Hinduism: A Postcolonial Perspective* (`wrk-imagining-hinduism-sugirtharajah`, 2003). She did not write a theological reform from within Hinduism along the lines of Ruether, Plaskow or Wadud, since Hinduism has no exact counterpart to a single central text or a unified clerical authority comparable to the church, the rabbinate or jurisprudence. Instead, she criticised how classical European Orientalism and modern Hindu reform movements (such as Neo-Vedanta) jointly "imagined" a unified, masculinised textual image of Hinduism that excluded the practices of local and informal women from the definition of the religious "essence." This places the Hindu branch at the intersection of the critique of Orientalism and feminist criticism together, rather than in the position of internal theological reform occupied by the other branches.
