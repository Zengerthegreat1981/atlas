# Greek and Roman Historians (Herodotus, Thucydides, Polybius, Tacitus)

Four figures established distinct methodological lines in classical Western historiography, ranging from the wide-ranging investigative narrative, through the strict critique of contemporary sources, to comparative political analysis.

## Representatives and Method

- **Herodotus** (c. 484–425 BCE): *The Histories* (*Historíai*), the first work to be called "history" in the sense of "inquiry/investigation", combining an account of the Greco-Persian Wars with extensive ethnographic material on the peoples of the Mediterranean and the East. He relied mainly on direct oral inquiry (autopsy and *akoē*: what he saw himself and what he heard from others), without a rigorous systematic critical examination. This led Cicero to call him the "father of history", while other ancient writers described him as the "father of lies" (Plutarch, *De Herodoti malignitate*) for his leniency in transmitting accounts he had not verified.
- **Thucydides** (c. 460–400 BCE): *History of the Peloponnesian War*. He represents a sharp methodological break from Herodotus. He committed himself to writing only about events he had witnessed or whose witnesses he had access to, explicitly stated that he examined conflicting accounts by comparing testimonies ("I did not write on the basis of whoever I happened to meet, nor on my own opinion alone"), and rejected explaining events through divine intervention in favour of analysing political and human motives. He is often credited with laying the foundation of "scientific/critical history" in the Western tradition.
- **Polybius** (c. 200–118 BCE): *The Histories*. A Greek historian who wrote about the rise of Rome and developed the concept of "universal history", which links the events of many regions in a single narrative with interconnected causes (*sumplokē*). He stressed that the practical aim of history (*pragmatikē historia*) is to teach statesmen through the study of causes, not merely to narrate events.
- **Tacitus** (c. 56–120 CE): *The Annals* and *The Histories*. A Roman historian renowned for his sharp political-psychological analysis of the motives of emperors and of institutional corruption, and for his concentrated, biting style. He showed explicit awareness that writing history under autocratic rule is subject to the pressures of censorship and flattery (he opens the *Annals* with a promise to write *sine ira et studio*, "without anger or partiality").

## Shared and Divergent Methodological Lines

All four share the use of **connected prose narrative** instead of epic poetry (Homer) as a means of recording the past. They differ in the rigour of their critique: Thucydides is the most committed to the contemporaneity of the witness and to the scrutiny of accounts; Herodotus has the broadest geographical and ethnographic scope but the least precision; Polybius is the most conscious of comparative causality among states; and Tacitus is the most conscious of how the political context (autocracy) affects the historian's own neutrality.
