# Classical Islamic Sufism (Tasawwuf)

A spiritual and cognitive tradition in Islam that arose in the first and second centuries AH out of asceticism, devotion and scrupulousness, and then became a discipline with its own terminology, methods and literature. It is concerned with purifying the soul, cleansing the heart and attaining knowledge and love of God through remembrance (dhikr) and striving (mujahada), and with what its adherents called "the science of the inner", in a way that does not contradict the outer. This presentation treats classical Sufism as a historical tradition, without judging the soundness of its doctrines, while noting what has been debated about it in Islamic thought.

## Origins and development

Asceticism began in Basra and Kufa with al-Hasan al-Basri (d. 110 AH) and then Rabi'a al-Adawiyya (d. 185 AH), whose name is associated with loving God for His own sake. In the third century the Baghdad school emerged, whose most important figure was al-Junayd (d. 297 AH), who ordered the experience of annihilation (fana) with the rule of "sobriety after effacement" and preserved adherence to the Sharia. Also mentioned are al-Harith al-Muhasibi (d. 243 AH) on self-examination, Dhu al-Nun al-Misri (d. 245 AH) on the stations and states, and Abu Yazid al-Bistami (d. 261 AH), to whom the saying "Glory be to me, how great is my majesty" is attributed.

## Al-Hallaj and the limits of expression

Al-Husayn ibn Mansur al-Hallaj, who was executed in Baghdad in 309 AH / 922 CE after a long trial, represented a decisive case, since his saying "I am the Real" was understood as union or indwelling, while his supporters interpreted it as annihilation of the self and speaking with the tongue of the Real. The trial pushed the Sufis of Baghdad toward greater caution in expressing experience, and toward separating "ecstatic utterance" (shath) from general teaching.

## Foundational literature

- **al-Luma'** by Abu Nasr al-Sarraj al-Tusi (d. 378 AH) and **al-Ta'arruf li-madhhab ahl al-tasawwuf** by al-Kalabadhi (d. c. 380 AH): the earliest systematic guides establishing the agreement of Sufism with the Sunnis.
- **Qut al-qulub** by Abu Talib al-Makki (d. 386 AH): an encyclopedia of acts of worship and stations.
- **al-Risala al-Qushayriyya** by Abu al-Qasim al-Qushayri (d. 465 AH): biographies and definitions of terms, and the most widely circulated of the books.
- **Kashf al-mahjub** by al-Hujwiri (d. c. 465 AH): the earliest Persian treatise on Sufism.
- **Ihya' ulum al-din** by al-Ghazali (d. 505 AH): the encyclopedia that brought Sufism into Sunni juridical and theological culture.
- **Awarif al-ma'arif** by al-Suhrawardi al-Baghdadi (d. 632 AH): the constitution of the Sufi orders.
- **al-Hikam al-Ata'iyya** by Ibn Ata Allah al-Iskandari (d. 709 AH): concise aphorisms that became a text for spiritual training.

## Central concepts

- **Stations and states**: repentance, asceticism, trust in God, patience and contentment are acquired stations, while fear, hope, intimacy, contraction and expansion are bestowed states.
- **Annihilation and subsistence**: the person's absence from his blameworthy attributes and his witnessing of God's action, and then his subsistence in God.
- **Gnosis and taste**: the inner knowledge obtained by experience, which al-Ghazali made the path of certainty after his crisis in 488 AH / 1095 CE, which he narrated in "al-Munqidh min al-dalal". In "Ihya' ulum al-din" he likened the heart to a mirror that sins make rusty and that striving and remembrance polish, so that realities are reflected in it.
- **Remembrance and audition**: remembrance by tongue and by heart, and the sessions of chanting and audition reserved for followers, on whose ruling the jurists were divided.
- **The shaykh and the disciple**: training at the hands of a shaykh with a chain of transmission connected to the Prophet, which is the basis of the orders.
- **Sainthood (wilaya)**: the concept of the saint and his miracles, and the ranks of the axes (aqtab) and substitutes (abdal) in the later tradition.

## The orders and their spread

The orders took shape from the sixth and seventh centuries AH: the Qadiriyya (Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani, d. 561 AH), the Rifa'iyya, the Suhrawardiyya, the Shadhiliyya (Abu al-Hasan al-Shadhili, d. 656 AH) in Egypt and the Maghreb, the Mevlevi order that arose after Jalal al-Din Rumi (d. 672 AH) in Konya under the organization of his son Sultan Walad, and the Naqshbandiyya (Baha al-Din Naqshband, d. 791 AH) in Central Asia, India and Turkey. Sufi literature was among the most prominent things transmitted in poetry and music, especially the Persian "Masnavi" that Rumi dictated to Husam al-Din Chelebi from about 1258 CE, which he opened with the tale of the reed flute cut off from its origin as a metaphor for the soul's longing for its homeland; Rumi had turned from teaching to poetry after meeting Shams al-Din Tabrizi in Konya in 1244 CE. Sufi orders and figures took part in resistance to colonialism, for example Emir Abd al-Qadir al-Jaza'iri, the Sanusiyya and the Mahdiyya.

## Classical Sufism and Ibn Arabi's Theosophy

Classical Sufism, as described above, was primarily a practice and a literature of states and stations, developed from the eighth to the twelfth centuries. The speculative system associated with Ibn Arabi (d. 638 AH / 1240 CE), expressed in works such as *Fusus al-Hikam* and *al-Futuhat al-Makkiyya*, belongs to a later phase that used philosophical vocabulary to describe the cosmos and the perfect human being. The phrase "unity of being" (wahdat al-wujud) was used by his followers and critics rather than being his own standard expression, and whether it fairly summarizes his thought is debated.

## Criticism and dispute

- Ibn al-Jawzi (d. 597 AH) wrote "Talbis Iblis", criticizing what he considered innovations in the ways of the Sufis.
- Ibn Taymiyya (d. 728 AH) distinguished between the Sufism of the early generations, which adhered to the Sharia, and what he called excess in the unity of being and indwelling, and his critique influenced modern Salafi movements.
- Modern reformers such as Rashid Rida criticized the rigidity of the orders and superstition, while others defended reforming the orders rather than abolishing them.
- The Orientalist debate over origins: earlier scholars such as Nicholson stressed Christian, Neoplatonic and Indian influences, while Massignon and Schimmel held that it arose from the Quran and Islamic experience, which is the prevailing view today among scholars of the field.
