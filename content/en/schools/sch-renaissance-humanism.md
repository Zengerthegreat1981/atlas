# Renaissance Humanism

An intellectual and cultural movement that arose in Italy in the fourteenth century and spread across Europe in the fifteenth and sixteenth. It centred on the *studia humanitatis*, the curriculum of grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy, and on the recovery and imitation of classical Latin and Greek literature. The Italian word *umanista* for a teacher of these subjects dates from the late fifteenth century, while the term "humanism" for the movement was coined only in the nineteenth century (by the German educator F. J. Niethammer in 1808, and given wide currency by Jacob Burckhardt and Georg Voigt). Following Paul Oskar Kristeller, historians stress that Renaissance humanism was first of all an educational and literary programme rather than a single philosophical doctrine, although it had philosophical consequences.

## Founding context

Italy in the fourteenth century had wealthy, largely self-governing city-states (Florence, Venice, Milan) with a lay, legally trained urban elite and a living memory of Roman antiquity. The papacy's residence at Avignon (1309–1377) and the Western Schism (1378–1417) weakened the Church's central authority. Greek learning became more accessible through teachers such as Manuel Chrysoloras, who taught in Florence from 1397, and through the Council of Florence (1438–1439), where the Byzantine scholar Gemistos Plethon (c. 1355–1452) impressed Cosimo de' Medici and others. After the fall of Constantinople (1453) more Greek scholars and manuscripts reached Italy.

## Leading figures

- **Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch, 1304–1374)**: often called the "father of humanism"; poet and scholar, author of the *Canzoniere*, the *Secretum* and *De remediis utriusque fortunae*, and a collector and reader of classical manuscripts.
- **Giovanni Boccaccio (1313–1375)**: author of the *Decameron* (c. 1349–1353) and of Latin works on classical mythology and famous men and women.
- **Coluccio Salutati (1331–1406)** and **Leonardo Bruni (1370–1444)**: chancellors of Florence; Bruni translated Plato and Aristotle and wrote the *History of the Florentine People*.
- **Poggio Bracciolini (1380–1459)**: recoverer of classical manuscripts, including Lucretius and Quintilian.
- **Lorenzo Valla (1407–1457)**: showed by philological argument that the *Donation of Constantine* was a forgery (1440) and wrote the *Elegantiae linguae Latinae*.
- **Leon Battista Alberti (1404–1472)**: writer, architect and theorist, author of *On the Family* and *On Painting*.
- **Marsilio Ficino (1433–1499)**: translator of Plato and Plotinus, and leading figure of Florentine Platonism.
- **Giovanni Pico della Mirandola (1463–1494)**: author of the *Oration on the Dignity of Man* (1486).
- **Desiderius Erasmus (1466–1536)**: the *Adagia*, the *Praise of Folly* (1511), and the first published printed Greek New Testament, the *Novum Instrumentum* (1516).
- **Thomas More (1478–1535)**: *Utopia* (1516).
- **Johann Reuchlin (1455–1522)**: author of *De rudimentis Hebraicis* (1506), an early Hebrew grammar and lexicon.
- **Michel de Montaigne (1533–1592)**: the *Essais* (first edition 1580), the master of the sceptical essay.
- **Joseph Justus Scaliger (1540–1609)** and **Isaac Casaubon (1559–1614)**: late humanist philologists who developed historical and textual criticism.

## Central concepts

- **Return to the sources (*ad fontes*)**: reading classical and biblical texts directly in the original languages instead of through medieval commentaries. Valla's critique of the Donation of Constantine and Erasmus's edition of the New Testament are classic examples of the resulting philological method.
- **Human dignity**: Pico's *Oration* presents the human being as placed by God in the middle of the world with no fixed nature, free to shape himself upwards or downwards. Humanist writers such as Giannozzo Manetti also wrote on the dignity of man, in part against the medieval theme of human misery.
- **Eloquence and education**: rhetoric and literature were treated as the basis of education for active civic and moral life, and humanist schools such as that of Vittorino da Feltre in Mantua shaped later European schooling.
- **History**: humanists developed a sense of historical distance from antiquity, and wrote political and civic history (Bruni, Flavio Biondo, later Machiavelli and Guicciardini).
- **Moral and civic philosophy**: humanists gave less weight to scholastic logic and metaphysics than to ethics and politics, drawing on Cicero, Seneca, Plutarch and Plato.
- **Religious critique and reform**: Erasmus and More combined classical learning with Christian piety, and advocated moderation and peace.

## Civic and religious strands

Historians distinguish a civic humanism associated with Florence in the early fifteenth century (Salutati, Bruni; Hans Baron's thesis of a "crisis" of 1400–1402 prompted by the Milanese threat is influential but debated), which valued active participation in the republic, from the Platonic humanism of Ficino and Pico, and from the Christian or "northern" humanism of Erasmus, More and Reuchlin, which directed philology toward scripture and church reform. Skeptical strands appear in Montaigne and, later, Pierre Charron.

## Relation to scholasticism and other movements

- **Scholasticism**: humanists often criticised scholastic Latin and method, but the relation was not simple opposition; Aristotle remained central to university teaching, and many humanists were themselves trained in scholastic philosophy (see Rummel 1995).
- **Reformation**: Erasmus's editions and critiques of the Church helped prepare the ground for the Reformation, but he refused to break with Rome and broke with Luther over free will (1524–1525).
- **Science**: humanist recovery of ancient mathematical and scientific texts (for example Archimedes) and of Greek medical texts contributed to the sixteenth-century growth of science, although humanism itself was not an experimental movement.

## Limits and criticisms

- **Definition**: scholars disagree about whether humanism was a coherent philosophy, as Burckhardt's portrayal of the "discovery of the individual" suggested, or mainly a cultural programme (Kristeller).
- **Elitism**: humanist education was largely confined to the male social elite, and the classical models it prized could encourage imitation over originality.
- **Church and conflict**: humanist criticism could provoke ecclesiastical opposition, although most humanists were Christians; Giordano Bruno, burned for heresy in 1600, is better described as a late Renaissance natural philosopher than a typical humanist.
- **Later uses of the term**: "humanism" in modern secular, existentialist and psychological senses differs from the Renaissance programme and should not be read back into it.
