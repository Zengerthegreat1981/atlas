# Economic Anthropology

Economic anthropology studies how people produce, exchange, share, own, and consume, and how these activities are bound up with kinship, ritual, politics, and moral judgment. It compares economies of many kinds, including capitalist firms and financial markets, and asks whether concepts drawn from market economics apply elsewhere.

## Early work

Bronisław Malinowski's account of the kula, a Melanesian exchange of shell valuables, in *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) rejected the image of people moved only by material need. Marcel Mauss's essay *The Gift* (1925) examined obligations to give, receive, and return. Raymond Firth wrote detailed studies of Polynesian economic life, and Melville Herskovits published *The Economic Life of Primitive Peoples* in 1940, revised in 1952 as *Economic Anthropology*.

## Formalists and substantivists

Karl Polanyi distinguished the formal meaning of "economic", concerning choice among scarce means, from a substantive meaning, concerning the institutions through which people gain their livelihood. He described reciprocity, redistribution, and market exchange as patterns of integration, and argued that economies have commonly been embedded in other social relations. Formalists, including Harold Schneider and, in a different way, Scott Cook, held that models of decision-making could be applied widely. George Dalton and Paul Bohannan developed substantivist comparison. Marshall Sahlins's *Stone Age Economics* (1972) proposed that some foragers could be seen as meeting limited wants with moderate labour, an argument whose time-use evidence has since been questioned.

## Marxism, households, and gender

French anthropologists such as Claude Meillassoux and Maurice Godelier analysed modes of production and the control of labour and reproduction. Feminist research showed that households are not necessarily single decision-making units and that unpaid work supports wage economies. Studies of peasants asked how family farming, subsistence concerns, credit, and state demands interact.

## Value, money, and markets

Later work moved beyond a simple opposition between gift and commodity. Chris Gregory elaborated that distinction, while Arjun Appadurai's edited volume *The Social Life of Things* (1986) followed objects as they entered and left commodity status. Jonathan Parry and Maurice Bloch argued that money acquires different moral meanings according to the cycles of exchange in which it moves. Annette Weiner drew attention to possessions that people strive to keep while giving others away. Ethnographies of traders, factories, finance, debt, and humanitarian aid, including Caitlin Zaloom's work on futures markets and Karen Ho's on Wall Street, examine markets as organised practices. David Graeber's *Debt* (2011) offered a broad argument about credit and obligation that has attracted both wide readership and criticism.

## Open questions

Researchers disagree about how far to generalise from local cases and how to combine ethnography with quantitative evidence. Calling an economy "embedded" does not by itself show who benefits or who bears risk. Current studies of platforms, informal work, microcredit, and cash transfers return to older questions about obligation and power in new institutional forms.
