# Maritime Anthropology

Maritime anthropology is the branch of anthropology that studies the peoples of the sea: fishers, sailors, divers, islanders, and coastal communities, and the ways of life that the sea makes possible. Its subject is everything that follows from living by and on the water — the knowledge of tides, winds, and fish, the ownership of boats and fishing grounds, the dangers of the sea and the rituals that meet them, and the distinctive social worlds of crews, harbours, and fishing villages. The field's founding insight is that the sea is not an empty space between lands but a lived environment, as richly known and as socially organized as any forest or field, and that maritime peoples have their own cultures, their own economies, and their own ways of thinking about nature, risk, and belonging.

## Origins and History

Maritime anthropology emerged in the 1970s, when anthropologists began to notice that the sea had been curiously absent from their discipline. Anthropology had been built on the study of villages and tribes on land; the sea appeared mainly as a route between them. Early exceptions were striking: Bronisław Malinowski's Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) had described the kula exchange voyages of the Trobriand Islanders in detail, and Raymond Firth's work on the Malay fishermen of Kelantan, published as Malay Fishermen (1946), showed that a fishing economy had its own logic of risk, cooperation, and credit. But it was not until the 1970s that the field coalesced. M. Estellie Smith's edited volume Those Who Live from the Sea (1977) gave it a name and a program, arguing that maritime peoples should be studied as a distinct type of society. In the 1980s the journal Maritime Anthropological Studies, launched in the Netherlands, gave the field a forum, and ethnographies of fishing communities multiplied on every coast.

The field's classic problem is the commons: the sea belongs to no one, and yet fishers must divide it. James Acheson's The Lobster Gangs of Maine (1988) showed how Maine lobstermen created an informal system of territorial rights — "harbor gangs" that defended their fishing grounds without any law — and argued that such territoriality could help conserve the lobster stock. Acheson's work became a landmark in the study of common property, read by economists, lawyers, and fisheries managers as well as anthropologists. At the same time, the field turned to the sea as a place of knowledge and identity: In 1976 the Hokule'a, a reconstructed double-hulled canoe, sailed from Hawaii to Tahiti without instruments, guided by the Carolinian navigator Mau Piailug. Ben Finney, one of the project's initiators, recounted that voyage in Hokule'a: The Way to Tahiti (1979) and a later voyage of 1985–1987 in Voyage of Rediscovery (1994), showing that non-instrument navigation by stars, swells, and birds could guide long ocean passages.

## Key Figures and Works

Bronisław Malinowski's Argonauts of the Western Pacific (1922) remains the field's distant ancestor. Raymond Firth's Malay Fishermen (1946) is its first modern classic. M. Estellie Smith's Those Who Live from the Sea (1977) founded the field as a subdiscipline. James Acheson's The Lobster Gangs of Maine (1988) and his later Capturing the Commons (2003) are the canonical studies of fishing, territory, and resource management. Ben Finney's Voyage of Rediscovery (1994) recovered Polynesian navigation as a scientific tradition. John Cordell's edited volume A Sea of Small Boats (1989) documented customary sea tenure and the knowledge of small-scale fishers in several parts of the world. More recent work includes the ethnographies of the North Atlantic fisheries crisis, the studies of women in fishing communities, and the research on migrant labour aboard industrial fishing fleets, which has documented exploitation at sea.

## Method

Maritime anthropologists work at sea as well as on land. The method begins with participant observation aboard boats: hauling nets, mending gear, and learning the embodied skills of seamanship that cannot be learned from books. The ethnographer maps fishing grounds, records the knowledge of currents and fish behaviour, and documents the social organization of crews — the division of shares, the authority of the skipper, and the risks that bind a crew together. The field is comparative, setting small-scale artisanal fisheries against industrial fleets, and it is increasingly multi-sited, following fish from the boat to the market, the processing plant, and the restaurant. A distinctive method is the study of traditional ecological knowledge: documenting what fishers know about the sea and using that knowledge in fisheries management. The field also works with oral history, recording the memories of retired fishers before their knowledge disappears with the fleets that carried it.

## Influence and Legacies

Maritime anthropology has changed fisheries policy and the study of the commons. Acheson's work on the Maine lobster fishery became a central case in the debate over common property resources, discussed in the work of Elinor Ostrom, Nobel laureate for her research on the governance of the commons, and it informed arguments that local institutions can help conserve stocks alongside, or sometimes better than, central regulation. The field's documentation of traditional ecological knowledge has been taken up by marine biologists and conservationists, and its studies of navigation have reshaped the history of science, showing that Pacific voyaging was a precise and systematic knowledge tradition. The field's influence extends to the study of climate change, as coastal communities are among the first to feel rising seas and warming waters, and to the study of migration, as the sea becomes a route of flight and a graveyard. Its current frontier is the deep ocean and the industrial sea: the factory ships, the offshore platforms, and the seabed mining that are remaking the ocean, and the question of who owns the sea when the fish are gone.
