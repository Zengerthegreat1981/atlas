# Spinozism

A philosophical tradition that takes its name from **Baruch (Benedictus de) Spinoza** (1632–1677), a Dutch philosopher of Sephardic Jewish descent. Spinozism is regarded as one of the most radical doctrines of early modern philosophy: it identifies God with Nature as one infinite substance ("*Deus sive Natura*"), explains everything by necessary causal laws, denies miracles and a purposive, personal God, and rejects the idea of personal immortality. Its supporters and opponents shaped the Radical Enlightenment, German Idealism and Romanticism, and its influence has continued in later thinkers such as Hegel, Nietzsche and Deleuze. The terms "Spinoza" (the historical philosopher) and "Spinozism" (a label applied, often polemically, to a family of later doctrines) are not identical, and the second often distorts the first.

## Context

Spinoza was born in Amsterdam to a family of Portuguese Jews who had left Portugal to escape the Inquisition. He received a traditional Jewish education, then studied Latin and the new philosophy and science, above all Descartes. He was influenced by Maimonides and other Jewish thinkers, by Hobbes and by Renaissance and Cartesian rationalism. In July 1656 the Amsterdam Portuguese-Jewish community issued a *cherem* (a ban) against him for his views. He afterwards lived in or near Rijnsburg, Voorburg and The Hague, earning his living by grinding optical lenses, and corresponded widely. He died in The Hague in 1677.

## Works

- *Principles of Cartesian Philosophy* (*Principia philosophiae Renati Des Cartes*, 1663), the only work published under his name in his lifetime.
- *Theological-Political Treatise* (*Tractatus theologico-politicus*, 1670), published anonymously.
- *Ethics, Demonstrated in Geometrical Order* (*Ethica ordine geometrico demonstrata*), the *Political Treatise*, the unfinished *Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect* and his *Letters*, all published after his death in the *Opera posthuma* (1677).
- The early *Short Treatise on God, Man, and His Well-Being* was not published in his lifetime and was rediscovered in the nineteenth century.

## Central concepts

- **One substance**: substance is "what is in itself and is conceived through itself". There can be only one, infinite, necessarily existing substance, identified with God or Nature. Attributes (of which we know thought and extension) are what the intellect perceives of substance as constituting its essence; modes (finite things, including human minds and bodies) are affections of substance.
- **Parallelism and the identity of mind and body**: mind and body are one and the same thing conceived under different attributes, and the order of ideas is the same as the order of things. There is no causal interaction between thought and extension.
- **Necessitarianism**: everything follows from the nature of God with necessity. There is no contingency and no divine free choice among alternatives; miracles as violations of natural order are impossible.
- **Denial of free will**: people believe themselves free because they are conscious of their actions but ignorant of their causes. Freedom consists in acting from the necessity of one's own nature, guided by adequate ideas.
- **Conatus and the affects**: each thing strives to persevere in its being. The emotions are analysed as modes of this striving, and human bondage consists in being governed by passive affects.
- **Three kinds of knowledge and the intellectual love of God (*amor Dei intellectualis*)**: opinion or imagination, reason, and intuitive knowledge. The highest blessedness lies in the intellectual love of God. Spinoza denies personal immortality but holds that the part of the mind that grasps eternal truths is eternal (Ethics V).
- **Religion and politics**: in the *Theological-Political Treatise* he argues that Scripture must be interpreted historically and that its practical message is obedience and charity, not speculative truth; the state should secure peace and freedom of philosophizing. In the *Political Treatise* he treats democracy as the most natural form of government.

## Reception and development

- **Bayle and the Radical Enlightenment**: Pierre Bayle's article "Spinoza" in his *Dictionnaire* (1697) presented Spinoza as an atheist and a figure of scandal. Jonathan Israel argues that Spinozism was the centre of a radical, secular tendency in the early Enlightenment.
- **Contemporaries**: Adriaan Koerbagh (1633–1669), Lodewijk Meyer (1629–1681, *Philosophia S. Scripturae Interpres*, 1666) and the publisher Jan Rieuwertsz (1616–1687) belonged to Spinoza's circle in the Dutch Republic. Henry More attacked Spinoza in his *Enchiridion metaphysicum* (1671). Leibniz visited him in 1676, was influenced by him, but rejected his necessitarianism and his single substance in favour of a plurality of monads.
- **German thought**: F. H. Jacobi's *Letters on the Doctrine of Spinoza* (1785) provoked the "pantheism controversy" with Moses Mendelssohn, and made Spinoza central to Herder, Goethe, Schelling and Hegel, who said that to be a philosopher one must first be a Spinozist.
- **Twentieth century**: Gilles Deleuze (*Spinoza et le problème de l'expression*, 1968; *Spinoza: philosophie pratique*, 1970), Louis Althusser, Pierre Macherey (*Hegel ou Spinoza*, 1979), Étienne Balibar and Antonio Negri revived Spinoza as a thinker of power, affect and politics. Einstein said in 1929 that he believed in "Spinoza's God", who reveals himself in the lawful harmony of what exists.

## Relation to other schools

- **Cartesianism**: Spinoza used Descartes's geometrical presentation but rejected his dualism, his free-willing God and his account of the passions.
- **Medieval Jewish philosophy**: Maimonides's philosophical reading of Scripture was an important background, although Spinoza went far beyond him in rejecting revelation as a source of speculative truth. Claims of direct Kabbalistic influence are contested.
- **Materialism and pantheism**: Spinoza is often called a pantheist (a term coined after his death) or, by some scholars, a panentheist or even a naturalist. He is not a materialist in the simple sense, since thought is an irreducible attribute of substance.

## Limits and criticisms

- **Religious opposition**: Jewish, Christian and Muslim authorities and many philosophers regarded him as an atheist, because he denied a personal, creating and providential God.
- **Determinism and freedom**: critics find it hard to reconcile necessitarianism with moral responsibility.
- **The status of the finite**: Jacobi and others argued that a monism of one substance leaves no room for real individuals or for a free creator. Leibniz and Hegel in different ways attempted to correct this.
- **Interpretation**: scholars dispute whether his system should be read as pantheism, naturalism, acosmism or idealism, and how to understand the relation of the attributes to substance.
