# Ethnoastronomy

Ethnoastronomy is the anthropological study of how peoples understand the sky: the sun, the moon, the stars, and the planets, and the meanings they attach to their movements. It asks how different cultures divide the heavens into constellations, how they use celestial events to organise calendars and agriculture, and how the sky figures in myth, ritual, and cosmology. Where astronomy studies the heavens as physical objects, ethnoastronomy studies them as cultural objects, showing that there is no single human sky but many skies, each shaped by the society that looks up at it.

## History

The field grew out of two older traditions. The first was archaeoastronomy, the study of the astronomical alignments of ancient monuments. Gerald Hawkins's *Stonehenge Decoded* (1965) argued that Stonehenge functioned as a prehistoric observatory, and Alexander Thom's *Megalithic Sites in Britain* (1967) claimed that Neolithic builders used precise astronomical alignments. These claims were controversial, but they established that ancient peoples watched the sky carefully and built accordingly.

The second tradition was the ethnography of sky knowledge. Anthony Aveni's *Skywatchers of Ancient Mexico* (1980) showed that Maya and Aztec astronomy was a sophisticated system of observation tied to calendars, agriculture, and kingship, not a primitive curiosity. Aveni became a central figure in the field, and the revised edition of the book, *Skywatchers* (2001), remains a standard synthesis. In the 1980s and 1990s, ethnoastronomy emerged as a distinct enterprise, concerned less with ancient monuments than with living knowledge: how contemporary peoples name the stars, predict the seasons, and navigate by the sky. Von Del Chamberlain's study of Pawnee cosmology, *When Stars Came Down to Earth* (1982), and Stephen Fabian's *Space-Time of the Bororo of Brazil* (1992) were landmarks of this ethnographic turn. The *Handbook of Archaeoastronomy and Ethnoastronomy* (2015), edited by Clive Ruggles, consolidated the field's methods and findings.

## Key Figures and Works

Hawkins (1965) and Thom (1967) launched the modern study of ancient astronomy, even as their bolder claims were debated. Aveni (1980, 2001) demonstrated the complexity of Mesoamerican astronomy and connected it to ritual and political power. Ruggles, in *Astronomy in Prehistoric Britain and Ireland* (1999), replaced speculation with careful statistical testing of alignments, setting the methodological standard for the field.

Chamberlain (1982) showed how Pawnee star knowledge was woven into village layout, ritual, and moral order. Fabian (1992) described how the Bororo of Brazil organise time and space through the sky, treating celestial cycles as a model for social life. David Kelley and Eugene Milone's *Exploring Ancient Skies* (2011) provided a wide comparative survey of astronomical traditions, from Babylon to the Andes. Together these works established ethnoastronomy's central claim: sky knowledge is not decorative but structural, embedded in calendars, cosmologies, and the organisation of daily life.

## Method

Ethnoastronomy combines ethnography with astronomy. Researchers record indigenous star names and constellations, often with the help of planetarium software and star charts that let them reconstruct what a particular sky looked like on a particular date. They document how celestial events — solstices, equinoxes, heliacal risings, eclipses — are used to time planting, hunting, festivals, and rituals. They measure the orientations of buildings, shrines, and settlements, and test statistically whether alignments with solar or lunar events are likely to be deliberate rather than coincidental.

The method demands caution. A single alignment proves little, and the field has learned to require systematic evidence: repeated alignments, ethnographic corroboration, and attention to the horizon as people actually see it. Ethnoastronomers also record the stories attached to the sky, because a constellation is never only a pattern of stars; it is a character in a myth, an ancestor, or a moral warning.

## Influence and Legacy

Ethnoastronomy transformed the study of ancient knowledge. It showed that peoples without writing or telescopes could possess precise and systematic astronomy, and that this knowledge was inseparable from religion, politics, and subsistence. It contributed to the recognition of indigenous knowledge systems in heritage policy, for example through UNESCO's Astronomy and World Heritage initiative, and it gave archaeologists a way to read monuments as instruments rather than as mere structures.

The field also speaks to contemporary concerns. Indigenous star knowledge is now recognised as an endangered heritage, threatened by light pollution and the loss of languages, and ethnoastronomers work with communities to record it. At the same time, the field's findings challenge the idea that science belongs only to modernity: the sky watchers of the Maya, the Pawnee, and the Bororo were observers, measurers, and theorists in their own right, and ethnoastronomy is the record of their science.
