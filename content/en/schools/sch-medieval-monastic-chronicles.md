# Early Medieval Monastic Chronicles (Bede the Venerable, Gregory of Tours)

An early Western European historiographical tradition, produced by monks within monastic institutions, which mixes ecclesiastical history (the spread of Christianity, the lives of saints) with political-royal history (the conflicts of the emerging kingdoms after the fall of the Western Roman Empire).

## Gregory of Tours: The History of the Franks

**Gregory, Bishop of Tours** (c. 538–594 CE), is the author of the *History of the Franks* (*Historia Francorum*), the most important surviving narrative source on the history of the early Merovingian Frankish kingdom. Gregory combines a detailed political narrative of the struggles and bloody violence of warring kings with a theological interest in the miracles of saints and the role of the Church, without a clear methodological separation between the two kinds of content. The Church and the emerging state are intertwined in his text as two faces of a single process.

## Bede the Venerable: English Ecclesiastical History

**Bede the Venerable** (c. 673–735 CE), the English monk, represents a more methodologically disciplined model in his *Ecclesiastical History of the English People* (*Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum*, 731 CE). He documents the conversion of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms, relying on written sources and correspondence with continental monasteries, with a precise chronological awareness. He is known for systematically using the Anno Domini (AD) dating system early on, which had a lasting influence.

## Method and Limitation

- **The monk as historian**: historical writing in this period was primarily an ecclesiastical function. The theological motive (documenting the triumph of Christianity, glorifying saints) always sits alongside political documentation, without a clear separation between them.
- **Scarcity of alternative sources**: the texts of Gregory and Bede are nearly the only surviving sources for their respective periods, which gives them a near-monopolistic source authority, making the critical cross-checking later developed by the Rankean tradition difficult.
- **A bridge toward the Arabic annalistic school**: this tradition structurally parallels early Arabic annalistic historiography (`sch-arabic-annalistic-historiography`) in its reliance on the religious-institutional authority of the narrator, although the mechanisms of documentation differ (oral isnad in al-Tabari versus the direct written record in Bede).
