# Advaita Vedānta (Non-dualism)

The non-dualist Vedantic school (Non-dualism): Brahman is the only reality, the manifold world is a conditioned appearance (maya), and the individual self (atman) is identical with Brahman upon realization. It is the best-known school of Vedanta, the tradition that interprets the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras and the Bhagavad Gita.

## Shankara and the Lineage

- **Adi Shankara (Śaṅkarācārya)**: the most prominent philosopher of this school; traditional accounts place him in 788–820 CE, while some contemporary scholars hold that he lived in the first half of the 8th century. He was born in Kerala and wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras and the Gita, as well as independent works such as the *Upadesha Sahasri*. Tradition attributes to him the founding of four monasteries at Sringeri, Dwarka, Puri and Jyotirmath.
- **Gaudapada**: the teacher of Shankara's teacher, and author of the *Mandukya Karika*, which is regarded as the earliest surviving systematic presentation of the thesis of non-origination (ajativada).

## Central Ideas

- **Brahman alone**: the doctrine is traditionally summarised in the verse "Brahma satyam, jagat mithya, jivo brahmaiva naparah" (attributed to Shankara in the tradition, though not found in his major commentaries): Brahman is real, the world has no absolute reality, and the individual self is none other than Brahman.
- **Maya and ignorance (avidya)**: the power by which the One appears as many. Commentators contrast attributeless Brahman (nirguna) with the God who has attributes (Ishvara), who is Brahman from the standpoint of maya.
- **Superimposition (adhyasa)**: Shankara opens his commentary on the Brahma Sutras with an analysis of superimposition: we project the attributes of the self onto the non-self, and vice versa, the famous example being the mistaking of a rope for a snake.
- **Levels of reality**: for Shankara there are in principle two levels (absolute reality and practical reality); later Advaitins added a third level, illusory appearance (pratibhasika), such as dream and mirage, so that the order became: paramarthika, vyavaharika and pratibhasika.
- **Liberation through knowledge (jnana)**: knowledge through the great statements ("tat tvam asi": you are that) removes ignorance, and it requires qualification through discrimination between the permanent and the transient, renunciation, self-control and longing for liberation.
- **Liberation in life (jivanmukti)**: the knower can attain liberation while alive.

## Prominent Later Philosophers

- **Suresvara and Padmapada**: the two direct disciples of Shankara.
- **Vachaspati Mishra** (9th or 10th century): author of the *Bhamati*, a commentary on the Brahma Sutras, from which the Bhamati school derives.
- **Prakashatman** (c. 12th–13th century; dates uncertain): author of the *Pañcapādikā-vivaraṇa*, from which the Vivarana school takes its name.
- **Vidyaranya** (14th century): the *Panchadashi*.
- **Madhusudana Sarasvati** (16th century): the *Advaitasiddhi*, in reply to rivals.
- **Appayya Dikshita** (16th century).

## Methods of Knowledge

The school relies on shruti (revealed text: the Upanishads) as the source of knowledge of Brahman, because sense perception and inference do not reach it; Shankara accepts reasoning as an auxiliary tool that rebuts objections and supports the meaning of the text. Practice rests on three stages: hearing (shravana), rational reflection (manana) and inner realization (nididhyasana). He holds that knowledge removes ignorance as the light of a lamp removes darkness, not as the production of something new.

## Influence and Spread

- The school became one of the most influential in Hindu thought, and Radhakrishnan and Vivekananda in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries presented it anew to the Western reader; Ramana Maharshi and his experience with "Who am I?" are associated with it.
- In the late Islamic period, Dara Shikoh (1615–1659) translated the Upanishads into Persian in 1657 and wrote *Majma' al-Bahrayn* (1655) comparing the terminology of Vedanta with Islamic Sufism, and he compared "Brahman" with the Real (al-Haqq) in the unity of being. The Vedantic *Yoga Vasistha* was translated into Persian and read among the Sufis. Al-Biruni (11th century) preceded them in introducing Indian doctrines in *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind*.
- In Europe the Upanishads were transmitted through Anquetil-Duperron's Latin translation (1801–1802) from the Persian, which influenced Schopenhauer.

## Limits and Critique

- Ramanuja accused it, in the *Sri Bhashya*, of having an inconceivable concept of ignorance: where does ignorance reside and what is its cause? He raised seven objections to the theory of maya.
- Madhva held that the world is real and that differences are eternal; Vaishnava critics of Advaita, including Madhva's school, charged it with being "crypto-Buddhism" (pracchanna bauddha), a charge Advaitins rejected.
- Some fear that explaining the world as maya may justify moral indifference; the Advaitins replied that ethics is a condition of qualification for knowledge and that the practical world is governed according to its own level.
- Contemporary studies ask how much of the works attributed to Shankara actually goes back to Shankara himself, and whether the school's whole system was the work of later thinkers.
