# Digital Anthropology

Digital anthropology is the ethnographic study of digital technologies and the social worlds they create. It examines how people use computers, mobile phones, social media, games, and online platforms — and how those technologies, in turn, reshape kinship, friendship, work, politics, religion, and identity. Rather than treating the digital as a separate, virtual realm, digital anthropologists study it as part of everyday life, continuous with the offline worlds in which people already live.

## History and Origins

The field grew out of the encounter between anthropology and the rapid spread of personal computing and the internet in the 1990s. Early studies focused on online communities and virtual worlds, often asking whether the internet created a new kind of society. Daniel Miller and Don Slater's The Internet: An Ethnographic Approach (2000), based on fieldwork in Trinidad, argued against this view: Trinidadians did not leave their social world behind when they went online, but extended it, using the internet to deepen family ties, conduct business, and express national identity.

A second wave of research followed the rise of mobile phones and social media. Heather Horst and Daniel Miller's The Cell Phone: An Anthropology of Communication (2006), based on research in Jamaica, showed how the mobile phone transformed courtship, remittances, and family obligation. Tom Boellstorff's Coming of Age in Second Life: An Anthropologist Explores the Virtually Human (2008) offered a full ethnography of a virtual world, demonstrating that avatar-based social life had its own norms, economies, and intimacies worthy of the same methods used in any field site.

The institutional consolidation of the field came with the launch of the world's first master's programme in digital anthropology at University College London in 2009, founded by Daniel Miller, and the publication of Digital Anthropology, edited by Heather Horst and Daniel Miller (2012), which set out six principles for the new subdiscipline, including the insistence that digital technologies should be studied in the context of wider social life, and that the digital is best understood through long-term ethnography rather than technological determinism.

## Key Figures and Works

Daniel Miller is the field's central figure. His books Tales from Facebook (2011), Social Media in an English Village (2016), and The Comfort of People (2017) traced how social media reshaped community life, while the Why We Post project (2012–2017), a comparative ethnography of social media in nine countries, produced a series of open-access volumes including How the World Changed Social Media (2016). The follow-up Anthropology of Smartphones and Smart Ageing project (2017–2022) studied how older people around the world adopted smartphones, culminating in The Global Smartphone (2021).

Heather Horst has been equally influential, writing on mobile phones, material culture, and digital media in Jamaica, Fiji, and among Pacific Islander communities, and co-founding the Digital Ethnography Research Centre at RMIT University. Gabriella Coleman studied hacker communities, notably in Coding Freedom: The Ethics and Aesthetics of Hacking (2012), and later documented the Anonymous movement in Hacker, Hoaxer, Whistleblower, Spy (2014). Mizuko Ito's Hanging Out, Messing Around, and Geeking Out: Kids Living and Learning with New Media (2009) showed how young people use digital media for friendship, learning, and creative production. Faye Ginsburg's work on disability media and digital storytelling connected the field to the anthropology of media and activism.

## Method

Digital anthropology adapts classic ethnographic methods to digital settings. Researchers conduct participant observation online — joining social media groups, following conversations, playing games, and tracing networks — while also spending time with people offline, in homes, schools, and workplaces, to see how digital practices fit into daily life. This combination, sometimes called digital ethnography, rejects the idea that online and offline can be studied separately.

The method raises new ethical and practical questions. Online material is often public yet personal; researchers must decide what can be quoted, how to protect informants' identities, and how to handle the vast scale of digital data. The speed of technological change also means that fieldwork can be outdated quickly, pushing researchers toward comparative and longitudinal designs, and toward collaborative projects in which teams study the same platform or technology across many countries at once.

## Influence and Contemporary Directions

Digital anthropology has reshaped how anthropology thinks about its core subjects. Kinship is now studied through messaging apps and video calls that sustain transnational families; economic anthropology examines platform labour, gig work, and digital money; political anthropology studies online activism, misinformation, and digital surveillance; and the anthropology of religion explores online ritual, virtual pilgrimage, and religious community on social media. The field has also contributed to debates about privacy, algorithmic governance, and artificial intelligence, insisting that these are social and cultural questions, not only technical ones.

Current directions include the anthropology of data and algorithms, the study of artificial intelligence and its cultural reception, research on digital health and ageing, and ethnographies of platform economies in the Global South. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the field's relevance, as work, schooling, and social life moved online almost overnight, and anthropologists documented how societies adapted. Digital anthropology's central lesson endures: technologies do not determine human behaviour; people make technologies their own, and in doing so reveal the cultures they already inhabit.
