# Vedānta (umbrella)

Vedānta means "the end of the Veda," and is the name given to a group of Indian schools of thought based on the interpretation of the Upanishads, the Brahma Sūtra, and the Bhagavad Gita. They investigate the nature of Brahman (the supreme reality), the Ātman (the self), the world, and the path to liberation. It is also called "Uttara Mīmāṃsā" (the later inquiry), to distinguish it from "Pūrva Mīmāṃsā," which is concerned with ritual. It is not one school but a family that agrees in its sources and differs in interpreting the relation among God, the soul, and the world.

## The Three Sources

The commentators call the authoritative texts the "Prasthāna Traya," that is, the three foundations: the Upanishads (Śruti, that is, what is heard), the Bhagavad Gita (Smṛti, memory), and the Brahma Sūtra (Nyāya Prasthāna, the logical foundation) attributed to Bādarāyaṇa (composed, on modern estimates, in the early centuries CE), which consists of extremely condensed aphorisms that gather the arguments for interpreting the Upanishads and reply to the other schools, and which cannot be understood without explanation. For that reason the schools branch according to the commentary that each founder wrote on these texts.

## The Schools

- Advaita (non-dualism): Shankara (traditionally dated between 788 and 820 CE, and placed by modern scholars in the 8th century), who holds that Brahman alone is real, that the multiplicity of the world arises from ignorance (avidyā) and "māyā," and that the self is Brahman. His followers include the Bhāmatī and Vivaraṇa schools and others.
- Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism): Ramanuja (traditionally 1017–1137; modern scholars place him in the 11th–12th centuries), who holds that the world and souls are real attributes of God and his body, that God is a personal being, Vishnu, and that salvation comes through love (bhakti) and surrender.
- Dvaita (dualism): Madhva (1238–1317 according to modern scholars, whereas tradition places him some forty years earlier), who establishes five real and eternal differences: between God and the soul, between God and matter, between souls and matter, among souls, and among material things.
- Bhedābheda: holds to unity and difference together; among its figures are Bhāskara and Nimbarka (Dvaitādvaita), and the Gaudiya tradition of Chaitanya (1486–1534), later systematized as Acintya Bhedābheda, that is, inconceivable unity and difference.
- Shuddhadvaita: Vallabha (1479–1531), who sees the world as a real manifestation of Krishna.

## The Central Questions

The disputes revolve around three questions: Is Brahman mere consciousness without attributes (nirguṇa) or a personal God with attributes (saguṇa)? Is the world an illusion or a reality that issued from God? Is the soul identical with God, a part of Him, or different from Him? And how is salvation achieved: by knowledge alone, by knowledge and action, or by love and grace? Each commentator formulated a detailed answer, argued from the texts of the Upanishads, and each party criticized the other's understanding of the texts.

## Modern Vedānta

In the 19th and 20th centuries a modern Vedānta appeared that combined the teachings of Advaita with the influence of Western thought. Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833) called for a monotheism drawn from the Upanishads; Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902), a disciple of Ramakrishna, spread Vedānta in the West from the Chicago Congress of 1893; and Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975) offered a comparative philosophical reading. The Arya Samaj of Dayananda, by contrast, rejected Advaita and affirmed the Veda, and is therefore not counted as Vedāntic.

## Vedānta and Islamic Sufism

The comparison between Vedānta and Sufi pantheism (waḥdat al-wujūd) has occupied Muslim and non-Muslim scholars. Al-Biruni (died after 1048), in *Tahqiq ma li-l-Hind* (Verifying What Belongs to India), noted that the Indians' statements about the union of the soul with the Absolute resemble the statements of some Sufis, and he mentioned al-Bistami and al-Hallaj. Prince Dara Shikoh (1615–1659), in *Majma' al-Bahrayn* (The Mingling of the Two Oceans), drew a terminological correspondence between Islamic Sufism and Vedānta, and held that the difference lay in expression, not in what is intended. Orthodox critics considered this a deviation, and Dara Shikoh, defeated by his brother Aurangzeb in the war of succession, was executed in 1659 on a charge of apostasy, for intertwined religious and political reasons. The exact parallel between Advaita and Ibn Arabi's waḥdat al-wujūd remains disputed: Shankara sees multiplicity as an illusion, while Ibn Arabi speaks of the manifestation of the One in the many without abolishing the sharia.

## Limits

The multiplicity of schools makes the expression "Vedānta" a generalization, and the traditional chronology of the founders' lives conflicts with the findings of historical study. Modern studies often present Advaita as the center of Vedānta, which is a simplification, since the personalist schools have had large followings in many regions.

## Method: Commentary and Debate

Vedānta took the form of continuous commentary (bhāṣya) and commentary on commentary, as the followers of each school wrote a series of sub-commentaries. One characteristic of its method is the presentation of the argument by which the author replies to the opposing view (pūrvapakṣa followed by siddhānta), so the texts include an exposition of the views of the other schools, which made them a source for knowledge of the ancient Indian sects such as Sāṃkhya, Vaiśeṣika, and Buddhism. Public debates in temples and royal courts were a means of affirming prestige, and the biographies of the founders recount debates in which they triumphed.
