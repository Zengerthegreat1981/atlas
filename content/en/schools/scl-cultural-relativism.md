# Cultural Relativism

Cultural relativism is the methodological claim that beliefs and institutions should be interpreted in the terms of the social world that sustains them, rather than ranked against the customs of the observer. In American anthropology it became a working rule in the first half of the twentieth century, closely tied to Franz Boas and his students. It was aimed at two habits: judging other peoples by a European standard of civilization, and explaining difference by race. The rule does not, by itself, say that every practice is morally right. That stronger reading, often called moral relativism, is a later and contested extension.

## Boas and the critique of race

Boas argued from measurement and from fieldwork that differences in custom are historical, not the expression of fixed biological types. *The Mind of Primitive Man* (1911) attacked the idea that "primitive" peoples think in a way that is racially inferior. His teaching at Columbia trained a generation to learn languages, record texts, and treat each culture as a history of its own. Relativism here was a research discipline: suspend the home standard long enough to see how a practice fits kinship, economy, and cosmology. Margaret Mead's *Coming of Age in Samoa* (1928) and Ruth Benedict's *Patterns of Culture* (1934) carried the argument to a wide public. Benedict's portraits of the Pueblo, the Kwakwaka'wakw, and Dobu were meant to show that what counts as a valued personality is patterned by culture.

Melville Herskovits gave the stance its best-known programmatic statement in *Man and His Works* (1948), linking relativism to respect for cultural difference and to opposition to colonialism and racial hierarchy. By then the position was both a method and a public ethic.

## What the method requires

A relativist description asks what a practice does for the people who live it, which categories they use, and which sanctions enforce it. Witchcraft accusations, for example, are not dismissed as error before one has asked who is accused, on what evidence, and with what consequence for kinship. The same restraint applies to the ethnographer's own society: Benedict urged readers to see their customs as one arrangement among others. Comparison remains possible. Boasian anthropology compared constantly. What it refused was a single ladder from "savage" to "civilized."

The method also limits what an ethnographer may claim to have explained. A custom can be intelligible without being admirable, and intelligibility is not the same as agreement. Fieldwork still depends on translation, and translation imports the writer's language. Relativism is a check on premature judgment, not a guarantee of a neutral account.

## Critiques

Several objections became standard after the 1950s. Philosophers asked whether the view can condemn slavery, genocide, or the subordination of women if each is "their custom." Anthropologists who had used relativism against racism were unwilling to treat every local hierarchy as beyond criticism. A second objection is that cultures are not sealed wholes. Colonial rule, trade, and mission Christianity already shaped many of the societies Benedict and Herskovits described. A third is political: a strict ban on judgment can excuse the powerful inside a society, and can be used by outsiders to withhold criticism that the people studied themselves make.

Mead's Samoan ethnography later became a public controversy, especially after Derek Freeman's *Margaret Mead and Samoa* (1983). That dispute is about evidence and about Samoa, not a refutation of the whole relativist rule, but it showed how much weight public argument had placed on a single case. Within the discipline, interpretive and historical work kept the demand to understand local meanings while dropping the picture of cultures as bounded personalities.

## What remains

Cultural relativism, as a school in the narrow Boasian sense, belongs to the period from Boas's early teaching through the mid-century textbooks. Its lasting piece is modest and still taught: do not explain difference by race, and do not describe a practice before asking how it is lived. Human-rights claims, feminist anthropology, and studies of violence all press the further question of when understanding should turn into criticism. That question is no longer settled by the 1948 formula.
