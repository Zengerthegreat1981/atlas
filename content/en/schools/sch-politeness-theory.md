# Politeness Theory and Face

A pragmatic current that studies how language is used to manage social relationships during communication, specifically how speakers mitigate the potential threatening effect of certain speech acts (such as requests, refusals or criticism) on the **face** of the addressee or their own. Its most influential formulation is that of **Penelope Brown** and **Stephen Levinson**, in a 1978 chapter ("Universals in Language Usage: Politeness Phenomena") that was reissued as the book *Politeness: Some Universals in Language Usage* (1987) with a new introduction, drawing on the concept of "face" developed by the sociologist Erving Goffman in his analysis of face-to-face social interaction.

## Positive and Negative Face

Brown and Levinson distinguish between two kinds of face possessed by every participant in an interaction: **positive face**, the individual's desire to be accepted, admired and socially appreciated, and **negative face**, the desire for freedom of action without constraint or imposition by others. **Face-threatening acts** are defined as any speech act that threatens one of these two dimensions, such as a request (which threatens the addressee's negative face) or a criticism (which threatens their positive face).

## Graduated Mitigation Strategies

Brown and Levinson proposed a sequence of five graduated strategies for "softening" a threat, from which the speaker chooses according to the expected size of the threat. This is a function of three factors: the social distance between the interlocutors, the power difference between them, and the degree of "weightiness" of the act in that culture. The strategies range from **bald on-record** speech with no redress, to **positive politeness** (claiming common ground and appreciation), to **negative politeness** (conventional indirectness, hedges, deference, and apology), to **off-record** hints and ambiguity, and finally to not performing the act. Brown and Levinson estimated the weight of a face threat from social distance, relative power, and the culturally ranked imposition of the act.

## The Universality Claim and Cultural Critique

Brown and Levinson argued that these strategies are **universal**, cutting across cultures. The claim, however, faced wide criticism. Researchers such as Yoshiko Matsumoto and Sachiko Ide argued that languages such as Japanese require honorific forms (Honorifics) built into the morphological system itself, rather than subject to free strategic choice. They also argued that the very notion of the "individual face" is biased toward Western individualist assumptions that do not apply as readily to collectivist cultures.

## Alternatives: Leech, Spencer-Oatey and Mills

**Geoffrey Leech** proposed a complementary alternative by formulating "politeness maxims" on the model of Grice's conversational maxims (such as the tact maxim and the sympathy maxim). **Helen Spencer-Oatey** developed the framework of "rapport management," which broadens the concept of politeness to cover the management of the social relationship as a whole, not merely the mitigation of threat. **Sara Mills** offered an influential feminist critique, arguing that politeness is not a property inherent in an utterance but a social judgment that is evaluated interpretively within a specific community of practice, and that preconceptions about "feminine politeness" carry gender biases that should be deconstructed rather than accepted.

## Impact

Despite these criticisms, the Brown and Levinson framework long remained a central reference in cross-linguistic work on politeness. It influenced institutional discourse analysis, the teaching of pragmatic routines in additional languages, and later work on socially tuned dialogue systems.
