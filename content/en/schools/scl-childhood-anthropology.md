# Childhood Anthropology

Childhood anthropology is the study of children and childhood as cultural phenomena in their own right. Rather than treating children as incomplete adults, or as passive recipients of socialisation, it asks how children understand their worlds, make meaning, form relationships, and shape the societies they grow up in. Childhood, in this view, is not a universal biological stage that simply unfolds, but a culturally constructed phase of life whose length, meaning, and expectations vary enormously across time and place.

## History and Origins

Anthropologists have always encountered children in the field, but for much of the discipline's history children appeared mainly as background figures in ethnographies of adults, or as evidence in debates about human nature. Margaret Mead's Coming of Age in Samoa (1928) made adolescence a central anthropological question by arguing that the turmoil of the teenage years was cultural rather than biological, and Ruth Benedict's essay "Continuities and Discontinuities in Cultural Conditioning" (1938) contrasted societies in which children were gradually introduced to adult roles with those in which childhood and adulthood were sharply separated. Franz Boas's studies of child growth had already shown that children's physical development varied with the conditions in which they were raised.

A decisive shift came in the 1970s. Charlotte Hardman's article "Can There Be an Anthropology of Children?" (1973) argued that children possess their own social worlds, partly independent of adults, and deserve to be studied as social actors. Lawrence Hirschfeld's 2002 essay "Why Don't Anthropologists Like Children?" in American Anthropologist sharpened the point, charging the discipline with neglecting a universal dimension of human experience. Meanwhile, the historian Philippe Ariès, in Centuries of Childhood (1960, English translation 1962), had argued that the idea of childhood as a protected, distinct phase of life was a historical development of early modern Europe. In Britain, the sociologists Allison James and Alan Prout, in Constructing and Reconstructing Childhood (1990), and with Chris Jenks in Theorizing Childhood (1998), gave the emerging field a theoretical programme: childhood is socially constructed, children are active agents, and children's own perspectives are legitimate sources of knowledge.

## Key Figures and Works

David Lancy's The Anthropology of Childhood: Cherubs, Chattel, Changelings (2008; third edition 2022) became the field's landmark synthesis, cataloguing more than a century of ethnographic research on infancy, play, work, schooling, and adolescence. Lancy argued that the Western model of the sheltered, school-bound child is a recent and unusual arrangement, and that in most societies children have been workers, carers, and economic contributors.

Other key figures include Elinor Ochs and Bambi Schieffelin, whose work on language socialisation showed how children learn not only words but cultural ways of being; Alma Gottlieb, who studied Beng infants in Côte d'Ivoire and challenged Western assumptions about infant care; and Robert LeVine, whose cross-cultural studies of childrearing linked parenting practices to the economic and ecological demands of different societies. In the study of children's play, Brian Sutton-Smith's The Ambiguity of Play (1997) and the folklorists Iona and Peter Opie, whose The Lore and Language of Schoolchildren (1959) documented children's own rhymes, games, and rituals, remain foundational.

The field also owes much to the study of children's agency in difficult circumstances: Sharon Stephens's edited volume *Children and the Politics of Culture* (1995), and the ethnography of street children, child labourers, and child soldiers, which showed children navigating violence, poverty, and displacement with resourcefulness rather than mere victimhood.

## Method

The central methodological commitment of childhood anthropology is to take children seriously as informants and collaborators. Ethnographers spend extended time with children in their own settings — homes, playgrounds, classrooms, streets — observing peer interaction, play, and daily routines, and listening to children's own accounts of their lives. This "child-centred" ethnography requires methods adapted to children's ages and communication styles: drawing, storytelling, photography, and play-based interviews alongside participant observation.

Such research raises distinctive ethical questions. Children cannot give legal consent, so researchers must negotiate assent from children themselves as well as permission from parents and institutions, protect children's privacy, and avoid exposing them to harm. These ethical demands have pushed the field toward participatory approaches in which children help shape research questions and interpretations, and toward a reflexive awareness of the power imbalance between adult researcher and child.

## Influence and Contemporary Directions

Childhood anthropology has reshaped neighbouring fields. It influenced the sociology of childhood, developmental psychology, and education, and its concerns intersect with debates around the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), which recognised children as bearers of rights as well as objects of protection. Its insistence on children's agency has informed policy debates on child labour, schooling, migration, and digital media, where anthropologists document how children use phones, games, and social platforms in ways adults rarely anticipate.

Contemporary work extends the field in several directions: the anthropology of youth and adolescence; studies of children's health, disability, and care; research on children in transnational families and refugee settings; and a growing interest in how climate change, urbanisation, and new technologies reshape the experience of growing up. What unites these strands is the founding conviction of the field: that childhood is not a waiting room for adult life, but a social world in its own right, and that understanding it is essential to understanding humanity.
