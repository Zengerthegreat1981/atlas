# Platonic Academy (Old / Middle / New Skeptical)

A philosophical institution founded by Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) near the grove of "Academus" (Ἀκαδημία) northwest of Athens around 387 BCE. It continued in various forms until the closing of the Athenian schools under the Emperor **Justinian** in 529 CE. Over this period its philosophical character changed radically. Institutional continuity is only indirect after Sulla's sack of Athens in 86 BCE and the devastation of the grove: the school that was called Platonic in the fifth century CE was a private circle in the house of one of its heads, not a physical institution continuous with the original grove.

## The Major Phases

- **Old Academy (387–265 BCE)**: Plato, Speusippus, Xenocrates, Polemo, Crates, Crantor.
- **Middle Academy (265–c. 160 BCE)**: Arcesilaus and his successors, in which the doctrine turned into **probabilistic skepticism**.
- **New Academy**: Carneades, Clitomachus, and Philo of Larissa, after which Antiochus of Ascalon turned against skepticism around 87 BCE.
- **Middle Platonism**: Plutarch, Alcinous, and Numenius of Apamea, who interpreted Plato outside the institutional Academy.
- **Athenian Neoplatonism**: Plutarch of Athens, Syrianus, Proclus, and Damascius, the last heads of the school.

## Central Concepts (First Phase)

- **Theory of Forms (ἰδέαι)**: sensible things derive from eternal, separate Forms, which are perfect reality. The Form is one (the horse), while sensible things are many (horses).
- **Knowledge and opinion (ἐπιστήμη and δόξα)**: knowledge concerns the unchanging Forms, and opinion concerns the changing sensible world.
- **Dialectic (διαλεκτική)**: the method of reaching truth through question and answer; for Plato it is the highest of methods.
- **The soul (ψυχή)**: the soul precedes the body, lives in the world of Forms before birth, and is then imprisoned in the body in order to return.
- **Political philosophy**: the *Republic* (Πολιτεία) envisions a society ruled by philosophers.
- **Ethics**: virtue is knowledge of the good, and vice is ignorance.
- **Eros (ἔρως)**: the emotional drive to seek beauty and truth, as in the *Symposium*.
- In the next phase Speusippus and Xenocrates began to link the theory of Forms to numbers in the Pythagorean manner, a tendency Aristotle criticized in the *Metaphysics*.

## Concepts in the Later Phases

- **Academic skepticism** (Arcesilaus and Carneades): rejecting the claim to certain knowledge while retaining the persuasive as the criterion for action.
- **Eclecticism** (Antiochus): in the first century BCE he tried to reconcile Platonism with Aristotelianism and Stoicism and regarded this as a return to the Old Academy.
- **The commentary tradition**: in the first centuries CE Platonism became a project of interpreting Plato's texts. Alcinous wrote the *Handbook of Platonism* and Plutarch wrote ethical commentaries, and in the third century Plotinus in Rome formulated a new system, Neoplatonism.

## Principal Philosophers

- **Plato** (428–348 BCE): the founder.
- **Speusippus** (c. 408–339 BCE): Plato's nephew and successor, who focused on mathematics and classification.
- **Xenocrates** (c. 396–314 BCE): a conservative Platonist who combined numbers with the Forms.
- **Arcesilaus** (c. 316–241 BCE): turned the Academy toward skepticism.
- **Carneades** (c. 214–129 BCE): the most prominent of its skeptics.

## Legacy

- **Neoplatonism**: the Platonic inheritance passed to Plotinus and Proclus.
- **Arabic-Islamic philosophy**: the Arabs knew Plato through translations and summaries, among them Averroes' (Ibn Rushd's) commentary on Plato's *Republic*, and al-Farabi wrote *The Philosophy of Plato, Its Parts and the Ranks of Its Parts*. The *Discourse on the Pure Good* attributed to Aristotle, derived from sources in Proclus, was translated, as was the *Theology*, which comes from Plotinus's *Enneads*. Arabic sources told the story of the transfer of philosophical teaching from Athens and Alexandria to Antioch, then Harran, then Baghdad, and connected with it is the episode of Damascius and his colleagues taking refuge at the court of Khosrow Anushirvan around 531 CE.
- **Christian theology**: Augustine was influenced by Neoplatonism and recast much theology in Platonic concepts.
- **The Renaissance**: Cosimo de' Medici is traditionally credited with founding the "Platonic Academy" in Florence (its institutional form is debated by modern scholars), Ficino translated Plato into Latin (1484), and Pico della Mirandola was influenced.

## The Closure

- The historian Agathias reports that Damascius, Simplicius, and four others left Athens after the teaching of philosophy was banned in Justinian's legislation around 529 CE, and then returned after the peace treaty with the Persians in 532. In 1969 Alan Cameron reread the episode and warned against exaggerating it: there was no continuous Academy in the institutional sense, and philosophy continued to be taught in Alexandria.

## Limits

- The "Academy" is not a school with a fixed doctrine; it underwent major transformations, and the Platonic dialogues themselves differ across Plato's phases.
- The dialogues are not school texts and are read as literary-philosophical writing. What Aristotle called the "unwritten doctrines" is available only through others.
- The writings of the early Academy's members are lost, and only titles and fragments have reached us.
- In modern languages the term "academy" has come to be used for any later educational or scholarly institution.
