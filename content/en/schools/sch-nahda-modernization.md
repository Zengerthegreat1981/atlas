# The Arab Nahda: Intellectual Modernization

A broad intellectual and cultural current in the Arab lands, from the late eighteenth century to the early twentieth, that sought to reformulate Arab and Islamic thought in response to European military, technological and political superiority. It combined, in a selective way, the revival of the classical Arab and Islamic heritage with the import of European tools of modernity in education, administration, law, journalism and literature.

## Origins and Context

The Nahda is conventionally dated from the French expedition to Egypt (1798), which exposed, for the ruling and intellectual elites, a wide gap in technical and military capacity between Ottoman Egypt and contemporary Europe. Muhammad Ali Pasha (ruled Egypt 1805–1848) turned that shock into a state project of military and educational reform. It included student missions to France, new schools, the Bulaq press (founded about 1820–1821) and a regular army trained on European lines. Rifa'a al-Tahtawi's stay in Paris (1826–1831) is a landmark of the Egyptian side of the movement. He returned with *Takhlis al-Ibriz fi Talkhis Bariz* (Bulaq, 1834), a sustained Arabic account of Parisian institutions, sciences and manners. A parallel literary revival developed in Bilad al-Sham. It drew on missionary schools, on Arab Christian scholars, and on an older Arabic print culture that included the press at the monastery of Shuwayr, founded in the 1730s. Butrus al-Bustani began *Da'irat al-Ma'arif* in 1876, often described as the first general encyclopedia in Arabic, and founded the National School in Beirut in 1863. He died in 1883, before the encyclopedia was finished; his family continued it.

## Intellectual Pillars

- **Religious reform and ijtihad**: Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, and then Muhammad Abduh, argued for renewed ijtihad (independent legal reasoning) and against uncritical taqlid. Their claim was that Islam, rightly understood, does not contradict reason or modern science, and that decline came from the way the tradition had been received. Abduh's Qur'an lectures were recorded in the journal *al-Manar* and continued by Rashid Rida as *Tafsir al-Manar*. Rida carried the commentary past Abduh's death in 1905, but it was never finished: it stops partway through Sura Yusuf. Abduh also issued reformist fatwas as Mufti of Egypt from 1899.
- **Political reform**: In *Aqwam al-Masalik fi Ma'rifat Ahwal al-Mamalik* (Tunis, 1867), Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi tied the strength of European states to consultation, published law and the accountability of ministers, and he argued for reforms of that kind inside an Islamic polity. He had already taken part in the Tunisian reforms of the 1860s. As prime minister (1873–1877) he worked on administration, finance and education, including the Sadiqi College (1875). The Tunisian constitution of 1861 had been suspended in 1864, before that premiership. He later served briefly as Ottoman Grand Vizier (1878–1879).
- **Language and education**: al-Tahtawi, al-Bustani and Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq worked to stretch Arabic so that it could carry modern sciences and political terms. al-Tahtawi directed the School of Languages (Madrasat al-Alsun), founded in 1835. al-Bustani worked through schooling and lexicography. al-Shidyaq did so through lexicography and through his Istanbul newspaper *al-Jawa'ib*, founded in 1861.
- **The woman question**: In *Tahrir al-Mar'a* (1899) and *al-Mar'a al-Jadida* (1900), Qasim Amin argued for the schooling of girls, tighter limits on polygamy and divorce, and an end to veiling the face. He based the case on a reformist reading of religious texts. The books are often linked to Abduh's circle; how far Abduh shaped them is disputed. They were not the first Arabic arguments for educating girls: al-Tahtawi had already made that case. They did open a long public controversy.
- **Islamic political solidarity**: al-Afghani's travels included India, Egypt, Istanbul, Paris and London. He urged political solidarity among Muslim peoples in the face of European expansion, and he treated religious reform as part of that politics, not as a juristic exercise alone.

## Notable Figures

- **Rifa'a Rafi' al-Tahtawi** (1801–1873): translator, educator and author of *Takhlis al-Ibriz*.
- **Jamal al-Din al-Afghani** (1838/1839–1897): religious reformer and advocate of Muslim political solidarity. He presented himself as Afghan; the biographical evidence places his birth at Asadabad in Iran.
- **Muhammad Abduh** (1849–1905): Mufti of Egypt and a leading reformist scholar.
- **Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi** (c. 1822–1890): Tunisian-Ottoman statesman and author of *Aqwam al-Masalik*.
- **Butrus al-Bustani** (1819–1883): Lebanese encyclopedist, lexicographer and educator.
- **Qasim Amin** (1863–1908): Egyptian judge and author of *Tahrir al-Mar'a* and *al-Mar'a al-Jadida*.

## Caveats

Historians disagree about the assessment of the Nahda along two axes. First, the extent to which it was an authentic response rooted in the Arab-Islamic heritage, as opposed to a reproduction of orientalist and colonial frameworks of "progress" and "backwardness" that imposed European standards on Arab elites for judging themselves. This critique has been advanced by scholars working in the wake of Edward Said's critique of Orientalism. Second, the limits of the reform project itself: its social base remained largely confined to a narrow urban elite (civil servants, reformist al-Azhar scholars and a rising merchant class), and its ideas reached the countryside and the popular classes only with considerable delay. The tension between the call for religious ijtihad and the call for secularizing institutions also remained unresolved. Later currents divided over this question, between reformist Salafism (Rashid Rida), secular Arab nationalism and constitutional liberalism.

## Legacy and Later Developments

After the First World War and the abolition of the Ottoman Caliphate (1924), writers who inherited the Nahda took different paths. One was secular Arab nationalism, later set out by Sati' al-Husri (1880–1968) and, in another key, by Constantine Zurayk (1909–2000). Another was the reformist Islam of some of Abduh's readers, notably Rashid Rida, which moved toward a more conservative Salafi orientation. A third was Egyptian constitutional liberalism between the 1919 uprising and 1952. Albert Hourani's *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age* studies the wider current from 1798 to 1939, a span longer than that Egyptian political period. The divisions continue an argument already present in the Nahda, between religious reform and the building of secular institutions.
