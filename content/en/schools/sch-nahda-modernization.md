# The Arab Nahda: Intellectual Modernization

A broad intellectual and cultural current in the Arab lands, from the late eighteenth century to the early twentieth, that sought to reformulate Arab and Islamic thought in response to European military, technological and political superiority. It combined, in a selective way, the revival of the classical Arab and Islamic heritage with the import of European tools of modernity in education, administration, law, journalism and literature.

## Origins and Context

The Nahda is conventionally dated from the French expedition to Egypt (1798), which exposed, for the ruling and intellectual elites, a shocking gap in technical and military capacity between Ottoman Egypt and modern Europe. Muhammad Ali Pasha (ruled Egypt 1805–1848) turned this shock into a broad state modernization project. It included sending educational missions to France, founding modern schools and the Bulaq press (1820), and establishing a regular army on the European model. The mission of Rifa'a al-Tahtawi to Paris (1826–1831) marked a symbolic turning point: he returned with his book *Takhlis al-Ibriz fi Talkhis Bariz* (The Extraction of Gold in the Summary of Paris), the first direct and systematic Arabic description of the political and scientific institutions of European modernity. A parallel renaissance developed in Bilad al-Sham, where missionary schools, presses (such as the Shuwayr press) and Arab Christian communities played a prominent role in reviving the Arabic language and its press. Figures in this movement included Butrus al-Bustani, who issued the first modern Arabic encyclopedia (*Da'irat al-Ma'arif*) and founded the National School in Beirut.

## Intellectual Pillars

- **Religious reform and ijtihad**: Jamal al-Din al-Afghani, and then his disciple Muhammad Abduh, called for reopening the gate of ijtihad (independent legal reasoning) and resisting legal stagnation (taqlid). Their argument was that Islam in its essence does not contradict reason and modern science, and that backwardness resulted from an accumulated misunderstanding of the text rather than from religion itself. Abduh embodied this in his Quranic commentary (*Tafsir al-Manar*, completed by his disciple Rashid Rida) and in his reformist fatwas as Mufti of Egypt.
- **Political and constitutional reform**: In his book *Aqwam al-Masalik fi Ma'rifat Ahwal al-Mamalik* (1867), Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi put forward a reformist vision that linked the progress of European states to guarantees of political liberty and accountability. He called for a constitution that would restrict the ruler's authority, and he applied these ideas in practice when he became prime minister in Tunisia and later Grand Vizier of the Ottoman Empire.
- **Language and education reform**: al-Tahtawi, al-Bustani and Ahmad Faris al-Shidyaq worked to modernize the Arabic language lexically and stylistically so that it could absorb modern concepts and sciences. They did this through systematic translation (al-Tahtawi supervised the School of Languages, a translation school) and through cultural journalism.
- **The woman question**: In *Tahrir al-Mar'a* (1899) and *al-Mar'a al-Jadida* (1900), Qasim Amin put forward the first systematic Arab call for women's education, the restriction of polygamy and the easing of the veiling requirement. He based these positions on a reformist interpretation of the religious text, in a line of argument influenced by Muhammad Abduh. The works provoked a broad controversy that lasted for decades.
- **Islamic solidarity against colonialism**: Through his extensive travels from Iran to Egypt, Istanbul, Paris and London, al-Afghani called for political solidarity among Muslim peoples in the face of European domination. He regarded religious reform as an instrument of political renewal rather than a matter of jurisprudence alone.

## Notable Figures

- **Rifa'a Rafi' al-Tahtawi** (1801–1873): pioneer of translation and modern education in Egypt.
- **Jamal al-Din al-Afghani** (1838/1839–1897): advocate of religious reform and Islamic political solidarity.
- **Muhammad Abduh** (1849–1905): Mufti of Egypt and renewer of reformist religious thought.
- **Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi** (c. 1820–1890): Tunisian-Ottoman statesman and political reformer.
- **Butrus al-Bustani** (1819–1883): Lebanese encyclopedist and lexicographer, a pioneer of the literary and lexicographical Nahda.
- **Qasim Amin** (1863–1908): Egyptian judge and thinker, a pioneer of the call for women's education and social emancipation.

## Caveats

Historians disagree about the assessment of the Nahda along two axes. First, the extent to which it was an authentic response rooted in the Arab-Islamic heritage, as opposed to a reproduction of orientalist and colonial frameworks of "progress" and "backwardness" that imposed European standards on Arab elites for judging themselves. This critique has been advanced by scholars working in the wake of Edward Said's critique of Orientalism. Second, the limits of the reform project itself: its social base remained largely confined to a narrow urban elite (civil servants, reformist al-Azhar scholars and a rising merchant class), and its ideas reached the countryside and the popular classes only with considerable delay. The tension between the call for religious ijtihad and the call for secularizing institutions also remained unresolved. Later currents divided over this question, between reformist Salafism (Rashid Rida), secular Arab nationalism and constitutional liberalism.

## Legacy and Later Developments

After the First World War and the abolition of the Ottoman Caliphate (1924), the legacy of the Nahda divided into several paths. One was a secular Arab nationalist path influenced by the ideas of Sati' al-Husri and Constantine Zurayk. Another was a reformist Islamic path that, among some of Abduh's disciples (such as Rashid Rida), shifted toward a more conservative and Salafi orientation. The third was a constitutional liberal path that took shape in Egypt during the Liberal Age (1919–1952), as studied by Albert Hourani. Today these divisions are viewed as a continuation of tensions that were never resolved within the original Nahda project itself: between religious reform and secular modernization.
