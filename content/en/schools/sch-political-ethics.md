# Political Ethics

## Meaning and Definition

Political ethics is a branch of practical philosophy that examines the ethical standards governing authority and its exercise. It asks how political legitimacy is acquired, what the limits of obedience to a ruler are, and whether political action is subject to ethical standards different from those of ordinary individual ethics (as in the questions of "diplomatic lying" or "just war"), or whether it remains subject to the same general standards.

## Historical Context

This inquiry goes back to Plato's *Republic*, which proposed an ideal vision of the philosopher-ruler who governs through wisdom rather than self-interest. A much-discussed turning point in the history of the field came with Machiavelli's *The Prince* (written 1513, published posthumously in 1532), which explicitly separated personal moral virtue from the effectiveness of political rule. Machiavelli held that a ruler may sometimes need to break traditional moral rules in order to preserve the state, and the work is often cited as a landmark of the tradition of "political realism." In the twentieth century, John Rawls placed distributive justice at the center of political philosophy in *A Theory of Justice* (1971). Michael Walzer, in *Just and Unjust Wars* (1977), developed a detailed ethical framework for regulating the resort to war (*jus ad bellum*) and conduct within it (*jus in bello*).

## Foundations in Islamic Political Thought

Islamic heritage offers an early and rich framework for political ethics, centered on the concept of "justice" ('*adl*) as a condition for the legitimacy of rule. Ibn Taymiyya, whose *Al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya* (Governance in accordance with Islamic law) stresses the primacy of justice in rule, is widely quoted as saying that God sustains a just state even if it is unbelieving and does not sustain an unjust one even if it is Muslim; the saying is usually cited from his collected writings, and it presents justice as a standard that governs rule regardless of the ruler's creed. Al-Mawardi, in *Al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* (The Ordinances of Government), detailed the conditions of the imamate and the reciprocal duties between ruler and subjects. Ibn Khaldun, in the *Muqaddimah*, developed a realistic analysis of the cycle of states that blends historical observation (closer to Machiavelli) with a normative framework holding that *'asabiyya* (group solidarity) alone is not sufficient without justice and legitimacy. In Islamic political jurisprudence, a distinction is drawn between the obligation to obey the ruler in what is right, and the refusal to obey him in disobedience to the Creator, based on the hadith "There is no obedience to a created being in disobedience to the Creator." This approximates the Western idea of civil disobedience, although its foundation is philosophically different.

## Central Ideas and Thinkers

Debates in the field revolve around the problem of "dirty hands," which Michael Walzer discussed in his 1973 article "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands." The question is whether a ruler may be justified in committing a morally wrong act (such as lying, or even limited violence) if its consequence is saving the state from a greater catastrophe. In his lecture "Politics as a Vocation" (1919), Max Weber drew an influential distinction between the "ethics of conviction" (*Gesinnungsethik*), which adheres to a principle regardless of consequences, and the "ethics of responsibility" (*Verantwortungsethik*), which holds the politician responsible for anticipating the results of his or her decisions. Weber stressed that the two are not absolute opposites and that responsible politics requires both passion for a cause and a sense of proportion about consequences, though he gave the ethic of responsibility priority for those in power.

## Impact and Application

These debates appear in practice in assessing the legitimacy of humanitarian military intervention, in the criteria for economic sanctions that harm innocent civilians, and in the controversy over the use of torture to extract intelligence, where the calculations of the public interest collide with absolute ethical constraints.

## The Theory of Just War

The theory of just war is one of the most practically applied branches of political ethics. In the tradition that Walzer's book revived, it is divided into two distinct sets of conditions: the justice of resorting to war (*jus ad bellum*), which requires a just cause (such as self-defense), right intention, and last resort after peaceful means have been exhausted; and the justice of conduct during war (*jus in bello*), which requires the distinction between combatants and civilians and proportionality in the force used. This framework finds a precise counterpart in Islamic jurisprudence on jihad, which, drawing on reports of the Prophet's instructions and of the first caliph Abu Bakr al-Siddiq's instructions to his army, prohibited the killing of women, children, the elderly, and non-combatant monks, and forbade mutilating the dead and wanton destruction of trees and buildings. Classical jurists differed on the details and on the permitted exceptions, and the rules are often compared with later laws of armed conflict.

## Corruption and Accountability

This field also addresses the ethics of corruption and the abuse of power. It distinguishes between a system in which the ruler is subject to accountability and oversight (such as the system of *shura* and supervision in Islamic political jurisprudence, which obliges the ruler to consult the people of "binding and loosing" (*ahl al-hall wal-aqd*) and to accept counsel), and an authoritarian system that places the ruler above accountability. Jurists have relied on the hadith "Each of you is a shepherd, and each of you is responsible for his flock" to establish the principle of reciprocal responsibility between ruler and ruled. This parallels the Western concept of the "social contract" in its requirement that the legitimacy of rule rests on the performance of mutual duties, although the founding source differs between a human contract and a trust under divine law.

## Concluding Example

All of these debates come together in assessing a contemporary foreign policy, such as the imposition of comprehensive economic sanctions on an aggressor state. The political utilitarian weighs the overall impact (curbing aggression against the suffering of civilians). The Kantian rejects punishing the innocent as a means of pressuring their rulers. The political jurist, meanwhile, balances averting the harm of aggression against the harm of a siege on the general population, invoking the maxim "the lesser harm is tolerated to avert the greater."
