# British Functionalism

British functionalism — also called structural-functionalism in its later form — was the dominant school of British social anthropology from the 1920s to the 1970s, and the tradition that defined what an ethnographic monograph was for a generation. Its central idea is simple and powerful: a society is a system of interrelated parts, and each part — a ritual, a kinship rule, a political office — exists because of the contribution it makes to maintaining the whole. Where the evolutionists before them had asked where institutions came from, the functionalists asked what they did, here and now. The result was a body of ethnography of extraordinary depth and a theory that, even after its fall from fashion, still shapes how anthropologists think about social order.

## History and Emergence

British functionalism emerged in the 1920s as a deliberate break with the speculative evolutionism of the Victorian era. Its two founders published their landmark monographs in the same year, 1922: Bronislaw Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific*, based on fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands of Melanesia, and A. R. Radcliffe-Brown's *The Andaman Islanders*, based on fieldwork in the Andaman Islands of the Indian Ocean. Malinowski's functionalism was psychological and individual: institutions exist to satisfy human needs — biological, psychological, and social. Radcliffe-Brown's was sociological and structural: what matters is not individual need but the contribution of a practice to the maintenance of social structure, a position he set out systematically in *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952).

The school institutionalized rapidly. Malinowski held the first chair of anthropology at the London School of Economics, and Radcliffe-Brown built departments at Cape Town, Sydney, Chicago, and finally Oxford. Their students — E. E. Evans-Pritchard, Meyer Fortes, Max Gluckman, Edmund Leach, Audrey Richards — carried functionalism across British Africa and the Pacific, and the monographs they produced became the discipline's classics. The school's later phase, from the 1940s onward, is often called structural-functionalism: under Radcliffe-Brown's influence, the emphasis shifted from Malinowski's needs to the analysis of social structure, especially kinship, politics, and law, as a system in equilibrium.

## Key Figures

Bronislaw Malinowski (1884–1942), a Polish scholar who became the central figure of British anthropology, founded modern fieldwork and the functional analysis of institutions. A. R. Radcliffe-Brown (1881–1955) gave the school its theoretical rigor and its comparative program. E. E. Evans-Pritchard (1902–1973) produced its most admired monographs — on the Nuer and the Azande — and later broke with the school, arguing that anthropology was closer to history than to science. Meyer Fortes (1906–1983) developed the structural analysis of kinship and of the Tallensi of Ghana. Max Gluckman (1911–1975) founded the Manchester school, which pushed functionalism toward the study of conflict and social change. Edmund Leach (1910–1989) both exemplified and subverted the tradition: his *Political Systems of Highland Burma* (1954) showed that social structure was not a stable system but a field of competing models. Audrey Richards (1899–1984) brought functionalism to the study of food, nutrition, and women's lives, and remains the school's most prominent woman ethnographer.

## Key Works

The canon is the set of monographs that defined the genre. Malinowski's *Argonauts of the Western Pacific* (1922) and *Crime and Custom in Savage Society* (1926) established the functional method. Radcliffe-Brown's *The Andaman Islanders* (1922) and *Structure and Function in Primitive Society* (1952) stated the structural program. Evans-Pritchard's *The Nuer* (1940) is widely regarded as one of the finest monographs in the tradition, and his *Witchcraft, Oracles and Magic Among the Azande* (1937) its most original. Fortes's studies of the Tallensi and Leach's *Political Systems of Highland Burma* (1954) showed the school's range — and, in Leach's case, its limits. The posthumous publication of Malinowski's field diaries as *A Diary in the Strict Sense of the Term* (1967) became a scandal in its own right: the diaries revealed the boredom, irritation, and racial attitudes that the published monographs had concealed, and they forced the discipline to confront the gap between the ethnographer's experience and the ethnographer's text.

## Method

The functionalist method was intensive, long-term fieldwork in a single society, conducted in the local language, with the aim of describing the whole social system — kinship, economy, politics, law, and religion — as an integrated functioning whole. The ethnographer mapped institutions and asked what each contributed to social order. Comparison was the route to generalization: by comparing the kinship systems of many societies, Radcliffe-Brown hoped to discover the laws of social structure. The method's strength was its holism and its depth; its weakness, as its critics showed, was its assumption of equilibrium. Societies were treated as systems tending toward stability, and history, conflict, and change were difficult to accommodate — a difficulty Gluckman and Leach began to address from inside the tradition, and that later schools made central.

## Influence and Debates

British functionalism's influence is incalculable. It made fieldwork the discipline's defining method, the monograph its defining genre, and social structure its defining object. Its ethnographies remain primary sources for the histories of the societies they describe, and they are still read, taught, and argued with.

The debates that brought the school down are also its legacy. The diaries controversy damaged the image of the detached, scientific ethnographer and helped launch the reflexive turn in anthropology. The charge of gender bias is equally live: the early functionalists studied men's politics and men's rituals, and women's lives were marginal in their monographs, a gap later feminist anthropology set out to fill. The charge of ahistoricism — that functionalism ignored colonialism, capitalism, and change — was the most damaging of all, and it is the reason the school was displaced by the historically minded anthropology of the 1970s and 1980s. What survives is the school's central question: how do the parts of a social world hold together, and what happens when they do not?
