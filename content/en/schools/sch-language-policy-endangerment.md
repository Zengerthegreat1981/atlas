# Language Policy & Endangerment

An applied branch of linguistics that studies the institutional and political decisions that determine the status of languages (official, educational, or media status), the accelerating phenomenon of language extinction, and efforts to revitalize them.

## The Language Extinction Crisis

The linguist Michael Krauss issued an influential warning in 1992: of roughly 6,000–7,000 living languages in the world, by the end of the twenty-first century perhaps only a small share may remain fully active, while the vast majority go extinct or stop being transmitted to new generations. Krauss compared this rate of loss with the extinction of biological species, and argued that it was proportionally far greater.

## Language Policies

This school examines how state decisions (the language of formal education, constitutional recognition of languages, the language of administration) shape the fate of languages: are minority languages supported, or marginalized in favor of a single national language? These policies vary between multilingual official models and monolingual models.

## Fishman's Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale

Joshua Fishman offered an influential diagnostic tool known as the "Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale" (GIDS), which classifies the vitality of a language across eight stages, ranging from the most endangered (the language is spoken only by scattered, socially isolated elderly individuals) to the safest (the language is used officially in government and the media at the national level). The scale provides a practical framework for assessing how threatened a given language is and for determining the intervention best suited to its situation (what suits a language at an advanced stage of extinction differs radically from what suits a language still being passed to children at home but institutionally threatened).

## Language Documentation vs. Language Description

Nikolaus Himmelmann distinguished between two complementary but methodologically distinct tasks: "Language Documentation," which is the compilation of a comprehensive and diverse record of actual language material in use (audio and video recordings of natural conversation, stories, rituals, and everyday talk), preserved in permanent digital formats that can be consulted in the future regardless of any particular theoretical analysis; and "Language Description," which is the theoretical analysis of this material (writing grammars and dictionaries). This distinction brought about an important methodological shift: documentation became a scientific goal in its own right rather than merely a preparatory step for description, especially when a language faces imminent extinction, making the preservation of raw material an urgent priority before it is too late.

## Models of Language Revitalization

The language revitalization movement has developed several practical approaches. The "Language Nest" is a model devised in New Zealand (Kōhanga Reo) for teaching the Māori language to infants and preschool children through full daily immersion, accompanied by elderly native speakers; the model was later adopted by Hawaiian language revitalization programs with notable success, after the language had nearly ceased to be learned by children by the 1980s. The "Master-Apprentice Method," developed by Leanne Hinton in California, requires an adult learner to spend many hours each week in direct, one-on-one language immersion with one of the last remaining native speakers, without recourse to English, with the aim of transmitting the language before those last speakers pass away.

## The Case of Hebrew Revival: A Rare Exception

Modern Hebrew is widely cited as the most striking historically documented case of a language that had completely ceased to be a daily spoken mother tongue (Hebrew remained for centuries a language of liturgy and religious writing only) and became once again a living mother tongue for millions of speakers. This was achieved through the efforts of Eliezer Ben-Yehuda and the Hebrew Language Committee (founded 1890) in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, combined with a large-scale national-settlement project that provided the exceptional social and political conditions necessary for this rare revival.

## Linguistic Landscape and the Measurement of Public Vitality

Rodrigue Landry and Richard Bourhis developed the concept of the "Linguistic Landscape": the study of the languages displayed on public signs, advertisements, street names, and shop fronts, as an indicator of the relative symbolic vitality of multiple languages in a shared public space. This approach was later taken up in studies such as those of Elana Shohamy, who treats public signage as a mechanism of de facto language policy, visible in the public space rather than only in official laws.

## Field Documentation and Research Ethics

This branch is closely linked to the methodology of field recording and language description. Linguists such as Ken Hale, Jost Gippert, Nikolaus Himmelmann and Ulrike Mosel contributed to developing methodological and ethical standards for this field work, including the necessity of direct collaboration with communities speaking the endangered language, respect for their intellectual property over their linguistic materials, and their participation in designing revitalization programs rather than imposing external academic solutions upon them.
