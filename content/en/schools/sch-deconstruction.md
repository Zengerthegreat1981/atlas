# Deconstruction (Déconstruction)

A philosophical and critical approach associated with **Jacques Derrida** (1930-2004), which examines the oppositions that organise philosophical and literary texts and shows how the marginalised term in each opposition is needed by the text that subordinates it. Deconstruction is not a neutral method applied from outside but a way of reading closely, attending to the points where a text's own argument unsettles its claims. Derrida himself resisted treating it as a method or a school.

## Intellectual context

Deconstruction grew out of several legacies:
1. **Phenomenology** (Husserl): Derrida wrote on Husserl from the start (his introduction to Husserl's *Origin of Geometry*, 1962; *La voix et le phénomène*, 1967).
2. **Structuralism** (Saussure, Lévi-Strauss): language as a system of differences. Derrida's lecture "Structure, Sign and Play in the Discourse of the Human Sciences" (1966) is often taken as a turning point toward post-structuralism, and *De la grammatologie* (1967) reread Saussure and Rousseau.
3. **Heidegger and Levinas**: Heidegger's "destruction" of the history of ontology and Levinas's ethical critique of ontology were both important to Derrida's work.

Derrida argued that Western philosophy has operated through hierarchical oppositions, which he called "logocentrism" and the "metaphysics of presence":
- presence / absence
- speech / writing
- soul / body
- nature / culture
- intelligible / sensible

In each pair one term is treated as original and the other as derivative or supplementary. Deconstruction asks how the text depends on the subordinated term and whether the hierarchy can be reversed and then displaced.

## Founding concepts

- **Différance**: a coined term combining the senses of "to differ" and "to defer" (French *différer*), spelled so that the difference is audible only in writing. It names the play of differences and deferrals through which meaning is produced, with no fully present origin of meaning.
- **Writing and the trace**: in *De la grammatologie* Derrida argues that the features traditionally ascribed to writing (absence of the speaker, repeatability) belong to language in general, so that speech is not more originary than writing.
- **The supplement**: in his reading of Rousseau, what is presented as an addition (writing, masturbation) turns out to be necessary to what it supposedly supplements.
- **Decentering**: in "Structure, Sign and Play" Derrida argues that the "centre" that grounds a structure is both inside and outside it, and so cannot fully ground it.
- **The *pharmakon***: in "Plato's Pharmacy" (1968, collected in *La dissémination*, 1972) Derrida reads the *Phaedrus*, where writing is called a *pharmakon* (remedy and poison) for memory; the term's double sense resists the clean opposition Plato wants.
- **Iterability**: a mark must be repeatable in new contexts, so context can never be completely saturated or controlled.

## Phases of Derrida's work

- **1960s**: *La voix et le phénomène*, *De la grammatologie* and *L'écriture et la différence* (all 1967).
- **1970s**: *La dissémination* and *Marges de la philosophie* (1972), *Glas* (1974).
- **1980s**: work on Heidegger (*De l'esprit*, 1987), on translation, and on institutions.
- **1990s and after**: more explicit attention to ethics, law and politics: "Force of Law" (a 1989 lecture, published 1990), *Spectres de Marx* (1993), *Politiques de l'amitié* (1994), and later the essays on mourning gathered as *The Work of Mourning* (2001).

## Reactions and debates

- **Derrida-Searle**: Derrida's "Signature Event Context" (a 1971 lecture, published 1972) discussed J. L. Austin's theory of speech acts (*How to Do Things with Words*, 1962); John Searle replied in "Reiterating the Differences" (1977), and Derrida answered in "Limited Inc" (1977). The exchange became emblematic of the gap between continental and analytic styles of philosophy.
- **The Yale critics**: Paul de Man (*Allegories of Reading*, 1979), J. Hillis Miller, Geoffrey Hartman and, more loosely, Harold Bloom contributed to *Deconstruction and Criticism* (1979) and made deconstruction a major force in American literary studies. Bloom's own work, such as *The Anxiety of Influence* (1973), is distinct from Derrida's.
- **Critical theory**: unlike the Frankfurt School's critique of ideology, which exposes domination from the standpoint of emancipatory reason, deconstruction works within texts and questions the very stability of such standpoints. Habermas criticised this in *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985), arguing that Derrida levels the distinction between philosophy and literature.

## Applications

- **Literary criticism**: reading texts for the tensions between what they state and what their rhetoric does.
- **Political philosophy**: *Spectres de Marx* (1993) reflects on Marx's legacy, on justice and on "hauntology" after the Cold War.
- **Law**: in "Force of Law" Derrida argues that law rests on a founding act of force that is not itself legal, and distinguishes law from an unconditional idea of justice that is never fully present.
- **Theology and religion**: John Caputo, *The Prayers and Tears of Jacques Derrida* (1997), reads Derrida's later work in relation to religion.
- **Architecture**: Derrida's essay on Bernard Tschumi's Parc de la Villette ("Point de folie - maintenant l'architecture," 1986) and his collaboration with Peter Eisenman on the Parc de la Villette project (1985-1986).

## Limits and criticism

- **Relativism**: critics charge that if texts are unstable, there is no criterion for distinguishing better from worse readings; Derrida denied that deconstruction implies that "anything goes," insisting on careful, rule-governed reading.
- **Political and ethical weakness**: critics such as Habermas argue that deconstruction offers critique without constructive normative resources; defenders point to Derrida's later work on justice, hospitality and democracy.
- **Academicism**: Derrida's difficult style, and the institutional spread of "deconstruction" in American universities, drew charges of obscurantism and of a self-referential culture.
