# Comparative Study of Digital Religion and Virtual Worship

A sub-field of comparative religion and media studies that examines how beliefs, rituals, communities and structures of religious authority are reshaped in digital environments, and how the line between the "in-person" and the "virtual" blurs in everyday religious practice. The field emerged in the mid-1990s under the name "cyber-religion" with the first wave of groups that moved worship onto the internet, and the term "Digital Religion" crystallized in the 2000s and 2010s with the spread of Web 2.0 and social networks.

## Beginnings: From Sacred Space to Networked Religion

Stephen O'Leary wrote in 1996, in the *Journal of the American Academy of Religion*, about "cyberspace as sacred space" and documented the first rituals conducted over networks. Jeffrey Hadden and Douglas Cowan then compiled *Religion on the Internet: Research Prospects and Promises* (JAI Press, 2000). In his chapter in that volume, Christopher Helland distinguished between "Religion Online", meaning the transfer of an existing practice to the internet, and "Online Religion", meaning new practices that arise within the digital space and have no existence outside it. This distinction became a key concept of the field, although later researchers, including Heidi Campbell and Stig Lövheim, argued that the separation between the "in-person" and the "digital" has eroded as the two merged into a single pattern of religiosity (*Information, Communication & Society*, 2011).

## Theoretical Framework: The Religious-Social Shaping of Technology

In *When Religion Meets New Media* (Routledge, 2010), Campbell set out the model of the "Religious-Social Shaping of Technology", and examined how Jewish, Christian and Muslim groups negotiated their use of the internet according to their values and structures of authority. The move into the digital is analyzed across the dimensions of identity, community, authority, ritual and sacred space. Her survey in *New Media & Society* (2017) maps the main theoretical approaches that the field has developed. Later work stresses that many believers combine in-person and digital practices in a single religious life rather than treating them as separate spheres.

## Islam on the Network: Fatwa and Authority

Islam has a particular presence in these studies. Gary Bunt documented, in *Virtually Islamic: Computer-Mediated Communication and Cyber Islamic Environments* (University of Wales Press, 2000), then *iMuslims: Rewiring the House of Islam* (University of North Carolina Press, 2009) and *Hashtag Islam* (2018), how websites, forums and live fatwa platforms produced a new space in which the authority of the institutional mufti competes with preachers and specialists and with groups that have no official religious authority, so that the exclusivity of traditional authority fragments. It was the case of "IslamOnline", associated with Yusuf al-Qaradawi, that gave this development a prominent example. During the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, Friday and congregational prayers were suspended in many countries by decisions of scholarly councils, and mosques broadcast sermons over platforms. The fatwas raised questions about whether prayer over a screen is valid and whether physical gathering is a requirement. In other traditions, churches broadcast Masses, and debate arose over "virtual communion", while the practice of "darshan" at a distance spread among Hindus.

## Current Topics

- Mourning and memory: study of digital rituals associated with death (memorial pages, archives of the deceased on social networks).
- The metaverse and immersive worship: emerging research that examines the effect of virtual reality environments on religious practice and psychological well-being.
- Chatbots and spiritual guidance, which are the subject of a separate field, the study of religion and artificial intelligence.

## Limits of the Field and Its Critique

The field relies largely on Western case studies and on Protestant Christianity, and its coverage of Islamic, Hindu and Buddhist traditions remains less mature. The speed of technology means that peer-reviewed literature lags behind practice, so many of its conclusions are preliminary. The field is also criticized for assuming that "religion" can be separated from "technology", whereas the role of media such as print and radio in shaping religion is long-standing, as Stig Hjarvard's concept of "mediatization" (*Northern Lights*, 2008) emphasizes. Finally, data on practice on private platforms is limited by privacy and by changes in algorithms.
