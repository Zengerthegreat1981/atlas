# Urban Anthropology

Urban anthropology is the study of cities as human worlds: of how people live together in dense, diverse, and unequal settlements, and of what city life does to kinship, identity, livelihood, and belief. It began with a problem. Anthropology was built on the study of small, face-to-face communities — villages, bands, neighborhoods of kin — and for a long time its practitioners assumed that the city, with its crowds and strangers, belonged to sociology. Urban anthropology's achievement was to show that the ethnographer's methods work in cities too, and that cities are not the opposite of the communities anthropologists had studied, but their transformation: places where the same questions about belonging, exchange, and power are asked at a different scale and under different pressures.

## History and Emergence

Urban anthropology's roots lie in the Chicago School of sociology. Robert Park's essay "The City" (1915; revised in the 1925 volume *The City*) treated the city as a social laboratory, a mosaic of "natural areas" — the slum, the ghetto, the bohemia — each with its own moral order. Louis Wirth's "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938) defined the city by size, density, and heterogeneity, and argued that these conditions produced impersonal, segmented, and superficial social relations. Anthropologists were skeptical of that picture, and later urban ethnographies put it to the test: they found that city dwellers did not live as isolated atoms but built dense networks of kin, neighbors, and fellow migrants.

The subfield took institutional shape in the 1960s and 1970s. Oscar Lewis's studies of poor families in Mexico City and San Juan — *Five Families* (1959) and *La Vida* (1966) — introduced the controversial idea of a "culture of poverty," arguing that the poor developed adaptive but self-perpetuating ways of life. Ulf Hannerz's *Soulside* (1969), an ethnography of a Black neighborhood in Washington, D.C., showed how ghetto life was organized by its own cultural logic rather than by pathology. In Africa, the Manchester school's studies of the Copperbelt — the mining towns of Zambia — showed how migrants moved between village and town, and how urban life created new forms of association, from trade unions to burial societies. Hannerz's *Exploring the City* (1980) then gave the field an influential synthesis, gathering the Chicago tradition, the African urban studies, and network analysis into a single framework.

## Key Figures

Robert Park (1864–1944) and Louis Wirth (1897–1952) supplied the sociological foundations, even though neither was an anthropologist. Oscar Lewis (1914–1970) brought ethnographic depth to the study of urban poverty and provoked the field's fiercest debate. Ulf Hannerz (born 1942), a Swedish anthropologist, became one of the field's main synthesizers and a leading theorist of networks, cultural complexity, and globalization. In Britain, the Manchester school — above all J. Clyde Mitchell (1918–1995) and A. L. Epstein (1924–1999) — pioneered the study of African towns and of social networks as a method. Setha Low (born 1948) reshaped the field in the 1990s by bringing space itself into focus, studying plazas, gated communities, and the politics of public space. James Holston showed, in his work on Brasilia, how the built form of a city embodies political utopias and produces new forms of citizenship.

## Key Works

The canon begins with Park's "The City" (1925) and Wirth's "Urbanism as a Way of Life" (1938), the two texts every student still reads. Lewis's *La Vida* (1966) remains the most famous — and most contested — urban ethnography. Hannerz's *Soulside* (1969) and *Exploring the City* (1980) define the field's mature form, combining network theory with ethnographic portraiture. Setha Low's *Theorizing the City: The New Urban Anthropology Reader* (1999) collected the field's spatial turn. Holston's *The Modernist City: An Anthropological Critique of Brasilia* (1989) showed how a city designed as a utopia produced unexpected forms of social life. Together these works trace the field's arc: from the question of whether cities could be studied ethnographically at all, to the study of cities as the central setting of contemporary human life.

## Method

Urban anthropologists use the classic tools of ethnography — participant observation, interviews, life histories — adapted to a setting where the "community" is not given in advance. The key methodological innovation is network analysis: rather than studying a bounded group, the ethnographer traces the ties that connect people — kin, neighbors, coworkers, fellow migrants — and maps how resources, information, and support flow through them. Multi-sited ethnography, which follows people between village and city or between neighborhoods, is also widely used. Contemporary work adds spatial methods: mapping, the study of the built environment, and the analysis of how plazas, markets, housing estates, and gated communities shape social life. The method's strength is its ability to connect the intimate scale of everyday life — a street corner, a market stall, a bus ride — to the large structures of the city: labor markets, housing policy, and the state.

## Influence and Debates

Urban anthropology's influence is now hard to overstate, because the city has become the default human habitat: more than half the world's population lives in urban areas, and the field's questions have become anthropology's questions. Its methods — network analysis, multi-sited ethnography, the ethnography of space — have spread across the discipline. Its findings have shaped debates about poverty, migration, informality, and the politics of public space. The "culture of poverty" debate, meanwhile, left a permanent caution: Lewis's idea was taken up by policymakers to blame the poor for their poverty, a use critics argued ran counter to Lewis's own emphasis on structural causes, and the field has been careful ever since about how its concepts travel into politics. The frontiers today are the megacity and the digital city: life in settlements of twenty million people, and how phones, platforms, and surveillance reshape urban sociality.

On the first, AbdouMaliq Simone's *City Life from Jakarta to Dakar* (2010) examines how residents of fast-growing cities in the Global South improvise livelihoods and collective life amid infrastructural precarity. On the second, Sarah Pink and colleagues' *Digital Ethnography: Principles and Practice* (2016) and the wider digital anthropology literature ask how phones and platforms are woven into urban routines, so that digital and physical urban space are studied together rather than apart.
