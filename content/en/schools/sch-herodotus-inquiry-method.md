# Herodotus and the Method of Inquiry

**The Father of History** (Herodotus, c. 484–420 BCE), who named his work *Histories* (Historiai), represents the first known systematic attempt not only to collect historical accounts, but to **actively investigate and verify reports through direct questioning and personal observation**.

## Inquiry as a Method

Herodotus did not merely transmit the reports he had received. He **travelled himself and questioned local witnesses and informants** (Egyptian priests, a Persian governor, craftsmen), recorded their accounts, and added his own observations on geography and customs. This commitment to **living verification** (though not always successful) distinguished him from earlier traditions that relied on purely oral transmission without organized critical examination.

## The Origin of the Word: histor and the Knowing Witness

The word *Historiai*, which Herodotus chose as the title of his work, derives from the Greek term *histōr*, which in ancient Greek legal usage originally meant **"the knowing witness" or "the expert judge"**: someone with direct knowledge of an event who is summoned to testify or to decide on it based on what he saw or verified, not on hearsay or guesswork. When Herodotus chose to call his project *historia* (meaning "inquiry" or "investigation"), he did not mean "narrating the past" in the sense the word later acquired. He meant **the act of questioning and verification itself**: going to witnesses, interrogating them, comparing their accounts, and then deciding what deserves recording. This etymological origin is what makes Herodotus, although he did not invent the word from nothing, the figure who established the **professional meaning** of "history" in the languages that inherited the Greek root (history, histoire, historia). The word itself, in its linguistic essence, carries the memory of this first investigative act before it became the name of a field of knowledge.

## Comparing Conflicting Accounts: The Example of the Egyptian Priests

One of the clearest examples of Herodotus' method of weighing competing testimonies is what he reports in Book Two of the *Histories* about Egypt. When he asks Egyptian priests about the antiquity of their civilization and the origins of certain religious practices, he finds that their accounts contradict what the Greeks were accustomed to believing about their gods and the origins of their rites. He records the Egyptian account explicitly, sometimes preferring it to the common Greek account, and declares that the priests of Egypt are "more careful in preserving their records," because they possess writing and priestly archives older than any Greek oral source. In other places he presents two conflicting accounts side by side without deciding between them, leaving the reader to weigh them, and declaring his famous formula that his duty is to "report what is said," without being obliged to believe everything he reports. This technique, placing competing accounts side by side with explicit evaluation at times and implicit commentary at others, makes Herodotus the first to practice a primitive form of **comparative source criticism**, although this criticism was less rigorous than the standard Thucydides later established.

## Ethnographic Digressions: Egypt, Scythia and Persia

Herodotus devotes large parts of his work to digressions that do not narrate political or military events as much as they describe **the customs, social, religious and geographical systems of peoples**. Book Two is devoted entirely to Egypt (the flooding of the Nile, mummification, the customs of priests, the building of the pyramids). Book Four provides an extended description of Scythia (nomadic life, burial rituals, methods of warfare), and parts of Books One and Three describe Persian systems of government and religion. These ethnographic digressions, which seemed to later readers a deviation from "true history" (meaning political and military narrative), are regarded by contemporary scholars as an **early form of descriptive anthropology**: an attempt to understand the "other" (the barbarian, the non-Greek) on his own terms rather than by the standards of Greek centrality alone. This understanding, however, remained in tension with a Greek-centred outlook that Herodotus never fully abandoned. François Hartog, in *The Mirror of Herodotus*, made these Scythian digressions the main subject of his study, showing how Herodotus builds the image of the "other" through rhetorical mechanisms of contrast and inversion.

## The Reception Dispute: Father of History or Father of Lies?

Herodotus' reception was not uniform across the ages. Cicero, in *De Legibus*, gave him the title **"father of history"** (pater historiae), in recognition of his pioneering role in establishing systematic prose historical writing. But this praise was not unanimous even in antiquity. The historian and essayist **Plutarch**, in his satirical treatise *On the Malice of Herodotus* (De Malignitate Herodoti), accused him of bias against certain Greek cities (especially Thebes and Corinth) and of preferring reports that were offensive to the Greeks at the expense of the truth, describing him as closer to "father of lies" than to father of history. This tension between the two titles, "father of history" and "father of lies," continued into modern historiography. Nineteenth-century positivist philology (led by Heinrich Stein and later Detlev Fehling) tended to classify Herodotus as an unreliable historian compared with the "scientific" Thucydides. Late twentieth-century studies (Luciano Canfora, John Gould, and later François Hartog and Rosalind Thomas) restored his reputation, highlighting that the modern criteria of "accuracy" do not apply directly to a text that emerged at the crossroads of oral epic and the nascent prose inquiry.

## Limits of the Method

Despite this effort, Herodotus accepted accounts of **miracles and divine acts** without open scepticism (the winged serpents, dreams that come true), which provoked the later criticism of **Thucydides**. The modern debate has not ended: nineteenth-century positivist scholarship regarded him as an untrustworthy source; contemporary readings have re-evaluated him, finding in his selection of accounts and his handling of conflicting sources a more refined logic of history.

## Long-Term Influence

Herodotus established the idea that **history is a writing that investigates and verifies**, rather than a passive narration, although his tools of verification remained primitive. The very name he gave his work, *Histories*, became the model for later historical writing.
