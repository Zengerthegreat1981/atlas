# Care Ethics & Feminism

## Overview

Care ethics is a feminist school of ethics that emerged in the early 1980s. Its central premise is that human relationships grounded in mutual dependence, care, and concrete attention to particular others, rather than abstract rules or the calculation of benefits, are the original source of moral action. The school presents itself as a critical feminist alternative to the three major traditional ethical theories (Kantian ethics, utilitarianism, and virtue ethics). It charges all three with ignoring the historical experience of female caregiving, and with adopting a conception of the moral agent as an independent, rational individual abstracted from relationships and emotions. Critics argue that this conception reflects male experience more than general human experience.

## Historical Context: From Kohlberg to Gilligan

The first spark came from a psychological rather than purely philosophical critique. The developmental psychologist Lawrence Kohlberg set out a model of stages of moral maturity based on empirical studies. His highest stage is one of universal ethical principles of justice, and studies using his scheme, often on male samples, sometimes found women scoring at a "lower" stage oriented to interpersonal relationships. The decisive response came from his Harvard colleague Carol Gilligan in her landmark book *In a Different Voice* (1982). She argued that what Kohlberg regarded as a moral "deficiency" is in fact a "different moral voice": not a lower rank, but a qualitatively different one. While many men reason in the logic of an "ethics of justice" (rights, rules, abstraction), many women, she reported, reason in the logic of an "ethics of care" (relationships, contexts, and concrete responsibility toward particular people). Gilligan stressed that the two voices are not defined by sex, and her aim was less a claim about women's nature than a critique of bias in a scale built largely on male samples. Her empirical claims have themselves been contested.

The American philosopher Nel Noddings developed this framework philosophically in *Caring: A Feminine Approach to Ethics and Moral Education* (1984). She offered an early and influential systematic philosophical formulation of care ethics as a normative theory, rather than a mere psychological observation. She distinguished between the "one-caring" and the "cared-for," and argued that moral action springs from natural caring and from the ethical ideal built on it, rather than from an abstract commitment to a universal rule.

## Key Thinkers and Distinct Positions

In *The Ethics of Care: Personal, Political, and Global* (2006), the philosopher Virginia Held offered a more mature formulation that is less specifically tied to women biologically. She treated care as a general human practice, carried out by parents, nurses, and teachers of both sexes, and as a political value on which the public sphere should be reshaped, rather than confining it to the private, domestic sphere alone.

The philosopher Eva Feder Kittay linked care ethics to the philosophy of disability in *Love's Labor* (1999), drawing on her personal experience of caring for her daughter, who has a severe disability. She argued that liberal theories of justice, such as Rawls's theory, mistakenly assume that the contracting parties are equal in capacity and autonomy. They overlook that every human being inevitably passes through phases of complete dependence (childhood, illness, old age), and that dependency, not independence, is the authentic human condition.

On the political side, the philosopher Joan Tronto linked care to a critique of the gendered, racial, and class-based division of labor in *Moral Boundaries* (1993). She showed that care work (nursing, cleaning, child-rearing) is socially assigned to women, ethnic minorities, and immigrant women, at low wages and low social status, even though it is essential moral work without which no society can survive. Later writers have spoken of a wider structural crisis of care.

## Objections and Replies

The first objection is "excessive particularity." If care is limited to those we know personally and genuinely care about, how can it ground moral obligations toward strangers or the victims of famine in distant countries? Does care ethics not lead to moral partiality that contradicts the principle of moral impartiality? Held and others reply that the circle of care can expand gradually (from the family to the local community to humanity as a whole) through institutional mechanisms and public policies built on the value of care. This does not mean that the obligation toward a stranger is equal to the obligation toward a child.

The second objection is the "paternalistic or exploitative danger." Does care ethics risk confining women once again to the role of the "natural carer," and justifying the historical exploitation of their unpaid labor, rather than liberating them? Tronto and other care theorists reply that the solution is not to reject the value of care, but to redistribute it socially (between the sexes, and between the state and the family) and to recognize it economically and politically, rather than to deny its central importance in human life.

The third objection comes from within moral philosophy itself: is "care" merely an emotion rather than a normative theory capable of resolving complex moral disputes? Defenders reply that caring involves attentive judgment about the other's actual needs (not merely a passing feeling), and that care ethics therefore offers criteria for distinguishing good care from domineering or neglectful care.

## Applied Example: Nursing Ethics and End-of-Life Decisions

Care ethics is especially visible in nursing at the end of life. Where Kantian ethics asks, "What is the abstract duty here (respecting the patient's autonomy)?", and utilitarianism asks, "Which decision maximizes overall benefit?", the nurse who adopts an ethics of care asks a different question: "What does this particular patient need, in this specific relationship, and how should his or her wishes be balanced with those of the family connected to him or her?" She listens to the details of the patient's life story and relationships, rather than applying a general protocol, and treats the anxiety and grief of the family as part of the moral situation, not as an obstacle to a rational, abstract decision. Care ethics has been influential in nursing and medical ethics, though some argue that it needs supplementing with principles of justice.

## Impact and Application

Care ethics has influenced several fields. In the philosophy of education, through Noddings, it called for curricula that value the educational relationship over rote transmission. It has also shaped social policy on parental leave and elder care, and international relations, where Fiona Robinson, in *Globalizing Care* (1999), and others proposed a care-based approach to global ethics as an alternative to interest-based realism. It has also formed common ground with virtue ethics, the philosophy of disability, and intersectional feminist theories in critiquing the individualism of liberal thought that dominates Western moral philosophy.

## Further Feminist Strands

Sara Ruddick's article Maternal Thinking (1980) and her book of the same title (1989) argued that the work of raising children, with its aims of preserving life, fostering growth and shaping social acceptability, generates a distinctive discipline of thought that men too can practise and that can inform a politics of peace. Annette Baier, in articles collected in *Moral Prejudices* (1994), made trust a central moral concept and asked how an ethics of obligation depends on relations of love and trust that it does not itself explain. Michael Slote's *The Ethics of Care and Empathy* (2007) sought to build a comprehensive theory in which empathy grounds both personal morality and social justice.

Feminist ethics is broader than care ethics. Alison Jaggar and Susan Sherwin, among others, stress the analysis of oppression and power; Sherwin's *No Longer Patient* (1992) applies this to health care. Uma Narayan's essay Colonialism and Its Others (1995) warned that talk of care and dependence has also served colonial rule, which presented domination as responsibility for people deemed unable to look after themselves.
