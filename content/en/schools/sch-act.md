# Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT, pronounced as one word, "act") is a therapeutic model within the third wave of cognitive-behavioral therapies that focuses on cultivating psychological flexibility through six core processes, represented by the Hexaflex model. ACT revolves around a central idea: human suffering stems primarily from experiential avoidance and the attempt to control thoughts and feelings, not from their mere presence.

## Intellectual and Historical Context

ACT was founded in Steven Hayes's laboratory at the University of Nevada, Reno, in the early 1980s (1981-1982). An early form of the protocol was called "comprehensive distancing" (Zettle and Hayes, 1986), and the name "Acceptance and Commitment Therapy" became established later, notably with the 1999 book by Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson. Its origins were influenced by the philosophical current known as Functional Contextualism and by Relational Frame Theory (RFT), which Hayes developed during the same period.

ACT came as a reaction to the limits of classical CBT in dealing with complex cases, especially those in which experiential avoidance recurs despite apparent success. Instead of trying to change the content of thoughts (as cognitive restructuring does), ACT proposes changing the relationship between the person and their thoughts, while committing to values-guided actions even in the presence of discomfort.

## The Founders and the First Generation

The principal founder is **Steven C. Hayes**, professor of psychology at the University of Nevada, Reno. Among the main early contributors are **Robert D. Zettle**, who co-authored the early comprehensive distancing work, and **Kirk D. Strosahl** and **Kelly G. Wilson**, who co-authored the 1999 foundational book with Hayes.

Today ACT is a broad research and clinical movement that goes far beyond its first founders. It is represented by an official body, the **Association for Contextual Behavioral Science** (ACBS).

## The Core Theoretical Frameworks

ACT rests on three theoretical pillars:

- **Functional Contextualism**: a scientific philosophy that studies behavior in its context and cares about what behavior does (its function) more than its outward form.
- **Relational Frame Theory (RFT)**: a psychological theory that explains how humans create meaning through symbolic relations among objects and thoughts. It is the cognitive foundation of ACT, and it explains why fusion with thoughts becomes a source of suffering.
- **The Psychological Flexibility Model**: the supreme therapeutic goal, composed of six core processes represented by the Hexaflex: acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment awareness, self-as-context, values, and committed action.

## The Hexaflex

The Hexaflex condenses the six processes of ACT into a visual geometric shape:

1. **Acceptance**: allowing feelings and thoughts to exist without trying to change or suppress them.
2. **Cognitive Defusion**: seeing thoughts as passing mental events, not as truths that demand belief or action.
3. **Present Moment Awareness**: mental presence in the current moment rather than being preoccupied with the past or worrying about the future.
4. **Self-as-Context**: seeing the self as a stable observer of thoughts and feelings, not as their content. "I am not my thoughts; I am the one who notices them."
5. **Values**: clarifying the fundamental directions in life (family, creativity, growth...) as a reference for making decisions.
6. **Committed Action**: translating values into actual behaviors, even in the presence of discomfort or fear.

## The Main Technique Categories

ACT techniques are arranged in six major categories that roughly correspond to the six processes in the Hexaflex:

1. **Cognitive Defusion**: Leaves on a Stream, naming thoughts, thanking your mind, Titchener's repetition.
2. **Acceptance**: willingness versus willfulness, expanding awareness, pain versus suffering, creative hopelessness.
3. **Present Moment Awareness**: grounding through the senses (5-4-3-2-1), mindful breathing, riding the wave of an urge.
4. **Self-as-Context**: the sky and weather metaphor, noticing "who is noticing," the chess board metaphor.
5. **Values**: the values clarification worksheet, the life compass, the Bullseye exercise, distinguishing values from goals.
6. **Committed Action**: taking the first step, the small-steps method, the ACT Matrix, if-then planning.

## Clinical Applications and Evidence

ACT has a substantial and growing evidence base, including a large number of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses. Applications that have been studied include depression, anxiety disorders, eating disorders, chronic pain, substance use, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and adjustment to chronic physical illness; the strength of evidence varies by condition and comparison. ACT has also been applied outside clinical settings, for example in sport, workplace, and educational contexts.

## Relationship to Neighboring Schools

ACT intersects with several schools:

- **Classical CBT**: ACT belongs historically and theoretically to the cognitive-behavioral family, but it explicitly criticizes cognitive restructuring as a core tool. The relationship is one of kinship and separation at once.
- **DBT (Dialectical Behavior Therapy)**: both belong to the third wave and share the use of mindfulness, but ACT differs in lacking the dialectical dimension that distinguishes DBT.
- **Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)**: overlaps in the use of mindfulness, but ACT is broader in integrating values and committed action.
- **Existential philosophy**: shares an interest in meaning and values, with a fundamental difference: ACT builds itself on the philosophy of functional contextualism, not on the existential tradition.

## Criticism and Limits

- **Theoretical complexity**: RFT is hard for new clinical practitioners to grasp, which makes it difficult to distinguish ACT from classical CBT in practice.
- **The problem of "acceptance"**: criticisms that "acceptance" may be misread as resignation or passivity, to which Hayes responded by distinguishing acceptance from giving up.
- **Cross-cultural application**: it was developed in an individualistic American context, with questions about how far it applies in collectivist contexts (the Far East, Africa, the Arab world).
- **Comparison with CBT**: trials have generally not shown ACT to be consistently superior to CBT, and critics note that some of the early evidence was of mixed methodological quality.

## Legacy and Influence

Hayes and his colleagues established the **Association for Contextual Behavioral Science** (ACBS) in 2005 as an academic home for ACT and RFT. ACT's influence has gone beyond psychotherapy to education, leadership, and sport. The six processes of the Hexaflex have become among the most widely used visual therapeutic models in contemporary clinical training, alongside the DBT skills model.

## Internal Controversies within ACT

- **"Full" ACT vs. "simplified" ACT**: a dispute between those who hold to the full Hexaflex model and those who accept simplified formulations (such as ACT in skills training only).
- **RFT as a mandatory theoretical framework**: a dispute between those who regard RFT as an indispensable foundation for ACT and those who see that clinical practice can be independent of RFT.
- **Emphasis on acceptance vs. change**: within ACT, a debate between those who emphasize the processes of acceptance and defusion and those who emphasize values and committed action.
- **Separation from Buddhism**: a dispute over how closely ACT is tied to Buddhist traditions. Hayes has emphasized that ACT is grounded in behavioral science and functional contextualism, but many practitioners draw on Buddhist concepts.

## Applications with Special Populations

- **Children and adolescents**: ACT for Children, with adjustments in language and examples.
- **Chronic pain**: one of ACT's strongest applications.
- **Substance addiction**: ACT in addiction treatment.
- **Physical health**: ACT in cancer, diabetes, obesity.
- **Athletic performance**: ACT in sport psychology.
- **Couples**: ACT-informed work with couples (a more recent application).

## Cultural and International Reference Points

ACT has been carried into most languages and cultures, with notable adaptations:

- **Transfer into Arabic**: translating "acceptance" and "commitment" requires a cultural context that distinguishes accepting from being content with a situation, and committed action from coercion.
- **Europe and Latin America**: active research communities and training programs, with many translations of the core texts.
- **East Asia**: adaptations that consider collectivist cultural values.
- **The Arab world**: interest is growing, though cultural adaptation is still at an early stage.
