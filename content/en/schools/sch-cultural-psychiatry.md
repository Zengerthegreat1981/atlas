# Cultural (Transcultural) Psychiatry

An interdisciplinary field between psychiatry and medical anthropology that studies how culture shapes the experience of mental illness, its expression and interpretation, and the therapeutic response to it. The field challenges the assumption that Western psychiatric diagnostic categories (as in the DSM and ICD) are universal and valid without modification across all cultural contexts.

## Origins and Context

The roots of the field go back to colonial ethnographic psychiatry in the early twentieth century (Emil Kraepelin himself visited Java in 1904 and published a short paper on "comparative psychiatry" the same year). It crystallized as an organized field from the 1950s and 1960s, notably around the Montreal group associated with Eric Wittkower and Raymond Prince at McGill University and the journal *Transcultural Psychiatric Research Review* (founded in 1956 and later renamed *Transcultural Psychiatry*). The decisive shift came in the 1970s and 1980s with the work of Arthur Kleinman, who combined his medical training with an anthropological background to propose a coherent theoretical framework. It distinguishes between "disease," meaning a biological dysfunction, and "illness," meaning lived experience and cultural meaning, drawing on his fieldwork in Taiwan and China.

## Core Concepts

- **Explanatory Models**: every patient and practitioner holds a particular cultural explanatory model of the cause of illness, its course, and the appropriate treatment. The physician should elicit this model explicitly rather than assume it.
- **Social Suffering and Social Forms of Pain**: Kleinman, together with his wife Joan Kleinman, formulated an account of how social structures (poverty, political violence, migration) mediate the experience of psychological and physical suffering. This is most evident in his work on China during the Cultural Revolution.
- **Idioms of distress**: a term associated with Mark Nichter for local cultural expressions of psychological distress that do not map directly onto Western diagnostic categories. Examples discussed in this literature include "nervous weakness" (*shenjing shuairuo*) in China, and attribution of distress to jinn in some Arab and Islamic contexts. These must be understood in their own context rather than mechanically translated into a biomedical classification.
- **Critique of Cross-Cultural Diagnostic Reduction**: challenging the idea that conditions such as depression appear in the same form everywhere. In many cultures, psychological distress is expressed through physical symptoms (somatization) more than through direct emotional expression.

## Key Figures

- **Arthur Kleinman**: a leading theorist of the field, author of *Patients and Healers in the Context of Culture* (1980) and *The Illness Narratives* (1988).
- **Roland Littlewood**: a British psychiatrist and anthropologist who worked on race, migration, and mental health in Britain. With Maurice Lipsedge, he criticized ethnic biases in psychiatric diagnosis.
- **Byron Good**: together with Kleinman, developed the approach of "meaning-centered medical anthropology," and analyzed illness meanings in Iran, including "heart distress," as a test case for the meaning-centered approach.
- **The American Psychiatric Association's DSM work on culture**: DSM-IV (1994) introduced the Outline for Cultural Formulation and a glossary of culture-bound syndromes. DSM-5 (2013) replaced the glossary with "cultural concepts of distress" and added the Cultural Formulation Interview (CFI), a 16-question semi-structured interview with supplementary modules and an informant version, retained in DSM-5-TR (2022).

## Limitations

The field is criticized from two opposing directions. Biological psychiatry sees the focus on cultural diversity as a loosening of scientific diagnostic rigor and a threat to the cross-cultural comparability of clinical research. A more radical anthropological critique, influenced by Michel Foucault and Edward Said, holds that even the concept of "cultural formulation" remains a Western framework. On this view, it adds "culture" as a secondary explanatory layer on top of a biomedical model that stays the final reference, rather than questioning the Western ontological assumptions about mental illness from the ground up. The field also faces a practical difficulty in training clinicians in precise ethnographic tools within the limits of clinical time. It must also avoid simplistic cultural stereotyping, the assumption that every patient from a given cultural background shares a single uniform explanatory model. This contradicts the field's own principle of respecting individual variation within each culture.

## Presence in the Arab and Islamic World

Cultural psychiatry has acquired particular importance in the study of disorders linked to local explanatory frameworks common in Arab and Islamic societies. Examples include attributing psychological symptoms to spirit possession, jinn, or sorcery, and expressing depression and anxiety through physical complaints rather than direct emotional ones. Clinicians and researchers in the region discuss how to take these frameworks into account alongside standard diagnostic tools, and tools such as the Cultural Formulation Interview are one proposed means of doing so. This entry is educational and descriptive, not clinical advice.
