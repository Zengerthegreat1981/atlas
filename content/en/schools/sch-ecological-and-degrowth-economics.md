# Ecological and Degrowth Economics

**Ecological economics** is a subfield of economics that places economic activity back within the physical ecological limits of the planet, rejecting a common implicit assumption in standard economic models: that economic growth can continue indefinitely without a real physical constraint, so long as technology and innovation are available. Two things should be kept apart: ecological economics is a broad academic field studying the interaction of the economy with ecological systems, without an inherent commitment to rejecting growth, while degrowth is a narrower normative movement calling for a reduction in the scale of rich economies.

## Daly and the steady-state economy

Herman Daly, who worked as an economist at the World Bank before becoming one of its leading critics, presented in his book *Steady-State Economics* (1977) an explicit theoretical alternative to the idea of unlimited economic growth: the **steady-state economy**, which maintains a relatively constant stock of physical capital and population, so that the rate of resource consumption and waste generation stays within the capacity of ecological systems to regenerate and absorb, rather than pursuing ever-increasing accumulation. Daly built on the entropy-based work of Nicholas Georgescu-Roegen (*The Entropy Law and the Economic Process*, 1971) and on the second law of thermodynamics, asserting that the economy is a subsystem within a larger, physically bounded ecological system, not an isolated system that can expand without accumulating environmental costs.

## From stability to degrowth

A more radical movement later developed under the name "degrowth", particularly in France and southern Europe. Its intellectual lineage includes Georgescu-Roegen and, in France, Serge Latouche. It goes further than Daly's call for stability, demanding the organized and deliberate contraction of the size of wealthy economies specifically — not of the whole world equally. Its argument is that continuing to pursue aggregate growth, even in economies already materially saturated, aggravates the climate crisis without improving real well-being in proportion to its environmental cost. It draws partly on empirical evidence showing that the link between rising income and reported happiness slows after a certain income threshold.

## Raworth and the doughnut economy

Kate Raworth, in her book *Doughnut Economics* (2017), presented a visual and conceptual framework that tried to reconcile two demands that may appear to conflict: an inner social boundary (guaranteeing a minimum level of human well-being for everyone: food, education and health) and an outer environmental boundary (not exceeding the nine planetary boundaries identified by Earth system scientists, such as climate change and biodiversity). The ideal economic goal is then to remain within these "doughnut" bounds between the two limits, rather than maximizing aggregate growth without a ceiling.

## Ostrom and commons managed collectively

Elinor Ostrom made a pivotal contribution that intersects with ecological economics. She empirically refuted the pessimistic assumption of the "tragedy of the commons" model — that any shared resource not owned privately will inevitably be overexploited — by documenting many cases around the world in which local communities succeeded in managing shared resources (fisheries, forests, irrigation systems) under self-organized rules, without full privatization or centralized government nationalization. She received the Nobel Prize in Economics for this work in 2009.

## Criticism from mainstream economics

The school faces criticism from standard growth economists, who argue that decoupling economic growth from the material consumption of resources (so-called "relative decoupling") is technically possible through innovation and energy efficiency, and that the degrowth call ignores the urgent need for growth in poor and developing countries specifically, to raise basic living standards. This is a deep normative disagreement about whether environmental limits or social needs should take priority in lower-income countries.

## Regional echoes

Questions of ecological economics are appearing with growing force in debates in the Gulf states, specifically about economic diversification away from oil, and in Egypt and other Arab countries about water scarcity and food security, treated as explicit physical environmental limits that intersect with the debate over economic growth. However, this direct regional application lies outside the foundational theorizing of the field in its original literature.

## Earlier roots and intellectual accumulation

Ecological economics draws on an intellectual heritage older than Daly himself, including the warnings of the 1972 report *The Limits to Growth* by the Club of Rome. That report used early computer modelling (the World3 model) to project scenarios in which continued exponential economic and population growth would, without change, collide with physical limits (resource depletion, accumulated pollution) within the twenty-first century. The report provoked wide controversy on its publication over the accuracy of its modelling assumptions, but it established vocabulary and concepts that remained present in subsequent debate on the limits to growth.

## Later institutional founding

The International Society for Ecological Economics was founded in 1989, along with its peer-reviewed journal *Ecological Economics*, to provide an organized academic forum bringing together economists, environmental scientists and complex-systems researchers. It distinguishes this field from the conventional environmental economics that dominates, which treats the environment mainly as a source of "externalities" that can be corrected through pricing within the standard neoclassical framework without reconsidering the assumption of continuous growth itself.
