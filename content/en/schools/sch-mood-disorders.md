# Mood Disorders

Mood disorders are a diagnostic class that groups conditions in which the primary disturbance lies in the regulation of emotional state (depression, mania, or alternation between the two), to a degree that exceeds the intensity, duration, or functional impact of ordinary mood fluctuations. DSM-5 (2013) and DSM-5-TR (2022) divide this class into two main sections, depressive disorders and bipolar and related disorders, which were a single group ("mood disorders") in DSM-IV, emphasizing the pathophysiological, therapeutic, and hereditary differences between them despite their shared depressive component.

## Origins and Context

The modern distinction between unipolar disorders (depression alone) and bipolar disorders (alternating depression and mania) goes back to Kraepelin's overarching category of "manic-depressive insanity," which Karl Leonhard (1957) divided into unipolar and bipolar forms, a distinction adopted by modern diagnostic systems. DSM-III (1980) introduced specific descriptive criteria for major depressive episodes and manic episodes, while DSM-IV (1994) distinguished between Type I, which requires a full manic episode, and Type II, which requires a major depressive episode and a hypomanic episode, without ever having a full manic episode.

## Diagnostic Anchors

- **Major depressive disorder (MDD)**: DSM-5-TR requires at least five of nine symptoms (one of which must be depressed mood or loss of interest or pleasure) for at least two consecutive weeks, with a noticeable functional impact. DSM-5 removed the "bereavement exclusion," which had prevented an MDD diagnosis in the first weeks after the loss of a loved one, replacing it with an explanatory note that calls for a careful clinical distinction between normal grief and a depressive episode.
- **Bipolar I and II disorders**: Type I requires at least one manic episode lasting one week (or any duration if hospitalization is necessary), usually accompanied by depressive episodes but not diagnostically required. Type II requires a hypomanic episode (at least four days) and a major depressive episode, without the manic symptoms ever reaching the threshold of a full episode.
- **Cyclothymic disorder**: chronic mood fluctuations (at least two years in adults) between hypomanic and depressive symptoms, none of which reach the full diagnostic threshold.
- **Persistent depressive disorder**: DSM-5 merged chronic dysthymia and chronic major depressive disorder into a single category requiring depressed mood for most of the day for at least two years.
- **Additions in DSM-5**: DSM-5 introduced disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (DMDD) for children (diagnosed between ages 6 and 18, with onset before age 10) to limit the overdiagnosis of bipolar disorder in children with recurrent severe anger outbursts, and premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) as an official diagnostic category after it had been a research appendix in DSM-IV-TR.
- **ICD-11 position**: ICD-11 classifies mood disorders under the category "Mood Disorders," with criteria for depressive and manic episodes that are initially close to those of DSM-5-TR, while differing in the details of exclusions and in the precise time criteria for some subcategories.

## Contributions and Key Figures

Frederick Goodwin, together with Kay Redfield Jamison, co-authored the reference work *Manic-Depressive Illness*, which organized clinical and biological knowledge about the illness and its natural course. David Kupfer chaired the general task force that prepared DSM-5, while Jan Fawcett chaired the mood disorders work group; under their leadership, the depressive disorders were separated from the bipolar disorders into two independent chapters.

## Special Clinical Contexts

Basic diagnostic categories are defined by uniform criteria, but the course and clinical presentation of mood disorders change across the life span and physiological contexts. Depression in adolescents sometimes appears more as irritability and social withdrawal than as overt sadness, which complicates distinguishing it from normal adolescent fluctuations or other behavioral disorders. In older adults, depression sometimes overlaps with cognitive symptoms resembling early dementia ("pseudodementia") or with chronic medical conditions that make it hard to isolate the purely mood component. DSM-5 also introduced the "peripartum onset" specifier for major depressive episodes, recognizing that depression beginning during pregnancy or in the four weeks after delivery has distinct risk factors and treatment approaches.

Assessment of suicidal behavior and its risk remains a transversal dimension that intersects with nearly all diagnoses in this group. Recurrent thoughts of death, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempts or plans make up one of the nine symptom criteria for a major depressive episode (self-harm without suicidal intent is not itself a criterion), and they require a systematic assessment separate from the categorical diagnosis itself. "Treatment-resistant" mood disorders generally refer to failure to achieve an adequate response to at least two appropriate pharmacological treatment attempts, in dose and duration. This clinical category has driven the development of alternative interventions such as electroconvulsive therapy, transcranial magnetic stimulation, and newer drug treatments.

## Prevalence by Subtype and Setting

The World Mental Health Surveys offer the most comparable cross-national figures. For DSM-IV major depressive episode, an analysis of eighteen countries found average lifetime prevalence of about 14.6 percent in ten high-income countries and 11.1 percent in eight low- and middle-income countries, while twelve-month averages were similar in the two groups, about 5.5 and 5.9 percent. Individual countries differed widely, with lifetime estimates in China and Japan far below those in France and the United States. Note that this measure counts episodes and is not identical to a diagnosis of major depressive disorder. For bipolar conditions, a study of eleven countries in the Americas, Europe, Asia, the Middle East and New Zealand reported pooled lifetime estimates of about 0.6 percent for bipolar I, 0.4 percent for bipolar II and 1.4 percent for subthreshold forms, 2.4 percent for the spectrum as a whole. Differences between settings may reflect real variation in risk as well as differences in recall, reporting and the cultural fit of interview questions. Comparable cross-national figures for cyclothymic, persistent depressive, disruptive mood dysregulation and premenstrual dysphoric disorders are not given here.

## Limitations

The boundary between normal hypomanic-like states and ordinary mood episodes remains a matter of clinical debate, particularly in diagnosing Type II bipolar disorder, where brief hypomanic episodes may be confused with ordinary mood changes or with personality disorders. The removal of the bereavement exclusion from DSM-5 also sparked wide debate about the risk of turning normal grief into a medical diagnosis (medicalization), a critique directly linked to the broader debate about the inflation of diagnostic criteria in contemporary psychiatry.
