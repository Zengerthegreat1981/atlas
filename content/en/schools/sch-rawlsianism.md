# Contemporary Political Liberalism (Rawlsianism and After)

Rawlsianism is a school of political and moral philosophy centred on John Rawls (1921–2002), professor of philosophy at Harvard, and his *A Theory of Justice* (1971), which revived the idea of the social contract in a Kantian form and made justice the first virtue of social institutions. It dominated Anglo-American political philosophy from the 1970s to the beginning of the twenty-first century, and provoked responses from many quarters.

## Connection to the Islamic Tradition of Justice

Justice holds a central place in Islamic thought: the Mu'tazila made "justice" one of their five principles, al-Mawardi (d. 1058) wrote *al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya* on the foundations of the imamate and governance, and Ibn Khaldun said in the *Muqaddimah* (1377) that injustice portends the ruin of civilization. Al-Farabi (c. 870–950), in *Opinions of the Citizens of the Virtuous City*, presented a conception of the just city founded on the virtues. Rawls, by contrast, began from the fact of pluralism, namely that citizens differ in their comprehensive conceptions of the good, so justice cannot be built on a single creed. Abdullahi An-Na'im, in *Islam and the Secular State* (2008), employed the idea of "civic reason", close to Rawls's "public reason", to defend a state that does not impose Shari'a as official law while Muslims remain free to advocate for it. Other Arab scholars examine how far Rawlsian ideas can be applied in societies with a strong religious frame of reference.

## The Main Idea in *A Theory of Justice*

The theory starts from a question: what principles would free and equal persons agree on to regulate the basic structure of their society? To answer, Rawls proposes the "original position", a hypothetical situation in which the parties choose principles behind a "veil of ignorance", so that no one knows his social position, his talents or his conception of the good, and they therefore choose fair rules. He argues that they would choose two principles:

- **The first principle**: each person has an equal right to the most extensive system of equal basic liberties compatible with a similar system for all.
- **The second principle**: social and economic inequalities are arranged so that they are to the benefit of the least advantaged (the "difference principle"), and are attached to offices open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity.
- **Lexical priority**: the first principle takes precedence over the second, so liberty is not sacrificed for economic gains.
- **Primary goods**: what every rational person wants whatever his plans: rights and liberties, powers and opportunities, income and wealth, and the bases of self-respect.
- **Reflective equilibrium**: a method that adjusts intuitions and principles to one another until they cohere.

## Political Liberalism

In *Political Liberalism* (1993), partly in response to communitarian and other critics, Rawls acknowledged that *A Theory of Justice* had relied on a comprehensive Kantian moral doctrine, which could not be the basis of agreement in a pluralist society. He therefore presented justice as a freestanding "political" conception capable of an "overlapping consensus" among different reasonable religious and philosophical doctrines. He made "public reason" a constraint on the kind of arguments acceptable in fundamental constitutional matters. He then extended the idea to the international sphere in *The Law of Peoples* (1999), and reformulated the doctrine in *Justice as Fairness: A Restatement* (2001).

## Figures and Reception

- **Students and expositors**: Samuel Freeman (*Rawls*, 2007), and Thomas Pogge, a student of Rawls, who wrote *Realizing Rawls* (1989) and *John Rawls: His Life and Theory of Justice* (2007) and argued for a broader global justice.
- **Rivals from the right and the communitarians**: Robert Nozick, in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), defended the minimal state, while MacIntyre in *After Virtue* (1981), Sandel in *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) and Walzer in *Spheres of Justice* (1983) criticized the conception of the self as detached from its society.
- **Feminist critique**: Susan Moller Okin, in *Justice, Gender, and the Family* (1989), held that the theory neglected the family as part of the basic structure.
- **Critiques from race, disability and capabilities**: Amartya Sen, in *The Idea of Justice* (2009), preferred realistic comparison to the construction of an ideal system; Martha Nussbaum built the capabilities theory in *Frontiers of Justice* (2006); and Charles Mills, in *The Racial Contract* (1997), criticized the social contract as founded on racial exclusion.

## Limits and Criticisms

- **The incentives principle**: G. A. Cohen, in *Rescuing Justice and Equality* (2008), holds that the difference principle permits incentives that are inconsistent with genuine equality.
- **The limits of the basic structure**: some ask why justice is confined to institutions and not to the conduct of individuals.
- **International application**: many doubt that Rawls was right to settle for a narrowly demanding law of peoples.
- **Religious pluralism**: critics hold that "public reason" excludes religious arguments and places a greater burden on the religious.
- **The original position**: debate remains open over whether choosing the "minimum" (maximin) is rational, and Harsanyi defended the average-utility criterion as an alternative.
