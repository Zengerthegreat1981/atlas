# Polyvagal-Informed Therapy

Polyvagal-Informed Therapy is a therapeutic framework built on Polyvagal Theory, developed by Stephen Porges in the 1990s. This approach differs from other somatic therapies in that it is **framed by a specific (though controversial) neurological theory** about the structure of the autonomic nervous system. The framework offers a "map" of the nervous system in the form of a three-rung "ladder" of states, and directs the therapist toward strengthening the "ventral vagal pathway" associated with safety and social engagement.

This framework is generally classified as a **"controversial theory, applications in development."** The theory is contested in neuroscience, but its clinical applications are spreading rapidly in the treatment of trauma and attachment.

## Intellectual and Historical Context

Polyvagal theory is a neurological theory proposed by Porges in his foundational 1995 paper ("Orienting in a defensive world: Mammalian modifications of our evolutionary heritage. A Polyvagal Theory"). It departs from the classical understanding of the autonomic nervous system (the dual division: sympathetic/parasympathetic) and proposes in its place **three evolutionary pathways**:

1. **The dorsal vagal pathway (Dorsal Vagal)**: said to be the oldest evolutionarily, associated with "freeze," collapse, and dissociation.
2. **The ventral vagal pathway (Ventral Vagal)**: said to be the most recent evolutionarily, associated with safety, social engagement, and facial expression.
3. **The sympathetic pathway (Sympathetic)**: associated with fight or flight.

The theory's basic claim: humans move through these pathways according to their assessment of safety (Neuroception). Great danger activates the sympathetic pathway, absolute danger activates the dorsal pathway (freeze), and safety activates the ventral pathway (engagement).

The foundational book: *The Polyvagal Theory* (2011) by Porges, followed by *Clinical Applications of the Polyvagal Theory* (2018) by Porges with Deb Dana.

## Founding Concepts

### Neuroception
A term coined by Porges: the autonomic nervous system's unconscious assessment of safety in the environment. It occurs before conscious perception and determines which of the three pathways is activated. Trauma disrupts neuroception, so the nervous system remains in a state of chronic alertness or dissociation.

### The Ventral Vagal Complex (VVC)
The "safe" pathway in the nervous system. It activates facial expression, tone of voice, eye contact, and listening. Strengthening it is a primary goal of therapy: helping the client learn to access "bodily safety."

### The Polyvagal Ladder
A metaphor from Deb Dana: three nervous system states that can be distinguished at any moment (safety, threat, collapse). The therapeutic work is helping the client "climb" the ladder toward safety.

### Co-Regulation
Regulation does not happen only individually, but through attunement with another. The therapist acts as a "regulating nervous system" for the client, and this idea links polyvagal theory to attachment theory.

### Autonomic Flexibility
The ability to move flexibly among the three pathways. The goal is not "to stay in the ventral pathway always," but **flexibility in moving between them** according to context.

## Clinical Applications

Polyvagal theory is not a therapeutic protocol in the classical sense. It is a **conceptual framework** that guides the application of other therapies. The most prominent applications:

- **Trauma therapy**: it guides somatic therapies (SE, SP, EMDR) toward working with autonomic states. The therapist continually monitors: is the client in the ventral, sympathetic, or dorsal pathway?
- **Attachment therapy**: attachment theory (Bowlby) and polyvagal theory are complementary, since co-regulation in the therapeutic relationship arises from ventral-pathway regulation.
- **Family and couples therapy**: working on regulating the nervous systems of both parties in the session.
- **Educational applications**: "self-regulation" programs in schools, especially for children exposed to trauma.

## Its Place in the Field of Trauma Treatment

The polyvagal-informed framework occupies a unique position:

- **It is a theoretical framework more than a therapy** (unlike SE, SP, EMDR). It is called a "therapy" in the broad sense, but in practice it guides other therapies.
- **It focuses on the autonomic nervous system** (Autonomic Nervous System), not on narrative or memory.
- **It offers a "map" for therapy** (The Ladder) that is easy for both therapist and client to learn.
- **It is scientifically contested** (Controversial in Neuroscience).

## Evidence and the Scientific Controversy

### Evidence
- **Wide dissemination**: polyvagal theory is widely cited and taught in trauma-oriented therapy training, although popularity is not evidence that its physiological claims are correct.
- **Clinical uptake**: many clinicians find the framework a useful language for talking about safety and regulation.
- **Varied applications**: the framework has been applied in multiple contexts (education, medicine, psychotherapy).

### Scientific Criticisms
- **Grossman and Taylor (2007)**: an earlier critique in *Biological Psychology* questioned the theory's account of respiratory sinus arrhythmia, vagal function, and the evolutionary sequence it proposes.
- **Grossman (2023)**: a further critical article in *Biological Psychology* argued that the theory's central premises about vagal anatomy, evolution, and function are not supported by the comparative and physiological evidence.
- **Expert evaluation (2026)**: Grossman and 38 co-authors, writing in *Clinical Neuropsychiatry*, concluded that the theory is not defensible on current neurophysiological and evolutionary evidence.
- **Porges's response**: Porges has published replies defending the theory, including one in the same 2026 journal issue arguing that the critique mischaracterizes it.
- **The manual problem**: there is no approved "Treatment Manual" for "Polyvagal-Informed Therapy" in the strict sense, which makes the "therapy" closer to applying the framework within other therapies.

## Limitations and Critique

- **A genuine scientific controversy**: the neurological criticisms (Grossman and Taylor 2007; Grossman 2023) touch the core of the theory.
- **Conceptual vagueness**: terms such as "Ventral Vagal" are used popularly without scientific precision.
- **The risk of superficiality**: therapists may use the framework superficially without engaging deeply with the neurological model.
- **A shortage of clinical evidence**: few randomized controlled trials test "polyvagal-informed therapy" as an independent entity.

## Legacy and Influence

Regardless of the scientific controversy, the influence of polyvagal theory on the contemporary therapeutic landscape cannot be denied. It brought the **autonomic nervous system** back to the center of attention in trauma therapy and provided a shared language for therapists from different schools. It combined neuroscience and psychotherapy in an attractive way, and influenced other therapies (SE, SP, EMDR, IFS), guiding them toward a deeper understanding of bodily states.
