# Ethnoarchaeology

Ethnoarchaeology is the study of living peoples in order to understand the archaeological record. Its premise is simple: archaeologists find things, not behaviour, and to interpret those things they need to know how behaviour creates them. Ethnoarchaeologists therefore live with contemporary communities, watch how people make, use, break, and discard objects, and record exactly what material traces each activity leaves behind. The result is a bridge between ethnography and archaeology — a set of tested links between the living world and the buried one.

Ethnoarchaeology must not be confused with the anthropology of archaeology, which studies archaeologists themselves, nor with archaeology as a whole, of which it is a method and a subfield. Its distinctive question is always the same: what does this pattern in the ground mean in terms of human action?

## History

The idea is older than the name. Nineteenth-century archaeologists already compared ancient tools with those of living peoples, but the comparison was usually loose and speculative. The field took its modern form with the New Archaeology of the 1960s and 1970s, when Lewis Binford argued that archaeology needed a rigorous theory connecting behaviour to material remains — what he called middle-range theory. Binford put the idea into practice in Alaska, living with Nunamiut hunters and recording in exhaustive detail how caribou were butchered, how bones were broken for marrow, and how camps were organised. The result, *Nunamiut Ethnoarchaeology* (1978), became a landmark text of the field and a model of what careful observation could achieve.

The late 1970s and early 1980s saw a rapid expansion. Carol Kramer edited *Ethnoarchaeology: Implications of Ethnography for Archaeology* (1979), the first programmatic collection, and Richard Gould's *Living Archaeology* (1980) offered a survey of the new research. Ian Hodder's *Symbols in Action* (1982), based on fieldwork in Kenya, Zambia, and Sudan, challenged Binford's approach: Hodder argued that material culture is not a passive reflection of behaviour but an active medium of meaning, used by people to negotiate identity, status, and power. The debate between Binford's processual ethnoarchaeology and Hodder's contextual approach shaped the field for decades.

In the 1990s and 2000s, ethnoarchaeology diversified. James O'Connell's work with the Hadza of Tanzania tested models of forager behaviour against observed patterns of bone discard, while Nicholas David and Carol Kramer's *Ethnoarchaeology in Action* (2001) synthesised half a century of research and extended the field beyond foragers and farmers to craft production, trade, settlement, and mortuary practice. Today ethnoarchaeology is practised on every continent, in villages, cities, and industrial settings.

## Key Figures and Works

Binford (1978) remains the touchstone, not only for its data on Nunamiut hunting but for its argument that interpretation must be built on observed linkages between behaviour and remains. Kramer (1979) defined the field's agenda and showed its relevance to ceramics, settlement, and site formation. Gould (1980) developed the approach, drawing notably on fieldwork in Australia's Western Desert, and emphasised the role of discard and abandonment in shaping the record.

Hodder (1982) supplied the major alternative, insisting that objects carry meaning and that ethnoarchaeology must interpret symbols as well as processes. Alison Wylie's "The Reaction against Analogy" (1985) provided the philosophical backbone, arguing that analogies between living and past peoples must be tested against independent evidence rather than assumed. David and Kramer (2001) brought the traditions together, showing how ethnoarchaeology could serve both processual and post-processual archaeology, and O'Connell's long-term Hadza research set the standard for the quantitative study of forager sites.

## Method

Ethnoarchaeological method is ethnography with a material focus. The researcher maps settlements and activity areas, records the life history of objects from manufacture to discard, measures the spatial distribution of debris, and excavates recently abandoned sites to compare what was observed in life with what survives in the ground. Experiments are common: butchering an animal and recording the bone fragments, firing a pot and tracking its use, or building a house and watching it decay.

Analogy is the method's core and its risk. Ethnoarchaeologists use the present to interpret the past, but they must show that the analogy is justified — that the conditions, materials, and behaviours are comparable — and they must test their inferences against independent lines of evidence. The field has also become attentive to ethics: research is increasingly collaborative, carried out with the consent and participation of the communities studied, whose houses and refuse are also their heritage.

## Influence and Legacy

Ethnoarchaeology gave archaeology its interpretive discipline. Before it, archaeologists often read the past through common sense or through unexamined assumptions; after it, they had a body of observed regularities against which to test their claims. It underpins the study of site formation, the interpretation of faunal remains, and the reconstruction of past settlement, craft, and trade. It also fed back into anthropology, showing ethnographers that material life — pots, houses, rubbish — is a rich source of social information.

The field's influence extends beyond archaeology. Its methods are used in the study of contemporary waste, in forensic archaeology, and in heritage management, where understanding how sites form is essential to preserving them. Ethnoarchaeology's lasting lesson is that the archaeological record is not a direct picture of the past but a transformed one, and that only careful study of the living can teach us how to read it.
