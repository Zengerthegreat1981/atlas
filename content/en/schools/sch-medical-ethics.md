# Medical Ethics

## Overview

Medical ethics is a field of applied ethics that examines the values and norms that should govern the physician–patient relationship, decisions at the beginning and end of life, the allocation of scarce health resources, and research on human subjects. The roots of this field extend through multiple civilizational traditions that predate the contemporary Western academic field known as "bioethics" (Bioethics), which did not crystallize under that name until the 1970s.

## The Islamic Tradition in Medical Ethics

Islamic civilization developed a rich tradition known as "the ethics of the physician" (*adab al-tabib*) and "the jurisprudence of medicine" (*fiqh al-tibb*), which blends professional ethics drawn from Greek medicine (Galen in particular) with Islamic religious and legal thought. An early work in the genre is *Adab al-Tabib* by Ishaq ibn Ali al-Ruhawi (9th century). Abu Bakr al-Razi (d. c. 925 CE) is credited with a treatise on the physician's conduct (*Akhlaq al-Tabib*), emphasizing the physician's compassion toward poor patients, the necessity of continuing to follow the patient even after treatment has failed, and the critique of charlatan physicians. Ibn Sina (d. 1037 CE), in *The Canon of Medicine* (*al-Qanun fi al-Tibb*), systematized medical knowledge and set out rules for testing drugs, while Ali ibn al-Abbas al-Majusi (10th century), in *The Complete Book of the Medical Art* (*Kamil al-Sina'a al-Tibbiyya*), is known for discussing the physician's duties and bearing toward patients.

On the jurisprudential level, the scholars of legal theory (*usul al-fiqh*) developed an integrated normative framework through the theory of the "objectives of Islamic law" (*maqasid al-shari'a*), developed by Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (d. 1111 CE) and later systematized by Abu Ishaq al-Shatibi (d. 1388 CE). "The preservation of life" (*hifz al-nafs*) is one of the five universal objectives (*al-kulliyat al-khams*) on which medical legal rulings are still built today, such as the permissibility of treatment, the ruling on organ transplantation, and the rulings on smoking. Jurists also developed rules such as necessity permitting what is otherwise forbidden (for example, dissection for therapeutic purposes despite the inviolability of the dead body in principle), and the maxim "no harm, and no reciprocating harm" (*la darar wa la dirar*), which remains a reference for contemporary Islamic jurisprudential councils, such as the International Islamic Fiqh Academy, in rulings on issues such as in vitro fertilization, human cloning and brain death.

## The Origins of Modern Western Bioethics

The oldest historical foundation in the West goes back to the Hippocratic tradition (5th-4th century BCE), whose Oath enjoins the physician to benefit patients, avoid harm and keep confidences. (The maxim *Primum non nocere*, "first, do no harm," is not found in the Oath itself, though it echoes the Hippocratic *Epidemics*.) But the contemporary field arose in response to glaring scandals. The most prominent were the experiments of Nazi physicians on prisoners in concentration camps, which led to the "Nuremberg Code" (1947), an early and influential statement requiring voluntary, informed consent (Informed Consent) for experiments on human beings. Another was the American Tuskegee syphilis study (1932-1972, exposed in the press in 1972), in which several hundred Black American men with syphilis were left untreated, without informed consent, so that the course of the disease could be observed, even after penicillin became available. This led to the "Belmont Report" (1979), which set out three founding principles: respect for persons, beneficence (doing good and avoiding harm), and justice in the distribution of the burdens and benefits of research.

## The Four Principles: The Beauchamp and Childress Framework

In their seminal book *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (1979), Tom Beauchamp and James Childress presented the framework most widely used in contemporary medical education, "principlism" (Principlism). It rests on four mid-level principles that are not arranged in a fixed hierarchy: (1) respect for patient autonomy (Autonomy), meaning the patient's right to accept or refuse treatment after being fully informed; (2) non-maleficence (Non-maleficence); (3) beneficence (Beneficence), meaning the active pursuit of the patient's benefit rather than merely refraining from harming them; and (4) justice (Justice), in the distribution of scarce health resources such as intensive-care beds or donated organs. The difficulty of this framework lies in the practical conflict among these principles. For example, when a competent patient refuses a life-saving treatment, respect for their autonomy collides with the principle of beneficence. This calls for a contextual, balancing judgment rather than the mechanical application of principles.

## Issues at the End and Beginning of Life

The American case of Karen Ann Quinlan (1975-1976), whose parents asked that their daughter be disconnected from a ventilator after she had fallen into a persistent vegetative state (the New Jersey Supreme Court ruled in their favor in 1976), prompted wide legal and ethical debate about the distinction between "active euthanasia" (Active Euthanasia), which is prohibited in most ethical and legal traditions, and "passive euthanasia," meaning the withdrawal of futile treatment (Withdrawal of Futile Treatment), which many jurists and medical ethicists permit. This includes some contemporary Muslim scholars, who rely on the distinction in Islamic jurisprudence between the prohibited "causing death" (*al-imatah*) and the permissible "not prolonging the dying process" (*adam italat al-ihtidar*).

As for the beginning of life, abortion is an arena of conflict between the "Pro-Life" position, which is based on the view that the fetus is a full human being from the moment of conception and has full sanctity, and the "Pro-Choice" position, which is based on the priority of the pregnant woman's autonomy over her own body. The dominant Islamic jurisprudence tends toward a middle position that ties the ruling to the stage of ensoulment (*nafkh al-ruh*), which many jurists place at one hundred and twenty days, based on a hadith, although some place it earlier.

## Applied Example: Allocating Ventilators During the Pandemic

During the COVID-19 pandemic, hospitals in a number of countries faced acute shortages of mechanical ventilators, which made it necessary to develop triage protocols (Triage) to determine who would receive a ventilator when more than one patient competed for it. The designers of these protocols faced a tension between the criterion of "saving the greatest number of life-years" (utilitarian in essence), the criterion of a "fair lottery," which treats patients equally without distinction by age or quality of life (closer to Kantian justice), and the criterion of priority for medical staff (on the grounds of reciprocity toward those who risk their lives to save others). This is a vivid example of the collision of the four principles under conditions of extreme scarcity that permit no solution satisfying everyone.

## Institutional Impact and Application

The field led to the establishment of research ethics committees (institutional review boards, IRBs) and clinical ethics committees in many hospitals and research centers, and to international instruments such as the Declaration of Helsinki (1964), issued by the World Medical Association to guide research on human beings. It also gave rise to a full academic specialization taught compulsorily in medical schools around the world, including universities in the Islamic world that combine the four Western principles with the objectives of Islamic law in teaching professional medical ethics.
