# The Academic Study of Religion and Sport (Religion and Sport Studies)

A sub-field within the comparative study of religion that examines the historical and social relationship between religious institutions and modern competitive sport. It does not treat sport as a marginal phenomenon. Instead, it regards sport in the modern American and Western context as carrying functions similar to those of religion (rituals, collective loyalty, symbolic sacred objects), and as historically entangled with actual religious institutions. The American historian William J. Baker, professor of history at the University of Maine and the author of earlier work on Jesse Owens and on sport in the Western world, is regarded as a leading figure of this field in academia, through his book *Playing with God: Religion and Modern Sport* (2007).

## Sport in the Islamic Tradition: Background for Comparison

The study of sport and religion did not arise within this Western field in Islam, but the Islamic tradition provides important comparative material. Jurisprudence and hadith distinguished between idle play and beneficial training. The hadith "There is no contest except in camels, horses or archery" (*la sabq illa fi khuffin aw hafirin aw nasl*) made horse racing, camel racing and archery permissible with a stake, and learning archery, swimming and horse riding was regarded in some traditions as an educational duty. Chivalric literature in the Abbasid and Mamluk periods (treatises on horsemanship and archery) organised physical training within an ethical religious framework. This system differs from the American situation the field examines, since sport in Islam did not acquire a status similar to religion. Nevertheless, the comparison shows that the relationship between the trained body and moral duty is old and not purely modern. In the present day, the participation of Muslim men and women in the Olympic Games and the World Cup has become a subject of debate in fatwas and in politics.

## The Historical Root: Muscular Christianity

The field traces its roots to the British-Victorian movement of "Muscular Christianity" in the mid-to-late nineteenth century. Its pioneers included Charles Kingsley and Thomas Hughes, author of *Tom Brown's School Days* (1857), which promoted organised sport as an instrument of moral and spiritual formation for young people. From it emerged institutions such as the Young Men's Christian Association (YMCA), with which the invention of basketball (James Naismith, 1891) and volleyball (William Morgan, 1895) became associated. Baker traces an older root as well: the ancient Olympic Games at Olympia dedicated to Zeus, the attitude of early Christianity towards them and towards the gladiatorial contests of the Roman amphitheatre, and the ending of the Games in the late fourth century under Theodosius I according to the commonly accepted account. He argues that the legacy of Muscular Christianity did not end but was transformed in the twentieth century into new forms in the United States: the pre-game prayer, evangelistic "Christian athlete" movements (the Fellowship of Christian Athletes, 1954, and Athletes in Action, 1966), and the sacralisation of the rituals of major games, such as the national anthem.

## Method

The field combines traditional social history (tracing institutions, figures and documents) with sociological and functional approaches closer to Durkheim, in reading mass sport as a site that generates a collective symbolic solidarity similar to a religious ritual. It does so without confusing this analytical comparison with the claim that sport is "a religion" in the literal theological sense; Baker stresses this distinction. The field thus separates sport as a functional or metaphorical civil religion from sport as a setting for actual religious practice, such as the prayers and evangelism of Christian athletes. Michael Novak had earlier, in *The Joy of Sports* (1976), offered a philosophical reading that regards sport as a natural religious experience. Robert Higgs then, in *God in the Stadium* (1995), provided a historical survey of the tension and cooperation between the church and sport in America. Joseph Price gathered essays in *From Season to Season: Sports as American Religion* (2001).

## Extension Beyond the American Context: Sumo and Shinto

In contrast to the dominance of the American-Christian context in establishing this field, Lee A. Thompson, professor at the Faculty of Sport Sciences of Waseda University in Japan, opened a parallel branch studying professional sumo in its living relation to Shinto rituals. Here sumo is not a functional metaphor as in the American case, but an actual, continuing ritual practice. The ring (dohyo) is defined by purification with salt and a roof shaped like a shrine. In his chapter on the invention of the yokozuna rank (1998), Thompson shows that the institutional framework surrounding these rituals (the ranks, the championship system, the shape of the roof above the ring) is relatively recent in its formulation, dating to the early twentieth century rather than to ancient times. This distinction is methodologically parallel to Baker's. Joseph Alter's work *The Wrestler's Body* (1992) on the Indian wrestling of *kushti* and its relation to Hinduism and brahmacharya is added to this.

## Analytical Questions

The field revolves around questions such as: does sport perform the function of religion in secular societies, providing meaning, belonging and ritual time? How is sport used for evangelistic or nationalist purposes? Does it replace religious ritual or coexist with it? Researchers observe the use of religious terms (miracle, saint, prayer) in sports discourse, and the position of the athletic body between sacrifice, discipline and commercial image.

## Limits of the Field

Critics fault the field for its narrow geographical basis, since it is dominated by the American Protestant example, and for the ambiguity of the concept of "religion" when applied to any enthusiastic gathering. This calls for caution against including every cheering crowd under the name of religion. Others argue that contemporary sport is a commercial institution that exceeds religious descriptions, and that the analogy with Durkheim does not explain the diversity of spectatorship or the differentiation of audiences. There is also a gap in field research with the athletes themselves and in how they understand their practice religiously.
