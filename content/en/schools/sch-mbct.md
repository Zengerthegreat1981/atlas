# Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)
Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT) is a structured group therapy developed by Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale in the late 1990s; its founding book was published in 2002. MBCT combines mindfulness techniques (inspired by Jon Kabat-Zinn's MBSR program) with elements of cognitive therapy in a short protocol (eight weeks) whose main aim is to **reduce the risk of depressive relapse** in people who have had recurrent episodes of depression. It is counted among the "third wave" therapies best supported by empirical evidence, and national guidance such as that of NICE in the United Kingdom includes group MBCT among the options for preventing relapse in people whose depression has remitted but who are at higher risk of relapse.

## Intellectual and historical context

MBCT arose from the meeting of three currents in the late 1990s: (1) the documented success of the MBSR program (developed by Kabat-Zinn from 1979; *Full Catastrophe Living*, 1990) in dealing with chronic pain and stress, (2) the aim of finding a preventive group intervention for people recovered from recurrent depression, building on cognitive models of vulnerability to relapse, and (3) new cognitive research on repetitive thinking patterns (Ruminative Processing) and their role in the persistence of depression.

The three founders wanted — and this is what distinguished MBCT — to move mindfulness training from the context of "stress reduction" to the context of **"relapse prevention."** The hypothesis (Teasdale's differential activation account): patterns of negative thinking that were active during past depression can be reactivated by even a mild dip in mood in people who have recovered. If the client learns to notice these thoughts without reacting to them, the risk of sliding into a new episode falls.

## The protocol: eight weeks

The program is delivered in eight weekly group sessions (about two hours each) with daily home practice. In outline:

- **Early weeks**: awareness of the body and breath and of "automatic pilot" (for example the raisin exercise and the body scan); dealing with a wandering mind and with obstacles.
- **Middle weeks**: staying present with difficult experience, recognizing aversion, and seeing thoughts as mental events rather than facts.
- **Later weeks**: noticing how mood and activity are linked, preparing a personal plan for early signs of relapse, and carrying the practice forward after the course.

The three-minute breathing space is introduced during the course and used as a brief practice in daily life.

## Founding concepts

### The Three-Minute Breathing Space
A central exercise in MBCT, used as a point of return in difficult moments. Three stages: (1) Noticing the present moment, (2) Gathering by focusing on the breath, (3) Expanding to include the whole body. The aim is not to calm the emotion but to **interrupt the pattern of automatic reaction**.

### Thoughts as Events
MBCT proposes that thoughts are not "facts" or "commands" that must be carried out, but mental events that appear and disappear. The aim is not to change the content but to **change the relationship** to the content. The program encourages seeing thoughts as passing mental events.

### Decentering
The ability to observe thoughts and feelings from "outside them," without being fused with them. It is also called the "Observer Self" or the "Metacognitive Stance."

### Automatic Pilot
The mind that runs without conscious attention, processing present experiences with past patterns. Mindfulness means breaking this pattern and returning to the "here and now."

### From Low Mood to Relapse
MBCT's rationale: a mild dip in mood reactivates negative thinking and rumination, which can deepen mood and lead to a new episode. Breaking the loop is possible at several points, and MBCT focuses on noticing the process early. How far this mechanism, and changes in mindfulness and decentering, actually explain MBCT's benefit is still under empirical investigation.

## Its place in the field of psychotherapy

MBCT occupies a unique position among the "third wave" schools (Hayes, Linehan, Segal et al.). It is **cognitive in origin** (from CBT) but **acceptance-oriented** (like ACT). It differs from ACT in that it focuses on **relapse prevention** (not the treatment of acute depression), and it differs from MBSR in that its **content is explicitly cognitive** (linking mood to thoughts). In the classification of mindfulness-oriented therapies (Mindfulness-Based Interventions), MBCT is counted among the "structured protocols" (MBP — Mindfulness-Based Programs), alongside MBSR, MBCP (childbirth and parenting) and MB-EAT (eating awareness).

## Empirical evidence

- **Teasdale et al. (2000)**: a randomized controlled trial in patients recovered from recurrent depression found that MBCT, added to usual care, reduced relapse among those with three or more previous episodes; no such benefit was seen for those with only two episodes.
- **Ma & Teasdale (2004)**: a second randomized trial that replicated the finding in a similar group.
- **Kuyken et al. (2016)**: an individual-patient-data meta-analysis in *JAMA Psychiatry* found that MBCT was associated with a lower risk of relapse than usual care, and also showed a lower risk than active treatments (hazard ratio 0.79), which included maintenance antidepressants.
- **NICE**: the 2009 depression guideline recommended MBCT for people who were currently well but had had three or more episodes; the 2022 guideline (NG222) offers group CBT or MBCT for relapse prevention to people in remission who are at higher risk of relapse.
- **Other applications**: MBCT has been studied for a wider range of conditions (for example anxiety and residual symptoms of depression), with less consistent evidence than for relapse prevention.

## Limitations and criticism

- **Adapting to states of active relapse**: the protocol is designed for prevention, not for treating acute episodes. Using it in acute depression needs adaptations.
- **Commitment requirements**: eight weeks, with substantial daily home practice. It is not suitable for every patient.
- **Cognitive depth of the case**: it may not be sufficient for those suffering from complex trauma or personality disorders.
- **Western / Buddhist critique**: it presents itself as "evidence-based," but its roots are in Buddhist meditation. Some reviewers believe the "scientific" framing loses part of the depth of the original practice.

## Legacy and influence

MBCT reshaped the relationship between cognitive psychology and meditation practices. It opened the door to "mindfulness-based therapies" (Mindfulness-Based Interventions) as an independent field, and showed that meditation can be integrated into structured, testable clinical protocols. It has since been adapted for other populations and conditions, and it is taught and applied in many countries.
