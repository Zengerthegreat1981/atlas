# Transpersonal Psychology

Transpersonal psychology is sometimes called the "fourth force" in psychology (after behaviorism, psychoanalysis, and humanistic psychology), a term associated with **Abraham Maslow**, who in the late 1960s described a psychology "transpersonal, transhuman, centered in the cosmos rather than in human needs." It extends humanistic psychology to what lies beyond the individual self (trans = beyond, personal): peak experiences, expanded states of consciousness, mystical experiences, self-transcendence, and psycho-spiritual development. It seeks to combine:

- **Methods of scientific psychology** (observation, measurement, and, where possible, experiment).
- **Attention to the spiritual traditions** (Buddhism, Hinduism, Sufism, Christian and Jewish mysticism, shamanic practices).

## Intellectual Context

- **Humanistic psychology** (Maslow, Rogers, Bugental): the "third force."
- **Analytical psychology** (Jung): the collective unconscious and archetypes, and the idea of a higher Self.
- **William James**: *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), an early psychological study of mystical experience.
- **Comparative religion and mythology** (Eliade, Campbell).
- **Asian contemplative traditions**, introduced to Western audiences by writers such as D. T. Suzuki and Alan Watts.

## Historical Stages

- **Precursors** (early to mid-twentieth century): James, Jung, and later Maslow's work on "peak experiences" (*Religions, Values, and Peak-Experiences*, 1964) and "plateau experiences."
- **Foundation** (1967-1972): the term "transpersonal" is adopted by Maslow, Anthony Sutich, Stanislav Grof, and colleagues; the *Journal of Transpersonal Psychology* begins publication in 1969; the Association for Transpersonal Psychology is founded in 1972. Charles Tart edits *Altered States of Consciousness* (1969).
- **Consolidation** (1970s-1980s): Grof's *Realms of the Human Unconscious* (1975) reports his research with LSD and, later, holotropic breathwork; Ken Wilber's *The Spectrum of Consciousness* (1977) and *Up from Eden* (1981); the Institute of Transpersonal Psychology is founded in California in 1975.
- **Integral and critical phase** (1990s-2000s): Wilber's integral theory (*Sex, Ecology, Spirituality*, 1995), which draws on the "Wilber-Combs lattice" (developed with Allan Combs) relating states and stages of consciousness, and critical discussion of how transpersonal claims can be tested; Walsh and Vaughan's *Paths Beyond Ego* (1993) as a collective statement.
- **Recent period** (2000s to the present): contemplative science and mindfulness research, renewed clinical research on psychedelic-assisted therapy (including work associated with MAPS, founded by Rick Doblin in 1986), and cross-cultural transpersonal work.

## Founding Concepts

- **Consciousness**: treated as a central subject of psychology rather than only a by-product of brain activity; transpersonal writers vary in how far they take non-reductionist positions.
- **Peak experiences (Maslow)**: moments of heightened awareness, unity, beauty, and meaning.
- **Altered and non-ordinary states of consciousness** (Tart, Grof): states that differ from ordinary waking awareness, including meditative and psychedelic states.
- **Unitive consciousness**: experiences of a dissolved boundary between self and world.
- **The Self**: in Jungian and some contemplative thought, a center deeper than the ego.
- **Spiritual development**: stage-like or gradual change described in many traditions, which Wilber and others have tried to map.
- **Meditation**: practices such as mindfulness, vipassana, and zazen, used as methods of attention training and personal transformation.
- **Psychedelic-assisted therapy**: investigational use of substances such as MDMA and psilocybin in clinical trials, under close supervision. This area is under active research and regulation; it is not a self-treatment.

## Key Figures

- **Abraham Maslow** (1908-1970): peak experiences; the "fourth force."
- **Anthony Sutich** (1907-1976): co-founder of the *Journal of Transpersonal Psychology* and the Association for Transpersonal Psychology.
- **Stanislav Grof** (1931-): psychedelic research, holotropic breathwork, perinatal psychology.
- **Charles Tart** (1937-): altered states of consciousness.
- **Ken Wilber** (1949-): the spectrum of consciousness and integral theory.
- **Roger Walsh** and **Frances Vaughan**: transpersonal theory, meditation, and clinical applications.
- **James Fadiman**: psychedelic research and transpersonal psychology.

## Major Sub-Currents

- **"Classical" transpersonal psychology** (Grof, Tart, Fadiman).
- **The spectrum and integral models** (Wilber).
- **Buddhist-influenced psychology** (for example Chogyam Trungpa and Jack Kornfield, and contemplative psychology more generally).
- **Shamanic approaches** (Michael Harner, Joan Halifax, Sandra Ingerman).
- **Psychedelic-assisted therapy** research (MAPS, Doblin; popular account: Pollan, 2018).
- **Holotropic Breathwork** (Grof): a technique developed by Grof and Christina Grof as an alternative to psychedelics.

## Clinical Applications

- **Psychedelic-assisted therapy** is being studied in trials for depression, PTSD, and addiction; findings are promising but preliminary, and regulatory status differs by country.
- **Meditation and mindfulness-based programs** (for example MBSR, which are related to but not specific to transpersonal psychology).
- **Holotropic Breathwork**: used for personal growth and self-exploration; it is not an evidence-based treatment for psychiatric disorders and carries risks for some people.
- **Spiritual counseling and end-of-life support**: work on meaning and transcendence in hospice and bereavement contexts.

## Relation to Neighboring Schools

- **Humanistic psychology**: the direct roots; transpersonal psychology extends its concern with growth toward spiritual experience.
- **Analytical psychology (Jung)**: shares interest in archetypes, the collective unconscious, and the Self.
- **Existential therapy**: shares an interest in meaning, though transpersonal writers place more weight on spiritual experience.
- **Third-wave behavioral therapies (ACT, DBT)** and MBCT: they use mindfulness in a secular, clinically tested form, whereas transpersonal psychology is explicitly concerned with spiritual dimensions.
- **Asian philosophical traditions** (Advaita Vedanta, Buddhism): a major source of concepts and practices, notably in Wilber's work.
- **Sufism**: contemplative practice and concepts of annihilation (*fana*) and subsistence (*baqa*) are sometimes discussed in transpersonal literature.

## Criticism and Limits

- **Testability**: many transpersonal claims are difficult to measure or falsify.
- **Mixing science and spirituality**: critics argue that applying scientific method to spiritual matters distorts both, and that some claims are metaphysical rather than empirical.
- **Psychedelic research**: raises questions of safety, informed consent, and therapist conduct, as well as hype.
- **Comprehensiveness**: Wilber's claim that his model integrates all knowledge is controversial.
- **Standing in the discipline**: the field remains marginal within mainstream academic psychology.
