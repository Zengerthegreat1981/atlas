# Academic Skepticism

A phase in the history of the Platonic Academy (about 265–80 BC), marked by a radical turn from **the Platonism of the Forms** (the Old Academy) to **probabilistic skepticism**. This turn was initiated by **Arcesilaus** (Arcesilaus of Pitane, about 316–241 BC) and reached its height with **Carneades** (Carneades of Cyrene, about 214–129 BC). The ancient tradition divides the period into a Middle Academy (Arcesilaus) and a New Academy (Carneades and his successors), though this division is more pedagogical than sharp.

## Context

In the early third century BC Greek philosophy was passing through a transition: Plato and Aristotle were gone, and their pupils did not preserve the clarity of the doctrine. In this vacuum **Stoicism** emerged as a powerful rival under the leadership of Zeno of Citium, grounding its theory of knowledge on the "cognitive impression" (καταληπτική φαντασία), which cannot be false. Arcesilaus confronted this claim with argument, influenced by the Socratic dialogical method, which examines and does not assert.

## Key figures

- **Arcesilaus** (about 316–241 BC): head of the Academy, who developed a method of refuting his opponent's arguments without a thesis of his own, and adopted "the reasonable" (εὔλογον) as the criterion of action.
- **Carneades of Cyrene** (about 214–129 BC): the greatest figure of the Skeptical Academy, who set out the graded theory of "the persuasive" (πιθανόν). He left no books, and his pupil Clitomachus preserved his teachings.
- **Clitomachus of Carthage** (about 187–110 BC): successor of Carneades, who wrote down and systematized his doctrine.
- **Philo of Larissa** (about 159–84 BC): the last head of the Skeptical Academy, who softened the skepticism in the "Roman Books", and was the teacher of Cicero.

## Central concepts

- **Suspension of judgment (ἐποχή)**: one should not assent to any proposition as certain, because the Stoic sage assents only to a reliable cognitive impression, and there is no impression that cannot possibly be false.
- **Non-apprehension (ἀκαταληψία)**: nothing is apprehended fully; we see appearances, not truths.
- **The persuasive (πιθανόν)**: for Carneades there are three degrees of practical assent: a persuasive impression; a persuasive impression not contradicted by anything that disturbs it; and a persuasive impression that is uncontradicted and has been examined from all sides. These degrees guide action; they do not impose certainty.
- **Critique of Stoic logic**: Carneades directed sorites arguments against the Stoics' claims about the gods and perception, showing that their definitions do not separate precisely, and he uttered his famous saying that without Chrysippus he would not have existed.
- **Action**: the Academic may act in accordance with the persuasive and the reasonable without claiming knowledge.

## The debate with Stoicism

- **Perception**: can an impression carry the mark of its own truth? Carneades adduced cases of twins, similar eggs, dreams and hallucinations to show that the false can be indistinguishable from the true in sense experience.
- **Ethics**: on the Athenian embassy to Rome in 155 BC Carneades delivered two successive speeches, the first praising justice and the second condemning it, to show that rational argument alone does not settle the matter. Cato the Elder objected to this and demanded that the embassy be sent away.

## Relation to Pyrrhonian skepticism

- Pyrrho of Elis (fourth century BC) preceded the Skeptical Academy, and ancient sources report some resemblance between his outlook and that of Arcesilaus, though the extent of any influence is disputed.
- In the first century BC Aenesidemus of Knossos left the Academy, accusing it of being a disguised Stoicism since it accepts that the sage may hold an opinion, and he revived Pyrrhonian skepticism.
- The disagreement between the two schools persisted: the Academy says knowledge is unattainable and accepts the persuasive, while Pyrrhonism remains in a state of inquiry and does not assert even negatively.

## Influence

- **Cicero** (106–43 BC): the most important Latin source, who wrote the *Academica* (about 45 BC), which contains the debate between Philo and Antiochus.
- **Augustine**: wrote *Against the Academics* (386 CE) and criticized Academic skepticism after having been influenced by it.
- **Renaissance and modernity**: Montaigne revived the ancient skepticism in the *Essays* (1580), and Richard Popkin traced it in the history of modern skepticism. David Hume titled the last section of the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748) "Of the Academical or Sceptical Philosophy", and defended a mitigated skepticism close to this heritage.
- **Arabic heritage**: no direct transmission of the Academics' texts into Arabic is known, but the works of kalam dealt with sophistry, agnosticism and subjectivism ('indiyya), and al-Ghazali's objection to sensory certainty in *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* (Deliverance from Error) came close to this problematic.

## The end of Academic skepticism

- **Antiochus of Ascalon** (about 125–68 BC): a pupil of Philo, who rebelled against his teacher and reasserted the possibility of knowledge, combining Platonism, Aristotelianism and Stoicism.
- After him skepticism receded within the Academy and turned into eclecticism and then Middle Platonism.

## Limits

- Our principal sources are hostile or late (Cicero, Sextus Empiricus, Plutarch and Numenius), and contain bias against the skeptics.
- We have no original text by the Academics themselves; everything we have is what others said about them.
- The boundary between the Skeptical Academy and the later "eclectic" Academy of Antiochus is not sharply defined.
- Scholars dispute whether Arcesilaus was a dialectical skeptic who tested the Stoics only on their own premises, or whether he held a positive position.
