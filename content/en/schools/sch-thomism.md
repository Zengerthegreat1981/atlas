# Thomism

A Christian philosophical-theological tradition deriving from **Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274), a Dominican friar and master at the University of Paris. Thomism is regarded as one of the most influential syntheses of Latin Scholasticism. Aquinas sought to unite Aristotelian philosophy (known to him largely through the Latin translations of William of Moerbeke and through the commentaries of Averroes and Avicenna) with Christian doctrine in a comprehensive, systematic whole. His contributions include a metaphysics centred on the act of being (*esse*), the theory of natural law, the Five Ways of arguing for God's existence, an account of the virtues, and sacramental theology. In 1879 Pope Leo XIII, in the encyclical *Aeterni Patris*, urged Catholic schools to restore the study of Aquinas, which gave rise to the twentieth-century movement known as Neo-Thomism.

## Life and context

Aquinas was born around 1225 at Roccasecca in southern Italy into a noble family. He joined the Dominicans (c. 1244) against his family's opposition, studied at Naples, Paris and Cologne under Albertus Magnus, and taught at Paris (1256–1259 and 1268–1272) and at Naples and in Italian Dominican houses. He died in 1274 on his way to the Council of Lyon, leaving the *Summa Theologiae* unfinished. He was canonized in 1323 and later called the "Angelic Doctor" and the "Common Doctor" (*Doctor communis*); he was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1567.

## Principal works

- ***Summa Theologiae*** (c. 1265–1273, unfinished): his principal work.
- ***Summa contra Gentiles*** (c. 1259–1265): a systematic exposition of Christian truth addressed to those who do not accept Christian revelation.
- ***De ente et essentia*** (*On Being and Essence*, early work) and the *Disputed Questions* and *Quodlibetal Questions*.
- **Commentaries** on Aristotle (*Metaphysics*, *Physics*, *Nicomachean Ethics*, *De anima* and others), on Boethius, on the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard, and on Scripture; and the *Catena aurea*, a commentary on the Gospels drawn from the Fathers.

## Central concepts

- **Being, essence and participation**: Aquinas distinguishes the essence of a thing (what it is) from its act of existence (that it is). In God essence and existence are identical; in creatures existence is received and participated from God. The world is created from nothing, and is sustained in being by God.
- **Hylomorphism**: material things are composites of matter and form. The human soul is the form of the body, yet, unlike other forms, it is subsistent and immortal; resurrection of the body is part of Christian doctrine.
- **The Five Ways**: arguments for God's existence in *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 2, a. 3, from motion, efficient causation, contingency and necessity, degrees of perfection, and the order of the world.
- **Analogy**: names such as "good" and "wise" are said of God and creatures neither univocally nor equivocally but analogically.
- **Knowledge**: the human intellect abstracts universal concepts from sense experience; the first objects of the intellect are being and truth; the mind and the world are in a relation of adequation.
- **Ethics and law**: human happiness consists ultimately in the vision of God. The moral life is formed by the cardinal virtues (prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance) and the theological virtues (faith, hope and charity). Law is distinguished into eternal, natural, human and divine law. Natural law is the participation of rational creatures in eternal law.
- **Grace and nature**: grace does not destroy nature but perfects it. Aquinas gives primacy to intellect over will in the analysis of human action, which later distinguished Thomists from Scotists in the debate on intellect and will.

## Development of the tradition

- **Early Thomists**: Aquinas's disciples such as Reginald of Piperno, then Hervaeus Natalis (d. 1323), John Capreolus (c. 1380–1444), the "prince of Thomists", and Cajetan (Thomas de Vio, 1469–1534), whose commentary on the *Summa* became standard.
- **Second Scholasticism**: Dominicans such as Francisco de Vitoria and Domingo Báñez, and Jesuits such as Francisco Suárez, who worked within Thomist traditions but differed from Aquinas on several points.
- **Neo-Thomism**: after *Aeterni Patris* (1879), Thomist teaching was promoted in Catholic institutions; Pius X's *Doctoris Angelici* (1914) and the 24 Thomistic theses (issued in 1914 and reaffirmed in 1916) were part of this effort, and the 1917 Code of Canon Law required Catholic seminaries to teach philosophy and theology according to the method and principles of Aquinas. Leading Neo-Thomists include Réginald Garrigou-Lagrange, Jacques Maritain (1882–1973) and Étienne Gilson (1884–1978), the latter two best known for applying Thomism to modern culture and for historical studies of medieval philosophy.
- **Transcendental Thomism**: Joseph Maréchal, Karl Rahner (1904–1984) and Bernard Lonergan sought to combine Aquinas with Kant's transcendental question about the conditions of knowledge.
- **Analytic Thomism**: philosophers such as Elizabeth Anscombe, Peter Geach, Anthony Kenny, Eleonore Stump and Fergus Kerr read Aquinas in dialogue with analytic philosophy.

## Influence

- **Theology and Church teaching**: Thomism shaped Catholic theology, moral theology and canon law, and many Anglican and Protestant scholars have also studied Aquinas.
- **Law and politics**: Thomist natural-law theory fed the work of Spanish scholastics such as Vitoria and Suárez on the law of nations and rights, and it remains a reference point in modern Catholic social thought.
- **Medieval philosophy**: Aquinas drew on Islamic and Jewish philosophy (Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides), and his position on the eternity of the world, on the unity of the intellect against Latin Averroism, and on faith and reason, were contested in the Parisian condemnations of 1270 and 1277.

## Limits and criticisms

- **Synthesis of Aristotle and Christianity**: critics, including some Protestant reformers and later theologians, question whether the combination faithfully preserves either source; others in the Franciscan tradition (Bonaventure, Scotus) contested his account of will and individuation.
- **Grace, predestination and freedom**: the relation of divine causality to human freedom was debated among later Dominicans and Jesuits (the *de auxiliis* controversy in the late sixteenth century).
- **Modern philosophy**: Kant criticized the traditional proofs of God's existence, and many modern thinkers reject Aristotelian natural philosophy that underlies Thomist metaphysics.
- **Plurality of Thomisms**: Neo-Thomism, Transcendental Thomism and Analytic Thomism differ markedly in how they understand Aquinas, so "Thomism" names a family of readings rather than a single system.
