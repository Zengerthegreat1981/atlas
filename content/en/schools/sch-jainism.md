# Jain Philosophy

An ancient Indian religious and philosophical tradition that appeared in the Ganges basin in the period in which Buddhism arose, among the "Shramana" movements (ascetics who rejected the authority of the Vedas). It rests on a realist, pluralist philosophy of existence, on a distinctive logic that recognizes the many-sidedness of truth (anekantavada), and on an ethic of nonviolence (ahimsa) that is pursued in it with exceptional rigor among Indian traditions. Its followers in India today number about four and a half million according to the 2011 census, with communities in Africa, North America, and Britain.

## Origins and History

The Jains regard themselves as followers of a succession of twenty-four "Tirthankaras" (ford-makers) beginning with Rishabha and ending with Mahavira (Mahāvīra), whose life the traditions date to 599–527 BCE; some historians consider it likely that he lived in the 5th century BCE. He was a contemporary of the Buddha, and a Pali text mentions him as "Nigantha Nataputta". The figure of Parshvanatha (Pārśvanātha), the twenty-third Tirthankara, is probably historical, and according to tradition he preceded Mahavira by about 250 years. Mahavira was born in Kundagrama near Vaishali, left his family at thirty, practiced asceticism for twelve years, then attained absolute knowledge (kevala jnana), and taught until he died at Pava. In later ages the community divided into two branches: the Digambara ("sky-clad"), whose monks go without clothing and who hold that a woman cannot attain liberation directly, and the Shvetambara ("white-clad"), who wear a white garment, affirm that women have a path to liberation, and hold to the "Agama" books that they say the Digambara lost.

## Central Ideas

- **Existents**: the world divides into "jiva" (souls, which are eternal and countlessly many, and have consciousness, energy, and bliss in potentiality) and "ajiva" (non-souls), which are matter (pudgala), motion (dharma), rest (adharma), space (akasha), and time (kala). This dualist realism is made clear in Umasvati's *Tattvartha Sutra*.
- **Karma is matter**: in Jainism, karma consists of fine material particles that adhere to the soul through the passions; what frees the soul is stopping this influx and purging the old karma until it attains liberation (moksha) and settles at the summit of the universe.
- **Seven categories**: the traditions add to jiva and ajiva: influx (asrava), bondage (bandha), stopping of influx (samvara), shedding (nirjara), and liberation (moksha).
- **The three jewels**: right vision, right knowledge, and right conduct, which are the path to liberation in the first sentence of the *Tattvartha Sutra*.
- **Anekantavada (many-sidedness)**: reality has countless aspects, and no single perspective encompasses it. From it branch "nayavada" (the theory of standpoints) and "syadvada" (the theory of "perhaps" or "in some respect"), which are seven forms of judging a thing: it exists in one respect, does not exist in another, exists and does not exist, is indescribable, and what is compounded from these.
- **Nonviolence**: the first of the five great vows (ahimsa, satya or truthfulness, asteya or non-stealing, brahmacharya or chastity, and aparigraha or non-possession). The Jains forbid knowingly killing any living being, and monks maintain nonviolence by carefully sweeping the path before them, and by observing strict vegetarianism; some Shvetambara monks and nuns also wear a mouth-cloth.

## Figures and Texts

The Shvetambara canon (the Agamas, in Ardhamagadhi Prakrit, including the surviving Angas) is regarded by the Digambara as lost. The Digambara instead treat as most authoritative a body of texts that includes the *Shatkhandagama*, together with the writings of Kundakunda and the *Tattvartha Sutra*.


- **Kundakunda**: a major Digambara thinker, author of the *Samayasara* and the *Pravacanasara*; his interpretation tends toward distinguishing the absolute from the practical standpoint.
- **Umasvati**: author of the *Tattvartha Sutra* (roughly 2nd to 5th century), the text accepted by both branches.
- **Siddhasena Divakara**: author of the *Sanmati Tarka* on logic (roughly 5th century).
- **Samantabhadra**: author of the *Aptamimamsa*, a critique of other views (6th century).
- **Akalanka** (8th century): a major Digambara logician, who debated with the Buddhists.
- **Haribhadra Suri** (8th century): one of the luminaries of the Shvetambara, author of the *Anekantajaya Pataka* and the *Shaddarshana Samuccaya*.
- **Hemachandra** (1089–1172): a great polymath from Gujarat.
- **Yashovijaya** (17th century): renewer of Jain logic (Navya Nyaya).

## Influence

- It entered the Indian philosophical field through debates with the Buddhists, the Nyaya, and the Vaisheshika, and with its theory of standpoints contributed to developing Indian epistemology.
- Mahatma Gandhi (1869–1948) was influenced by ideas of nonviolence through his Jain friend Shrimad Rajchandra (1867–1901), and then used them in the national movement.
- The Jain libraries in Jaisalmer and Patan preserved thousands of ancient Indian manuscripts, on which students of India's cultural history depend.

## Limits and Criticism

- Shankara (in his commentary on the *Brahma Sutra*) criticized the theory of "syat" as leading to doubt or to contradiction, since whoever affirms every aspect also affirms its opposite; the Jains replied that context limits a statement to a specific standpoint.
- The strict ethics were accused of being hard to apply in civil life, and the concentration of Jains in trade and finance is often explained, at least in part, by the avoidance of agriculture, which kills minute beings.
- The disagreement between the two branches over the text of the Agamas and over the question of women and the abandonment of clothing remained influential in the community's history.
