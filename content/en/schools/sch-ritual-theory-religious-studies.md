# Ritual Theory in Comparative Religion (Robertson Smith to Hubert & Mauss)

A foundational current in the study of religious ritual that emerged in the late nineteenth century and shares a specific methodological thesis: ritual precedes belief. That is, collective practice (sacrifice, observance and celebration) is the primary analytical unit for understanding religion, not doctrine or text. In its first generation it is represented by the Scottish Semitist William Robertson Smith. It then developed in a more precisely sociological form in the work of the French sociologists Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss, and ultimately Durkheim.

## Robertson Smith and the Arab Heritage: From the Hijaz to the Lectures

William Robertson Smith (1846–1894) was proficient in Arabic. He travelled in Egypt and Arabia (including the Hijaz) in the late 1870s and early 1880s and wrote *Kinship and Marriage in Early Arabia* (Cambridge University Press, 1885). His study of Semitic religion was therefore built on Arabic sources, not on Hebrew alone. In his *Lectures on the Religion of the Semites* (1889) he made extensive use of Arabic material: reports on the idols of pre-Islamic Arabia in Ibn al-Kalbi's *Kitab al-Asnam*, pre-Islamic poetry, and the testimony of the monk Nilus of Sinai (c. 400 CE) on the camel sacrifice of the Arabs of Sinai, in which the participants slaughter the camel and eat its flesh raw before sunrise. Smith saw in this scene a picture of the oldest form of sacrifice: a collective feast that unites the community and the god. Each participant partakes of the sacred animal and thereby renews the bond of kinship. The Islamic connection reaches its limit in the verse "It is neither their flesh nor their blood that reaches God; it is your piety that reaches Him" (al-Hajj: 37), which denies that sacrifice is food for the deity and transfers its meaning to piety. It also appears in the jurists' rulings on the sacrificial animal, which call for dividing the meat between the family and the poor, making the solidary function of the feast explicit.

## From Robertson Smith: Totemic Sacrifice and the Communal Feast

Smith put forward a central hypothesis: the oldest forms of sacrifice among the Semites were not gifts offered to a transcendent god. They were communal feasts in which the god and the community together ate the flesh of the sacred totemic animal, so that the kinship bond between the community and its god was renewed. This thesis established an influential method: the study of ritual as a social practice that creates solidarity, not as the expression of a belief that precedes it.

## Hubert and Mauss: Toward a General Theory of Sacrifice

Henri Hubert and Marcel Mauss, working in the circle around Durkheim's journal *L'Année sociologique*, developed this line sociologically in *Essai sur la nature et la fonction du sacrifice* (1899, in *L'Année Sociologique*), later translated as *Sacrifice: Its Nature and Function* (translated by W. D. Halls, 1964). They drew on Vedic and Hebrew texts. They proposed a procedural model of sacrifice as a three-stage process: entry, meaning the preparation of the sacrificer and the victim; the act of sacrifice, which connects the sacred and profane worlds through a mediator, the victim that is destroyed or consecrated; and exit from the state of sanctity. This three-stage structure is often compared with Arnold van Gennep's three-phase "rites of passage" (separation, margin and incorporation; 1909), and with Victor Turner's later concept of "liminality."

## Methodological Impact and Contemporary Extensions

The current represents a starting point for later anthropological studies that give priority to practice over belief. Durkheim drew on Smith's analysis of sacrifice for the sacred/profane dichotomy in *Les Formes élémentaires de la vie religieuse* (1912). In the contemporary generation, Catherine Bell, in *Ritual Theory, Ritual Practice* (Oxford University Press, 1992), developed a critique of the thought/action dichotomy presupposed by these early theories, proposing the concept of "ritualization" instead of treating ritual as a fixed, intrinsic entity. Roy Rappaport, in *Ritual and Religion in the Making of Humanity* (Cambridge University Press, 1999), after his fieldwork among the Tsembaga Maring of New Guinea, viewed ritual as a performance that publicly binds the participant to what is performed, linking the sacred to ultimate premises that admit no verification.

## Limits and Criticism

Smith's hypothesis about Semitic totemism did not survive ethnographic revision. E. E. Evans-Pritchard, in *Theories of Primitive Religion* (Clarendon Press, 1965), argued that Smith's reconstruction of Semitic totemism and sacrifice rested on slender and selectively read evidence. Smith is also criticised for relying, in the matter of the camel sacrifice, on Nilus's single account, whose representativeness of the Arabs of the peninsula is unknown. Hubert and Mauss are criticised for basing their model on priestly texts (Vedic and Biblical) that express a theoretical organisation rather than actual practice. The lasting value of their work is methodological: the priority of ritual over belief, and the linking of sacrifice to a social function, rather than ethnographic detail.
