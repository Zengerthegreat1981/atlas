# Anthropological Political Economy

A strand of Marxist-influenced anthropology and political economy associated above all with Eric Wolf, which links the study of local communities to the history of world capitalism and colonialism.

## Central Idea

This school rejects the assumption that the societies studied by classical anthropology (the "tribes" and local "cultures") were isolated, unchanging units without history. It insists instead that these societies had for centuries been embedded in worldwide networks of trade, migration, colonialism and capitalist expansion. Any correct understanding of a local society therefore requires placing it within its entangled global history, rather than isolating it in a presumed "ethnographic present" outside of time.

## Major Works and Representatives

- Wolf, *Europe and the People Without History* (1982): a rewriting of the history of world capitalism from a perspective that places non-European societies at the centre of the narrative.
- Wolf, *Peasant Wars of the Twentieth Century* (1969): a comparative study of the major peasant revolutions of the twentieth century.

## Historical Context

This school took shape within a broad American anthropological critique of the "ethnographic present" convention that prevailed in structural-functionalist studies, which described the societies under study apart from their actual history. Eric Wolf took part in Julian Steward's Puerto Rico project, later carried out research in Mexico, and gradually moved toward a broader Marxist synthesis that linked anthropology to world economic history, in dialogue with Marxist historiography and with world-system theory, which Immanuel Wallerstein formulated in the 1970s.

Alongside Eric Wolf, the school includes Sidney Mintz, who studied the history of sugar, slavery and capitalism in the Caribbean in his book *Sweetness and Power* (1985), showing how a single commodity, sugar, formed a worldwide network of enslavement and consumption linking Africa, the Americas and Europe. Wolf's work also intersected with Maurice Godelier and Claude Meillassoux in France, within what became known as "structural Marxism", which analysed pre-capitalist modes of production, drawing on Meillassoux's work among the Gouro of Côte d'Ivoire and Godelier's among the Baruya of New Guinea.

## Impact and Criticism

This school brought anthropology into dialogue with world-system theory (Immanuel Wallerstein) and world economic history, and established an anthropological tradition that refuses to study "cultures" apart from their global economic and political context. The approach has sometimes been criticised for its tendency to explain every local phenomenon by reference to a large global economic structure, which may weaken attention to specific local cultural meanings that cannot be fully reduced to relations of production and exchange.

## Key Concepts

Wolf distinguishes three main "modes of production" that have governed relations between societies throughout history: the kinship mode, in which economic relations are organised through ties of descent and kinship; the tributary mode, in which a ruling elite extracts economic surplus from direct producers through political or religious authority (as in the great agrarian empires); and the capitalist mode, in which producers are separated from the means of production and their labour is sold as a commodity in the market. Wolf argues that most of the societies studied by classical anthropology were not "primitive" in isolation from history, but were sites where these three modes intersected over centuries of trade, conquest and colonialism.

## Institutional Context and Later Influence

Wolf wrote his central work in the context of the Cold War crisis and the wars of liberation in the Third World. He studied in particular how European commercial capitalism, from the fifteenth century onward, reshaped the fur trade in North America, the African slave trade and sugar cultivation in the Caribbean, within a single interconnected world system rather than separate isolated communities. This view later influenced a generation of anthropologists who studied "development" and "globalisation" as an extension of this long colonial history rather than as an entirely new phenomenon.

## Relation to Neighbouring Fields

This approach intersects with development anthropology and postcolonial studies, offering a long-term historical framework for understanding how present-day economic disparities between the "North" and "South" of the world arose. They are not the result of natural differences in resources or culture, but the cumulative product of historical relations of exploitation extended over centuries of colonial trade and the international division of labour. This is why Wolf's work became a foundational reference in development studies and international political economy, even beyond the narrow field of anthropology.

The school's value today lies in its constant reminder that any anthropological analysis of a local community, however small or geographically isolated it may seem, remains incomplete unless it is placed within the wider network of economic and political relations that historically shaped it, whether through trade, colonialism, migration or war.
