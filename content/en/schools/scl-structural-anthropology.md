# Structural Anthropology

Structural anthropology is the approach founded by Claude Lévi-Strauss, which holds that beneath the variety of human cultures lie shared, unconscious structures of the mind. Where others asked what a custom meant or what function it served, Lévi-Strauss asked what underlying pattern it expressed. A kinship rule, a myth, or a food taboo mattered not in itself, but as an expression of a deeper logic — most often binary oppositions, such as raw and cooked, combined and transformed according to rules. The ambition was scientific: anthropology could discover laws of human thought as rigorous as those of linguistics, showing that all peoples think in the same fundamental way.

This separates it from the other schools of its time: British functionalism explained customs by the social needs they satisfied; cultural relativism stressed the historical particularity of each culture. Structural anthropology rejected both: uninterested in function and indifferent to history, it sought the timeless architecture of the mind. Its influence extended far beyond anthropology, making structuralism the dominant movement of post-war France.

## History

It emerged from Lévi-Strauss's encounter with structural linguistics. Trained in philosophy, he did fieldwork among the Nambikwara and Bororo peoples of Brazil in the 1930s, and in wartime New York he met the linguist Roman Jakobson, whose phonology showed that sounds acquire meaning only through their relations of difference to other sounds. Lévi-Strauss asked whether kinship, myth, and classification were also systems of differences governed by unconscious rules.

His first major book, The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949), applied this insight to marriage. Against the idea that kinship was a matter of blood ties, Lévi-Strauss argued that the incest taboo forces groups to exchange women, creating alliances between them. Kinship is thus a system of communication, and its elementary structures are the rules of exchange. Tristes Tropiques (1955) and Structural Anthropology (1958), the essays that gave the school its name and method, followed.

The 1960s were the school's high point. In 1959 Lévi-Strauss took the chair of social anthropology at the Collège de France. Totemism (1962) and The Savage Mind (1962) argued that so-called primitive thought was not pre-logical but a rigorous science of the concrete. The four volumes of the Mythologiques (1964–1971) applied structural analysis to hundreds of Amerindian myths, tracing how the same structures were transformed from one myth to another.

## Key Figures

Claude Lévi-Strauss (1908–2009) is the school's founder and overwhelmingly its central figure. Edmund Leach used structural analysis in Burma and Ceylon, though he remained more concerned with history and politics. Rodney Needham developed the structural analysis of kinship and classification and translated Lévi-Strauss for English-speaking readers. In France, Louis Dumont applied structural ideas to caste in India, and Maurice Godelier combined structural analysis with Marxism.

Outside anthropology, its allies were Roman Jakobson, whose phonology provided the model, and Roland Barthes, who applied structural analysis to fashion and food. Its most important interlocutors, however, were its critics: Jacques Derrida, whose 1966 lecture "Structure, Sign, and Play" challenged the idea of a fixed centre to any structure, and Pierre Bourdieu, whose Outline of a Theory of Practice (1972) argued that structuralism ignored practice, strategy, and power.

## Method

The method begins by collecting related phenomena — variants of a myth, marriage rules, food prohibitions — and searching for the relations between their elements rather than the elements themselves. The analyst looks for binary oppositions, inversions between variants, and mediating terms. The goal is a model: a formal statement of the rules that generate all the observed variants, tested against new cases. Meaning does not reside in any single element but in the system of differences as a whole, just as a phoneme means nothing alone but everything in contrast to other phonemes.

The method was deliberately formal and comparative. Lévi-Strauss worked not from a single field site but from the comparative literature — hundreds of ethnographies and myth collections — treating myth as a collective thought experiment, a way for societies to think through contradictions they could not resolve in practice. Its strength was revealing unexpected connections across vast distances; its weakness was distance from the lived experience of the people it analysed.

## Influence and Contemporary Debates

Structural anthropology reshaped twentieth-century thought. Within anthropology it transformed the study of kinship, myth, and classification. Beyond anthropology it supplied the model for structuralism in literary theory and philosophy, shaping the humanities for a generation. An important legacy is its argument that human thought is universal in structure even when diverse in content — an argument for cognitive equality against racist ideas of primitive mentality.

The critiques are as important as the achievements. The charge of ahistoricity is the most persistent: structural analysis froze cultures in a timeless present, ignoring colonialism and change. Feminists argued that its models of exchange treated women as objects passed between men. Bourdieu charged that it mistook the analyst's model for the actors' practice, and Derrida dissolved the idea of a stable structure, replacing it with play and difference. The school declined after the 1970s, but its questions — what is universal in human thought, and how can difference be analysed without hierarchy — still frame debate.
