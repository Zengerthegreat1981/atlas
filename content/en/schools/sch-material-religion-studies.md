# Material Religion Studies: David Morgan and the Turn from Belief to Objects

Material religion studies is a sub-field within comparative religious studies that arose from a methodological critique of an old bias in the discipline: the tendency to treat "religion" as essentially a system of doctrines and texts, while sacred objects, images and sensory rituals are reduced to secondary "manifestations" of belief. The American scholar David Morgan (see thk-david-morgan) proposed a methodological alternative: the study of religion begins from the material object itself (the sacred image, the statue, the prayer card, the architectural space), as a site where religious meanings are actually made, rather than a passive reflection of a belief that precedes it.

## From Religious Art to "Visual Culture"

Morgan began his project by studying popular American Protestant religious images (not the "high religious art" that art historians were accustomed to studying), developing the concept of "visual piety" to describe how the everyday practices of looking at a religious image (hanging it in the home, kissing it, carrying it) produce religiosity as genuinely as the professed doctrine does. His best-known case is Warner Sallman's *Head of Christ* (1940), reproduced in enormous numbers in American Protestant homes and churches, which owners often live with in an intimate daily relationship that shapes their religiosity as much as abstract doctrine does.

## Key Works and Extensions

Sally Promey extended this method by editing a collective volume entitled *Sensational Religion*, which examined how religious practice addresses all the senses rather than sight alone (touch, smell, taste), as channels for producing religious meaning. Birgit Meyer, the Dutch scholar specialising in Ghanaian Christianity, in her edited volume *Aesthetic Formations* developed the concept of "aesthetic formations" to analyse how sensory media (image, sound, dress) shape religious and political communities together, showing that the aesthetic, the political and the religious are structurally intertwined rather than separate.

The journal *Material Religion*, founded in 2005, became the field's main scholarly forum; Morgan is among its founding editors.

## Connection to the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The Islamic tradition offers rich cases for this analytical framework despite its historical reservations about figurative representation. The Qur'an itself as a material object (its calligraphy, its binding, the manner of carrying and kissing it, and not touching it except in a state of purity) is the subject of a material piety wholly parallel to what Morgan described for Christian images, despite the difference in content (a written text rather than a painted image). Likewise the prayer beads (*subha*), prayer rugs, and distinctive religious garments (such as the *jallabiya* or *abaya*) represent material objects that participate in producing everyday religious identity as much as the verbally professed belief does, and this has begun to be studied within a more specialised later sub-field, the study of religion and dress.

## Impact and Critique

This field has redirected religious studies away from the implicit Protestant bias that assumes the "true" essence of religion is inner faith stripped of any material mediation, a bias the Western academy inherited from its own theological history. Some critics, however, have warned of the risk of dissolving the difference between religion and general material culture if the concept of "material religion" is stretched too far, so that it becomes hard to distinguish a religious object from any ordinary consumer item that carries cultural symbolism.

## Additional Examples: Orthodox Icons and Hindu Images

The field also examines the case of icons in Orthodox Christianity, which are not treated as pictorial decoration but as a real window onto divine presence, kissed by worshippers and stood before for prayer, so that the icon itself carries a ritual sacredness rather than merely a didactic illustrative value. Similarly, statues of deities in Hinduism (*murti*), after the rite of "vitalising" (*prana pratishtha*), are treated as a real dwelling of divine presence rather than a mere symbolic representation, which explains the daily care rituals performed for them (feeding, dressing, bathing them) that may seem strange to someone who assumes the "true" religion is merely internal belief without material mediation.

## Critique of Protestant Bias in the History of the Field

Morgan shows that the roots of Western academic bias against material religion go back to the Protestant Reformation itself, which attacked the veneration of Catholic icons and statues as "idolatry" or "superstition", asserting instead the primacy of the read and spoken word (the Bible, preaching) over image and material object. This historically specific Protestant theological inheritance later seeped into the methodology of the secular Western humanities that studied religion, so that they inherited an implicit "Protestant" bias even in their study of other religions that do not share this attitude toward matter at all (such as Catholicism, Hinduism and Eastern Orthodoxy), which this field seeks to correct methodologically.
