# Early Confucianism

An ethical and political philosophical current that arose in China in the late Spring and Autumn period and the Warring States period (6th to 3rd centuries BCE). It places human relationships guided by ritual (li 禮) and moral self-cultivation at the center of individual happiness and social stability. Its founding is attributed to Confucius (Kong Fuzi, 551–479 BCE), with strong reservations about attributing everything in the *Analects* to him directly, since the text was assembled over generations after his death. China called it "Rujia", the school of the learned or of the ritualists.

## Context

Confucius lived in the state of Lu in an age when the feudal Zhou order had collapsed and the states were at war. His concern became reviving the virtues of the early ancestors, the kings and princes of Zhou, through the education of rulers and the teaching of the elite. He was an itinerant teacher who tried, without great success, to find a ruler who would apply his ideas, and in his last years he returned to teaching. His disciples passed on his words, and they were then recorded in the *Analects* (Lunyu) over the following two centuries. In the "Age of the Hundred Schools" Confucianism competed with Mohism (Mozi), Daoism, Legalism and the School of Names (the logicians), and was the closest of these schools to the ancient ethical and political heritage.

## Central Concepts

- **Li (禮)**: ritual, etiquette and custom. These are not superficial courtesies but a network of bodily and social practices that shape character before they shape outward behavior, from respect for parents to the ceremonies of sacrifice.
- **Ren (仁)**: compassionate humaneness, the comprehensive virtue, accompanied by rightness (yi), wisdom (zhi) and trustworthiness (xin). His answer on its meaning: "to love people."
- **Xiao (孝)**: filial piety toward parents and ancestors, the source of the other social virtues.
- **Junzi (君子)**: the morally superior person, not by birth but by cultivation.
- **The rectification of names (正名)**: each social role has its fitting conduct, and disorder begins when names do not match realities; he says: "Let the ruler be a ruler and the subject a subject."
- **Heaven (天 tian) and its mandate**: not a personal god in the Abrahamic sense but a cosmic moral order that grants and withdraws authority according to the ruler's virtue; this is the idea of the "Mandate of Heaven" that Confucianism inherited from the Zhou rulers.
- **Leadership by virtue**: the best government is by example (de), not by laws and punishments alone.

## Extensions Within the Early Period

- **Mencius (Mengzi, c. 372–289 BCE)**: human nature inclines toward goodness in the sense that it has four "sprouts": compassion, shame, modesty, and the discrimination of right from wrong; and it is a corrupt environment that corrupts it. He is the one who put forward the teaching of the five relationships and the people's right to remove an unjust ruler.
- **Xunzi (c. 310–235 BCE)**: human nature is at its origin inclined toward evil in the sense of selfishness and disorder, and through ritual and education a person becomes virtuous. He is considered the most naturalistic of the early Confucians in his interpretation of Heaven, which for him is a natural order that does not intervene in human affairs. Han Feizi and Li Si, leading figures of Legalism, studied under him.
- **The classical texts**: the "Five Classics" (Wu Jing): the Book of Songs, the Book of Documents, the Book of Changes (Yi Jing), the Book of Rites, and the Spring and Autumn Annals. In 136 BCE Emperor Wu of Han gave them the post of "Erudites" in the imperial academy, and they became the core of official education. As for the "Four Books" (Si Shu), Zhu Xi selected them in the 12th century CE.
- **Archaeological discoveries**: at Guodian (1993), manuscripts on bamboo slips dating to about 300 BCE were found, containing early Confucian texts on nature and the emotions, such as "Nature Comes from Mandate", and they have reopened the question of the history of the transition from Confucius to Mencius.

## Position Among the Schools

Mozi (c. 470–391 BCE) criticized the Confucians for spending on rituals and lavish funerals and for distinguishing between kin and strangers, and he called for "impartial care" and the common benefit. The Daoists, among them Zhuangzi, attacked rigid moral standards. The Legalists said punishment and reward are more useful than virtue in governing the state. The distinction of Confucianism is that it did not offer a complete cosmic system but built its philosophy on living relationships: the family, kinship, authority and ritual. This made it adaptable to later transformations, but also exposed it to being enlisted by authority.

## Influence and Relation to Arabic

Under the Han, Confucianism became the ideology of the state and was renewed in the Song period with Zhu Xi. The Chinese Muslims of the "Han Kitab" in the 17th and 18th centuries, among them Wang Daiyu and Liu Zhi, undertook an early and rich attempt at a dialogue of Islam with Neo-Confucianism, studied by Sachiko Murata in *Chinese Gleams of Sufi Light* (2000). Confucius's ideas became known in modern Arabic literature through translation and Orientalism.

## Limits and Criticism

- There is no text written by Confucius himself, and the layers of the *Analects* differ, so caution is needed in attributing statements to him.
- It has been said that the emphasis on hierarchical roles reinforces patriarchy and subordinates women and subjects, a criticism made of it by contemporary feminist scholars.
- The Daoists and the Legalists faulted it for exalting ritual while neglecting nature and law.
- The concept of "human nature" remains a point of dispute between Mencius and Xunzi that the tradition did not settle.
