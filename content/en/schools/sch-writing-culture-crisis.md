# Writing Culture & the Crisis of Representation

A critical, reflexive current within American anthropology that crystallized around the book *Writing Culture* (1986), edited by James Clifford and George E. Marcus. It examines the ethnographic text itself as a rhetorical and literary construction rather than a transparent reflection of reality.

## Central Idea

This current argues that classical ethnography (as in the work of Malinowski and Evans-Pritchard) relied on particular writing strategies (the third-person voice, descriptive certainty, and the silencing of individual informants' voices) to create an impression of full scientific objectivity. These same strategies, however, are rhetorical choices among other possible ones. If the ethnographic text is "made" in this sense, the question of the "crisis of representation" presses itself: how can one continue to claim reliable representation of another culture?

## Proposed Alternatives

The leading figures of this current proposed alternative writing strategies. Polyphonic texts highlight the voices of the actual informants rather than the researcher's voice alone. Dialogism presents the process of producing ethnographic knowledge as a dialogue between researcher and subject, rather than as a finished result. Explicit acknowledgment of the researcher's own position, and of its influence on what they write, is termed "reflexivity."

## Historical Context

The current grew out of a workshop held at the School of American Research in Santa Fe in 1984, which brought together anthropologists and scholars of comparative literature. The workshop produced the collective volume *Writing Culture* (1986), edited by James Clifford and George E. Marcus. The current emerged in a broader intellectual context shaped by French deconstruction (Jacques Derrida) and post-structuralist literary theory, and by an earlier direct critique by Edward Said in *Orientalism* (1978) of the relationship between Western knowledge and colonial power. The authors of "Writing Culture" applied that critique to anthropology itself as one of the instruments of this knowledge.

## Key Works and Figures

Alongside the founding collective volume, James Clifford made a parallel theoretical contribution in *The Predicament of Culture: Twentieth-Century Ethnography, Literature, and Art* (1988), in which he examined collecting and museum display as forms of "representation" of other cultures that are subject to the same critical problems. The anthropologist Renato Rosaldo applied a parallel critique in *Culture and Truth* (1989), focusing on the way the traditional ethnographic text excludes the researcher's personal emotions and subjective stances.

## Impact and Critique

This current brought about a comprehensive "literary turn" in American anthropology and pushed later generations of researchers to write with greater awareness of their subjective position and authorial power. It was later criticized from within the field itself, chiefly for an excessive preoccupation with analyzing the text and its rhetoric at the expense of actual fieldwork and concrete questions of social justice. It was also criticized for an excessive epistemic relativism: if every ethnographic text is considered merely a rhetorical construction among other possible alternatives, with no criterion for preferring one over another, then any reliable comparative claim between cultures becomes difficult to sustain.

## Echoes of the Crisis in Feminist Anthropology

Feminist anthropologists engaged critically with "Writing Culture." They observed that the founding volume included contributions almost entirely by men, even though feminist anthropologists had been applying similar principles of reflexivity and experimental writing for years, as in Lila Abu-Lughod's later work *Writing Women's Worlds* (1993). This led the field to acknowledge that the "crisis of representation" was in part a crisis of who holds the power to frame the theoretical question in the first place.

## Geertz and the Analysis of Authorial Style

Although Clifford Geertz did not participate directly in the "Writing Culture" volume, he made a parallel and influential contribution in *Works and Lives: The Anthropologist as Author* (1988). In it he analyzed the distinctive literary style of four classical anthropologists (Lévi-Strauss, Evans-Pritchard, Malinowski, and Ruth Benedict), showing how each built his or her scientific authority through markedly different stylistic choices. This reinforced the broader "Writing Culture" thesis about the ethnographic text as a rhetorical construction.

## Application Beyond Anthropology

The debate over the "crisis of representation" later spread to neighboring fields such as oral history, investigative journalism, and media studies, wherever the researcher faces a similar question: how to convey the voice of those they interview without undeclared substitution of their own voice for theirs. This is what made "Writing Culture" a methodological reference invoked far beyond the narrow boundaries of anthropology.
