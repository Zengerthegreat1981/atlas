# Berger & Luckmann's Social Constructionism

The approach set out by Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann in the sociology of knowledge, a major reference point for later "social constructionist" theory across the social sciences.

## The Central Idea

In *The Social Construction of Reality* (1966), Berger and Luckmann argued that social "reality," although we feel it to be a solid objective given external to us, is in essence a human product, made through a continuous three-part dialectical process. In "externalization," humans produce a social world through their repeated actions (habits, institutions). In "objectivation," this human product acquires the quality of objectivity and apparent independence from its makers, so that to later generations it appears as a solid external given and not a human creation. In "internalization," the individual (especially through primary socialization in childhood) learns this "objective" reality and internalizes it as part of his own subjective identity, and in turn reproduces it without awareness of its man-made origin. This three-part cycle explains how social institutions (such as religion, law, gender roles) appear "natural" and fixed even though they are purely historical human constructions that are, in principle, changeable.

## Intellectual Context: Schutz and the Sociology of Knowledge

Berger and Luckmann wrote their work in the context of an attempt to redirect the "sociology of knowledge" associated with Max Scheler and Karl Mannheim away from its confinement to the analysis of ideologies and "grand" (philosophical and political) ideas, toward the study of everyday "common-sense knowledge" by which ordinary people live their lives without theoretical reflection. Methodologically they drew on the social phenomenology of Alfred Schutz, who drew on Edmund Husserl's phenomenology, emigrated to America and developed a careful analysis of how the everyday "life-world" (Lebenswelt) and its subjective self-evidence are formed for social actors. Luckmann himself was a direct student of Schutz, and after Schutz's death he completed his unfinished work, published as *The Structures of the Life-World* (Schutz and Luckmann; German 1975 and 1984, English translation 1973 and 1989), which explains the depth of the phenomenological foundation of their joint book.

## The Three-Part Elaboration: Externalization, Objectivation and Internalization

Each term of the three-part dialectic deserves elaboration. In the phase of "externalization," repeated human activity produces "habits" (Habitualization) that gradually turn into "institutionalization" when a whole group, not one individual, shares them, so that they become expected reciprocal patterns of behavior between categories of actors (such as occupational or family roles). In the phase of "objectivation," these institutions acquire increasing layers of "legitimation" (Legitimation), from simple linguistic justification to comprehensive "symbolic universes" (Symbolic Universes), such as religions and the great ideologies, which give institutions an integrated cosmic meaning that goes beyond direct practical explanation. In the phase of "internalization," "primary socialization" (in childhood through the family) and "secondary" socialization (later, through specialized institutions such as school and work) play a decisive role in anchoring this objective reality in the individual's subjective consciousness until it becomes part of his personal identity and not merely information external to him.

## Impact and Critique

This work became a standard reference text for "social constructionism" in contemporary social science, influencing many fields from the sociology of knowledge to gender, identity and religion studies. It is often placed alongside, though intellectually distinct from, Michel Foucault's analyses of discourse and power. Nevertheless, the work was criticized from two opposing directions. On the one hand, Marxist critics saw that the analysis neglects structures of power and material class conflict and settles for a relatively abstract cognitive-cultural analysis. On the other hand, some advocates of critical realism saw that the exclusive focus on the social "construction" of reality could be wrongly understood as denying the existence of real objective constraints (biological, material, environmental) that go beyond mere social agreement on naming and interpreting them.

## Major Works

*The Social Construction of Reality* (1966) remains the founding reference work, but Berger developed closely related ideas in *The Sacred Canopy* (1967) on the function of religion in particular as the most powerful "symbolic universe" by which human society has legitimated its social order throughout history, by linking it to the cosmic and the absolute. Luckmann, for his part, presented in *The Invisible Religion* (1967; German original 1963) an analysis of how the functional need for transcendent "legitimation" continues even in modern secular societies, through religious forms less institutionally organized than the traditional church.

## Concluding Methodological Note

Part of the strength of this framework lies in its avoiding a slide toward either a purely individual psychological explanation or a purely deterministic structural one. It insists that humans make their institutions themselves through history, but that these institutions, once they are objectivated and internalized across successive generations, acquire a real coercive power over the later individual, no less than that of any material natural fact in its everyday practical effect.

## Additional Remark

It is worth noting that Berger, despite his fame as one of the fathers of social constructionism, later distinguished this analytical framework from any extreme epistemic relativism that denies any real objective limits to social construction. He held that the aim of the analysis is to reveal the mechanism by which social reality is made, not to deny its concrete practical reality for those who live it.
