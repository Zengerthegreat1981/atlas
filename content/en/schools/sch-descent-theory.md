# Descent Theory (Anthropology of Kinship and Descent)

A branch of British structural functionalism developed by E. E. Evans-Pritchard and Meyer Fortes. It specializes in analyzing how kinship systems organize political and social life in societies without a centralized state.

## Central Idea

This school studies "lineage groups": groups of relatives united by real or presumed descent from a common ancestor, usually unilineal (patrilineal or matrilineal). It examines how these groups expand and divide into subgroups ("segmentary lineage system"), so that closely related branches ally against more distant branches when needed. In this way they organize politics, war and peace, and the inheritance of property without the need for a centralized authority or state.

## Additional Concepts: Lineage Unity and Bilateral Descent

Theorists of this school developed further distinctions. One is between single unilineal descent (purely patrilineal or purely matrilineal), "double descent" (in which different rights pass through the father's and the mother's lines, as Daryll Forde described among the Yakö), and "bilateral" kinship, which recognizes ties to both parents without forming descent groups. They also studied how these systems handle exceptional cases such as adoption or the absence of male heirs, through flexible customary mechanisms (such as adoption or the Nuer practice of "ghost marriage," in which a woman is married in the name of a deceased kinsman). These mechanisms preserve the continuity of the lineage group without breaking its basic formal structure.

## Key Works

- Evans-Pritchard, *The Nuer* (1940): a classic description of the segmentary system among the Nuer people of southern Sudan.
- Fortes and Evans-Pritchard (eds.), *African Political Systems* (1940): a systematic comparison of African political organization systems, from centralized kingdoms to stateless societies.
- Fortes, *The Web of Kinship among the Tallensi* (1949): an in-depth study of the Tallensi people of Ghana.

## Criticism

Descent theory was later criticized by the Manchester School (Max Gluckman and his students), which accused it of overstating static structural harmony and neglecting actual conflict, individual maneuvering, and historical change within these societies.

## The Mechanism of Segmentary Balance

Evans-Pritchard explains how the segmentary system works through the principle of "complementary opposition." Two branches of the same tribe may oppose each other as rivals when there is no external threat, but they unite immediately as one body against a more distantly related branch when a common threat appears. The Nuer as a whole may unite against another people, such as the Dinka, when the need is greatest. This dynamic and flexible balance allows a degree of political and defensive order to be maintained without a permanent king or central ruler. The "tribe" expands and contracts according to the level of external threat, with striking organizational flexibility.

## The Colonial Context of the Field

Evans-Pritchard carried out his Nuer fieldwork in the 1930s under the auspices of the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan government. This context later raised critical questions about the relationship between this kind of detailed anthropological knowledge and colonial interests, particularly in understanding local power structures in order to govern them more efficiently. This does not negate the immense descriptive scientific value of his ethnographic work itself.

## Meyer Fortes and the Analysis of the Tallensi

In his study of the Tallensi people in Ghana, Meyer Fortes offered an important complementary model, focusing on the temporal dimension of descent. He showed how an individual's authority and position within the lineage group change over the full course of their life, from childhood to old age. Sons gradually gain authority over the affairs of the group as their fathers grow older and then die, in a renewing cyclical pattern that maintains the continuity of the lineage across generations, even as the living individuals who make it up constantly change.

## Defining the Lineage Group

This approach draws a careful distinction between "genealogical descent," which can actually be traced generation by generation, and "presumed" or mythical descent, which links an entire group to a distant first ancestor whose actual historical relationship to his supposed descendants cannot be verified. Presumed descent nonetheless remains socially and politically effective, as long as it provides a recognized framework for organizing alliances and reciprocal obligations among the different branches of the group.

## Relevance to Arab Kinship Systems

This framework provides a useful comparative tool for understanding traditional Arab tribal kinship systems based on patrilineal descent. The mechanisms of alliance and segmentary division among the clans and lineages of Arab tribes resemble the model Evans-Pritchard described among the Nuer, despite the complete difference in geographic and historical context. Later anthropological studies of tribes in the Arabian Peninsula and the Levant drew on this comparison.

These comparative case studies later provided essential material for training generations of anthropology students in the precise structural analysis of kinship systems, even after the school itself declined as the dominant theoretical framework in favor of approaches more concerned with individual agency and history.

This school remains one of the purest examples of the capacity of British functionalist anthropology to dismantle the assumption that a stable political system necessarily requires a central state or a unified coercive authority. It offers an alternative model of social order that arises from the structure of kinship itself, rather than being imposed from above.
