# Intensive Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy (ISTDP)

A dynamic therapeutic approach developed by the Iranian-born Canadian psychiatrist Habib Davanloo (1927-2023) at Montreal General Hospital and McGill University from the 1960s and 1970s, with major publications appearing from 1978 onward. It rests on the idea that identifying and working through **defenses against feeling**, so that the patient experiences previously avoided emotion and anxiety in the session, can bring about change in far fewer sessions than classical psychoanalysis. It is classed among the short-term dynamic psychotherapies, with a theoretical and technical identity of its own.

## Historical Context

Davanloo began developing the method in the psychiatry department of Montreal General Hospital, working with patients whose problems had not responded to other treatment. He recorded sessions on video and used the recordings to study how patients resist emotional closeness with the therapist and how those resistances can be addressed in real time rather than interpreted slowly over years. His main books are *Basic Principles and Techniques in Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy* (1978), *Short-Term Dynamic Psychotherapy* (1980), and *Unlocking the Unconscious: Selected Papers of Habib Davanloo* (1990). The approach spread through training programs and societies in North America, Europe, and elsewhere, and later authors such as Allan Abbass have written accessible accounts of it.

## Core Theoretical Principles

### The Triangles
ISTDP uses the two triangles familiar from the short-term dynamic literature (notably David Malan): the **triangle of conflict** (feeling, anxiety, and defense) and the **triangle of person** (current relationships, past relationships, and the relationship with the therapist). The therapist works to help the patient notice the defenses that block feeling, to experience the feelings and anxiety they avoid, and to link these to current and earlier relationships.

### Anxiety and Its Pathways
Davanloo described how anxiety is discharged through different channels (for example striated muscle tension, smooth muscle symptoms, or cognitive-perceptual disruption), which clinicians use to judge how much anxiety a patient can tolerate and whether they can work with feeling directly. The capacity to tolerate anxiety without losing contact with the therapist is regarded as central to patient suitability.

### Defenses and Resistance
Defenses and resistance are the **main subject of the therapeutic work**. They are grouped by function, for example tactical defenses (such as vagueness, intellectualization, or changing the subject) and the more characterological resistance against emotional closeness, and the therapist addresses them actively and early.

### The Central Dynamic Sequence
In the course of an interview the therapist typically moves from inquiry, through **pressure** to feel and **challenge** to the defense, to the rise of resistance in the transference and, if this succeeds, to a **breakthrough** of mixed feelings toward important people (often rage, guilt, and grief) and then to exploration of the patient's history. Davanloo called the resulting access to previously avoided material "unlocking of the unconscious." The initial extended interview, called **trial therapy**, also serves to assess how the patient responds.

### Superego Resistance
Davanloo also described how a harsh, self-punishing conscience can sustain symptoms and shape the patient's response to the therapist, and he treated work on this superego resistance as a distinct step in the approach.

## Clinical Technique

The therapist uses **active, systematic inquiry and confrontation**: pointing to contradictions, drawing attention to what the patient is doing in the session at this moment, and highlighting the function of the defense, while maintaining the therapeutic alliance. The approach requires specialized training and supervision, often with review of recorded sessions, and sessions, especially the initial ones, are often extended. Treatment is described as brief, typically a small number of sessions to a few dozen depending on the patient.

## Evidence Base

Evidence comes mainly from open trials, cohort studies, and a small number of randomized trials. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Abbass, Town, and Driessen (2012) reported positive outcomes across a range of conditions, including depression, anxiety, personality disorders, and somatic symptom problems, but it drew on studies of varying quality, many with small samples and some by the approach's own proponents. The atlas therefore rates the evidence at the "probably efficacious" level. A recurring criticism is that a treatment depending heavily on the therapist's skill is difficult to standardize and test in controlled trials.

## Relation to Neighboring Schools

ISTDP overlaps with classical psychoanalysis in its framework of unconscious conflict and transference but departs from it in duration and style: active confrontation instead of neutrality, and a deliberate focus on a limited number of problems. It belongs to the family of brief dynamic therapies that includes the work of Malan, Sifneos, and Mann. It differs from transference-focused psychotherapy (TFP, Kernberg), which is typically long-term. Superficial parallels exist with cognitive-behavioral approaches that address avoidance, but ISTDP aims at experiencing avoided feelings and understanding their roots rather than modifying beliefs directly.

## Critique and Limits

- **Individual skill**: heavy dependence on the therapist makes it hard to standardize the protocol and to measure outcomes.
- **Selective suitability**: patients with severe impairment of reality testing or very low anxiety tolerance are generally not considered suitable for the more intensive technique, which is adapted for them.
- **Clinical ethics**: an active confrontational style raises ethical questions when applied to fragile patients without adequate training.
- **Sustainability**: access to well-trained therapists is limited.
