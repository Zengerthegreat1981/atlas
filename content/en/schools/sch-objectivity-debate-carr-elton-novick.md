# The Objectivity Question: Carr, Elton, Novick

A major debate in modern historiography and the critical philosophy of history addressing an essential epistemological dilemma: **Can the historian reconstruct the past objectively "as it actually happened," or is every historical narrative inevitably conditioned by the subjectivity, social position, and present concerns of the historian?** Spanning the mid-twentieth century through the onset of the linguistic turn, this controversy engaged empirical defenders of professional craftsmanship, moderate constructionists, and intellectual historians of the discipline.

## Edward Hallett Carr: History as a Dialogue Between Present and Past

In his influential 1961 Trevelyan Lectures at the University of Cambridge, published as *What Is History?*, the British diplomatic historian **E. H. Carr** (1892–1982) mounted a trenchant attack on the nineteenth-century positivist belief in autonomous, self-interpreting historical facts. Carr satirized the naive empiricist view that facts exist independently like "fish on a fishmonger's slab," waiting for the historian to collect them. Instead, he argued that facts are inert until the historian actively selects, verifies, and contextualizes them according to specific questions generated by contemporary concerns.

Carr formulated his central dictum: "Before you study the history, study the historian." He defined historical inquiry as "a continuous process of interaction between the historian and his facts, an unending dialogue between the present and the past." While rejecting naive positivism, Carr expressly distanced himself from radical epistemological skepticism. He insisted that historical interpretations can be rationally evaluated, arguing that historical objectivity is not a passive detachment from values, but the capacity of the historian to transcend immediate personal bias and perceive how the past projects into the future.

## Geoffrey Elton: Defending the Autonomous Empirical Craft

In *The Practice of History* (1967), the prominent Tudor historian **Geoffrey Elton** (1921–1994) mounted an uncompromising defense of traditional empirical method against Carr's sociological approach. Elton argued that the historical past has an objective reality independent of the historian's consciousness, preserved in primary documentary records.

For Elton, rigorous archival discipline—training the historian to ask what the evidence says rather than imposing modern sociological agendas—enables the researcher to establish verifiable historical truth. Elton warned that Carr's emphasis on the subjective present threatened to slide into intellectual nihilism, erasing the vital boundary between rigorous historical scholarship and political propaganda or imaginative fiction.

## Peter Novick: Historicizing the "Noble Dream"

In his landmark study *That Noble Dream: The "Objectivity Question" and the American Historical Profession* (1988), the American historian **Peter Novick** shifted the debate from philosophical prescription to historical analysis. Novick analyzed the concept of objectivity as the foundational "normative myth" that unified the American historical profession from its founding in the late nineteenth century.

Tracing the ideal through a century of political and intellectual upheavals, Novick argued that "objectivity" was repeatedly compromised by external pressures:
- National mobilization during World War I and the Cold War compelled historians into ideological conformism.
- The social upheavals of the 1960s, the emergence of the New Left, Black history, and feminist historiography exposed how the traditional "objective" consensus had marginalized non-elite perspectives.
- The arrival of the linguistic turn, deconstruction, and cognitive relativism in the 1970s and 1980s shattered the profession's epistemic confidence, leaving the historical community divided into mutually skeptical interpretive enclaves without a shared consensus.

## Methodological Legacy

The Carr-Elton-Novick debate remains a standard reference point for historical methodology. Later syntheses, such as Richard J. Evans's *In Defence of History* (1997), sought a viable middle path: affirming that while historians cannot escape their subjectivity, rigorous adherence to primary source verification, evidentiary rules, and peer scrutiny distinguishes legitimate historical knowledge from arbitrary fiction.
