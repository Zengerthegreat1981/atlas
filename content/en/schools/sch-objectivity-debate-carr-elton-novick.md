# The Objectivity Question: Carr, Elton, Novick

A central debate in the philosophy of history on a question that seems simple but has deep consequences: **Can the historian be truly "objective" in describing the past, or is every historical writing inevitably seen from the standpoint of the present?**

## Edward Hallett Carr: History as a Dialogue Between Present and Past

In his well-known lectures *What Is History?* (1961), the British historian **E. H. Carr** sharply attacks the naive Rankean illusion of absolute, neutral objectivity. He offers his famous definition: history is "an unending dialogue between present and past." The historian selects the facts he deals with and organizes them through questions posed by the interests of his own present, not from a perfectly neutral standpoint free of his intellectual and social position. However, Carr does not move to absolute relativism (unlike some later readings of Hayden White). He insists that some interpretations are better than others by standards that can be rationally defended, and that not every narrative is as good as another.

## Geoffrey Elton: Defending the Empirical Craft

The Tudor historian **Geoffrey Elton** responds in *The Practice of History* (1967) with a forceful defence of the empirical Rankean tradition. He argues that Carr's exaggeration of the relativity of the historian's selection undermines the possibility of any reliable historical knowledge at all. Elton insists that rigorous methodological discipline in dealing with primary evidence makes it possible to reach conclusions that can be defended objectively in the traditional empirical professional sense, even if they are not absolute certainty.

## Peter Novick: Dismantling the "Noble Dream"

The American historian **Peter Novick**, in *That Noble Dream: The "Objectivity Question" and the American Historical Profession* (1988), offers a critical history of the development of the idea of "objectivity" itself within the American historical profession over a full century. He shows how it changed from a firmly held doctrine (influenced by a simplified reading of Ranke's motto) into a source of growing doubt with the rise of the linguistic turn and critical theory. Novick does not settle the debate in favour of either side. Instead, he documents the historicity of the question itself.

## Methodological Importance

This debate marks a live point of tension between the legacy of the Rankean school (`sch-rankean-historical-positivism`) and the challenges of the linguistic turn and critical philosophy of history. Can one reconcile the recognition that every historian writes from a specific historical position with the maintenance of methodological standards that distinguish serious historical research from propaganda or fiction? This question remains open in contemporary philosophy of history.
