# Moral Relativism

## Definition

Moral relativism is the thesis that the rightness or wrongness of a moral judgment is not absolute and universal, but relative to a particular framework (a culture, a society, an individual, or a historical era). On this view the same act may be right in one culture and wrong in another without contradiction, because the standard of rightness itself changes from one framework to another. Relativism divides into types that must be carefully distinguished: descriptive relativism, an anthropological observation that societies do in fact differ in their moral standards, which raises no major philosophical controversy; meta-ethical relativism, which holds that there is no objective standard above all cultures by which to judge which of them is correct; and normative relativism, which draws a practical conclusion: that one should not judge the practices of another culture by the standards of one's own culture (cultural tolerance).

## Classical Roots and Early Islamic Debate

The earliest philosophical expression of relativism goes back to the Greek sophist Protagoras (5th century BCE) in his saying "Man is the measure of all things: of what is, that it is; of what is not, that it is not." The statement was traditionally interpreted as a declaration of both epistemic and moral relativism, and it led Plato to attack it fiercely in the dialogue *Theaetetus*, on the grounds that it refutes itself: if every opinion is relatively true, then the opinion of someone who rejects relativism is also relatively true, so relativism contradicts its own universal claim.

In the Islamic tradition, relativism in the explicit modern sense did not appear. The roots of the debate over the objectivity of moral value, however, emerged in the dispute between the Mu'tazila and the Ash'ari over good and evil (*al-husn wa-l-qubh*). The Mu'tazila held that moral values are objective by reason: injustice is evil in itself, regardless of revelation or culture, a realist position opposed to relativism. The Ash'ari held that good and evil are known only through revelation, and that had God commanded lying, lying would become good. This position is known as Divine Command Theory. It is closer to a relativism conditioned by the divine will than by human culture, but it shares with relativism the denial of an independent, rational, objective standard prior to the commanding authority. Al-Ghazali weighed the two positions. He is generally read as rejecting the independence of reason from revelation in determining reward and punishment in the afterlife, while allowing that reason recognizes worldly benefit and harm (a theme later developed in the theory of the *maqasid*), thus leaving room for a partial objectivity.

## Modern Relativism: Anthropology and Analytic Philosophy

In the twentieth century, cultural anthropology gave relativism new momentum, especially through the work of Franz Boas and his students, notably Ruth Benedict in her book *Patterns of Culture* (1934). Benedict documented the vast variation among moral practices across cultures (such as polygamy, mortuary rituals, and standards of honor), suggesting that what a society calls "morality" is largely its socially approved custom, and rejecting the idea of a universal standard above all cultures. The philosopher James Rachels, however, noted in his influential chapter "The Challenge of Cultural Relativism" (*The Elements of Moral Philosophy*, 1986) that this philosophical conclusion (normative relativism) does not logically follow from the anthropological observation (descriptive relativism). The actual difference between cultures does not prove that there is no single correct standard that some people are ignorant of or misapply, just as the historical disagreement of societies about their astronomical beliefs does not imply the relativity of astronomical truth.

The philosopher Gilbert Harman developed a more philosophically refined version, first in "Moral Relativism Defended" (1975), drawing on an analogy with relative physical motion. Just as "speed" has no meaning except relative to a frame of reference, moral "ought" judgments are implicitly relative to implicit conventions among parties who share a history of interaction and mutual understanding. So when we say that the act of a person from a very distant culture is "wrong," we are using the language loosely, because that person does not share the same implicit convention as we do.

## Major Objections

The most famous objection is the "problem of condemnation." If relativism is correct, it becomes philosophically impossible to condemn practices that are regarded as monstrous by a near-universal consensus (such as the Nazi genocide or the slave trade), so long as their perpetrators act according to the standards of their own culture. Most moral philosophers, including many liberals sympathetic to cultural tolerance, regard this conclusion as morally unacceptable in itself. Some relativists reply that this is not a logical objection but an emotional reaction to an uncomfortable result, and that the difficulty of accepting a philosophical conclusion does not make it false.

A second objection is "self-contradiction." When the relativist says "we ought to tolerate the cultures of others," he uses "ought" in an absolute, universal sense; he does not say that tolerance is merely relatively required for the culture of those who believe in tolerance. The call for tolerance thus becomes an absolute moral standard that contradicts the original relativist thesis. A third objection, pressed by Rachels and others, is that there are indeed values that appear nearly universal across all the cultures studied by anthropology, such as the prohibition of killing arbitrary members of one's own group and the care of children. The anthropologist Donald Brown identified these in his study of "human universals," which weakens the original empirical claim of absolute moral variation with no shared common ground.

## Applied Example: Female Genital Cutting in International Human Rights Debate

The debate over female genital cutting (also called female genital mutilation) in some African and other societies illustrates the practical difficulty of relativism. Some anthropologists argue that condemnation of this practice by Western human rights organizations is a form of "moral imperialism" that imposes Western standards on other cultures. Critics of this relativist stance, including activists from the affected societies such as the Somali-born Ayaan Hirsi Ali, argue that the physical and medical harm, which is medically documented, provides an objective basis (not merely an imposed cultural judgment) for condemning this practice worldwide. They rely on a standard of "harm" that goes beyond the boundaries of any single culture. This reflects the sharp practical tension between respect for cultural pluralism and the rejection of practices that violate what appear to be basic, nearly universal rights.

## Contemporary Impact and Application

Moral relativism remains strongly present in international human rights debates, notably in the discussion of the "universality" of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights versus the cultural particularism that some states invoke. It is also present in the philosophy of anthropology and in the teaching of applied ethics across cultures. In these areas, the fine distinction between respecting legitimate cultural diversity and abandoning all universal critical standards remains one of the most difficult moral dilemmas of the present day.
