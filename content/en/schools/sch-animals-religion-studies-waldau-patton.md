# The Academic Study of Animals and Religion: Paul Waldau and Kimberley Patton

A relatively recent academic branch that arose at the intersection of Animal Studies (Human-Animal Studies) with the comparative study of religion. It examines how different religious traditions conceive the place of non-human animals, morally, ritually and symbolically, without adopting a normative theological or animal-rights position on the validity of any conclusion. Paul Waldau and Kimberley Patton are among the scholars who helped establish the field academically. Waldau specializes in ethics and animal studies; Patton is a professor of the history of religions at Harvard Divinity School. Together they edited a foundational reference volume for the field.

## Animals in the Arab-Islamic Heritage

The Arab-Islamic heritage made an early contribution that predates this academic field by centuries. The Qur'an contains references to beasts and birds as "communities like your own" (al-An'am 38), to the bee to which "your Lord has revealed" (al-Nahl 68), and to the glorification of creatures. The authentic hadith tells of a woman who entered Hell because of a cat she imprisoned, and of a man forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog. In jurisprudence the chapter on ritual slaughter (dhakah) and the conditions of kindness to the animal slaughtered arose, and the torture of animals and the baiting of animals against one another were forbidden. In literature and thought, al-Jahiz wrote *Kitab al-Hayawan* (the ninth century CE), which combined natural observation, literary anecdote and theological debate about animal perception and guidance. Al-Damiri wrote *Hayat al-Hayawan al-Kubra* (completed 773 AH / 1372 CE), a dictionary that gathers legal rulings, properties and proverbs. In the tenth century, the Brethren of Purity (Ikhwan al-Safa') included in their epistles a dialogue titled "The Animals Complain against Man before the King of the Jinn," in which animals protest human tyranny before the king of the jinn, in a dramatic pleading about the claim of human supremacy. Richard Foltz, in *Animals in Islamic Tradition and Muslim Cultures* (2006), offers a modern academic reading of this material. This material contains no animal rights in the modern sense, but it provides an ethical basis for compassion and a conception of the animal as a creature of value before God. Comparative studies often draw on it.

## The Founding of the Field and Its Methodological Axis

Before the turn of the millennium, the treatment of "the animal" in religious studies was usually marginal or implicit. It was mentioned as a symbol, a sacrifice or a subject of dietary jurisprudence, without constituting a subject of specialization in its own right. Walter Burkert's work on sacrifice in *Homo Necans* (1972; English 1983) and René Girard's reflections on violence and sacrifice had linked the animal to the origin of ritual, but both treated it as a means rather than as a subject. Waldau's *The Specter of Speciesism* (2002) changed this. It applied the concept of "speciesism," borrowed from moral philosophy (coined by Richard Ryder in 1970 and popularized by Peter Singer in *Animal Liberation*, 1975), to a critical comparative reading of Buddhist and Christian conceptions of the animal. It examined how traditions vary in the extent to which they widen the circle of moral consideration beyond the human. Then came the joint work with Patton, *A Communion of Subjects: Animals in Religion, Science, and Ethics* (2006), which gathered dozens of scholars from multiple disciplines (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism and indigenous religions) into a single volume, establishing the field as an interdisciplinary specialization in its own right. Waldau later published *Animal Studies: An Introduction* (2013).

## The Methodological Constraint: Description, Not Rights Advocacy

This branch adheres to academic description rather than advocacy. Its subject is how religious traditions conceived the animal and why. It does not defend animal rights as an independent normative position, although the editors have interests in animal ethics outside this academic work. The distinction between the descriptive and the normative is necessarily sharp here, because the field was born from an intersection with a normative philosophical field, animal ethics. The theologian Andrew Linzey wrote *Animal Theology* (1994) from an explicitly normative position, and it is an example of the boundary that separates religious inquiry from descriptive academic study.

## The Expansion of the Field: Gross, Ambros and Hobgood-Oster

The field has expanded in several directions. In *The Question of the Animal and Religion* (2015), Aaron Gross applies the question of the animal's status to a specific case: Jewish ritual slaughter (kashrut) confronted with the modern food industry. This connects the field to the study of religion and food. Barbara Ambros, in *Bones of Contention: Animals and Religion in Contemporary Japan* (2012), examines the rites and memorials Japanese Buddhist institutions provide for animals, including pets, adding an ethnographic-historical approach tied to a specific cultural context. Laura Hobgood-Oster, in *Holy Dogs and Asses: Animals in the Christian Tradition* (2008), applies a feminist-historical perspective to the Christian tradition, highlighting the presence of the animal in saints' lives, art and ritual. The field thus has four axes: comparative philosophical (Waldau and Patton), applied and food-related (Gross), Japanese ethnographic (Ambros), and Christian-feminist-historical (Hobgood-Oster).

The field also overlaps with religion and ecology, associated with Mary Evelyn Tucker and John Grim's Forum on Religion and Ecology (founded at Harvard and now based at Yale), since both widen the circle of moral concern beyond the human, although the two developed separately.

## Criticism and Limits

Critics argue that the field is influenced by an activist tendency that makes "the animal" a unified category, concealing the differences between an insect, a higher mammal, a domestic animal and a predator. They also argue that models of moral consideration borrowed from Western philosophy may be imposed on traditions that do not base ethics on individual rights. Coverage is also uneven: there is much on Christianity, Buddhism and Hinduism and little on the religions of Africa and the Native Americas, despite the abundance of material there. The gap between text (what the books say about animals) and practice (what people do in the slaughterhouse and on the farm) remains a methodological challenge, and calls for more fieldwork.
