# Contemporary Arab Intellectual Movements

An umbrella that gathers the currents of Arab thought from the Nahda of the late nineteenth century to the present: Arab nationalism and Nasserism, Arab leftist and socialist thought, Arab feminism, the critique of Orientalism and postcolonial theory, reformist Islamic modernism, and the democratization movements that followed the "Arab Spring."

## Major currents

- **Reformist Islamic modernism**: an extension of the Nahda project (Muhammad Abduh, Rashid Rida), which sought to reconcile religious reform with social modernization without a complete break with the Islamic frame of reference, and without a blind imitation of the Western model.
- **Nasserism and Arab nationalism**: a political-intellectual current associated with the Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser (president 1956–1970), resting on the principles of Arab unity, Arab socialism and non-alignment. Its intellectual influence outlasted Nasser's own death, and it remains a reference point in the discourse of sovereignty and national dignity in Arab political debate to this day.
- **Arab leftist thought**: communist and socialist parties and circles, organized from the 1920s (including the Egyptian Socialist Party of 1921 and the communist parties of Syria and Lebanon) and more visible in public life from the 1940s. They sometimes overlapped with Arab nationalism and sometimes disputed whether class or nation should come first.
- **Arab feminism**: an extension of Nahda debates on women (Qasim Amin's *The Liberation of Women*, and Huda Sha'rawi, who presided over the Egyptian Feminist Union established in 1923) toward later twentieth-century writers such as Nawal El Saadawi. In her writings (such as *Woman and Sex*, 1972) she linked the liberation of women to a critique of social, religious and medical structures alike, which exposed her books to confiscation and her person to repeated official harassment.
- **Critique of Orientalism and postcolonial theory**: Edward Said, a Palestinian-American critic based mainly in the United States, made *Orientalism* (New York: Pantheon, 1978) a reference point for Arab writers criticizing colonial representations of the East and rereading cultural identity after colonialism.
- **Contemporary democratization movements**: since the 1990s, and especially after the 2011 protests ("the Arab Spring"), a generation of thinkers and activists has emerged that examines the conditions of democratic transition and the relationship between religion, the state and civil society in the Arab context.

## The founding questions of the Nahda

The modern debate began with the question of backwardness and progress. Rifa'a al-Tahtawi (1801–1873) returned from Paris with *Takhlis al-ibriz fi talkhis Bariz* (*The Extraction of Pure Gold in the Summary of Paris*, Bulaq, 1834), calling for European sciences alongside a preserved identity. Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi wrote *Aqwam al-masalik fi ma'rifat ahwal al-mamalik* (*The Surest Path to Knowledge of the Condition of Kingdoms*, Tunis, 1867) on reform of the state. Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh followed. Albert Hourani, in *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age*, described a liberal age from 1798 to 1939, in which questions were set that the twentieth century answered in opposing ways: religion and the state, nation and homeland, reason and transmitted authority, and the position of women.

## Self-criticism after 1967

The defeat of 1967 pushed many intellectuals to criticize their own societies as well as the adversary. Constantine Zurayk had already written *Ma'na al-nakba* (*The Meaning of the Disaster*, 1948) on the Palestinian catastrophe. Sadik Jalal al-Azm published *al-Naqd al-dhati ba'd al-hazima* (*Self-Criticism after the Defeat*, Beirut: Dar al-Tali'a, 1968). Abdallah Laroui's *L'idéologie arabe contemporaine* (Paris: Maspero, 1967) had appeared in the same years. Muhammad Abid al-Jabri's *Critique of Arab Reason* began with *Takwin al-'aql al-'arabi* (1984). Mohammed Arkoun called for a critique of Islamic reason, and Hisham Sharabi analyzed patriarchal structure in *Neopatriarchy* (1988). Their methods differ. Al-Jabri divided the classical heritage into *bayan* (explication), *'irfan* (gnosis) and *burhan* (demonstration). Arkoun used anthropology and linguistics. Al-Azm defended a secular rationalism. Said's critique of Orientalism circulated in the same decades and was put to different uses.

## Political Islam versus secularism

Alongside these currents, political Islamic movements emerged, among them the Muslim Brotherhood, founded by Hasan al-Banna in 1928, and the writings of Sayyid Qutb in the 1950s and 1960s. They regarded the modern state as a threat to identity and called for an alternative project. Reformist and secular thinkers responded with differing arguments, and the conflict between the two currents continued in universities, the press and the constitutions of Arab states.

## The common thread

What unites these diverse currents is one recurring question: how the Arab and Islamic "authenticity" is to be redefined in the face of Western modernity and the colonial legacy, without the answer being either a copy of the West or a complete traditionalist closure. This explains the multiplicity of competing solutions (nationalist, leftist, reformist Islamic, feminist, liberal) that this era produced.

## Caveats

- This is an umbrella grouping currents of great political and ideological diversity. Placing them together does not imply intellectual homogeneity: these currents have often clashed directly (nationalism versus the left, Islamism versus secularism).
- The "contemporary" period is long and its boundaries are not settled; some scholars prefer to treat the period after 2011 as a separate phase.
