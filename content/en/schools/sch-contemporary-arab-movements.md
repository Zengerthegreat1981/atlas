# Contemporary Arab Intellectual Movements

An umbrella that gathers the currents of Arab thought from the Nahda of the late nineteenth century to the present: Arab nationalism and Nasserism, Arab leftist and socialist thought, Arab feminism, the critique of Orientalism and postcolonial theory, reformist Islamic modernism, and the democratization movements that followed the "Arab Spring."

## Major currents

- **Reformist Islamic modernism**: an extension of the Nahda project (Muhammad Abduh, Rashid Rida), which sought to reconcile religious reform with social modernization without a complete break with the Islamic frame of reference, and without a blind imitation of the Western model.
- **Nasserism and Arab nationalism**: a political-intellectual current associated with the Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser (president 1956–1970), resting on the principles of Arab unity, Arab socialism and non-alignment. Its intellectual influence outlasted Nasser's own death, and it remains a reference point in the discourse of sovereignty and national dignity in Arab political debate to this day.
- **Arab leftist thought**: Arab communist and socialist parties and movements from the 1940s onward, which at times intersected with Arab nationalism and at other times clashed with it over whether class or nation should take priority.
- **Arab feminism**: an extension of the roots of Nahda feminism (Qasim Amin's *The Liberation of Women*, and Huda Sha'arawi, founder of the Egyptian Feminist Union in 1923) toward the more radical voices of the twentieth century, such as Nawal El Saadawi. In her writings (such as *Woman and Sex*, 1972) she linked the liberation of women to a critique of social, religious and medical structures alike, which exposed her books to confiscation and her person to repeated official harassment.
- **Critique of Orientalism and postcolonial theory**: although Edward Said was not an activist "Arab" working within the region alone, his book *Orientalism* (1978) became a landmark reference point, widely used by Arab thinkers to critique the colonial image of the East and to reread Arab cultural identity after colonialism.
- **Contemporary democratization movements**: since the 1990s, and especially after the 2011 protests ("the Arab Spring"), a generation of thinkers and activists has emerged that examines the conditions of democratic transition and the relationship between religion, the state and civil society in the Arab context.

## The founding questions of the Nahda

The modern debate began with the question of backwardness and progress. Rifa'a al-Tahtawi (1801–1873) returned from Paris with *An Extraction of Gold in the Summary of Paris* (1834), calling for the adoption of European sciences while preserving identity. Khayr al-Din al-Tunisi wrote *The Surest Path to Knowledge of the Condition of Nations* (1867) on the reform of the state. Then came Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad Abduh. Albert Hourani, in *Arabic Thought in the Liberal Age*, described this phase as the liberal age extending from 1798 to 1939, in which the questions were set that the twentieth century answered in opposing ways: religion and the state, nation and homeland, reason and transmission, and the position of women.

## Self-criticism after 1967

The defeat of 1967 was a turning point that pushed intellectuals to criticize themselves rather than their adversary alone. Constantine Zurayk had written early on about the meaning of the Nakba in 1948. Then Sadiq Jalal al-Azm published *Self-Criticism After the Defeat* (1968), and Abdallah Laroui published *Contemporary Arab Ideology* (1967). Mohammed Abed al-Jabri's projects in the *Critique of Arab Reason* (beginning in 1984), Mohammed Arkoun's critique of Islamic reason, and Hisham Sharabi's critique of patriarchal structure followed. These thinkers differ in method: al-Jabri analyzes the structure of traditional reason into *bayan* (explanation), *irfan* (gnosis) and *burhan* (demonstration); Arkoun uses the tools of anthropology and linguistics; and al-Azm defends secularism. Their collective critique intersects with Edward Said's critique of Orientalism.

## Political Islam versus secularism

Alongside these currents, political Islamic movements emerged, among them the Muslim Brotherhood, founded by Hasan al-Banna in 1928, and the writings of Sayyid Qutb in the 1950s and 1960s. They regarded the modern state as a threat to identity and called for an alternative project. Reformist and secular thinkers responded with differing arguments, and the conflict between the two currents continued in universities, the press and the constitutions of Arab states.

## The common thread

What unites these diverse currents is one recurring question: how the Arab and Islamic "authenticity" is to be redefined in the face of Western modernity and the colonial legacy, without the answer being either a copy of the West or a complete traditionalist closure. This explains the multiplicity of competing solutions (nationalist, leftist, reformist Islamic, feminist, liberal) that this era produced.

## Caveats

- This is an umbrella grouping currents of great political and ideological diversity. Placing them together does not imply intellectual homogeneity: these currents have often clashed directly (nationalism versus the left, Islamism versus secularism).
- The "contemporary" period is long and its boundaries are not settled; some scholars prefer to treat the period after 2011 as a separate phase.
