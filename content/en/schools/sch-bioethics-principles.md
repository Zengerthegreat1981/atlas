# Bioethics Principles

## Meaning and Definition

Bioethics (biomedical ethics) is an applied field that examines the ethical questions arising from medical and biological advances: from experiments on human subjects, to informed consent, to the end of life, to genetic engineering. Its best-known systematic formulation is the four-principles framework devised by Tom Beauchamp and James Childress: respect for autonomy, non-maleficence, beneficence, and justice.

## Historical Context

The field emerged as an independent discipline in the late 1960s and early 1970s in response to major medical scandals. The most prominent was the Tuskegee study of syphilis among African American men in the United States (1932–1972), in which patients were denied a known treatment for decades, without their knowledge, so that the natural progression of the disease could be observed. Public reaction contributed to the National Research Act of 1974 and to the Belmont Report (1979), which set out the basic ethical principles (respect for persons, beneficence and justice) for research on human subjects. Beauchamp and Childress, with their book *Principles of Biomedical Ethics* (first edition 1979), gave the field its most widely used framework for teaching and discussion.

## Roots in Islamic Jurisprudence and Medical Ethics

Islamic jurisprudence formulated ethical controls for medical practice centuries earlier. Jurists established the maxims "harm must be removed" and "necessity is measured by its extent" as the basis for weighing a medical intervention against its risks. They required the patient's consent before treatment, except in cases of unconsciousness and compelling necessity, and prohibited mutilating the body, even after death. Contemporary juristic councils, such as the International Islamic Fiqh Academy and the Islamic Organization for Medical Sciences, have issued detailed resolutions on organ transplantation, resuscitation and its discontinuation, and the definition of brain death. They draw on the five higher objectives of the Shariah (preservation of religion, life, intellect, progeny, and property) as an objectives-based framework parallel to the four-principles framework. The source of obligation differs, however: for the jurists it is revealed law supported by public interest, whereas for Beauchamp and Childress the principles are drawn from a shared "common morality" and refined through specification, balancing and reflective equilibrium, without resting on a single religious source.

## Central Ideas and Thinkers

Beauchamp and Childress elaborate the four principles as "midlevel principles" that do not require commitment to a single ethical theory, whether Kantian or utilitarian. They are applied in practice and weighed against one another when they conflict, without a rigid hierarchical order. The framework has been criticized by virtue ethicists of medicine, such as Edmund Pellegrino, who argued that it neglects the character of the physician (such as compassion and honesty) in favor of procedures. Care ethicists and some feminist writers have argued that it gives too little weight to relationships and care, and others, such as K. Danner Clouser and Bernard Gert, have criticized it as lacking a unifying theory. Beauchamp and Childress have revised their text in response over successive editions.

## Impact and Application

The framework is applied in clinical ethics committees that evaluate the protocols of clinical trials, and in discussions of refusal of treatment, where respect for autonomy is weighed against the physician's duty of beneficence. One example is a competent adult patient who refuses a blood transfusion for religious reasons despite the risk to his life.

## Balancing the Principles in Conflict

Physicians face cases daily in which these four principles conflict. A competent patient may refuse life-saving treatment (autonomy versus beneficence), or the limited resources of a hospital may be contested between two patients (beneficence versus justice). The Beauchamp and Childress framework offers no rigid hierarchy among the principles; it leaves the weighing to the judgment of physicians and ethics committees in each particular case. This resembles the method of the jurists' "jurisprudence of balancing," which gives priority to one interest over another according to its rank among essentials, needs, and embellishments, without a mechanical rule applied automatically to every case.

## End-of-Life Questions

Among the most contested issues in this field are end-of-life questions. Is withdrawing life support from a patient with no prospect of recovery "mercy killing," which is forbidden, or a permissible allowance of natural death? Contemporary jurists distinguish between active mercy killing (administering a lethal substance), which is prohibited by the major juristic bodies, and withholding or withdrawing futile treatment, which some juristic councils have permitted under specified medical conditions (for example, resolutions of the International Islamic Fiqh Academy on resuscitation devices, 1986). Reasoning varies, but a common argument is that continuing resuscitation in such a state prolongs the process of dying rather than sustaining life. Positions differ among scholars and councils, especially over the definition of death. This distinction intersects methodologically with the distinction in Western medical ethics between "killing" and "letting die."

## Animal Research Ethics

A parallel debate branches from this framework concerning the ethics of using animals in medical experiments before treatments are applied to humans, where the interests of medical progress are balanced against the welfare of living creatures. Research institutions have adopted the "Three Rs" principle, formulated by W. M. S. Russell and R. L. Burch in *The Principles of Humane Experimental Technique* (1959): replacement with non-animal methods wherever possible, reduction of the number of animals used, and refinement of their conditions to reduce suffering. This intersects with the Islamic prohibition of torturing animals without genuine necessity.
