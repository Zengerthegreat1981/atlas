# Communitarianism

## Definition

A philosophical and political current that arose in the Anglo-American world during the 1980s as a critical response to individualist liberalism as formulated by John Rawls in *A Theory of Justice* (1971). The pioneers of this current (Alasdair MacIntyre, Michael Sandel, Charles Taylor, Michael Walzer, and Amitai Etzioni) share a critique of the idea of the "unencumbered self" that liberals assume behind the "veil of ignorance," proposing instead a conception of a self rooted in its society, history, and traditions.

## Core Theses

The communitarians hold that an individual's moral identity is not built from nothing but is formed through belonging to actual communities (the family, the nation, the religious community, the local society), which give the individual a moral language and a framework of meaning prior to any individual choice. Hence, for them, the concept of the "good" cannot be separated from shared practices and traditions, as the liberals claim when they make the state neutral toward differing conceptions of the good. In *After Virtue* (1981) MacIntyre criticized this neutrality as an illusion that conceals the absence of a coherent moral framework after the collapse of the Aristotelian traditions, while Sandel argued in *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) that Rawls presupposes a self prior to its ends, a conception he argued is untenable.

## Major Figures and Contributions

- **Alasdair MacIntyre**: In *After Virtue* (1981), MacIntyre argued that modern moral discourse consists of fragmented survivals from an Aristotelian-Christian framework whose teleological foundation has collapsed, rendering ethical disputes interminable. He defined a "practice" as a cooperative activity with internal standards of excellence, grounding virtues in the narrative unity of human life. In *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988), he developed the concept of tradition-constituted inquiry. MacIntyre has noted reservations about the label "communitarian," viewing his project as a comprehensive critique of liberal modernity rather than an internal reform.
- **Michael Sandel**: In *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982), Sandel critiqued Rawls's deontological liberalism for presupposing an "unencumbered self"—a subject conceived as prior to and independent of its constitutive ends and community ties. In *Democracy's Discontent* (1996) and *Justice* (2009), Sandel argued that procedural liberalism has hollowed out the public square, championing a politics of civic virtue and moral engagement.
- **Charles Taylor**: In *Sources of the Self* (1989) and his landmark essay "The Politics of Recognition" (1992), Taylor offered a Hegelian critique of atomism, arguing that human agency and identity are dialogical—formed through interaction with and recognition by significant others. He defended differential minority rights (such as language protections in Quebec), arguing that institutional neutrality can inflict harm by misrecognizing minority identities.
- **Michael Walzer**: In *Spheres of Justice* (1983), Walzer proposed "complex equality," maintaining that distinct social goods (wealth, political office, education, health care) possess distinct social meanings and internal distributive criteria. Injustice occurs when an advantage in one sphere is improperly converted into dominance in another (e.g., wealth purchasing political power).
- **Amitai Etzioni and Responsive Communitarianism**: Developing a sociological and policy-oriented branch, Etzioni founded the Communitarian Network in 1993 and the journal *The Responsive Community* (1991), advocating a practical equilibrium between individual rights and social responsibilities in family life, education, and civic institutions.

## The Liberal-Communitarian Debate

In *Political Liberalism* (1993), John Rawls responded to communitarian criticisms by emphasizing that his theory was not intended as a metaphysical doctrine of human nature, but as a "political" conception of justice designed to secure an overlapping consensus among diverse, reasonable comprehensive doctrines. Communitarians countered that the state's supposed neutrality inevitably embodies substantive, contested normative commitments. Sociological treatments, notably Robert Bellah and colleagues' *Habits of the Heart* (1985), explored how radical individualism weakens democratic commitment and social solidarity in modern American society.

## Global Reception and Comparative Perspectives

Beyond the Anglo-American academy, communitarian themes resonated with non-Western intellectual traditions. Daniel A. Bell (*Communitarianism and Its Critics*, 1993; *East Meets West*, 2000) explored intersections between communitarian thought and East Asian Confucian ethics, while noting the risk of authoritarian co-optation by proponents of "Asian values." In the Arab-Islamic sphere, scholars have drawn parallels between communitarian critiques of atomism and traditional concepts such as *ummah* (community), *shūrā* (consultation), and *maṣlaḥah* (public interest), comparing MacIntyre's account of tradition-constituted rationality to the jurisprudential continuity of Islamic legal traditions (*fiqh*).

## Criticism and Limitations

Critics argue that communitarianism risks justifying oppressive, patriarchal, or exclusionary customs under the mantle of cultural preservation. Liberal theorists maintain that without robust protections for individual rights and personal autonomy, individuals—particularly dissenters and vulnerable subgroups—are left defenseless against majority tyranny. Furthermore, critics note that modern societies are irreducibly pluralistic, making the consensus on a single "shared good" envisioned by communitarian theory difficult to sustain without state coercion.
