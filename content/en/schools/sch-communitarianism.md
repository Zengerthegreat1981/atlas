# Communitarianism

## Definition

A philosophical and political current that arose in the Anglo-American world during the 1980s as a critical response to individualist liberalism as formulated by John Rawls in *A Theory of Justice* (1971). The pioneers of this current (Alasdair MacIntyre, Michael Sandel, Charles Taylor, Michael Walzer, and Amitai Etzioni) share a critique of the idea of the "unencumbered self" that liberals assume behind the "veil of ignorance," proposing instead a conception of a self rooted in its society, history, and traditions.

## Core Theses

The communitarians hold that an individual's moral identity is not built from nothing but is formed through belonging to actual communities (the family, the nation, the religious community, the local society), which give the individual a moral language and a framework of meaning prior to any individual choice. Hence, for them, the concept of the "good" cannot be separated from shared practices and traditions, as the liberals claim when they make the state neutral toward differing conceptions of the good. In *After Virtue* (1981) MacIntyre criticized this neutrality as an illusion that conceals the absence of a coherent moral framework after the collapse of the Aristotelian traditions, while Sandel argued in *Liberalism and the Limits of Justice* (1982) that Rawls presupposes a self prior to its ends, a conception that is in fact impossible.

## Internal Diversity

The communitarians do not form a unified school. Taylor offers a Hegelian critique of individualism tied to the philosophy of language and recognition; Walzer develops a theory of multiple "spheres of justice" tied to different social contexts; and Etzioni leans toward a practical, policy-oriented formulation on which he founded the "communitarian movement" in the 1990s, calling for a balance between individual rights and social responsibilities.

## Criticism and Influence

Its opponents have accused communitarianism of possibly justifying the preservation of oppressive customs in the name of tradition, and of offering no clear criterion for distinguishing good communities from bad ones. Nevertheless it has deeply influenced contemporary civic republicanism and the debates on multiculturalism and recognition in Taylor and Axel Honneth, and it has remained a principal party to the debate with Rawlsian liberalism to this day.

## Taylor and the Interpretive Politics of Identity

In his essay "The Politics of Recognition" (1992), Charles Taylor made a pivotal contribution to communitarianism, arguing that individual identity is formed dialogically through interaction with others, and that withholding recognition from a particular culture or group inflicts real psychological and social harm on its members, not a mere neutral omission. This argument founded the intersection of communitarianism with debates on multiculturalism and the rights of national minorities, as Taylor defended the legitimacy of differential policies (such as protecting the French language in Quebec) that go beyond the strict liberal principle of neutrality.

## The Policy-Oriented Communitarian Movement

Unlike the academic philosophers, Amitai Etzioni developed a practical version aimed at public policy, founding the "Communitarian Network" in 1990 and the journal *The Responsive Community*. He called for a balance between individual rights and social responsibilities through concrete policies in education, health, and the family, far from the purely philosophical abstraction that characterized the academic debate of MacIntyre and Sandel.

## Communitarianism Outside the Anglo-American Context

The theses of communitarianism found an echo in similar debates outside the Anglo-American world, such as the debate on the role of the community and tradition in contemporary Confucian thought, and in attempts to reconcile liberal individualism with traditional social values in Asian and Arab contexts. As a result, some elements of the communitarian critique of Western individualism became a borrowed reference in non-Western philosophical debates about identity and belonging.

## MacIntyre and the Return of the Virtues and Traditions

In *After Virtue* (1981), Alasdair MacIntyre builds a historical argument: modern moral language is a fragmented remnant of an Aristotelian-Christian context that has lost its framework, and so moral disagreements have become interminable by rational means. He defines a "practice" as a cooperative activity with internal standards of excellence and goods internal to it, and he ties the virtues to the attainment of these goods and to the "narrative unity" of an individual's life. In *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?* (1988) he develops the idea of "tradition-constituted rationality": argument proceeds within a tradition that is tested by its ability to resolve its internal problems and overcome its epistemological crises. MacIntyre himself refused to describe himself as a communitarian, because he wants a critique of liberal modernity as a whole, not a reform of it.

## Sandel and Walzer

Sandel deepens his critique in *Democracy's Discontent* (1996) and then *Justice* (2009), holding that liberal proceduralism has hollowed out civic life and left a void filled by markets and narrow forms of identity, and he defends a "republic of civic virtue." Walzer, in *Spheres of Justice* (1983), proposes that every social good, such as money, office, education, and health, has a shared meaning that determines its principle of distribution, and that injustice is a good's transgression of its sphere, as when money buys offices. He thus builds a pluralist justice based on shared understanding rather than a single universal formula.

## The Debate with Liberalism

In *Political Liberalism* (1993), Rawls replied that he had not offered his theory as a metaphysics of the self but as a political conception seeking an overlapping consensus among reasonable comprehensive doctrines. He held that the communitarians confuse the political self with the metaphysical self. In turn, the communitarians pointed out that the neutrality sought implicitly carries normative content. The debate has remained open, to the point that many philosophers now see the disagreement as less radical than it seemed in the 1980s, and as a disagreement of degree between stressing autonomy and stressing belonging. The most prominent product of the movement in public life was the book *Habits of the Heart* (1985) by Robert Bellah and his colleagues, on individualism and commitment in American life.

## Communitarian Philosophy Outside the West

Daniel Bell, in *Communitarianism and Its Critics* (1993) and *East Meets West* (2000), took an interest in the link between communitarianism and Confucian thought and in what has been called "Asian values." He noted that Western formulations do not match the Asian tradition and that using them may justify state authoritarianism. In the Islamic context, the discussion draws on the concepts of the ummah, shura, and the public interest (maslaha), and researchers compare MacIntyre with the traditions of jurisprudence (fiqh) as interconnected traditions of inquiry whose rationality forms within the interpretive community. This is an analytical comparison and does not imply historical influence.
