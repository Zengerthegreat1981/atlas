# Language Evolution

A multidisciplinary research field (linguistics, evolutionary biology and cognitive anthropology) that asks how and why the capacity for language evolved in humans specifically and not in other species, and what evolutionary mechanisms produced this capacity. The modern field is often dated from Pinker and Bloom's 1990 argument that the language faculty evolved by natural selection.

## The question of specialized adaptation

An influential article by Hauser, Chomsky and Fitch (2002) raised a central question: did the "faculty of language in the narrow sense" (Faculty of Language — Narrow), specifically the property of recursion, evolve as a biological adaptation specialized for language alone? Or do most components of language share their basis with other general cognitive abilities, with only a very narrow component specific to language? This proposal met direct criticism from Steven Pinker and Ray Jackendoff (2005), who questioned the uniqueness of recursion and the narrowness of what is truly specific to language.

## The usage-based alternative

Michael Tomasello offers an alternative explanatory path: the origin of language is not a separate anatomical mutation for grammar, but the product of the evolution of a deeper social capacity, "shared intentionality," which allowed humans to cooperate and communicate symbolically in ways absent in other great apes.

## Comparative approaches

The field also relies on comparative studies of communicative abilities in non-human primates and songbirds, seeking to determine which elements of the human language capacity are truly unique and which are evolutionarily shared with other species. Among the most prominent of these studies are attempts to teach chimpanzees sign language or artificial symbols. The most famous is Herbert Terrace's study of the chimpanzee "Nim Chimpsky" (a pun on Noam Chomsky's name) in the 1970s. Terrace concluded that Nim's linguistic production was essentially a responsive imitation of his trainers' signals, not genuine generative grammatical use in the human sense. This conclusion provoked wide debate about the limits of the ability of non-human primates to acquire a grammatical linguistic system.

## The gestural origin hypothesis

Michael Corballis and other researchers propose that human language may have begun essentially with a manual gestural medium (the Gestural Origin Hypothesis), before gradually shifting to the vocal-articulatory medium. This is based on the flexibility of manual motor control in primates, compared with the limited voluntary control of their vocalization, and on the existence of contemporary human sign languages as fully grammatical linguistic systems that use the manual medium as a complete alternative to the vocal medium.

## FOXP2 and the KE family

The identification of a mutation in FOXP2 in the British "KE family" (Lai, Fisher, Monaco, and colleagues, 2001) provided rare genetic evidence bearing on a developmental speech and language disorder. The mutation is associated with an inherited impairment of speech articulation and of aspects of language across generations. FOXP2 is widely discussed as an early case of a gene tied to such a disorder. Simon Fisher and colleagues have studied its neural roles, while cautioning against treating it as a single "language gene," since it participates in regulating broader neural networks.

## The social grooming and gossip hypothesis

Robin Dunbar proposes an alternative social evolutionary explanation: language arose as an effective substitute for the physical "social grooming" rituals used by other primates to maintain the cohesion of the social group. But these physical rituals limit the size of the group that can be kept cohesive, since one can groom no more than one or two individuals at a time. Language, and in particular social "gossip" (the exchange of information about who does what with whom), allowed much larger human groups to maintain their cohesion through a vocal medium that can be directed to several individuals at once.

## The iterated learning model

Simon Kirby offers a different experimental and computational approach: the "Iterated Learning Model," which simulates the transmission of a linguistic system across successive generations of learners (humans in laboratory experiments, or computational agents). It shows that the structural properties of language (such as compositionality, meaning built from meaningful parts) arise spontaneously as a product of the repeated cultural transmission between generations themselves, not necessarily as a purely innate biological adaptation, since the patterns that are easier to learn and transmit across successive generations are favored.

## Shared intentionality and symbolic cooperation

Tomasello's argument rests on careful experimental comparisons between young human children and great apes (chimpanzees and bonobos) in simple cooperation and gestural communication tasks. These show that children spontaneously understand the cooperative intention of a signal (such as a finger point to direct a partner's attention, not to request something for themselves) at a very early age. Great apes, by contrast, generally lack this purely cooperative communicative motivation. Tomasello sees this as the true evolutionary foundation that later paved the way for the emergence of complex symbolic language.
