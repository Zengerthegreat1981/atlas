# Theories of Myth in Comparative Religion

A multi-stranded current that addresses one question: what is the function of myth, and does it carry a structure or meaning that goes beyond its apparent narrative content? This field brings together distinct orientations that converge on this question and differ radically in their answers: structuralism (Lévi-Strauss), popular Jungian interpretation (Campbell), and the philosophy of working on myth (Blumenberg), alongside phenomenology (Eliade) and functionalist explanation (Malinowski). It is distinguished from the early evolutionary theories of Tylor, Frazer and Müller in that it treats myth as a semantic system that produces meaning, not as a mistaken primitive science or a linguistic disease.

## Myth in Arab Culture: "Tales of the Ancients"

The concept of myth was not easily accepted in Arab culture, because the Qur'an reports the accusation of the polytheists that what the Prophet brought was "tales of the ancients" (*asatir al-awwalin*; Al-An'am 25, Al-Anfal 31, An-Nahl 24, Al-Mu'minun 83), so the word in religious usage became synonymous with superstition and falsehood. Some conservative authors drew on this to reject any description of the Qur'anic narratives as mythic. In modern Arabic studies a distinction has been drawn between the pejorative usage and the scientific concept: Firas al-Sawah, in *Mughamarat al-'Aql al-Ula: Dirasa fi al-Ustura, Suriya wa-Bilad al-Rafidayn* ("The First Adventure of the Mind: A Study of Myth, Syria and Mesopotamia", 1976), presented a reading of Syrian and Mesopotamian myths as humanity's first attempts to explain existence and as the origin of the later religions, and Sayyid al-Qimni, in *al-Ustura wa-l-Turath* ("Myth and Heritage"), examined the effect of myth on the Arab heritage. These writings provoked sharp debate between those who see myth as a semantic product of the community and those who see describing the sacred in such terms as an affront to it. They showed that the field does not mean "falsehood" but "founding narrative", and that Islamic societies themselves produce founding narratives (the stories of the prophets, the Isra' and Mi'raj, and the stories of the saints) that deserve analysis with the tools of this field.

## Lévi-Strauss: Myth as a Logical Structure

In *Mythologiques* (four volumes, 1964–1971), Claude Lévi-Strauss applied his structural method, originally developed for studying kinship systems, to hundreds of Native American myths. His thesis: myth is not a random story but a logical apparatus that processes contradictions that cannot be resolved in reality (such as the contradiction between nature and culture, or life and death) through symbolic mediators, such as fire, which mediates the raw and the cooked. For him, myth is not read in the linear order of its narration; it is broken down into "mythemes" (mythic units) that are compared across multiple versions to reveal the underlying structure. He first set out the method in "The Structural Study of Myth" (1955), using the Oedipus myth as his example.

## Campbell: The Monomyth and the Psychological Journey

In *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949), Joseph Campbell offered a counter-model: a single psychological narrative structure (the "monomyth") that recurs across the world's myths, namely the hero's journey from the call to adventure to the return bearing the elixir. Campbell drew this model from a blend of Jungian archetypes and rites of passage. It is a method more widely popular than Lévi-Strauss's but less rigorous methodologically, and it has been criticised by historians of religion on the grounds that it crushes cultural and historical diversity into a presumed universal psychological template.

## Blumenberg: Myth as Continuing Cultural Work

In *Work on Myth* (1979), Hans Blumenberg opposed both of the preceding directions from a philosophical angle: myth is not a ready-made logical structure nor a single psychological pattern, but a continuing cultural mechanism of work by which humanity confronts the anxiety of exposure before a reality without ready-made meaning, converting what he called the "absolutism of reality" into names and stories. This work does not stop at modernity but continues in new forms, as appears in the reappropriation of the Prometheus myth by Goethe and others.

## Eliade and Malinowski: Sacred Myth and Charter

In *The Myth of the Eternal Return* (1949; English translation 1954) and *Myth and Reality* (1963), Mircea Eliade linked myth to sacred time: it tells how reality came into being through supernatural beings in the "first times", and ritual reactivates that time. Bronisław Malinowski, by contrast, in *Myth in Primitive Psychology* (1926), saw myth as a "charter" that lends legitimacy to existing customs and institutions, so that it is functional rather than explanatory. Rudolf Bultmann (1941) introduced the notion of "demythologisation" of the New Testament, to reinterpret its mythical language existentially.

## Segal's Methodological Critique of Campbell

In *Theorizing about Myth* (1999) and *Myth: A Very Short Introduction*, Robert A. Segal directed a precise methodological critique at Campbell's model: a confusion between observing a superficial structural similarity among distant myths and explaining this similarity by a single universal Jungian psychological origin, a leap for which Campbell offers no sufficient evidence. Rather than discarding the model entirely, Segal proposes reclassifying it as a useful literary-pedagogical tool rather than a rigorous scientific theory.

## The Intersection with Sacrifice and Ritual (Girard)

This current intersects with René Girard's thesis (sacrifice and the mechanism of the scapegoat as the origin of both myth and religious ritual), which makes "theories of myth" and "theories of ritual" two faces of a single question: does ritual precede myth, or the reverse? The field has no decisive consensus on this order. On the other side, Talal Asad, in *Genealogies of Religion* (1993), pushes toward examining the category "religion" itself as a Western historical construction, which places all such theories under the question of the limits of generalisation.
